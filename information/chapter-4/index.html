Mutual information · Three faces of one quantity

Mutual information is built three ways that give one number: uncertainty before minus after, divergence of the joint table from independence, and shared entropy. It is never negative, zero only under independence, symmetric, and bounded by each entropy; a table of alpha power and heart rate gives 0.296 bits. Plug-in estimates are biased upward, so values need context.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the ballroom. This is chapter 4 and the central quantity of the whole series. In a ballroom, couples move together. If you know where one dancer is, you can make a good guess about the other. How good a guess in bits is the mutual information. Today we build it three ways and discover that the three constructions are one and the same quantity.

Start with intuition. Two variables X and Y. Before you look at either you are uncertain about Y. That uncertainty is its entropy. Now someone tells you x. Your remaining uncertainty about Y is the conditional entropy of Y given X. The drop, the uncertainty that knowing X removed, is the information X carries about Y.

That is the first face. Mutual information, written I of X semicolon Y, equals the entropy of Y minus the entropy of Y given X. Uncertainty before minus uncertainty after.

Let's compute it on our illustrative table from chapter 1. Alpha power high or low. Heart rate high or low. Cells 45, 15, 5, and 35%. The entropy of heart rate is 1 bit from its 50-50 margin. The conditional entropy of heart rate given alpha from episode 2.4 is about 0.704 bits. The difference about 0.296 bits. Knowing alpha removes about three-tenths of a bit of uncertainty about heart rate.

Now the second face from chapter 3. Mutual information is the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the joint distribution from the product of the marginals. In words, how far the true table is from the table you would expect if the two variables were independent.

Here is the calculation in one picture. The margins are 60-40 for alpha and 50-50 for heart rate. Under independence, the cells would be 30, 30, 20, and 20%. The real cells are 45, 15, 5, and 35. For each cell, take the log of real over independent, weighted by the real probability and sum. The result, about 0.296 bits. The same number.

And the third face. Mutual information equals the entropy of X plus the entropy of Y minus their joint entropy. 0.971 plus 1 minus 1.675. Again, about 0.296 bits. In the two-circle picture of episode 2.4, this is the overlap, the part the two entropies share.

Three constructions, one number. In nats, the same quantity is about 0.205. That is not coincidence. Each face follows from the others by the chain rule of entropy in two lines of algebra. And each face tells you something different about the quantity. Here they are. The chain rule says that H of X and Y equals H of X plus H of Y given X. Rearrange, H of Y minus H of Y given X equals H of X plus H of Y minus H of X and Y. The first face becomes the third. And writing the third face as a sum over cells gives exactly the divergence formula of the second.

The first face says mutual information is a reduction in uncertainty. It is measured in the same units as entropy, bits, or nats, and it can never exceed the entropy of either variable. Knowing X cannot remove more uncertainty about Y than Y had to begin with. In our table, alpha has an entropy of about 0.971 bits and heart rate 1 bit, so no relationship between them, however strong, could exceed 0.971 bits. Our 0.296 bits is about 30% of that ceiling.

The second face says mutual information is a divergence from independence. Since a divergence is never negative, mutual information is never negative. And since the divergence is zero only when the two distributions are equal, mutual information is zero if and only if the joint equals the product of the marginals, if and only if X and Y are independent. That is the property correlation lacks, as episode 1.4 showed.

The third face says mutual information is symmetric. The formula treats X and Y in exactly the same way. The information X carries about Y equals the information Y carries about X. There is no direction in mutual information. Direction will need other tools in chapter 8.

There is a special case worth noticing. The information a variable carries about itself is its entire entropy, I of X semicolon X equals H of X. That is why entropy is sometimes called self-information.

A word on how neuroscientists use the first face. Show a participant 2 stimuli equally often. The stimulus entropy is one bit. Record a response, say the amplitude of an evoked potential, cut into a few categories. The mutual information between stimulus and response is how much the response reduces uncertainty about which stimulus was shown. One bit would mean perfect discrimination from a single trial. Zero would mean the response carries nothing about the stimulus. Real values sit in between, and how far in between is the question.

Now look inside the second phase, cell by cell. Each cell contributes log of real over independent. For the alpha high heart rate high cell, the ratio is 45 over 30, and its log base 2 is about plus 0.58 bits. This combination happens more often than independence predicts. For the alpha low heart rate high cell, the ratio is 5 over 20, and its log is minus 2 bits. This combination is rarer than independence predicts. These cell-by-cell values are called pointwise or local mutual information. They can be negative even though their average the mutual information never is. Chapter 7 builds on them.

What is mutual information sensitive to? Three things to keep in mind from the start.

First, it depends on the joint distribution and only on it. Not on the values themselves. Relabel the categories or transform a continuous variable in any invertible way and the mutual information does not change. That is the subject of the next-but-one episode.

Second, when estimated from data, it inherits all the estimation problems of entropies and adds its own. Plug-in mutual information is biased upward. On two independent signals, a finite sample always finds a little information. The fewer the samples and the more categories, the larger this phantom information. Chapters 5 and 6 are devoted to this.

Third, it is a property of a pair of random variables defined by a question. Alpha high or low versus alpha power in 10 bins versus alpha power as a continuous value. Three questions, three different mutual informations with heart rate.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports that two signals share 0.3 bits and concludes they are strongly coupled. Strong compared with what? 0.3 bits out of a possible one if the signals were cut in two. Out of a possible five if they were cut into 32 bins. A mutual information value only makes sense alongside the entropies it is bounded by, the number of samples, and a null distribution built from data with no dependence. A number of bits on its own is not an effect size. Report it with the entropies of both variables, the number of samples, and the null distribution so that readers can judge it. Episode 6.1 comes back to this.

The companion notebook computes the three faces on the illustrative table, checks that they agree, computes the pointwise values, and shows the upward bias of the plug-in estimate on independent data.

To recap, mutual information has three faces. Uncertainty before minus after, H of Y minus H of Y given X. Divergence from independence, the KL divergence of the joint from the product of the margins. Shared entropy, H of X plus H of Y minus H of X and Y. It is never negative, zero exactly under independence, symmetric, and bounded by the entropy of each variable. Its pointwise values can be negative. And estimated from data, it is biased upward.

PLATE 4.28:06

MI vs correlation

Three dancing couples, scored with correlation and mutual information, expose the difference. Pure noise scores near zero on both and a diagonal scores high on both, but a parabola scores zero correlation and the highest mutual information. Correlation measures only linear dependence, while mutual information detects any dependence; for Gaussian signals the two tell the same story.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the ballroom. Tonight, three couples share the dance floor and we are going to ask a simple question about each of them. If I know where one dancer is, what do I learn about the other?

Neuroscience asks this question all the time. Does this electrode tell me something about that one? Does the heart tell me something about the brain? The usual tool is the correlation coefficient. Today, we put it next to mutual information on the same data and watch where they agree and where they part ways.

Let's lay the dance floor out as a plot. Each footprint is one moment in time. The horizontal position is the first dancer, X. The vertical position is the second dancer, Y. 10,000 footprints per couple.

This example comes from a 2005 paper by Cellucci, Albano, and Rapp in Physical Review E. In all three cases, X simply walks from minus 3 to plus 3. Only the partner changes.

Couple A dances alone. The partner Y is pure random noise, completely independent of X. The footprints form a shapeless cloud.

Couple B dances in step. The partner copies X plus a little noise. The footprints line up along a diagonal.

Couple C does something more interesting. The partner follows X squared plus the same little noise. When X steps out to the left, Y rises. When X steps out to the right, Y rises too. The footprints draw a parabola.

Now let's score each couple twice. First with Pearson's correlation r. Then with mutual information in bits.

Couple A. The correlation is minus 0.0037. Essentially 0. The mutual information is 0.136 bits. Small and as we will see not significant.

Couple B. The correlation is 0.9934. Almost perfect. The mutual information is 2.92 bits. Both measures agree, these two move together.

Couple C. The correlation is 0.0001. 0. The mutual information is 3.03 bits. The highest of the three.

Read that again. The couple whose partner is almost completely determined by x gets a correlation of zero. If we had looked only at r we would have written, no relationship.

Why does correlation miss it? Correlation fits the best straight line through the cloud and asks how tightly the points hug it. On the parabola, the left half goes down and the right half goes up. The two slopes cancel. The best straight line is flat so r says zero. Correlation measures linear dependence and only linear dependence.

Mutual information asks a different question. Remember its first face from the last episode? The uncertainty about y minus the uncertainty about y that remains once x is known.

Look at y on its own. On the parabola, y can be anywhere between 0 and 9. That is a lot of uncertainty.

Now slice the floor at one value of x. Inside that thin slice, y is pinned down to a narrow band, the width of the noise. Knowing x has removed almost all the uncertainty about y.

Do this for every slice and average. That average reduction in uncertainty is the mutual information. It does not care whether the relationship is a line, a curve, or a zigzag. It only asks, does knowing one narrow down the other?

A word on the unit. With logarithms in base 2, mutual information is counted in bits. One bit is one fair yes or no question answered. Three bits is roughly like going from eight equally likely possibilities down to one. So the parabola couple at three bits is a couple where watching one dancer tells you a great deal about the other.

Where does this happen in the brain? Take a slow rhythm and a fast rhythm. Often the fast rhythm grows strong at one particular phase of the slow rhythm and weak at the opposite phase. Plot the fast amplitude against the slow phase and you get a bump not a line. A correlation between the two can come out near zero depending on where you cut the phase circle. A measure built on information theory sees the bump directly. That is the idea behind the modulation index used for phase-amplitude coupling, which we meet again in chapter 3.

The same thing happens between body and brain. Physiological relationships are rarely straight lines. They saturate, they have thresholds, they peak and fall. Every time the relationship bends, correlation underestimates it and mutual information does not.

So mutual information is zero only when the two variables are truly independent. Correlation can be zero even when they are strongly dependent.

One more shared property. Mutual information is symmetric. What x tells you about y is exactly what y tells you about x. Correlation is symmetric too. So neither of them says who leads and who follows. That question waits for chapter 8.

Is correlation useless then? Not at all. There is one important case where the two tell exactly the same story.

If x and y are jointly Gaussian, the whole relationship is captured by the correlation rho. And the mutual information has a closed form. It is minus one half times the log of 1 minus rho squared.

Let's put numbers on it in bits. A correlation of 0.5 gives 0.21 bits. A correlation of 0.9 gives 1.2 bits. A correlation of 0.99 gives 2.8 bits.

Notice the shape of this curve. For weak correlations, the mutual information stays tiny. As rho approaches 1, it climbs without bound. A perfect linear relationship between continuous variables would carry infinite information.

So for Gaussian signals, mutual information adds nothing that the correlation did not already know. It is just a different scale for the same thing. Mutual information earns its keep when the relationship is not linear or the signals are not Gaussian.

What is mutual information sensitive to? Any form of dependence, linear or not, monotonic or not. That is its strength.

What does it give up? Three things. It has no sign, a rising curve and a falling curve can carry the same information. It has no shape, it tells you how much you learn not how. And it is much harder to estimate than a correlation.

That last point is already hiding in our table. Couple A is pure noise. Its true mutual information is exactly zero. Yet the estimate came out at 0.136 bits. The authors' own test says this value is not significant. It is estimation error, and mutual information estimates from finite data tend to land above zero. Chapter 5 is entirely about this.

Here is the classic trap in both directions. First, a correlation near zero does not mean no relationship. Plot the data and compute a measure that can see curves. Second, a positive mutual information does not tell you what the relationship looks like. Always look at the scatter plot, and always compare to a null distribution before calling a value real.

In the notebook for this episode, you will rebuild the three couples, compute r and mutual information yourself, and compare the estimates to the true values which we can compute exactly for these simulations.

To recap, correlation measures straight lines. Mutual information measures any kind of dependence. For Gaussian signals, they are two scales for the same thing. And mutual information comes with a price. It must be estimated with care.

PLATE 4.37:29

Invariance · What MI ignores

Mutual information does not change under any invertible transformation of either signal, such as a change of unit, a logarithm, a cube or a replacement by ranks, while correlation does. The stretch factors cancel between the joint and marginal densities, so only the copula matters. Non-invertible steps such as squaring or binning can only lose information, and estimators should be checked for the invariance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the ballroom. Today a property that sounds technical and turns out to be the most practical thing about mutual information. Change the scale of a signal, take its logarithm, replace its values by their ranks. The mutual information with another signal does not move. Correlation moves. Differential entropy moves. Mutual information stays put. By the end of this episode you will see why, where the property stops, and how an entire family of estimators is built on it.

Start with intuition. Two dancers move together. Film the dance and measure the position of the first dancer in centimetres, in inches, or on a strange curved ruler that stretches near the walls. The second dancer's position is still exactly as predictable from the first as before. Relabelling the floor does not change how the couple moves. Mutual information measures the coupling, not the labels.

Here is the property stated precisely. If you apply an invertible transformation to X and possibly a different invertible transformation to Y, the mutual information between them is unchanged. Invertible means that you could undo the transformation, recovering the original values exactly. Multiplying by a non-zero constant, adding a constant, taking a logarithm of a positive signal, cubing and replacing values by their ranks, when there are no ties, are all invertible.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Two signals simulated with a Gaussian dependence, a correlation of 0.8. The true mutual information is minus one-half log of one minus 0.8 squared, about 0.737 bits.

Now transform the first signal and estimate the mutual information each time with a nearest neighbour estimator on 20,000 samples. Original about 0.734 bits. Multiplied by one millionth as if exported in volts instead of microvolts. 0.734. Exponentiated 0.738. Cubed 0.736. Replaced by ranks 0.731. Within estimation noise the same value every time.

Now look at the correlation for the same transformations. Original 0.8, exponentiated about 0.61, cubed about 0.62, ranks about 0.78. Correlation changes with every nonlinear transformation because it only measures how well a straight line fits and a nonlinear transformation bends the cloud.

Why does mutual information stay put? For discrete variables, the answer is almost obvious. An invertible transformation just relabels the categories, and the joint table keeps exactly the same probabilities in permuted cells. For continuous variables, recall the last two chapters. A transformation of X stretches the density of X and changes its differential entropy by the average log of the stretch. But it stretches the joint density of X and Y by exactly the same amount. Mutual information is the entropy of X plus the entropy of Y minus the joint entropy, and the two stretches cancel. Equivalently, mutual information is a KL divergence built from a ratio of densities, and the stretch factors cancel in the ratio.

Now an important limit. The transformation must be invertible. Square the first signal instead and information is lost. From the square, you can no longer tell a negative value from a positive one. In our simulation, the mutual information drops from about 0.74 bits to about 0.28 bits. You can never gain information by transforming a variable, only keep it or lose it. Episode 4.5 turns this into a theorem, the data processing inequality.

There is a deeper consequence. Since any invertible transformation of each variable leaves mutual information unchanged, you may as well transform each variable to have the same, convenient distribution. Replace each signal by its ranks, and you have a uniform distribution for each. What remains is purely the way the two signals are tied together. The dependence structure which statisticians call the copula. Mutual information depends only on the copula, not on the individual distributions. Chapter 5 builds an entire estimator on this. Transform each signal to ranks then to a Gaussian and apply the closed-form Gaussian formula. That is the Gaussian copula estimator.

What is this sensitive to? First, invertibility as we just saw. A transformation that merges values like squaring, rounding, thresholding into high and low, or clipping discards information. Binning a continuous signal is such a transformation. It can only lower the mutual information, never raise it, in the true distribution.

Second, the estimator. The invariance is a property of the true quantity. Estimators do not always share it. A histogram estimator with equal-width bins gives different answers after a logarithm, because the bins land differently. Nearest neighbour estimators measure distances, so in practice each variable is usually standardized or rank-transformed before estimation. A good estimator should respect the invariance, and it is worth checking that yours does.

Here is the classic pitfall. Two analyses of the same data, one on raw power and one on log power, report different coupling strengths with another signal, and the difference is interpreted as meaningful. If the measure is correlation, a difference is expected, and says only that the relationship is not a straight line in both scales. If the measure is mutual information, the true values are identical, and any difference is an artefact of the estimator. Either way, the difference says nothing about the brain.

And the flip side for differential entropy. Last chapter, we saw that the same EEG has differential entropies differing by 13.8 nats in microvolts and volts. Its mutual information with any other signal is exactly the same in both units. That is one reason this series puts mutual information at the centre.

The companion notebook reproduces the transformations, estimates mutual information and correlation after each one and shows what squaring and binning do.

To recap, mutual information is invariant under any invertible transformation of each variable, change of unit, logarithm, cube, ranks. Correlation is not. The reason is a cancellation of stretch factors between the marginal and joint densities. Non-invertible transformations can only lose information. Mutual information depends only on the copula, the rank structure of the dependence, which grounds the Gaussian copula estimator. An estimator should be checked for the invariance that the quantity has.

PLATE 4.49:13

Conditional MI · Explaining away

Conditional mutual information measures what one variable tells about another once a third is known. Conditioning on a common cause, such as a shared stimulus, can explain a dependence away, while conditioning on a common effect, as in the exclusive-or example and Berkson's paradox, can create one. Conditioning is therefore not a neutral cleaning step, and estimation bias grows with each added variable.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the ballroom where a band has just started to play. Two dancers on opposite sides of the room both step on the beat. They have never met, yet their movements are strongly related. Knowing one tells you a lot about the other. But once you hear the music, the first dancer tells you nothing more about the second. Today we measure that idea — conditional mutual information.

Start with intuition. Mutual information asks how much X tells you about Y. Conditional mutual information asks how much X tells you about Y once you already know a third variable, Z. It is written I of X semicolon Y given Z.

Its definition follows the first face of mutual information, with Z added everywhere. The uncertainty about Y given Z, minus the uncertainty about Y given both X and Z. What does X add once Z is known?

Like mutual information, it can never be negative. Learning X can never make you on average more uncertain about Y, whatever you already know. And it is zero exactly when X and Y are independent given Z. When, within every value of Z, the joint table of X and Y is the product of its margins.

Another way to see it, compute the ordinary mutual information between X and Y separately within each value of Z, then average those values, weighted by how often each value of Z occurs. Conditional mutual information is the average of the within-slice mutual informations. For Gaussian variables it has a closed form, minus one half log of one minus the partial correlation squared, the correlation that remains between X and Y after removing what Z explains linearly from each.

But here is the surprise of this episode. Conditioning on Z can make the information between X and Y go down. And it can make it go up. Both directions happen for different reasons.

First down. Here is the calculation in one picture. Two EEG electrodes in a simulation both driven by the same stimulus. Each electrode records the stimulus plus its own independent noise. The stimulus has a variance of 1, each noise a standard deviation of 1 half. The two electrodes are then correlated at 0.8, and their mutual information is about 0.74 bits. Strongly related.

Now condition on the stimulus. Once the stimulus is known, what remains in each electrode is its own private noise, independent of the other. The conditional mutual information is zero. All the information the electrodes shared came through the stimulus. Conditioning explained it away, but only if you condition on the right thing measured well. Suppose your record of the stimulus is itself noisy, with a measurement error of standard deviation one-half. Conditioning on that imperfect record leaves a partial correlation of about 0.44 between the electrodes and about 0.16 bits of conditional mutual information. A residual dependence that looks like a direct link but is only the part of the common cause your measurement missed.

This is the dancers and the band. It is also the logic of a common cause. Two signals can be dependent only because something drives them both. Conditioning on that common driver removes the dependence and reveals that there was no direct relationship.

This idea runs through neuroscience. In hyperscanning, two brains listening to the same speech will share information through the speech itself, without any interaction between them. Conditioning on the speech envelope is how chapter 12 separates shared stimulation from genuine interpersonal coupling. In fNIRS, regressing out systemic physiology, the heartbeat and breathing that reach every channel is a form of conditioning on a common cause. In fMRI, removing a global signal plays a similar role, with well-known controversies about what else it removes.

Now up, conditioning can create information where there was none. Here is the classic example. X and Y are two independent fair coins. Z is their exclusive or Z is one when exactly one of the two coins shows heads and zero otherwise.

On their own, X and Y are independent, their mutual information is zero bits. Knowing one coin tells you nothing about the other. But now suppose you know Z. If Z equals 1, the coins differ, so knowing X tells you Y exactly. If Z equals 0, the coins agree, and again X tells you Y exactly. Given Z, X determines Y completely. The conditional mutual information is one full bit. In the language of the definition, given Z alone, Y is still a fair coin, one bit of uncertainty, given Z and X together, no uncertainty is left. 1 minus 0. Zero bits without the context, one bit with it. The information about Y is not in X alone, nor in Z alone. Each, on its own, tells you nothing about Y. It exists only in the combination of the two. That is the first appearance of synergy, the subject of chapter 9.

The same structure appears in a familiar statistical trap. Suppose admission to a programme requires either strong grades or strong research experience. Among admitted students, grades and research will appear negatively related, even if they are independent in the general population. Conditioning on admission, a common effect of both created a dependence. This is known as Berkson's paradox and in causal language conditioning on a collider. Selecting trials, participants, or channels on a criterion that depends on two variables can manufacture a relationship between them.

So conditioning is not a neutral cleaning step. Conditioning on a common cause removes spurious dependence. Conditioning on a common effect creates it. Which one you are doing depends on the causal structure, and the data alone cannot always tell you.

One more relation ties everything together. The information that two variables Y and Z together carry about X equals the information Z carries about X plus what Y adds given Z. This is the chain rule of mutual information and it is the subject of episode 4.6.

What is conditional mutual information sensitive to? First, to what you condition on as we have seen, the same pair can gain or lose information. Second, to estimation. Conditioning requires estimating dependence within each value or each neighbourhood of Z. Every conditioning variable multiplies the cells or adds a dimension to the space, and the samples available in each part shrink. Bias grows quickly. Episode 5.8 treats nearest neighbour estimators for conditional mutual information, which can even return slightly negative values on finite data. Those should be reported, not silently set to zero.

Here is the classic pitfall. A researcher finds strong mutual information between two brain regions and concludes they communicate. A reviewer asks, conditioned on what? On the stimulus, both regions may be silent towards each other. On the global signal, the result may vanish. On a third region that drives both, it may disappear entirely. Without conditioning, mutual information measures shared information of any origin. Claims about direct relationships need conditioning, and conditioning needs a causal argument for what to condition on. And remember the noisy record of the stimulus. Imperfect conditioning leaves residual dependence that can masquerade as a direct link in brain-to-brain coupling as much as in brain-to-body studies.

The companion notebook simulates the two electrodes and the common stimulus, computes mutual and conditional mutual information, builds the exclusive-or example exactly, and reproduces Berkson's paradox with simulated admissions.

To recap, conditional mutual information, I of X semicolon Y given Z, measures what X tells about Y beyond what Z already does. It is never negative and zero exactly under conditional independence. Conditioning on a common cause can explain dependence away. 0.74 bits became zero for two electrodes sharing a stimulus. Conditioning can also create dependence. Zero bits became one bit for the exclusive or, the first glimpse of synergy, and conditioning on a common effect produces Berkson's paradox. Choose what to condition on with a causal argument, measure it as well as you can, and beware estimation bias as conditioning variables accumulate.

Next time, a theorem that no preprocessing pipeline can escape, the data processing inequality.

PLATE 4.56:50

The data processing inequality

Information cannot be created by processing. If Z is computed from Y alone, Z carries no more information about X than Y does, as the chain rule shows. Preprocessing can make information easier for a finite-sample estimator to read but never adds any, and using the stimulus labels in the pipeline breaks the chain and leaks information in.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the ballroom for a game of telephone. A dance move is whispered from one couple to the next, and the next around the room. Each couple copies imperfectly. By the far end the move has blurred. No couple along the way can make it sharper than it was when they received it. That everyday observation is a theorem, and every analysis pipeline in neuroscience is subject to it, the data processing inequality.

Start with intuition. A stimulus produces a brain response. We record the response as an EEG. Then we process the EEG, filter it, downsample it, average it, extract features. Each step depends only on the output of the step before. Can any of these steps increase how much the data tell us about the stimulus?

The answer is no. Information about the stimulus can be kept or lost at each step. It can never be created.

Here is the statement. Suppose X, Y, and Z form a Markov chain, X influences Y, and Z is computed from Y alone with no other access to X. Then the information Z carries about X is at most the information Y carries about X. I of X semicolon Z is less than or equal to I of X semicolon Y.

The proof takes one line with the chain rule of mutual information. The information that Y and Z together carry about X can be split in two ways. Either the information in Z plus what Y adds given Z, or the information in Y plus what Z adds given Y. But Z adds nothing once Y is known because Z was computed from Y alone. So the information in Y equals the information in Z plus something that can never be negative. The information in Z cannot be larger.

Here is the calculation in one picture with a toy chain. The stimulus is one of two images shown equally often one bit of entropy. The EEG response reduced to a binary feature matches the image 90% of the time and is wrong 10% of the time. The information between stimulus and feature is one minus the binary entropy of 0.1, about 0.53 bits.

Now add a processing step that is itself imperfect, say a thresholding that flips the feature 10% of the time. The final output is now wrong 18% of the time. Two independent 10% errors combine into 18% since two errors cancel. The information between stimulus and output drops to about 0.32 bits. From 1 bit to 0.53 to 0.32. Each link can only lose.

When does the inequality become an equality? When the processing step keeps everything in y that is relevant to x. If z is an invertible function of y, nothing is lost, as episode 4.3 showed. More generally, if Z is a sufficient statistic of Y for X, it summarizes Y without loss for the purpose of predicting X. Averaging trials, for instance, keeps everything about the stimulus only if the trial-to-trial variation is pure noise unrelated to the stimulus.

So what are preprocessing and feature extraction for if they cannot add information? They can make information easier to read. A raw EEG at 1000 Hz on 64 channels contains the same information about the stimulus as before filtering or more. But an estimator or a decoder with limited data cannot read it all. Filtering out irrelevant noise, choosing a frequency band, or projecting onto a few spatial components can make the remaining information far more accessible to a finite-sample estimator. Estimated information can go up after preprocessing. True information cannot.

That distinction is easy to state and easy to forget. A better estimate of a smaller quantity can look like more information.

The inequality has many relatives. Every f-divergence from episode 3.4 obeys a version of it. Passing two distributions through the same channel can only make them harder to tell apart. And it underlies the bound of episode 3.3. A decoder's held-out performance gives a lower bound on the information in the data because the decoder's output is a processed version of the data.

What is this sensitive to? To the Markov condition above all, the inequality holds only if the processing step has no other access to X. If a pipeline uses the stimulus labels, for example by selecting channels, time windows or components according to how well they discriminate the conditions, then the output is no longer a function of the data alone. The chain is broken, and the output can appear to carry more information about the stimulus than the data did. That is not a violation of the theorem. It is information leaking in from the labels, and it is called circular analysis or double dipping.

Here is the classic pitfall. A methods paper reports that a new artifact-removal algorithm increases the mutual information between EEG and stimulus. Read carefully. Either the algorithm improved an estimate by removing noise that confused the estimator, or it used the label somewhere in which case the comparison is circular, or the claim concerns a decoder's accuracy, not the information itself. Each reading may be legitimate, but they are three different claims. Only the first is about preprocessing helping, and even then the true information did not increase.

Episode 6.8 returns to this from the other side, how filtering and downsampling can distort directed information measures and even create apparent directions that do not exist.

The chain rule of MI and information diagrams

The information two variables carry together splits into what the first gives plus what the second adds given the first. Order changes the split, never the total, as two noisy electrodes show; copies add nothing, while exclusive-or variables add everything at the second step. Information diagrams draw these quantities as areas, with seven regions for three variables.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the ballroom where dancers join the floor one at a time. The first dancers' moves tell you something about the music. The second adds some more, but only what the first did not already show. Today we write that idea as the chain rule of mutual information, and we learn to draw information diagrams.

Start with intuition. How much do two variables Y and Z tell you together about a third X? You can count it in two steps. First what Y tells you about X. Then what Z adds once Y is known. The total is the sum.

Here is the rule. I of X semicolon Y and Z equals I of X semicolon Y plus I of X semicolon Z given Y. The joint information split into a first contribution and a conditional, incremental contribution. With more variables, the chain simply continues. Each new variable adds what is new given all the previous ones.

Here is the calculation in one picture. A stimulus X with a Gaussian distribution of variance 1. Two electrodes, Y and Z, each recording the stimulus plus its own independent noise, also of variance 1. The first electrode alone carries half a bit about the stimulus. 1 half log base 2 of 1 plus its signal-to-noise ratio of 1.

Both electrodes together carry more. Averaging them halves the noise and the joint information is 1 half log base 2 of 3, about 0.79 bits. So the second electrode adds about 0.29 bits, given the first. Less than the half bit it would carry on its own, because part of what it says, the first electrode already said.

Now count in the other order. Electrode Z first, half a bit. Then electrode Y given Z, 0.29 bits. Same total, 0.79 bits. In this symmetric example, the pieces happen to be the same. In general, they are not. The order changes how the total is split between the steps. It never changes the total.

That point matters every time someone asks which variable contributes most. The answer often depends on who enters first. A feature that looks essential when added first can look useless when added last, if the others already carry its information. Statements like this region contribute so many bits are only meaningful with the order, or with a method that does not depend on order. One such method averages the increment over every possible order, an idea borrowed from game theory and known in machine learning as Shapley values. It removes the arbitrariness of a single order, at a computational cost.

Two extreme cases make the rule vivid. Suppose Z is an exact copy of Y. Then Z adds nothing given Y. The conditional term is zero and the total is just what Y carries. Complete redundancy. Now take the exclusive or from episode 4.4. X is the exclusive or of two independent fair coins, Y and Z. Y alone tells you nothing about X, zero bits. But given Y, Z tells you everything, one full bit. The total one bit is carried entirely by the second step. Complete synergy. The chain rule handles both cases, but it splits them very differently depending on order.

Now the diagrams. For two variables, we already drew two overlapping circles in episode 2.4. The overlap is the mutual information, and the rest of each circle is a conditional entropy. Raymond Yeung formalized this correspondence in 1991. Shannon's quantities behave like areas, with joint entropy like a union, conditional entropy like a difference, and mutual information like an intersection. The chain rules become the rules of adding areas.

For three variables, draw three overlapping circles, one per variable. They create seven regions. The three outer crescents are each variable's entropy given the other two. The three pairwise lens-shaped regions outside the centre are conditional mutual informations, like I of X semicolon Y given Z. And the single central region shared by all three has a special name, the co-information or interaction information.

With this picture, the chain rule is plain geometry. The information Y and Z share with X is the part of X's circle covered by the union of the other two circles. Count Y's overlap first, then add the part of Z's overlap not already covered by Y. Or the reverse. The total area is the same.

There is a catch and it is a big one. For three variables, the central region can be negative. Then the picture of areas breaks down. Episode 4.7 shows exactly when, and why that broken picture motivated a whole chapter of this series.

What is this sensitive to? To order for the individual terms as we have seen. And to estimation, each conditional term needs a conditional estimate, and the bias grows with the number of conditioning variables. A total computed as a sum of separately estimated terms accumulates their biases. When possible, estimate the joint information directly and compute the increments from the same estimator and samples.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study adds features one by one to predict a stimulus in an order chosen by convenience and concludes that the features added late are uninformative. They may simply be redundant with the early ones. Reverse the order and the conclusion can reverse. Report the order or use measures designed to handle redundancy and synergy symmetrically, like the decompositions of chapter 9.

Two noisy electrodes, half a bit, plus 0.29 bits given the first, 0.79 bits together. Copies add nothing, synergistic variables add everything at the second step. When the Venn diagram breaks · Co-information

Co-information, the central region of the three-circle diagram, is positive for redundancy and negative for synergy, which breaks the picture of areas. It is a single number, so redundancy and synergy can cancel to zero in a system containing both, and sign conventions differ between authors and libraries. This limitation motivates the partial information decomposition.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the ballroom for the moment when the tidy picture breaks. Last time we drew three overlapping circles, one for each variable, and read Shannon's quantities as areas. Today we look at the central region shared by all three circles. It has a name, co-information, and it can be negative. An area smaller than nothing. That one fact is why chapter 9 exists.

Start with intuition. Three variables X, Y, and Z. How much information do all three share? A natural guess, the information X and Y share minus the part of it that Z accounts for. In symbols, co-information is I of X semicolon Y minus I of X semicolon Y given Z. If Z explains away some of the shared information between X and Y, the difference is positive, and that positive amount is the region all three share.

Here is the calculation in one picture, starting with a case where everything is tidy. Let X, Y, and Z be three exact copies of the same fair coin. X and Y share one bit. Given Z, they share nothing more since Z already tells you everything. The co-information is 1 minus 0, one bit. A positive central region, exactly the size of the coin. All three circles coincide and the picture works perfectly. That is redundancy.

Now the exclusive or. Y and Z are two independent fair coins and X is their exclusive or. Any two of the three variables are independent. I of X semicolon Y is zero. But given Z, X determines Y completely. I of X semicolon Y given Z is one bit. The co-information is zero minus one, minus one bit.

A central region of minus one bit. No arrangement of circles can draw that. Yet the definition is symmetric. Whichever variable you condition on, you get the same minus one. The three variables are tied together by something that lives in no pair, and the Venn diagram has no way to show it. That is synergy.

So co-information is positive when the shared information is redundant, and negative when it is synergistic. That seems useful, a sign that tells you which kind of interaction dominates. Here is a less extreme example. X is the logical and of two independent fair coins, one only when both coins show heads. Each coin alone tells you something about X, about 0.31 bits. The co-information is about minus 0.19 bits. Negative, the and gate is on balance synergistic.

But here is the problem and it is serious. Co-information is a single number that mixes redundancy and synergy together. When both are present, they cancel.

Consider this system. Three independent fair coins A, B, and C. Let Y be the pair A and B. Let Z be the pair A and C. And let X be the pair made of A and the exclusive or of B and C. Now X shares the coin A with both Y and Z, one bit of pure redundancy. And X contains the exclusive or of B and C, which Y and Z can only reveal together, one bit of pure synergy.

Compute the co-information. I of X semicolon Y is 1 bit. I of X semicolon Y given Z is also 1 bit. Co-information 0. 0 for a system with a full bit of redundancy and a full bit of synergy. A value of 0 does not mean that the three variables do not interact. It can mean that two strong interactions of opposite kinds cancel exactly.

There is also a matter of convention. William McGill introduced the quantity in 1954 as interaction information, and Anthony Bell revisited it in 2003 as co-information. Some authors define it with the two terms swapped, which flips its sign for three variables. In one convention, the exclusive or gives minus one bit, in the other plus one. Python libraries differ too. The dit library, for instance, provides both with opposite signs for an odd number of variables. Always check the definition before interpreting a sign.

What is co-information sensitive to? To the convention as just said. To cancellations between redundancy and synergy. And when estimated from data, to the biases of the four or more information terms it combines, which can easily be larger than the co-information itself.

This quantity has a long history in neuroscience. Timothy Gawne and Barry Richmond, in 1993, used it to ask whether neighbouring neurons in the inferior temporal cortex carry independent messages. Elad Schneidman, William Bialek, and Michael Berry, in 2003, analysed its meaning carefully for population codes, and showed how synergy and redundancy measures of this kind can mix several effects. Their lesson carries over to EEG and fMRI signals today.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study computes co-information among three brain signals, finds a value close to zero, and concludes that their interactions are purely pairwise. As we just saw, a value of zero is compatible with large redundancy and large synergy at the same time. A negative value says synergy dominates, not that redundancy is absent. Co-information alone cannot separate them.

That limitation is exactly what motivated Paul Williams and Randall Beer in 2010 to propose the partial information decomposition, a way to split the information two sources carry about a target into separate non-negative pieces, redundant, unique, and synergistic. For two sources, co-information turns out to equal redundancy minus synergy. Chapter 9 develops that idea, its rival definitions, and its extensions to many variables and to dynamics.

It is positive for redundancy, plus one bit for three copies, and negative for synergy, minus one bit for the exclusive or. A negative area breaks the Venn diagram. Because redundancy and synergy cancel, a value near zero can hide both. Channels and capacity

An information value belongs to a neuron and a stimulus distribution together, not to the neuron alone. The same simulated neuron gives 0.41 bits with four equally frequent stimuli and 0.53 bits with two. TRANSCRIPTEN

Last dance in the ballroom and a question about who chooses the music. When we say that a neuron or a brain region carries so many bits about a stimulus, whose number is that? The neuron's, or the experiment's? Today's answer comes from the original setting of Shannon's theory, communication channels and their capacity.

Start with intuition. A channel is anything that takes an input and produces a noisy output. A telegraph wire, a radio link, a synapse, a neuron. What defines the channel is how it responds to each possible input. For every input x, the probability of each output y. In symbols, the conditional distribution of Y given X. That part belongs to the channel.

But mutual information needs the full joint distribution of input and output. And the joint distribution is the channel times the input distribution, how often each input is sent. That part belongs to whoever chooses the inputs. For a neuron in an experiment, that is the experimenter.

Here is the calculation in one picture. A simulated neuron, four possible stimuli, A, B, C, and D. The neuron fires in response to A 90% of the time and to each of the others only 10% of the time. That is the channel.

Experiment 1 shows all four stimuli equally often. The mutual information between stimulus and firing is about 0.41 bits.

Experiment 2 shows only A and B, half the time each. Same neuron, same response probabilities. The mutual information is now about 0.53 bits. Higher because the experiment now pits the neuron's favourite stimulus against one non-favourite, and the neuron's single response distinguishes them better than it distinguishes A from the group of three.

Same neuron, two experiments, two values. The information was never a property of the neuron alone. It is a property of the neuron together with the stimulus distribution chosen by the experimenter.

Is there a number that belongs to the channel alone? Yes, the largest mutual information it could ever transmit over all possible input distributions. That maximum is called the channel capacity. For our neuron, a numerical search finds it at about 0.53 bits per response, reached for example by showing A half the time and spreading the other half evenly over B, C, and D. Any input distribution with A half the time against the rest reaches the same value.

The search itself has a classic algorithm, published independently by Richard Blahut and by Suguru Arimoto in 1972. It alternates between two easy steps until the input distribution stops changing.

For some channels, the capacity has a closed form. A binary channel that flips its input with probability 1 tenth, the binary symmetric channel, has a capacity of 1 minus the binary entropy of 0.1, about 0.53 bits per use reached with equally likely inputs. A channel that adds Gaussian noise to a power-limited Gaussian input has a capacity of 1 half log base 2 of 1 plus the signal-to-noise ratio per use. You met that formula with the two electrodes in episode 4.6.

Why does capacity matter? Because of Shannon's noisy channel coding theorem, the second great theorem of 1948. At any rate below capacity, there exist codes that transmit information with an error probability as small as you like, by using the channel many times and adding clever redundancy. At rates above capacity, no code can. Capacity is the speed limit of reliable communication. Engineers have since built codes that come remarkably close to it.

For neuroscience, the lesson is more modest but important. An information value measured in an experiment depends on three things, the neural channel, the stimulus distribution, and the estimator. Capacity removes the second by maximizing over it, but it is hard to estimate and rarely what the experiment measures. What experiments measure is the information under their own stimulus ensemble.

That has consequences. Sensory neurons often transmit more information per second with naturalistic stimuli than with artificial ones, a result reported for example in auditory nerve fibres of the bullfrog, with sounds shaped like natural frog calls, by Fred Rieke, David Bodnar, and William Bialek in 1995. Is that a property of the neurons or of the stimuli? Both, inseparably, the neurons may be tuned to the statistics of their natural inputs, and the stimulus ensemble determines how much of that tuning an experiment can reveal.

What is all this sensitive to? To the stimulus distribution as we have seen, which stimuli and how often. To the response variable you choose, spike count, spike timing, an evoked potential's amplitude. And to the time window, bits per response, bits per second, and bits per spike are different quantities. Chapter 12 returns to them.

Here is the classic pitfall. Two studies report the information a brain area carries about faces. 0.2 bits in one, 0.6 in the other. The first used 20 faces shown equally often, the second used two faces. The difference may say nothing about the brain area. The stimulus entropies are not even the same, 4.3 bits against one bit. Always report the stimulus set, its distribution, and its entropy alongside any information value, and compare studies only with that in hand.
