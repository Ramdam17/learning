What stays when you stretch

A ring and a blob of two hundred points each have nearly identical means, variances and correlations, yet the ring has a loop. Counting pieces, loops and voids, the Betti numbers, survives stretching. But data are points: at scale 0.1 the ring falls into seventeen pieces, while at 0.5 and 1.2 it is one piece with one loop, so the count depends on scale.

TRANSCRIPTEN

For thirteen chapters, we asked one question about two objects. How far apart are they?

This chapter asks a question about one object. What shape does it have?

You have met a shape before. In episode 9.8, Chaudhuri and colleagues recorded head-direction cells in seven mice, and found that the activity sits on a one-dimensional ring. A ring. But what does it take to call a cloud of points a ring?

Here are two clouds of two hundred points each. One is a ring. The other is a round blob. Both are simulated, with the seed fourteen. Here they are two columns of numbers, so you can draw them. With twenty columns you could not, and you would need a way to see a hole without looking at it.

Now run the summaries of the first chapters on them. The mean of each cloud sits within about six hundredths of the origin. Their variances are almost the same: about 0.47 along the first axis, and 0.52 along the second. And both have a correlation that is almost zero.

Everything we learned to summarise about a cloud says the same thing about these two. And yet nobody would call them alike.

This is a known trap. Matejka and Fitzmaurice, in 2017, showed how to generate datasets with identical statistics and very different drawings, by nudging points a little at a time. Same numbers, different picture.

Do the distances of the earlier chapters see the difference? They do. The energy distance of chapter seven compares all the distances between the clouds with all the distances inside each cloud. Between the ring and the blob, it is 0.23. Between two draws of the same cloud, it is only 0.08 for the blob and 0.12 for the ring. The transport distance of chapter six asks for the cheapest way to move one cloud onto the other. It is 0.49, against 0.20 and 0.23 for two draws of the same cloud. That is one draw each, so read these floors as orders of magnitude.

So a distance does separate them. But it answers how much. It never answers in what way. The ring and the blob are far apart, and the number does not say why.

Here, the difference has a name. The ring has a hole.

So we change the question. We stop measuring, and we count. How many pieces is the shape made of? How many loops does it make? How many closed voids does it enclose?

A piece is a part you can walk through without ever jumping. A loop is a closed path that you cannot shrink to a point without leaving the shape. A void is a closed hollow, like the air inside a ball.

These three counts are called Betti numbers. The name goes back to Poincaré, in 1895, as the mathematician Siersma reports it. His numbers were one higher than the ones we use today.

Take a circle. One piece, and one loop. It encloses no void.

Take a hollow sphere, the skin of a ball. One piece. No loop, because any loop you draw on a sphere can be shrunk down to a point. And one void, the air inside.

Take the surface of a doughnut. One piece. Two loops, one that goes around the hole, and one that goes around the tube. And one void, the inside of the doughnut.

Take the blob. One piece, and nothing else. Any loop you draw inside it can be shrunk to a point, because the blob has no hole to catch it.

So the ring has a loop and the blob does not. That is the whole difference, and no mean, no variance and no correlation can show it. It cannot be measured. It can only be counted.

Now stretch. Imagine the cloud printed on a sheet of rubber. Start with the circle, and stretch it into an ellipse. Bend it into the outline of a bean. Move it, turn it, make it bigger. Each of these still has one piece and one loop.

Even the distance of episode 12.6, which needs no common frame, tells a circle from an ellipse, because their lengths differ. The counts do not.

The counts do not change, as long as you tear nothing and glue nothing.

A potter knows this. You can squeeze a ball of clay into a bowl, and the counts stay the same. Poke a hole right through it, and a loop appears.

Episode 10.6 called shape what remains when you remove position, size and orientation. Here we remove one more thing, stretching. What remains is only the way the object holds together. This branch of mathematics is called topology.

Now we fill in the card of the series, in its usual order.

The object is a shape. The distance is not a distance: two shapes have the same three numbers, or they do not.

What does it ignore? All lengths. That is deliberate. A long thin loop and a round one count the same.

What does it cost? Nothing, on a shape that is drawn. On data, it will cost something, and episode 14.6 says how much.

And the trap. Data are not a shape. Data are points.

Take our ring. Read literally, two hundred points are two hundred separate pieces, and no loop at all. Nothing touches anything.

So let us link the points that are closer than some scale, and count again. At a scale of 0.1, the ring falls into seventeen pieces. At 0.5, it is one piece with one loop. At 1.2, it is still one piece with one loop.

The blob, at 0.1, is a hundred and twenty-five pieces. At 0.5, it is seven pieces with two small loops, which come from chance. At 1.2, it is one piece and nothing else.

Same points, different answers. The count depends on the scale at which you look.

So here is the trap. Two hundred points are two hundred pieces and no loop. To see the ring, you must choose a scale.

Why does the ring's loop survive so many scales, and the blob's loops vanish so quickly? That is what we will measure.

From points to a shape

A computer sees thirty points around a circle as thirty pieces. Linking points closer than a chosen scale and filling triangles builds a world whose pieces and loops are counted from a table of distances alone. At scale 0.5 there are three pieces, at 0.8 one loop, at 1.8 the loop is filled. Two analysts choosing 0.5 and 0.8 describe different shapes.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here are thirty points. They were drawn around a circle of radius one, with a little noise. To your eye, they make a ring.

To a computer, they are thirty separate points. Nothing in the list says that they go around. Last time we learned to ask three questions of a shape: how many pieces, how many loops, how many voids. Now we need a way to get those counts out of a list of points. So how do we hand a computer a shape?

The recipe has three gestures. First, choose a scale. Think of it as a length of cord. Second, link two points whenever their distance is less than that scale. Third, fill in a triangle as soon as its three sides are drawn. Do the same for groups of four points. To count loops, triangles are enough. To count voids, you also need the groups of four.

What you get is a small world made of points, edges and filled triangles. A piece is a set of points joined by edges. A loop is a cycle of edges around a hole that nothing fills. On this world, counting pieces and loops is no longer a matter of looking. It is elimination on tables of zeros and ones, the same row operations you use to solve linear equations. One table says which points are joined by which edges. Another says which edges bound which triangles. Reducing them tells you how many pieces and how many holes are left.

Those tables come from a table of distances. Here are our thirty points, sorted by angle around the circle, and the table of their distances. Darker means farther. Thirty points make four hundred and thirty-five pairs.

Now pick the scale 0.5. Every pair closer than the scale becomes a one. Every other pair becomes a zero. The ones gather in three blocks along the diagonal. Three pieces, with seventeen, ten and three points. You can read the count straight off the table.

Look at what the recipe needed. A table of distances, and nothing else. No coordinates. Any distance table of this series can be the input.

The idea goes back to Leopold Vietoris, in 1927. The usual accounts say that Eliyahu Rips used it to study groups, and that Gromov made it popular in 1987. The double name, Vietoris and Rips, is credited to Hausmann, in 1995. That history comes from secondary sources.

Now let the scale grow. At 0.1, almost nothing is linked. Twenty-three pieces, no loop. At 0.2, fourteen pieces. At 0.3, ten. At 0.5, three. Edges appear first between the closest neighbours. Triangles need three neighbours that are all close to each other, so they come later.

At 0.8, one piece and one loop. That is the ring. The complex has a hundred and fourteen edges and two hundred and one triangles. The pieces have joined, and the middle of the ring is still empty. At 1.2, still one loop. At 1.6, still one loop.

At 1.8, the loop is gone. The triangles across the ring have filled the hole, and everything is one solid piece.

Why does a loop need filled triangles? An empty triangle is itself a loop of three edges. A filled triangle is not, because it has no hole. The Graph Theory series shows this in its episode 15.5, More than pairs. Here, as the scale grows, triangles stretch across the ring until nothing is left empty.

Too small, and the data is dust: thirty pieces, nothing to see. Too large, and it is a blob: one piece, no loop, no structure. Both are correct descriptions of the same points, at different scales. Nothing tells you in advance where the middle is. The cord has to be cut to some length, and the points do not say which.

There is another way to build a shape. Inflate a ball around each point, and look at the union of the balls. Two balls overlap when the points are closer than twice the radius, so the radius is half the scale. Link a group of points when their balls share a common point. A theorem credited to Leray and Borsuk, in the nineteen forties, says that the object built this way has the same shape as the union of the balls.

The Vietoris and Rips version looks only at pairs of points. It is not a union of balls. But it is sandwiched between two ball constructions: balls of some radius, and balls of twice that radius. So it is close, up to a factor of two. Here are the two pictures side by side, at scale 0.8. The ring is there in both. And it needs only a table of distances.

That is what ties this chapter to the whole series. The distance between two signals from chapter 4, between two matrices from chapter 8, between two nodes of a graph: each of them can fill that table and enter here.

Does it count correctly on other shapes? Take sixty points on a figure eight, two rings side by side. At scale 1.0, one piece and two loops. The two rings are close enough to join at this scale, and each leaves its own hole.

Take three hundred points on a sphere. At scale 1.0, one piece, no loop, and one void: a hollow closed inside. The sphere is a drawing projected onto the screen. The points live in three columns. Pieces, loops and voids are the Betti numbers of the last episode. One, two, zero for the eight. One, zero, one for the sphere.

Now the card. What does this recipe ignore? Position, orientation, the order of the points: everything that keeps the distances. What does it cost? The number of triangles. At scale 1.2, our thirty points make 175 edges, 487 triangles, and 858 groups of four. At 2.1, there are 3,875 triangles, almost all of the 4,060 possible. Episode 14.6 counts the cost for larger clouds.

And the trap? The scale.

Picture two analysts with the same thirty points. The first chooses 0.5, and reports three pieces. The second chooses 0.8, and reports a ring. Neither made a mistake. At 0.5, these thirty points are three pieces. At 0.8, they are a ring. Two people who choose two scales describe two shapes.

Nothing in the points chooses for us. For data, a shape is always a shape at a given scale. Asked how many loops there are, the honest answer begins with a question: at which scale?

Keep every scale

Instead of choosing one scale, a dial turns from zero to two on thirty points and every piece and loop is logged as a bar from birth to death. The ring is one long loop bar, born at 0.59 and dying at 1.66. The piece bars equal the single-linkage merge heights, and the tree's edges are the same lengths.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, you chose a scale. You linked every pair of points closer than that scale, and you filled the triangles. At scale 0.5, the thirty points were three pieces. At 0.8, they were a ring.

Two people with two scales told two different stories. So today, nobody chooses.

Here are the same thirty points, drawn in the same place. Picture a kiln with a dial. The dial is the scale. We turn it from zero up to two, slowly, and we write down everything that happens.

At zero, every point is alone. Thirty points, thirty pieces. As the dial turns, the closest pairs link. Two pieces become one. A piece that joins another one stops being a piece of its own. At 0.1, twenty-three pieces are left. At 0.3, ten. At 0.5, three. You have seen these counts before. This time, we do not stop at any of them.

Look at the log. Each piece gets a bar. It is born at scale zero, when the dial starts. It dies on the day it merges into a neighbour. A short bar is a piece that merged early.

Twenty-nine of the thirty bars end. The middle one ends at 0.18. The last one ends at 0.56. And one bar never ends, because the last piece is the whole cloud, and it stays.

Now the loop. Nothing, nothing, nothing. Then, at scale 0.59, the pieces close up into a ring, and a loop is born. It lives, and the hole stays open, until scale 1.66. There, the triangles fill the middle and the loop dies. It lived for 1.07.

This is why we do not have to choose. The ring is the one long loop bar. It is there from 0.59 to 1.66, and that covers 0.8, where you saw it last time.

Check it on a perfect case. Take a circle of radius one, covered with points so close that there are no gaps. Its loop is born almost at zero. It dies at 1.73, which is root 3. That is the scale where the triangle drawn inside the circle fills in. Our noisy thirty points die a little earlier, because they are not a perfect circle.

This method has a name. It is persistent homology. Homology is the machinery that counts pieces, loops and voids. Persistent means that we follow each one across all the scales, and note how long it survives.

You can draw the answer in two ways. One is the barcode we just built, with one bar per feature. The other puts every feature at one point. Its birth goes on the horizontal axis. Its death goes on the vertical axis. This is the persistence diagram. A point on the diagonal was born and died at once. A point close to the diagonal lived only briefly. A point high above the diagonal lived long.

Our loop is one point, 0.59 across and 1.66 up. The twenty-nine pieces all stand on the left edge, because they were all born at zero. Their heights are their deaths. The piece that never dies is drawn as an arrow, going up through the top.

Now, a surprise. You already know the bars of the pieces.

Take the thirty points and group them by their nearest neighbour. Start with every point alone. At each step, merge the two groups that hold the two closest points. We call it single-linkage grouping. It is not clustering in the sense of chapter seventeen. It is only this one rule, written as a tree of merges.

The heights where the groups merge are the deaths of our piece bars. Not roughly. Exactly. We computed both on our thirty points, and the twenty-nine values are the same.

There is more. Each time two groups merge, draw a line between the two closest points. After twenty-nine merges, you have a tree that links all thirty points and has no loop. Among all such trees, it has the smallest total length. It is the minimum spanning tree. The graph theory series builds it in its episode 2.6, titled The cheapest network. So the piece bars are also the lengths of the edges of that tree.

This is a classic fact. We checked it by computation. A published source: Gower and Ross, in 1969, showed that the single-linkage grouping of a set of points can be read from its minimum spanning tree. That is what the abstract says, and we did not go further. Episode 15.6 comes back to this tree.

The pieces are the ground floor. Loops and voids are the floors above.

Two rings of thirty points, touching to form a figure eight. Two long loop bars, 1.16 and 1.05. The next one is 0.03.

Three hundred points on a sphere. One void bar of 1.04. And no loop longer than 0.30.

Now the surface of a ring buoy. The data are six hundred points. They live in a space with four columns, so any drawing of a buoy is only a sketch, and we label it so. Two loops, 1.43 and 1.42. The next one is 0.35. One void, 0.98. The next one is 0.07.

So we read one, two, one. One piece, two loops, one void. That is the Betti numbers of a buoy, read from a list of bars.

Who invented this? The idea was found more than once. In his short history of persistence, Perea says it was introduced, independently, by Frosini, around 1992, and by Robins in 1999. Barannikov, in 1994, is also cited, though not in Perea's account. We claim no first place for anyone.

The algorithm and the diagram come from Edelsbrunner, Letscher and Zomorodian, in a paper dated 2000, and published in 2002. The algebraic form is by Zomorodian and Carlsson, in 2005. The barcodes come from Carlsson and coauthors in 2004, and Ghrist presented them in 2008.

Now the card of this summary. Its object is a cloud of points, seen at every scale. As a distance, it is empty. It is a summary, and episode 14.4 will give it a distance.

What it ignores. A move, a turn, the order of the points. But not the size. A circle twice as large gives bars twice as long. So the diagram mixes shape and size.

What it costs. Counted in triangles, not in points. That is episode 14.6.

And its trap. Reading long bars as structure, and short bars as noise. It is a good first rule, and no more. Bubenik and coauthors, in 2020, argue in their abstract that short bars carry geometric information. They prove that persistent homology detects the curvature of the disks the points were sampled from. And episode 14.5 will show bars in pure random noise.

So remember this. We do not choose the scale. We keep them all, and note how long each trait of the shape survives. A cloud of points becomes a list of births and deaths.

How far apart are two diagrams?

A persistence diagram is compared with another by matching points, with the diagonal as a wall; the bottleneck distance is the worst move of the best matching. A circle and its turned copy sit at zero, while one extra point at the centre of 61 moves the diagram by 0.732. The stability theorem is a ceiling, and it runs one way only.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a cloud of points became a diagram. Every loop is one point: its birth on the horizontal axis, its death on the vertical axis.

Now we treat that diagram like every other object of this series. How far apart are two diagrams?

Start from a circle of 60 regular points, with radius 1. Its diagram has one point. The loop is born at 0.105, and it dies at 1.732. A point close to the diagonal lived only briefly. A point far from it is a loop that lasted.

Here is the idea. Picture a dance floor. Every point of the first diagram must find a partner in the second diagram. Or it may step back against the wall, and the wall is the diagonal. Stepping to the wall says: this loop does not exist in the other cloud.

Each pair has a cost, and the cost is how far the point has to move. We take the larger of the horizontal shift and the vertical shift. Going to the wall costs half the length of the bar, so a short bar costs almost nothing.

The bottleneck distance is the cost of the worst move, in the best matching. Among all the ways to pair the points, keep the one whose worst move is the smallest.

This is the worst point of episode 3.5, with a matching as in chapter 6. The worst point, because we keep the largest move. The matching, because we choose who goes with whom.

Here is the circle against the same circle with a noise of 0.15 on each coordinate. The noisy diagram has four points. The main loop moves a little: that is the worst move, 0.46. The three small loops drop onto the diagonal for almost nothing.

Now the row of changes. What does this distance see? The same circle, turned and moved: 0. Nothing changed in the diagram.

With a noise of 0.05: 0.141. With a noise of 0.15: 0.46.

Stretch it into an ellipse, 1.5 by 1: 0.257.

Make it twice as big: 1.627. Size is seen. Here the best matching sends both loops to the wall, and the cost is half the length of the bigger loop.

Two circles side by side, a figure eight: 0.761. The eight has two loops and the circle has one. One loop finds a partner. The other drops to the wall, at half its length.

A round blob: 0.814. The circle's loop has no partner at all, so it falls to the wall.

These are single draws, simulated with the seed 14, so read the noisy rows as orders of magnitude.

Now the result that makes diagrams usable on real measurements: stability. Two clouds that are close have diagrams that are close.

Let us say it with the right distance. Chapter 12.6 gave us the Gromov-Hausdorff distance, the one that needs no common frame. Link two points when their distance is less than the scale, as we did all chapter. Then the bottleneck distance is at most twice the Gromov-Hausdorff distance. That is Chazal, de Silva and Oudot, in 2014, and the bound turns out to be tight. Chazal and colleagues had proved a first version in 2009.

The factor two goes with this convention. Measure the scale as a radius instead, and the factor disappears.

The founding result is older, and it is about functions. Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner and Harer, in 2007: two functions that stay close have diagrams that stay close.

Let us check. Take the noisy cloud and the circle, in the same frame. Their Hausdorff distance is 0.146. That distance only bounds the Gromov-Hausdorff distance from above, so the bound is at most twice that: 0.29. We measured 0.141. Inside.

The circle twice as big: Hausdorff 1.0, bound 2.0, measured 1.627. Inside again.

Now the turned and moved copy. In the frame, its Hausdorff distance is 9.9. But the two clouds have exactly the same distances between their points, so their Gromov-Hausdorff distance is zero. The bound is zero, and the bottleneck distance is zero. The plain Hausdorff distance is a valid bound only when the two clouds sit in the same frame.

But this guarantee is the guarantee of the worst point. Add one single point to the circle: 61 points instead of 60.

At the centre, the loop now dies at 1.0 instead of 1.732. The diagram moves by 0.732. The Hausdorff distance between the clouds is 1.0, so the theorem is respected.

The same point at mid-radius: the loop dies at 1.41, and the diagram moves by 0.32.

The same point far outside, at 3 on the horizontal axis: for the loops, nothing changes. Zero. And yet the Hausdorff distance is 2.0.

So the theorem is always respected, but it is a ceiling, not a forecast. One point out of 61 was enough to move the diagram by 0.73.

The remedy replaces the distance to the nearest point by a distance to the k nearest points, which resists isolated points. It is called the distance to a measure, by Chazal, Cohen-Steiner and Mérigot, in 2011. Methods built on the nearest-point distance are known to fail in the presence of outliers, as Guibas, Mérigot and Morozov put it.

What about the summed version? Add the moves, instead of keeping the worst. It is a transport distance between diagrams, and it sees every loop. On our rows, the figure eight gives 0.91 against 0.76. The blob gives 1.11 against 0.81. The circle twice as big gives 1.73 against 1.627.

But its guarantees hold only under conditions. Cohen-Steiner and colleagues proved them in 2010 with constants that depend on the space and on how fast the function can vary, and the result fails for functions that are only continuous. Skraba and Turner study the question again in a preprint.

Do the four rules hold? Between diagrams, yes. Never negative, and the same both ways. We checked the triangle rule on 990 ordered triples, taken among eleven diagrams of this episode: it never broke.

Between clouds, no. Two different clouds can have the same diagram, and then the distance is zero. The turned copy is one. The circle with a far point is another.

What does it ignore? Everything the diagram ignores, and above all where the loops are. It tells you that a loop exists. It does not tell you where.

What does it cost? One matching, as in chapter 6. Between our circle and the blob, one point against fourteen, it is a table of fifteen by fifteen. That is small next to the work of building the diagrams, which is the subject of episode 14.6.

And now the trap. The theorem goes one way only. Close clouds give close diagrams. Close diagrams do not tell you that the clouds are close. A distance of zero between two diagrams has just been shown for two clouds that are 2.0 apart in Hausdorff distance.

To remember: the object is a diagram. The distance is the worst move of the best matching, with the diagonal as a wall. It ignores position, and where the loops are. It costs one matching. Its trap is that the theorem goes one way.

Diagrams as data

Two samples of the same noisy circle are never at distance zero, so that noise floor must be read before comparing diagrams. A hundred random points in a plane still draw about twenty loops. Means of diagrams may not be unique, as a two-point example shows with two equally good answers, and permutation tests need settings fixed beforehand.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Picture a study with two groups of participants, and one persistence diagram for each person. The diagrams are illustrative.

Now treat them like any other data. Is this distance large? What is the average person of each group? Do the two groups differ? A diagram is not a number, and it is not a list of fixed length. So each of those questions needs a tool. This episode builds three: a floor, a mean, and a test.

The floor comes first. It is the reading of a scale when nothing is on the pan.

Take the noisy circle of the last episode: sixty points, noise zero point zero five. Draw two samples of it. Compute their two diagrams, and the bottleneck distance between them. Same circle, same noise. Only the draw differs. Repeat that a hundred times.

The distance is never zero. It is zero point one two on average, and zero point three zero for one pair in twenty.

Two samples of the round blob: zero point one zero on average, and zero point one six five for one pair in twenty.

A circle against a blob: zero point five nine on average, and never less than zero point three eight, in a hundred draws.

Now remember the number from episode fourteen point four. A regular circle and its noisy copy were zero point one four one apart. On its own, that number meant nothing. Put it on the floor, and it sits inside what noise gives. It is not a difference of shape. It is the weather.

There is a second floor, and it is stranger. What does pure chance draw?

Take a hundred points at random in a plane. No circle. No structure. Compute the loops. About twenty of them appear. Take the longest bar, and divide it by the median distance between two points. On average it is zero point one eight, and zero point three zero for one draw in twenty.

Raise the number of columns to ten. Seventy-three loops, and the longest bar falls to zero point one four. With a hundred columns, one hundred and thirteen loops, and zero point zero six. That is forty draws for each. A ring of a hundred points gives zero point nine one.

That random points have holes, over a whole range of scales, was studied by Kahle in two thousand eleven. He worked out the scales where they appear.

And notice the trend. More columns, more loops, and shorter ones. That is the concentration of distances of episode two point six. When almost every pair is at almost the same distance, nothing stands out, and everything makes small loops.

So the question is never: is there a bar? It is: is this bar longer than chance's?

Now the mean.

You cannot average two diagrams cell by cell. They do not have the same number of points. So we do what episode one point six did. We define the mean by a distance. It is the diagram that makes the sum of squared distances to the data as small as possible.

The idea of a mean in this space was studied by Mileyko, Mukherjee and Harer, in two thousand eleven. It exists under mild conditions. Turner and colleagues gave, in two thousand fourteen, a way to compute one. It reaches a local minimum, not always the best.

But it need not be unique. Here is a small example, ours, for illustration. The first diagram holds two points: two, six, and four, eight. The second holds two, eight, and four, six.

To compare them, match the points. Match them in place, and each point moves by two. Match them crosswise, and each point moves by two again.

Add up the moves: both ways cost four. Square them first, as the mean of episode one point six does: both ways cost eight. The worst move is two either way. Two ways, exactly the same price. I checked every matching, by computation.

Each matching gives a mean: the midpoints. The first gives two, seven, and four, seven. The second gives three, six, and three, eight. With squares, those are the only two minima. Between them, the cost rises. Two means, equally good, and nothing in the data to choose.

So before you use a mean, ask whether it is unique.

There is another road. Turn each diagram into an object you do know how to average.

A family of curves, the persistence landscape. Its average obeys the usual theorems of statistics. That is Bubenik, in two thousand fifteen.

An image, the persistence image, from Adams and colleagues, in two thousand seventeen.

Or a kernel between diagrams, and all of chapter seven applies. Reininghaus and colleagues proposed one in two thousand fifteen. Carrière, Cuturi and Oudot proposed another in two thousand seventeen.

Now the tests.

A confidence band around the diagonal tells which bars cannot be told from noise, at a chosen level. That is Fasy and colleagues, in two thousand fourteen. They build it by resampling.

To compare two groups, shuffle. Robinson and Turner, in two thousand seventeen, described a permutation test built on distances between diagrams.

Here is a small version. Twelve noisy circles of radius one, and twelve of radius one point one five. Sixty points each, noise zero point zero five. Inside a group, diagrams are zero point one three apart on average, which is the floor again. Between groups, zero point two eight. The gap is zero point one five.

Now shuffle the labels two thousand times, and recompute the gap. The shuffled gaps stay around zero. The largest is zero point zero eight. None of the two thousand reaches zero point one five. The groups differ, and we know how much chance could have done.

But here is the trap. A curve, an image or a kernel needs settings. A resolution. Weights. A width.

Take the same diagrams, with a grid of sixteen by sixteen cells. With a narrow width, the noisy circle is one point four eight away from the regular one, and the circle twice as big only one point zero zero. The bottleneck distance says zero point four six, and one point six three. With a wide image, the order flips back: zero point eight four, and zero point nine eight.

Same story with a kernel. At a narrow width, the circle twice as big is one point two six away from almost everyone. Each diagram is alone in the dark, as in episode seven point one. Change the width, and the table changes.

These are not details. They change the result. So fix them before you look at the data. Write them down, and keep them.

So a diagram is a piece of data like any other. It has a floor, a mean that may not be unique, and tests. And chance has loops.

PLATE 14.66:39

What it costs, and what a loop is not

The cost of persistent homology is counted in blocks, not points: 100 points give 3,921,225 groups of four, and 1,000 give 41 billion. A loop in a delay cloud is often read as a rhythm, yet noise 0.5 drops a sine's bar to 0.06, and filtered noise can reach 0.42. Run a shuffled control before trusting any loop.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You now have a recipe for the shape of a cloud. Grow the scale, and watch pieces, loops and voids appear and disappear.

Two questions are left. What does the recipe cost? And what does a loop really tell you?

Start with the cost. It is not counted in points. It is counted in blocks. A block is an edge between two points, a triangle of three points, or a group of four points. The recipe has to look at all of them, because a loop only counts if the triangles around it are checked, and a void only counts if the groups of four are checked.

Take 100 points. There are 4,950 possible edges. There are 161,700 triangles. And there are 3,921,225 groups of four.

Now take 1,000 points. That is ten times more points. The edges grow about a hundred times, to 499,500. The triangles grow about a thousand times, to 166 million. And the groups of four grow about ten thousand times, to 41 billion.

So the bill follows the blocks. In the worst case, the standard algorithm needs a time that grows as the cube of the number of blocks. Ten times more blocks, a thousand times more time. That is the worst case, not the usual one. Morozov built an example that reaches this, in 2005.

In practice, good programs avoid building most of those blocks. Here is a published benchmark. Bauer, in 2021, compared programs on a sphere of 192 points, with loops and voids. An older program, Dionysus, took 596 seconds and 3.4 gigabytes. His program, Ripser, took 0.66 seconds and 116 megabytes. That is a published figure, from his machine, not ours. It shows that the program matters as much as the data.

Here is what we measured, on one laptop. Each time is the median of three runs. Loops of 2,000 points: under a second. Voids of 500 points: about four seconds. The torus of 600 points from episode 14.3, voids included: about twelve seconds. These times depend on the program and on the machine. Keep the order of magnitude, not the digits. Loops, a few thousand points. Voids, a few hundred. Adding the void dimension is what makes the bill jump.

Beyond that, you have three options. Draw a subset of the points. Stop the scale early. Or look only at loops. Otter and colleagues compared the programs and discussed such options, in 2017.

Now the catch. A loop is often read as a rhythm. Here is how. Take a signal, and build a cloud from it. Each point pairs the value of the signal now with its value a little later. This is the delay trick, from Takens, in 1981.

Our signal is a sine wave, with a period of 50 samples. The delay is 12 samples, about a quarter of the period. A quarter of a period later, a sine has become a cosine. And a sine against a cosine is a circle. So a periodic signal draws a closed curve, and a signal that never repeats does not.

A closed curve is a loop, and a loop has a bar. Perea and Harer showed, in 2015, that the longest bar measures how periodic a signal is. We divide it by the diameter of the cloud, so that signals can be compared. A bar as long as the cloud is wide would be one. For the clean sine, the longest bar is 0.73 times the diameter.

Now add noise. Everything here is simulated. Each value is the median of 30 draws, and the noise is its standard deviation, with the sine at amplitude 1. Noise 0.05: the bar is 0.62. Noise 0.1: 0.49. Noise 0.2: 0.27. Noise 0.3: 0.14. Noise 0.5: 0.06.

White noise alone, with no rhythm at all, gives 0.04. So at noise 0.5, the bar is at the level of pure noise. The rhythm is still in the signal, and your eye would still see it. But the loop is gone. The noise filled the hole. A small bar does not mean there is no rhythm. It means this tool no longer sees it.

Smoothing can bring it back. Take the signal with noise 0.3, and average it over nine samples. The bar climbs from 0.14 back to 0.50. Smoothing averaged away the noise that was filling the hole.

Now a surprise. Take white noise again, with no rhythm. Pass it through a narrow filter, around the period of 50. The median bar is 0.10. But the best of 30 draws reaches 0.42. A filter makes loops. It keeps only the frequencies near the period of 50, so the noise that comes out looks, for a while, like a wave.

So three warnings.

First, a loop says that the cloud closes on itself. It does not say that an oscillator exists.

Second, suppose the stimulus itself goes around a circle, an orientation or a direction. Then the responses follow the stimulus around the circle, whatever the brain does with it, and the ring comes from the experiment itself.

Third, every step of preprocessing, dimension reduction included, can dig a hole or fill one. A filter, a smoothing window, a projection onto a few components: each one moves the points, so each one changes which loops exist.

And the trap is to forget the control. Run the same computation on the same data, shuffled. Or on noise filtered the same way. For the clean sine, shuffling the values drops the bar from 0.73 to 0.09. For filtered noise, the control is the noise itself, and it already reaches 0.42 once in 30 draws. Only a control tells you how long a bar your own chain can make alone. If the control gives a bar as long as yours, the chain made the loop, not the data.

The cost is in the number of blocks, not in the number of points. And a loop says that a cloud closes on itself, not why. Before you read a rhythm or a map in it, look at what the filter, the stimulus and the preprocessing make on their own.

Is there a hole in your data?

Persistent homology has solid results where a variable turns: mouse head direction cells form a ring, and grid cells of three rats show six modules with the signature of a doughnut surface. Classifying patients is weaker. In hyperscanning the idea stays a lead: on simulated signals, 300 points cannot reveal the torus, so calibrate first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Is there a hole in your data? This chapter gave you a tool. It counts pieces, loops and voids, and it keeps every scale. Today we ask where the tool has been used, and what it found.

We sort the claims the way a potter sorts a workshop. On the left shelf, fired pieces: results that have held up. On the right shelf, raw clay: ideas worth trying, nothing more. And every study is said at the level we read it. Some we read in full. Some only through the abstract.

First, the fired shelf. The strongest results are about variables that turn.

Take head direction cells in the mouse. Chaudhuri and colleagues, in 2019, analysed recordings from seven mice. The usable sessions held between nine and fifty neurons. The activity of those cells forms a ring, awake and in sleep. The analysis found no evidence of a torus, or of anything more complicated. We read this paper in full.

Rybakken, Baas and Dunn found a circular feature in mouse recordings too, without telling the method which variable to look for. We read the abstract only.

Now the grid cells of the rat. Gardner and colleagues, in 2022, recorded from three rats. They found six modules, with 66 to 189 cells each. Each module gave one piece, two loops and one void. That is the signature of the surface of a doughnut. The four long bars were longer than any bar in a thousand shuffles of the data. Two things to keep in mind. The cloud was first reduced to six principal components, and then passed through a neighbourhood step. And about sixty cells were needed to detect the torus more than half of the time. We read this paper in full.

In the visual cortex of the macaque, Singh and colleagues, in 2008, found that spontaneous activity is consistent with the topology of a sphere. Consistent, not proven. We read it in full.

Outside the brain, Carlsson and colleagues, in 2008, looked at the highest-contrast patches of photographs, three pixels by three. These patches lie on a surface with one side only, the Klein bottle. Their result combines a calculation and a model. We read it in full.

A second family of work starts from a network, not from a cloud. We know these through their abstracts. Sizemore and colleagues, in 2018, looked for groups of fully connected regions, and for cavities, in the wiring of the human brain. Petri and colleagues, in 2014, studied fMRI networks under psilocybin. Giusti and colleagues, in 2015, studied correlations between place cells. Lee and colleagues, in 2012, compared grouping trees of brain networks with the distance of episode 12.6. Their data came from PET scans of children, not from EEG or fMRI.

One more tool summarises a cloud by a graph, not by bars. Singh, Mémoli and Carlsson proposed it in 2007. Saggar and colleagues, in 2018, used it to follow brain states during tasks. Abstracts again.

The balance is less clear when we classify people. Caputi, Pidnebesna and Hlinka, in 2021, say in their abstract that for schizophrenia, topological data analysis performs close to random. For epileptic seizures in scalp EEG, it did not beat the raw connectivity. And we found no published criticism of the grid cell result. That is one search, not a proof.

Now the raw clay.

First idea. Measure the periodicity of an EEG window by the length of its loop. It does not assume a waveform. Keep the control of episode 14.6: noise filtered around the same period gave a median of 0.10, and the best of thirty draws reached 0.42. Others have already applied averageable curves, called persistence landscapes, to EEG trial by trial: Wang, Ombao and Chung, in 2018, and a short review by Xu, Drougard and Roy, in 2021. We read the first page of the first, and the abstract of the review.

Second idea. Compare two conditions with the distance between diagrams, a permutation test, and a floor: two halves of the same condition.

Third idea. Two people recorded together. Two independent rhythms should draw the surface of a doughnut. Two locked rhythms should draw a simple ring. So the shape of the joint cloud would tell the coupling.

We checked it on simulated data. Here, we know the phases. Take 300 points, two independent phases. The cloud has two loops of 1.32 and 1.31, and a void of 0.77. Lock the two phases, and one loop remains, of 2.04. The idea works when you see the phases.

But you see signals, not phases. We summed two unrelated sines, delayed them into four columns, and kept 282 points. The two longest loops are 0.105 and 0.096 times the diameter. Plain noise gives 0.06 in the median of thirty draws, and 0.08 at most. That is barely above the floor. Ten times more points did not help: 2,382 points gave 0.109 and 0.089. So at 300 points we cannot see the torus on these signals. Calibrate first.

To our knowledge, these bars have hardly been used in hyperscanning. That is the result of a limited search, not a proof. A neighbouring work, Tadić and colleagues in 2016, counts groups of fully connected channels in networks across several brains. It uses no bars. For the measures themselves, see the phase locking episode of the Hyperscanning series.

One question separates the two shelves. Would a simpler distance from the earlier chapters have seen the same thing?

So, here is what is established. Where a variable turns, a direction or a position, the shape was expected, and it was found. Elsewhere, ask the question of this chapter: what does the hole say that the distance did not?
