The artificial neuron

The artificial neuron is a spring-held door pushed by bees: it multiplies inputs by weights, adds a bias, and answers through a smooth sigmoid instead of a hard threshold, because learning needs slopes. Weights and bias are the knobs. The analogy keeps summation and thresholding but leaves out spikes, dendritic computation and time, so the neuron is inspired by biology, not a model of it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the beehive. Every cell of the hive receives messages from its neighbors and passes one on. A network is a hive of such cells. Today we look at a single one: the artificial neuron. By the end, you will be able to write it in one line of code, and you will know exactly what it borrows from the brain, and what it leaves behind.

First, the real thing. A neuron collects signals through its dendrites, thousands of them at once. In the cell body, those signals add up. If the total is large enough, the neuron fires a spike down its axon. Either it fires, or it does not. A human brain holds about eighty-six billion of these cells, according to a 2009 count.

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published a famous paper, A logical calculus of the ideas immanent in nervous activity. Their idea was bold and simple. Treat a neuron as a unit that receives on-or-off inputs, adds them up, and fires when the sum reaches a threshold. They showed that networks of such units can compute logic. That is the ancestor of everything in this chapter.

Let's build one. Picture a small door in the wall of the hive, held shut by a spring. Three bees come to push on it. Each bee brings a number, an input. Here, two, one, and three. These numbers are illustrative.

But the door does not feel every bee equally. Each bee has a strength, called a weight. A positive weight pushes the door open. A negative weight pulls it shut. Our weights are zero point five, minus one, and zero point four. Weights are the knobs of chapter 1: they are the numbers that learning will turn.

How hard is the door pushed overall? Multiply each input by its weight, and add up. Two times zero point five is one. One times minus one is minus one. Three times zero point four is one point two. The total push is one point two. You have seen this recipe before: it is the dot product from chapter 2.

The spring has one more setting. Even with no bee at all, it holds the door shut with some force. We call it the bias, and we add it to the total. Our bias is minus zero point five, so the net push is zero point seven. In NumPy, the whole thing is one line: the weights, the at sign, the inputs, plus the bias.

Now, what does the door do with that push? In the model of McCulloch and Pitts, it is a hard door. Below the threshold, it stays shut. Above, it swings fully open. Nothing in between. In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt described the perceptron in the journal Psychological Review. It kept that hard door, and added something new: its weights were not fixed by hand, but adjusted from examples.

Today we use a softer door. It opens gradually, following a smooth S-shaped curve called the sigmoid. A strong push on the shut side gives almost zero. A strong push on the open side gives almost one. In between, the opening changes smoothly. Our push of zero point seven opens the door to zero point six seven. Lower the bias to minus two, and the net push becomes minus zero point eight: the door opens only thirty-one percent.

Why soften the door? Because learning needs slopes. In chapter 2, the gradient told us which way to turn each knob. A hard door has no slope: nudge a weight a little and nothing changes, until suddenly everything does. A soft door always tells you which way to turn.

So the artificial neuron is three steps. Multiply the inputs by the weights, add the bias, and pass the result through a soft threshold. Its knobs are the weights and the bias. Training means turning them to fit the data, exactly as in chapter 1.

Now, the honest part. What does the analogy say? Real neurons integrate many inputs, connections differ in strength, and the cell responds once the total crosses a threshold. The artificial neuron keeps these three ideas.

What does it leave out? The spikes: our door opens by an amount, a real neuron fires an all-or-none pulse. The dendrites: in a real cell they do their own computing, and one study needed a deep network, several layers deep, to imitate a single cortical neuron. And time: our neuron has no past. So an artificial neuron is inspired by a neuron. It is not a model of one.

To sum up. A neuron adds its weighted inputs, adds a bias, and answers through a soft threshold. The weights are the knobs. The analogy keeps the sum and the threshold, and forgets spikes, dendrites, and time. Next time: layers, and why non-linearity matters.

Layers, and why non-linearity matters

A layer is a matrix, a bias and a bend. Two linear layers multiply into one matrix, so without a bend depth is worth nothing; ReLU zeroes negatives and acts as a crease in a strip of paper; more creases follow a curve more closely. The universal approximation theorem promises a good network exists, not that learning finds it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the beehive. Last time we looked at a single cell: it weighs its inputs, adds a bias, and answers through a soft door. Today we build the hive. A hive is not one cell. It is floors of cells, stacked one above the other. By the end of this video, you will know why a stack of floors is useless unless each floor bends the signal, and you will meet a famous theorem about how far such a stack can go.

Here is one floor. It holds several cells side by side. Every cell receives the same inputs from the floor below, and each has its own weights and its own bias. Put all those weights in a table, and you have a matrix, the machine of episode 2.2. A necklace goes in, every cell takes its dot product, and a new necklace comes out. Then each cell adds its bias and answers through its door. A layer is a matrix, a bias, and a bend. And every one of those numbers is a knob, just like the knobs of chapter 1. There are simply many more of them.

A network is floors in a row. The necklace leaving one floor goes in at the bottom of the next. It is tempting to think that more floors always means more power. Let's test that with the smallest possible example, and with the bend left out for a moment.

Take the recording from episode 2.2: three, minus one, two, one. The first floor is the matrix from that episode, two rows and four columns. It sends out thirteen and zero. Now add a second floor, with its own matrix. Its first row is one, minus one, and its second row is two, one. Fed with thirteen and zero, it sends out thirteen and twenty-six. Two floors, two matrices, one answer. These numbers are illustrative.

Now multiply the two matrices together first. The result is a single matrix, with two rows and four columns: one, minus two, four, zero, and five, minus one, five, zero. Feed it the same recording, and out come thirteen and twenty-six. The very same answer. This is not luck. Two linear floors always collapse into one floor, whose matrix is the product of the two. A hundred floors would collapse the same way. Without a bend between them, a tall hive is a single floor in disguise.

So we need a bend. The simplest one is called ReLU, short for rectified linear unit. It does almost nothing. If a number is positive, let it through. If it is negative, replace it with zero. That is all: a flat line, then a slope, with a kink at zero. Let's put one between our two floors, and feed in another recording: minus one, two, zero, one. The first floor sends out minus four and one. ReLU turns the minus four into zero, and keeps the one. The second floor now sends out minus one and one. Without the bend, the single combined matrix would have given minus five and minus seven. The hive no longer collapses. Two floors now compute something that is no longer one matrix.

What does a bend do to a whole function? Picture a long strip of paper, perfectly straight. That is a straight line. Now crease it. A crease is a ReLU: the strip stays flat up to the crease, then it turns and rises with a new slope. Each unit is one crease. Its position is called the knot, and its weight says how sharp the crease is. A positive weight folds the strip up, a negative weight folds it down. Add creases, and the strip takes the shape of a mountain range.

Let's try it on a gentle hump, half a period of a sine wave. This target is illustrative, and every number here was computed with NumPy. I set the weights by hand to match the curve. Finding them by learning is another story. With two creases, at zero and at one half, with weights two and minus four, the strip makes a tent. The largest gap between the tent and the hump is zero point two one. With four creases, the strip hugs the hump much closer, and the largest gap falls to zero point zero seven. With eight creases, the gap is zero point zero one nine. Each time we double the creases, the gap shrinks by a factor of about three or four.

Notice what happened. We never changed the ingredients, only their number. Each crease adds a little shape. With enough of them, the strip follows the curve as closely as we like.

This is the idea behind a famous result. In 1989, two papers proved a theorem of this kind. George Cybenko published his in the journal Mathematics of Control, Signals and Systems. Kurt Hornik, Maxwell Stinchcombe and Halbert White published theirs in the journal Neural Networks. Today it is called the universal approximation theorem. Take a network with a single hidden floor, and any continuous function on a bounded region. Give that floor enough cells, and the network can come as close to the function as you want. Cybenko worked with S-shaped doors. In 1993, Leshno, Lin, Pinkus and Schocken showed that the door only has to avoid being a polynomial, which includes ReLU.

Now read the theorem carefully. It says that a good set of weights exists. It does not say that we will find it: learning, the knob turning of chapters 1 and 2, may still fail. And it does not say how many units we need, only that enough is enough. In our toy example, eight creases left a gap of zero point zero one nine. A harder curve, in many dimensions, may ask for far more. The theorem is a promise of possibility. It is not a recipe.

It still matters for this series. When we say a network can learn to encode an image, or to predict a representation, we count on networks being flexible enough. The theorem says they are flexible in principle. Whether they learn what we want is the real question of everything that follows.

To sum up. A layer is a matrix, a bias, and a bend. Two linear layers collapse into one, so without a bend, depth is worth nothing. ReLU is the bend: each one is a crease, and each crease adds shape. With enough creases, a single floor can approach almost any curve, but the theorem promises that a solution exists, not that we will find it. Next time: backpropagation, and how a hive with a great many knobs finds out which ones to turn.

PLATE 3.35:53

Backpropagation

Backpropagation finds the gradient of every knob at once. A forward pass computes the loss; a backward pass sends a slip of paper the other way, multiplying by each station's sensitivity, which is the chain rule; a knob's blame is the slip times what entered its station. One backward pass costs about twice a forward pass, however many knobs there are.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the hive. In chapter 2, the gradient told us which way is downhill for every knob, and gradient descent took the step. But one question was left hanging. A network can have millions of knobs, stacked in layers. Where does the gradient of each knob come from? By the end of this video, you will know the answer, and why it is the reason very large networks can be trained at all.

Let's build the smallest network we can: a production line with three stations. A number x comes in. Station one multiplies it by a knob, w one. Station two bends the result: a negative value is cut to zero, a positive value passes straight through. That bend is called ReLU. Station three multiplies by a second knob, w two. Out comes the answer, y. Our numbers are illustrative. The input x is two, w one is zero point five, and w two is one point five.

Run the line forward. Two times zero point five is one. The bend leaves one untouched. One times one point five is one point five. The target was zero point five, so the error is one, and the loss is that error squared: one. This trip, from the input to the loss, is the forward pass.

Now we want two slopes. If we nudge w one, how does the loss move? And if we nudge w two? Here is the trick. Start at the end, where the loss is, and send a slip of paper backward down the line. On the slip is a single number: how much the loss changes when the answer y changes. For a squared error, that is two times the error. The slip reads two.

The slip arrives at station three, and two things happen. First, this station owns a knob. The blame for w two is the slip, times whatever went into the station: two times one, which is two. That is the slope of the loss with respect to w two. Second, the slip must keep travelling, so it gets multiplied by this station's sensitivity: how much its output moves when its input moves. For a multiplication by w two, that is w two itself, one point five. The slip now reads three.

Station two is the bend. Its input was positive, so it passes everything through: a sensitivity of one. The slip still reads three. Station one owns the knob w one. The blame is the slip, times what went in, which was x: three times two is six. Two knobs, two numbers: blame two for w two, blame six for w one. This is the chain rule of calculus. The slope of a chain is the product of the slopes of its links, and the slip carries that product along, one station at a time.

Let's check it the slow way. Nudge w one up by a tiny amount, run the whole line again, and see how the loss moves: six. Nudge w two: two. The slip got it right. Now take the step from the last chapter, with a learning rate of zero point zero five. W one goes from zero point five to zero point two, and w two goes from one point five to one point four. The answer drops from one point five to zero point five six, and the loss falls from one to zero point zero zero three six. One forward pass, one backward pass, one step.

Here is the big idea. The slow way, nudge and rerun, costs one extra forward pass for every knob. With two knobs, that is nothing. With a million knobs, it means a million and one passes for a single step. The slip needs just one trip backward, and that one trip delivers the blame for every knob at once. It is also cheap. For the language models studied by Kaplan and colleagues in twenty twenty, the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass. So one forward pass and one backward pass cost about three forward passes, however many knobs there are.

Why so cheap? Because the slip reuses what the forward pass already computed: the input of every station. That is why the forward pass has to remember its numbers, and why training a network takes more memory than simply running it.

In code, none of this is written by hand. In PyTorch, you mark the knobs as needing gradients, run the forward pass, and call loss dot backward. Each knob then holds its blame in its dot grad. On our line, it prints six and two.

This procedure is called backpropagation, and its history has several chapters. In nineteen seventy, in Helsinki, Seppo Linnainmaa described how to accumulate derivatives backward, in a master's thesis about rounding errors. Paul Werbos proposed using it for neural networks, in the nineteen seventies and early eighties. Who deserves which share of the credit is still debated. In nineteen eighty-six, David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published a paper in Nature, showing that it lets the hidden layers of a network learn useful features. That paper brought the idea to a wide audience.

One last thing about the slip: it can be cut. Later in this series, we will sometimes cut it on purpose. A stop-gradient is a station that lets values pass forward, but rips up the slip on the way back. It is how some methods keep one part of a network still while another part learns. We will meet it in chapters 7 and 11.

To sum up. The forward pass runs the line and computes the loss. The backward pass sends a slip the other way, multiplying by each station's sensitivity: the chain rule. A knob's blame is the slip, times what went into its station. One backward pass gives every gradient, for about twice the price of the forward one. PLATE 3.46:56

Convolutional networks

A convolutional network slides a small stencil of weights over an image, taking a dot product at each position to ask whether the patch matches its pattern. A three-by-three edge detector lights up exactly along the vertical edges of a bright rectangle. Reusing the same nine weights everywhere replaces thousands of connections, and stacked layers build edges into textures, shapes and objects.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hive, the bees have a new job: looking at pictures. A picture is a grid of numbers, one per pixel. Even a tiny picture, twelve pixels by twelve, has one hundred and forty-four of them. Last time we stacked layers, and every cell listened to every number. Today we see why that is wasteful. By the end, you will know how a network finds an edge, with nine weights where you might expect thousands.

Here is a paper stencil, with a window cut in it, three pixels by three. Around the window, nine numbers are printed. They are weights. Lay the stencil on the top left corner of the image. Through the window you see nine pixels. Multiply each pixel by the weight printed next to it, and add everything up. That gives one number. Slide the stencil one step to the right, and do it again. Every position writes one number into a new grid, the output map. Slide along every row, and the map fills in.

Look at a single position. Nine pixels, nine weights. Multiply pair by pair, then add. That is the dot product from episode two point one. So the stencil asks one question, again and again: does this patch look like my pattern? A big positive number means yes. Zero means no. A negative number means the patch is the opposite of the pattern.

Let's try it on a real, tiny example. Our image is twelve by twelve. It shows a bright rectangle on a dark background, five pixels wide and six tall. The pixels are illustrative. Our stencil is an edge detector. Its left column holds minus one, its middle column zero, its right column plus one. In words: I light up where it is dark on my left and bright on my right. That is a vertical edge.

Put the stencil on pure background, and every product is zero. Put it fully inside the rectangle, and the minus ones and plus ones cancel: zero again. Now put it across the left side of the rectangle. The left and middle columns of the patch are dark, the right column is bright. Only the three plus ones count, and the sum is three. Across the right side, it is bright on the left and dark on the right, and the sum is minus three.

Now slide over all one hundred positions. The output map is ten by ten, because a window of three fits ten times along a row of twelve. We computed it with NumPy. Two stripes light up in pink, with threes in the middle and smaller values toward the corners, where the window only half meets the edge. Two stripes light up in blue, with minus threes. Everywhere else the map is zero. Where the image is flat, the stencil stays silent. It has found the vertical edges, and it tells us exactly where they are.

Turn the stencil a quarter turn, and it looks for horizontal edges instead. Same image, a different map: now the top and bottom of the rectangle light up. One stencil, one kind of pattern. A network uses many stencils at once, each one writing its own map.

Notice what never changed while we slid. The same nine weights were used at every position. The stencil recognises the edge wherever it appears. If you followed the EEG series, you have met this before. Episode two point four introduced convolution on a signal, along time. Today it is the same idea in two dimensions, along the rows and columns of an image. One footnote: deep learning libraries skip the flip of the textbook convolution, and so compute a cross-correlation. Since the weights are learned, the flip changes nothing.

Now count. Our stencil has nine weights. Suppose we used a fully connected layer instead, like those of last time, to turn the same image into a ten by ten map. Each of the hundred outputs would connect to each of the hundred and forty-four pixels. A hundred times a hundred and forty-four is fourteen thousand, four hundred weights. Nine, against fourteen thousand four hundred: sixteen hundred times fewer. And the fully connected layer would have to learn the edge detector separately at every position. The stencil learns it once, and uses it everywhere.

Where do the nine weights come from? Here we chose them by hand. In a network, nobody does. Backpropagation, from episode three point three, trains the kernels like any other weights. In the famous network that won the ImageNet competition in 2012, many first-layer kernels turned out to be selective for the orientation of an edge.

Then comes the second layer. Its stencils slide over the maps of the first layer, not over the pixels. Where a vertical edge meets a horizontal one, a corner appears. The next layer combines corners and edges into textures and simple shapes. Then parts, and then whole objects. Each layer sees a larger piece of the image than the one before, even though every stencil is still three by three. Edges, then textures, then shapes, then objects, layer after layer.

This idea has a history, and it starts with the person who later proposed JEPA. In 1989, Yann LeCun and colleagues at Bell Labs published Backpropagation applied to handwritten zip code recognition, in the journal Neural Computation. A network with convolutional layers, trained by backpropagation, read digits from United States Postal Service mail. In 1998, LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner published Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition, in the Proceedings of the IEEE. It described LeNet-five, a convolutional network with about sixty thousand trainable parameters. And it described a system for reading bank cheques. According to the paper, that system was part of NCR's line of cheque readers, fielded in several banks across the United States since June 1996, reading millions of cheques.

So the author of JEPA is also an author of convolutional networks.

There is one more bridge, and it leads to the brain. In 1962, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel published their work on the cat's visual cortex. They found cells that prefer an edge of a given orientation in a small part of the visual field. And they proposed a scheme in which a higher-order cell receives input from several such cells, all preferring a vertical edge, each at a slightly different position. That cell responds to a vertical edge wherever it falls. It looks very much like our stencils. We come back to it at the end of the series.

To sum up. A small stencil slides over the image, and at every position it computes a dot product: it lights up where it recognises its pattern. The same weights are used everywhere, so nine weights did the work of fourteen thousand. Stack the layers, and edges become textures, shapes, and objects. Next time: attention and transformers.

PLATE 3.55:56

Attention and transformers

In attention, every bee makes a question, a key and a value from her necklace. Her question is compared with every key by dot product, a softmax turns the scaled scores into weights, and the weights blend the values into a new necklace; several heads run in parallel and position codes restore order. Cut into patches, a picture becomes a sentence a transformer can read.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the hive. In a hive, nobody works alone, and every bee has to decide whom to listen to. A network faces the same problem. When it reads a word, or a patch of a picture, which of the other pieces deserve its attention? Today we build that mechanism. By the end, you will be able to read the one line of code at the heart of every transformer, and you will see how a picture becomes a sentence.

A little history first. In 2014, Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio let a translation network soft-search the source sentence for the parts that matter to the next word. That was attention, added to a network that still read one word at a time. Then, in 2017, eight researchers published a paper with a bold title: Attention Is All You Need. They dropped the one-at-a-time reading altogether and kept only attention. That model is the transformer. On English to German translation, it scored twenty-eight point four BLEU points, better than every earlier result, including ensembles of models.

Let's meet the bees. Four bees sit in a row, and each one carries a necklace, the same kind of necklace as before. Now every bee makes three new necklaces out of her own. She passes it through three different matrices, the machines of episode 2.2, whose entries are knobs that learning will turn. Out come a question, a key, and a value. Our necklaces have four beads, and their values are illustrative.

The question says what this bee is looking for. The key says what this bee offers, like the label on a honey pot. The value is the honey itself: what she will actually hand over if someone listens to her.

Now follow one bee, the one on the left. She holds up her question, and compares it with the key of every bee in the hive, herself included. How? With the dot product, the tally jar of episode 2.1. Her question against the first key gives two. Against the second, one. Against the third, minus one. Against the fourth, minus two. A high score means a good match. A negative score means no interest.

There is one small adjustment. Divide every score by the square root of the length of the necklace. Ours has four beads, so we divide by two. Why? The authors suspected that with long necklaces, the dot products grow large, and that pushes the next step into a region where its gradients are extremely small. Dividing keeps the scores in a comfortable range. Our scores become one, one half, minus one half, and minus one.

Next, a softmax turns the four scores into four weights. Softmax has its own episode, in chapter 2. Here, you only need what it gives: weights that are all positive and add up to one. Our bee gets fifty-one percent for the first bee, thirty-one for the second, eleven for the third, and seven for the fourth. We draw them as listening threads. The thicker the thread, the harder she listens.

Now, the honey. She takes the value of each bee, scales it by that bee's weight, and adds everything up. Half of the first pot, a third of the second, and a pinch of the others. The result is a single new necklace: the old bee, now informed by the hive. And every bee does the same thing at the same moment.

That is the whole mechanism. In one line of code: softmax, of Q times K transposed, divided by the square root of d, then times V. Pack all the questions into one matrix, and all the bees run at once. It is nothing but matrix products, which a GPU does very well. The line is equation one of the paper.

One set of questions is not enough, because a hive has many things to ask at once. So each bee makes several sets of question, key and value necklaces, called heads, and they run side by side. Their answers are glued together. The base model of the paper uses eight heads, each with necklaces of sixty-four beads. Eight times sixty-four is five hundred twelve.

There is one catch. Attention by itself ignores order. Shuffle the bees, and each one still gets the same weights. So the paper adds to every necklace a code that says where in the row the bee sits. It is called a positional encoding.

Now for the surprise. Take a picture. In 2020, Dosovitskiy and colleagues asked whether a transformer could read it with almost no change. Their paper, published at ICLR in 2021, is called An Image Is Worth Sixteen by Sixteen Words. Cut the picture into squares of sixteen by sixteen pixels. A picture two hundred twenty-four pixels wide gives fourteen by fourteen squares: one hundred ninety-six patches. Flatten each patch into a list of numbers, pass it through one learned matrix, and it becomes a necklace. Add a position code, and the picture is a sentence of one hundred ninety-six words. The same transformer reads it. This model is called the Vision Transformer.

Once a picture is a row of necklaces, a new game becomes possible. Hide some of the patches, and ask the network to guess what was under them. Nobody has to label anything, because the picture itself holds the answers. This is the basic move of masked autoencoders, in episode 6.5, which guess the missing pixels. And it is the basic move of I-JEPA, in chapter 11, which guesses the missing necklace instead. Same game, different thing to guess. That choice, what to predict and in which space, is the thread of this whole series.

To sum up. Each bee makes a question, a key and a value. Question against keys gives scores, the softmax turns them into weights, and the weights blend the values. Cut a picture into patches, and it becomes a row of necklaces. Next time: training in practice, and what changes when everything gets big.

PLATE 3.66:46

Training in practice

Training is a cook nudging the knobs of a soup pot. A flame too low is slow and one too high boils over, so the dashboard's loss curve reveals the learning rate; small batches give noisy spoonfuls, and rising validation loss signals overfitting. Normalization puts layers on one ruler, GPUs run many multiply-adds at once, and scaling laws make loss predictable but not meaningful.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the beehive. We have the parts: neurons, layers, backpropagation, convolutional networks, attention. Today we ask what it takes to actually train one. By the end of this video, you will know how to read a training dashboard, why a few settings decide whether training works, and why size changes everything.

Picture a kitchen at the edge of the hive. A big pot of soup sits on the stove. The soup is our network, and its knobs are the weights. The cook has one goal: make it taste right. Taste is the loss from chapter one, one number for how wrong we are. After every taste, the cook nudges the knobs in the direction the gradient points. Backpropagation, from episode three point three, computes that direction. And how far to nudge is the learning rate, from episode two point four. That is the flame under the pot.

On the wall hangs the dashboard. Its main dial is the loss, plotted after every step. We made a toy to show it, and it is only an illustration: one knob, sixty-four noisy points, and plain gradient descent, computed in NumPy. Same pot, same start, three flames.

With a tiny flame, the loss falls, but so slowly that after twenty steps it has only gone from eighteen point eight to ten point seven. With a good flame, it drops to zero point two three in about seven steps, and then it flattens. With a flame that is too high, every step overshoots the bottom of the valley, and lands higher up the other side. The loss goes up by about ten times at each step: eighteen point eight, then one hundred ninety-six, then two thousand fifty-five. The pot boils over. On a real dashboard, that curve shoots off the top of the chart.

The second setting is the spoon. Tasting the whole pot before every nudge is accurate, but slow. So instead, the cook tastes one spoonful, a small batch of examples, and nudges after each. In the toy, we used the same flame and three spoon sizes: one point, eight points, and all sixty-four. The full batch gives a smooth, steady curve. The single point is jumpy: the soup tastes worse again every few steps, because that one point was not typical. All three end near the same loss. This is the stochastic gradient descent of episode two point four: noisy steps that are cheap, against smooth steps that cost more.

A real dashboard draws two curves. One is the training loss. The other is the validation loss, measured on examples the network never nudged itself on, as in episode one point three. When training keeps falling and validation turns upward, that is overfitting, from episode one point four, and it is the signal to stop.

Now a problem that only deep networks have. Each layer receives numbers from the layer before. If those numbers drift to wildly different sizes, the next layer must keep readjusting, and a flame that suits one layer scorches another. Normalization is the fix, and the picture is simple. Before passing the numbers on, line them up on one ruler: shift them so their average is zero, and stretch them so their spread is one.

Batch normalization measures that average and spread across the batch. Ioffe and Szegedy introduced it in twenty fifteen. On an image classifier, they report reaching the same accuracy in fourteen times fewer training steps, and it allowed much higher learning rates. Layer normalization, by Ba, Kiros and Hinton in twenty sixteen, measures them across one example instead, so it does not depend on the batch at all. Transformers use it after every sub-layer. And why does it help? That is still discussed. In twenty eighteen, Santurkar and colleagues argued that the original explanation was mostly wrong, and that normalization mainly makes the loss landscape smoother.

Now the stove itself. Remember matrices, from episode two point two. Each output is a pile of multiply-adds, and none of them depends on the others. So they can all happen at the same time. A processor for general use is a few strong cooks. A graphics processor, a GPU, is a huge crowd of simple cooks, all doing the same step on different numbers. That is why training moved there.

Now the key moment: scale. What happens if the pot gets bigger? In January twenty twenty, Kaplan and colleagues trained many language models of different sizes and watched the loss. It fell as a power law with the size of the model, the size of the data set, and the amount of compute, across many orders of magnitude. On a chart with logarithmic axes, a power law is a straight line. And a straight line can be extended. You can estimate what a bigger run will give before you pay for it. That predictability is a large part of the momentum behind bigger models.

Two years later, Hoffmann and colleagues asked how to split a fixed budget between model size and data. They trained over four hundred models, and their paper says its results differ considerably from Kaplan's. Their answer: for every doubling of model size, double the training tokens too. They tested it with a model called Chinchilla. It had seventy billion parameters and saw one point four trillion tokens. Gopher, the model they compared it with, was four times bigger, with two hundred eighty billion parameters, but saw only three hundred billion tokens, with the same compute budget. Chinchilla did better on a wide range of tasks. Their table suggests about twenty tokens per parameter. That is a reading of the table, not a law of nature. Both papers measure loss on language, not understanding.

Here is why this matters for our series. Every training step pays for a prediction, and compute is the budget. If the thing we ask the network to predict is expensive and mostly unpredictable, like every pixel of the next frame, then the budget goes to details no model could know. We will see that in episode six point five. Scaling laws tell you how the loss falls as you spend. They do not tell you whether the loss measures something worth predicting. So what to predict is also a budget question, and it is the thread of this whole series.

To sum up. The dashboard shows the loss, and a good curve falls and flattens. The learning rate is the flame: too small is slow, too large boils over. The batch is the spoon: small is noisy, large is smooth but costs more. Normalization puts every layer on one ruler, and GPUs do many multiply-adds at once. Scale is predictable, but what to spend it on is a choice. Next time: embeddings, turning anything into a necklace. Chapter four begins.
