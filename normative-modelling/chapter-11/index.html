PLATE 11.16:10

Who is in your reference?

A norm describes only the people who built it. In simulation, healthy people from a site missing in the reference get an average z-score of minus 0.73 and nearly a quarter fall below the 2.5th centile, though none do against their own site's curve. A z-score then mixes the person's deviation with their group's distance from the reference.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we walked the gallery to its last room. Alzheimer's disease, where the deviations mostly point the same way. Every portrait in that gallery was placed on a chart. Today we ask a question we have been postponing since the first episode. Who drew the chart?

Welcome to the palace of mirrors. Each mirror in this hall bends the norm in its own way. The first one is the simplest. It shows only some of the people.

Start with the idea of a reference. A normative model gives a centile curve. That curve describes the people who were used to build it. Nobody else. A norm is never better than the population behind it.

Let us look at a real reference. In 2022, a large team published brain charts for the human lifespan, in the journal Nature. They pooled about one hundred and one thousand people, from more than one hundred studies. The ages run from before birth to one hundred years. It is the largest reference of its kind, and members of the same team say plainly where it is thin.

Now look at the map. Each study has a place on it, and the places are not spread evenly. Later work describes these charts as drawn mostly from European and North American samples. A commentary by members of that team says it more bluntly. It speaks of an overrepresentation of European and North American populations, and it calls that a bias shared with the wider neuroimaging literature.

The mirror is not hiding this. The question is what it does to a person who was not in the room.

Here is a simulated example. Everything on the next screens is simulated, with fictional units.

We have a fictional brain measure, and an age range from eight to eighty years. Two big sites, A and B, and one small site, C. Site C only has people from forty-five to eighty. Its measurements read a little lower than site A. At age sixty, the true average is about ninety-five and a half at site A, and about ninety-one and a half at site C. Four units apart, and nothing is wrong with anyone.

Now take the sixty people of site C, and score every one of them against the curve of site A. Each person is the same healthy person they always were. Only the mirror has changed.

The average z-score of site C comes out at minus point seven three. Fourteen of the sixty fall below the 2.5th centile of site A. In a fair reference, we would expect about one or two. Nearly a quarter of these healthy people look abnormally low.

Now score the same sixty people against the curve of their own site. The average z-score is minus point zero five. None falls below the 2.5th centile.

Same people, same measurements. Two very different stories. In the first, part of every z-score was never about the person. It was their distance from the people who built the reference.

That is the key moment of this episode. A person who is absent from the reference gets a z-score that measures two things at once. How far they are from the norm of their own group, and how far their group is from the reference. The z-score cannot tell the two apart.

The site is only one kind of absence. A reference can also miss ages, countries, ancestries, or family incomes. In our simulation we know the true answer. For ancestry, nobody does. That is why it is dangerous.

Even a careful reference makes a choice. Think of the World Health Organization child growth standards. Their reference study followed children in six places. Pelotas in Brazil, Accra in Ghana, Delhi in India, Oslo in Norway, Muscat in Oman, and Davis in California. It ran from 1997 to 2003. It chose healthy, breastfed children, in communities selected for high socioeconomic status. So even a deliberately international reference defines who counts as the norm. It is a choice, and the choice is written in the protocol.

For the brain, population-specific charts are starting to appear. As of November 2026, a 2025 preprint from a Chinese consortium reports charts built from about forty-three thousand people. It reports that these charts predicted healthy measures, and detected deviations in Chinese clinical groups, better than charts built on Western data. It is a preprint, and not yet peer-reviewed. But the direction matters. A reference that matches the person gives a fairer score.

So what can you do, when your person is not in the reference?

First, say it. Read the model label. Look at the reference line, and ask who is in it. Ages, sites, countries. If a line is empty, that is where the problem hides.

Second, measure it. Compare your controls to the reference. If healthy controls from your site have a mean z-score far from zero, your reference does not fit them. In the Information series, the distance between two distributions has a name, the Kullback–Leibler divergence. You do not need it here. The mean and the spread of the control z-scores already tell you a lot.

Third, adapt the model locally. This is the transfer we met with site effects in chapter five. In the fMRI series, scanner differences have the same flavour. A small set of local controls can recentre the curve, as scoring site C against its own curve showed. The braincharts team shares models and a multi-site transfer set, drawn from six OpenNeuro datasets, so that others can do just this.

Fourth, contribute. A more diverse reference makes the mirror fairer for everyone.

So, to sum up. A norm describes the people who built it. A person who was not in that reference gets a z-score that partly measures their distance from the others. Say who is in the reference, measure the gap, adapt locally, and add data that were missing.

Next time, a different mirror. It keeps the corridor going past the wall, where there is no data at all. Off the edge, extrapolation.

PLATE 11.24:41

Off the edge: extrapolation

Past the last data point a model keeps answering. In a simulation fitted up to age sixty, a line, a parabola and a cubic give very different medians at eighty, and the cubic is off by about sixty-four units. Polynomials do not know where their data stopped, so the valid range must be checked before scoring.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we asked who is in the reference. A person who is missing from the population gets a score that partly measures how far they sit from everyone else. Today the missing part is not a person. It is a stretch of the axis. An age, or a site, that the model has never seen.

Picture a long corridor in the hall of mirrors. It runs straight, then it hits a wall. But one mirror, at the end, repeats the corridor beyond the wall. It looks like more of the same. It is only a reflection. That mirror is extrapolation.

Here is the setting. It is a simulated dataset. The measure is a fictional cortical index, in arbitrary units, against age. We take site A, and we keep only the people aged up to sixty. That leaves one hundred and seventy-six people. Nobody older goes into the fit.

Now we fit three models. They are simple, and they differ only in how flexible they are. A straight line. A parabola, which is a curve of degree two. And a cubic, a curve of degree three. Each one draws a median through the grey dots, and each one is happy.

Inside the data, the three medians follow the cloud. Move the eye to sixty, the last place where there are people. Nothing looks wrong.

Now we walk past the wall. We ask each model for the median at age eighty.

The line says eighty-nine. The parabola says ninety-four. The cubic says twenty-three and a half.

Three confident answers. They cannot all be right. Each one comes with its centile lines, drawn as calmly as inside the data. And here we have something real life never gives us. This dataset is simulated, so we know the truth. At eighty, the true median is eighty-seven and a half.

The line lands closest. But nothing in the fit told us that it would. The parabola misses by about six and a half units. The cubic misses by about sixty-four. At seventy, the cubic already says sixty-five, when the truth is ninety-two. At one hundred, it is far below zero.

This is the key moment. A polynomial has no idea where its data stopped. Past the last point, the highest power takes over, and the curve swings wherever its algebra sends it. The model does not know that it does not know. It just keeps answering.

And a wrong median does not stay a wrong median. It becomes a wrong score. If the cubic is the norm at eighty, a perfectly typical eighty-year-old looks far above it. The deviation would come from the model, and not from the person.

So what should happen at the wall? Here is where MIST comes in. Remember the two kinds of uncertainty. The yellow ribbon is the spread between people. The blue haze is what the model does not know for lack of data. Even in the full simulated set, the edges are thin. Only thirteen people, across all three sites, are between seventy-four and eighty. Past the last data point, the haze should thicken, and quickly. A model that is honest about its edges widens that band.

Some models do. A Gaussian process falls back toward its prior far from the data. Its prediction relaxes to the prior mean, and its uncertainty grows back toward the prior spread. In the Information series, this is the part of the uncertainty that more data could remove.

Other models do not. A natural cubic spline is forced to go straight beyond its outer knots. A straight line is a calm answer. It is also a made-up one. It is the shape the method requires, not something the data said.

And the same wall exists for sites. Site C, in our simulated set, has nobody under forty-five. A model of site C asked about a thirty-year-old is not measuring that person. It is reading its own assumptions.

So here is the habit. Before you compute any score, read the model label. One line of it is called valid range. Age range, sites, sex, scanner. Check that the person you are scoring sits inside it. If the line is empty, the label shows it empty, in a dashed pink frame. That is often where the trouble is.

Published charts do this. The brain charts of 2022 state their span. They draw on more than one hundred studies, and cover ages from one hundred and fifteen days after conception to one hundred years. Inside that span, a score has a meaning. Outside, it has none.

So, to sum up. A model knows nothing outside its support, and it will answer anyway. Check the valid range before you score anyone.

PLATE 11.35:55

Leakage and double dipping

Leakage lets information from the people being judged slip into the model that judges them, and double dipping uses the same data to choose and to test. Four forms are shown, including harmonisation before the split and controls scored by their own norm. In simulation, leakage alone makes patients look nearly twice as deviant.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a mirror stretched the corridor past the wall, and a model made confident predictions where it had never seen a single person. Today the mirror does something sneakier. It shows you the answer before you have asked the question.

The mistake has a name. Leakage. Information from the people you are going to judge slips into the model that does the judging. Its twin has another name. Double dipping. You use the same data twice, once to choose or to build something, and once to test it.

The rule that prevents both is old and simple. Whatever you compare against the norm must not have shaped the norm.

Let us walk through four ways to break it. Four mirrors.

The first mirror is the easiest one. Take a group of controls and a group of patients. Fit the model on controls. Now, to have more data, slip some of the test controls into the training set. Those people helped draw the centile lines. Of course they sit close to the lines. Their z-scores are small, because the model has already seen them.

The patients were never in the training set. So their z-scores come out larger. The patients look more deviant, and the only reason is who was allowed into the room.

Here is the proof, on simulated data. We take the simulated cortical index, site A, and draw two groups at random. Sixty controls, and sixty patients. The two groups come from exactly the same distribution. By construction, no disorder exists in this data.

We fit a flexible curve to the controls, and compute z-scores for everyone. Averaged over five hundred random draws, four point six percent of the controls fall beyond one point nine six. But eight point five percent of the patients do. Nearly twice as many, with no disease at all. That is an effect produced by leakage alone.

The second mirror hides in the preprocessing. Remember harmonisation, the correction that removes differences between scanners and sites. A common tool is called ComBat. It estimates each site's offset and scale from the data it is given. If you compute it on everyone, and only then split into training and test, information from the test people has already flowed into every score. In a study of one thousand seven hundred and forty healthy people from thirty-six datasets, Marzi and colleagues showed that harmonising before the split gave inflated performance, in both simulated and real data. The fix is to fit the harmonisation on the training set only, then apply those stored numbers to the test set.

The third mirror is the model chosen by peeking. You try five models. You look at how well each one separates patients from controls. You keep the winner, and report its separation. But the patients helped pick the model. This is double dipping in its classic form. Kriegeskorte and colleagues, in 2009, reviewed one hundred and thirty-four brain imaging papers published in 2008. They found that forty-two percent, fifty-seven papers, contained at least one analysis that was not independent of how the data had been selected. Choose your model using controls only, or using a separate set of data that the comparison will never touch.

The fourth mirror is the quietest. The same people define the norm, and are then compared to it. Say a site recruits sixty controls, fits its own normative model, and then reports deviations for those same sixty. Their scores are shrunk toward zero, just like the first mirror. And the fewer the people, the more flexible the curve, and the stronger the effect. A flexible model with few people can almost trace each person individually.

There is a standard remedy for the fourth mirror. Cross-validation. Cut the controls into folds. Fit the model on all folds but one, and score the people in the fold that was left out. Then rotate. Every control gets a z-score from a model that never saw them. And the patients are scored by a model fitted on controls alone.

Notice what this costs. Fewer people draw the lines at each step. That is the price of honesty, and it is worth paying.

Now, the key moment. Look at what the four mirrors have in common.

Every shortcut makes controls look more normal, and patients look more deviant, than they really are. None of them makes the result worse in an obvious way. Each one makes it look better. That is why they survive. A clean-looking result is a reason to check, not a reason to relax.

The problem is not special to our field. A review by Kapoor and Narayanan, published in 2023, found leakage affecting at least two hundred and ninety-four papers across seventeen research fields. Leakage is everywhere that models meet data.

So here is a short checklist to carry away. Four questions.

One. Did any person in the test set also appear in the training set? Check identifiers, including repeat scans of the same person.

Two. Was every step that learns from data fitted on the training set only? Harmonisation, scaling, choosing the number of components, all of it.

Three. Were the choices of model and settings made without looking at the patients?

Four. When controls are scored, are they controls the model has never seen?

In the EEG series, double dipping gets an episode of its own, with the same trap in a different place.

So, to sum up. Leakage and double dipping let the answer sneak into the question, and they always flatter the result. Keep what builds the norm apart from what is compared to it.

PLATE 11.45:13

Controls are not a random sample

Controls are recruited, screened, scanned and filtered, and each step can flatter the norm. Screening out the least healthy fifth moves the reference's 2.5th centile to the twenty-second of the whole population. Patients who move more look thinner without any brain change, so held-out controls and motion covariates test what else could produce a deviation.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a mirror showed you the answer before you asked the question. The people you judged had already shaped the norm. Today the mirrors do something gentler. They flatter.

Here is the quiet assumption behind every normative model. The controls we recruited stand for the population. Look at who actually walks through the door, and that assumption starts to bend.

Start with the people. A control group is rarely a random sample. Volunteers answer an advert. Many are screened. No history of illness, no medication, no family story. Some studies go further and screen controls harder than they screen patients. This has a name. Supernormal controls, or well controls.

The statisticians Schwartz and Susser showed, with worked numerical examples, that this practice biases a comparison, and they found no case where it improved validity. The idea is simple. If you screen out illness in controls but not in patients, the groups differ in more than the disorder.

Even big cohorts are not a mirror of the street. The UK Biobank invited about nine point two million people. Five point five percent took part. Compared with the general population, the participants smoked less, were less often obese, and reported fewer health conditions. The authors call it a healthy volunteer selection bias.

Now picture what that does to a norm. Here is an illustrative case, not real data. Say the controls are screened so that the least healthy fifth of the population never enters the reference. The reference is narrower, and shifted. Its twenty-fifth centile, and its two point five centile, move inward.

Do the arithmetic. The lowest fifth is gone. So the reference's two point five centile sits at the twenty-second centile of the whole population. A line meant to flag one person in forty now flags more than one in five. An ordinary person, from the street, looks deviant.

So the first mirror makes the norm too tight. Now the second mirror, and it is about the scanner.

Patients often move more than controls. In a study of more than two thousand people, from open autism, attention and schizophrenia datasets, every clinical group moved more than the healthy controls. And motion is not harmless. Reuter and colleagues scanned twelve healthy volunteers who moved on purpose. Estimated cortical thickness and volume fell by about seven tenths of one percent for every millimetre of motion per minute. That held even in scans that passed a visual check.

Read that again. A person who moves looks thinner in the cortex, with no change in the brain at all.

Put the two together. The patient group moves more, so it looks thinner. The norm was built on still, screened controls. So the patient gets a negative z-score. Is that the disorder? Is it movement? Or is it the selection of the controls? The map cannot tell you.

The third mirror hides in quality control. Every pipeline throws some scans away. Bad segmentation, blur, a failed reconstruction. But the rejection is not equal. If more patients than controls are thrown out, the patients who remain are the calm ones, and the comparison changes shape. In the EEG series, we saw that the share of data rejected is not the same in every population. The same is true here.

And there is a trap inside the trap. Remove the worst scans from the patients only, and you have made the patients look more normal. Remove them from the controls only, and the norm gets tighter, as before. Either way, the cut itself becomes part of the result.

So how do you test for it? Here are four checks you can run.

First, hold out some controls. Their z-scores should have a mean near zero, and a standard deviation near one. Better still, score a sample of the general population, if you have one. If they come out tight, or shifted, your reference is not the population.

Second, look inside the controls. Plot their z-scores against motion and against the quality score. If the z-scores follow motion, the norm is measuring the scanner.

Third, deal with motion directly. Put a motion or quality measure in the model as a covariate, or match patients and controls on it, and see which deviations survive.

Fourth, count. Report how many people each group lost at every quality step. Then repeat the analysis with a stricter and a looser threshold. A deviation that appears only at one threshold is a warning.

None of this makes controls useless. It makes the claim smaller. The norm describes the controls we recruited, not the population. And a patient's deviation is a mixture of the disorder, the movement, and the way the controls were chosen.

So, to sum up. Controls are chosen, scanned and filtered, and each step can flatter the norm. Before you read a deviation as a disorder, test what else could have produced it.

PLATE 11.56:10

From deviation to diagnosis, too fast

A flag beyond the 97.5th centile is not a diagnosis. In an invented example with one percent prevalence, an eighty percent detection rate and a five percent false alarm rate, only about fourteen percent of flagged people have the disorder. Many regions multiply false alarms, and a rare score does not say why.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that the norm describes the controls we recruited, not the population. Today we visit the last mirror of the palace. It is the one that makes everything bigger. It takes a deviation, and shows us a diagnosis.

Start with a number that looks reassuring. A deviation map flags a person. The flag sits far from the middle, beyond the 97.5th centile, or beyond a z-score of plus 1.96. And suppose that flag catches a disorder in most of the people who have it.

Let us build an example, and keep it small. Everything on this screen is illustrative. These are not measured results. They are invented numbers, chosen to make one point.

Imagine a disorder present in one person out of a hundred. One percent. Imagine a deviation flag that catches it in eighty percent of the people who have it. That is a good flag, better than most things we measure in the brain. And imagine that the same flag also appears in five percent of the people who do not have the disorder.

Five percent is not a bad habit of this flag. It is what a centile cut means. If you draw the line at the 2.5th and the 97.5th centiles, then five percent of the reference population lies outside it, by definition. Healthy people sit in the tails too. That is what a tail is.

Now count. Take ten thousand people. A hundred of them have the disorder. Nine thousand nine hundred do not.

Among the hundred who have it, the flag catches eighty. Among the nine thousand nine hundred who do not, the flag fires for five percent. That is four hundred and ninety-five people.

Now look at everyone who got flagged. Eighty people with the disorder, and four hundred and ninety-five without. Five hundred and seventy-five flagged people in all.

Pick one of them, at random. What is the chance that this person has the disorder? Eighty out of five hundred and seventy-five. About fourteen percent. This is called the positive predictive value. About six flagged people in seven are healthy.

Here is the moment that surprises almost everyone. The flag was good. Eighty percent is a real detection rate. Five percent is a modest false alarm rate. And yet, most of the flagged people do not have the disorder. The reason is the base rate. When a disorder is rare, even a small false alarm rate, applied to a very large healthy majority, produces more false alarms than true ones.

Now change only one number. Let the disorder be present in ten percent of the people, not one. Same flag, same eighty, same five. Now the flagged group is made of eight hundred people with the disorder, and four hundred and fifty without. The chance that a flagged person has the disorder is about sixty-four percent. Same flag. A very different meaning. The value of a flag depends on who you are testing, not only on the flag.

There is a second trap, and it comes from the brain map itself. A deviation map has many regions. Each region has its own five percent. Suppose there were a hundred regions, and suppose they were independent. Then the chance that a healthy person has at least one region beyond 1.96 is more than ninety-nine percent. Real regions are correlated, so the true number is lower, but the lesson stays. One extreme region is not a finding. We come back to this when we count deviations, and again at the end of the series.

And a third. In the Information series, a rare event carries a lot of surprise. But the surprise of a score is not evidence about a disease. A very low centile tells us the person is unusual compared with the reference. It does not tell us why.

Marquand and colleagues say it plainly, in their 2019 review in Molecular Psychiatry. Normative models do not directly indicate whether the deviations obtained are clinically relevant. A deviation may be biologically meaningful, and still be unrelated to psychopathology. And remember the chapter we are finishing. A deviation can also come from the reference, from motion, from the site, or from how the controls were chosen.

So today a normative model is a research tool. It places a person in a population, with an uncertainty. Whether that placement means anything for a diagnosis is a separate question. It needs its own evidence. We will climb that ladder in chapter twelve.

Here is what we can do with this, in practice. Some sentences to avoid, and what to say instead.

Avoid: this patient has the disorder, because the map shows deviations. Say instead: this person's measures are unusual compared with the reference population.

Avoid: the model detects the disorder with eighty percent accuracy. Say instead: report the detection rate, the false alarm rate, and the prevalence of the group where it was tested. Together, they give the predictive value.

Avoid: this map is normal, so the disorder is ruled out. Say instead: in our example, the flag missed twenty out of every hundred people who have the disorder. A flag that misses people cannot rule anyone out.

Avoid: telling a family that their child sits at the second centile of the brain, as if it were a result. Say instead: nothing, until there is evidence that this number changes a decision. If it ever does, the words must come with the uncertainty, and with the people who live with the answer.

And when you read an article, ask three questions. How rare is the disorder in the group they tested? What happens in the people who do not have it? And was the flag chosen on the same data that it is judged on?

So, to sum up. A deviation tells you where a person sits in a reference. It does not tell you, by itself, what they have. And when a disorder is rare, most flagged people are healthy.
