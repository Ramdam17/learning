The estimation problem

Mutual information is a property of a true distribution, while an estimate is a property of a finite sample and a recipe. The plug-in estimator, which bins data and treats frequencies as probabilities, finds about 0.36 bits between two independent noise signals. The bias is always upward, shrinks roughly as one over the sample size, and grows with the number of bins.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the river. Somewhere in this water there is gold, the true amount of information between two signals. We cannot see it directly. All we can do is scoop up a pan of sand and gravel, a finite sample of data, and sift it. This chapter is about the sieves and about the gold they invent.

Let's start with the distinction that everything else rests on. Mutual information is a property of a probability distribution. It is a fixed number defined by the true joint law of the two signals. We never have that law. We have a recording, a finite number of samples.

An estimator is a recipe that takes the samples and returns a number. The true value is the gold in the river. The estimate is what ends up in your pan. And the two are not the same thing.

The most natural recipe is called the plug-in estimator. Cut each signal into bins. Count how many samples fall into each cell of the grid. Divide by the total and treat those frequencies as if they were the true probabilities. Then plug them into the formula for mutual information.

It sounds harmless. Let's test it on the one case where we know the answer for sure.

Take two signals that are completely independent. Two streams of Gaussian noise generated separately. Their true mutual information is exactly 0 bits. Not approximately 0. 0.

Now record 100 samples of each. Cut each axis into 8 bins and compute the plug-in estimate. We get around 0.36 bits. Not 0. The sieve has found gold in a river that has none.

Is this bad luck? Let's repeat the experiment 300 times, each time with fresh noise. Each repetition gives one estimate. Stack them up into a histogram.

Two things appear. First, the whole pile sits to the right of zero. Its center is about 0.36 bits. The distance between the center of the pile and the true value is called the bias. Second, the pile has a width. Each new recording gives a slightly different number. That spread is called the variance.

In numbers, with 100 samples, the estimates spread with a standard deviation of about 0.065 bits around their center of 0.36. So the bias is more than five times larger than the spread. Repeating the experiment does not make the problem go away. Every repetition is wrong in the same direction.

Bias and variance are the two ways an estimator can fail. Bias means it is wrong on average. Variance means it is unreliable from one sample to the next. A good estimator keeps both small, and there is usually a trade-off between them.

Why is the bias positive and not just random in both directions? There is a short and beautiful argument.

The plug-in estimate is itself a mutual information, the mutual information of the frequency table we counted. And a mutual information can never be negative. So every single estimate is zero or above. If the true value is zero, the only way for the average to be zero would be for every estimate to be exactly zero. With noisy samples, that essentially never happens, so the average must sit above zero.

There is a second way to see it. The plug-in estimate of entropy is too low on average. With few samples, many cells stay empty, and the distribution looks more concentrated than it really is. The joint table has many more cells than each marginal, so its entropy is underestimated the most. Mutual information is the sum of the two marginal entropies minus the joint entropy. Subtract something that is too small and the result comes out too large.

Here is a simple way to feel why. 8 bins per axis make an 8x8 grid, 64 cells. With 100 samples, that is about one and a half samples per cell. Many cells stay empty, others get two or three samples by pure chance. The true joint distribution of two independent signals is perfectly smooth, but the counted table is lumpy. And the plug-in recipe reads every lump as a little piece of dependence.

How does the bias depend on the amount of data? Let's run the same experiment with more and more samples.

With 50 samples, the average estimate is about 0.69 bits. With 100, 0.36. With 500, 0.07. With 1000, 0.03. With 10,000, 0.003.

Each time we multiply the data by 10, the bias shrinks by about 10. It falls roughly like 1 over the number of samples. And it grows with the number of bins. We will put an exact formula on this in the next episode.

The bias does not only appear when the true value is zero. It sits on top of real information too. Take two Gaussian signals with a correlation of 0.5. Their true mutual information is 0.21 bits. With 100 samples and the same 8 bins, the plug-in estimate averages about 0.48 bits. More than twice the truth. With 1000 samples, it comes back close to the true value.

What is this bias sensitive to? The number of samples, the number of bins, or more generally the number of cells the estimator has to fill, and the number of variables. Every variable you add multiplies the number of cells. This is why conditional mutual information, transfer entropy, and higher-order measures are so hungry for data.

Here is the classic trap. Two conditions, two different numbers of trials. Condition A has 50 trials, condition B has 500. You compute mutual information in each, and it comes out higher. It looks like a finding. But part or all of the difference can be pure bias, because the smaller sample is biased upward more.

Think of a typical experiment. 40 trials per condition, one EEG feature per trial, and a stimulus with a handful of categories. The number of samples is small, the grid is not, and the bias can easily be as large as the effect you are looking for.

The same trap hides in group comparisons, in sliding windows of different lengths, and in any analysis where the amount of data changes from one estimate to another.

So, three rules for now. Never read a raw mutual information value as if it were the true value. Compare estimates only when they are computed with the same amount of data and the same settings. And always compare with a null, the same estimator applied to data where the dependence has been destroyed. Chapter 6 is entirely about building that null correctly.

The rest of this chapter is a tour of better recipes. Corrections that subtract an estimate of the bias. Estimators that avoid bins altogether, like nearest neighbors. Estimators that trade generality for stability, like the Gaussian copula. And estimators built on neural networks. Each one reduces some errors and introduces others. None of them makes data appear where there is none.

In the notebook for this episode, you will run the experiment yourself. Independent noise, 300 repetitions, and a range of sample sizes. You will watch the pile of estimates move toward zero and measure how its center falls with the number of samples.

To recap, the true mutual information is a property of the distribution. The estimate is a property of your sample and your recipe. The plug-in recipe is biased upward and the bias shrinks roughly like one over the number of samples. Gold appears in empty rivers when the pan is small.

Binning

Binning decides the result of a plug-in estimate. On independent noise, going from eight to thirty-two bins per axis turns almost nothing into over a bit; the Treves and Panzeri formula gives the bias from the number of cells and samples, while too few bins lose information. Bins should be fixed in advance, and estimates compared with a null.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the river, in the last episode, we saw that the plug-in estimator finds gold where there is none. Today we look at the sieve itself, the bins. How many, how wide, and why this one choice can decide your result.

To compute mutual information from continuous signals with the plug-in recipe, we first have to turn each signal into a small set of categories. That is binning. Each axis is cut into slices, the plane becomes a grid, and we count the samples that land in each cell.

There are two common ways to cut. Equal-width bins slice the range of values into intervals of the same size. Simple, but the tails of the distribution get bins that are almost empty, while the center is crowded.

Equal-count bins, also called quantile bins, are cut so that each bin receives the same number of samples. Every marginal then becomes flat, each bin holds the same share of the data. And because only the ranks of the values matter, the result does not change if you stretch or squash a signal with any increasing transformation. That is the invariance of episode 4.3, and it makes equal-count bins a good default for continuous signals.

Now the key question, how many bins?

Let's go back to the case where we know the truth, two independent noise signals, true mutual information exactly zero. 500 samples each, equal-count bins.

With 8 bins per axis, the plug-in estimate averages 0.07 bits. With 16 bins, 0.38 bits. With 32 bins, 1.43 bits.

Read that again. Same data, same independent noise. Just by doubling the number of bins twice, we went from almost nothing to well over a bit of information. The sieve is manufacturing gold.

There is a formula for this, worked out by Alessandro Treves and Stefano Panzeri in 1995. To first order, the bias of the plug-in mutual information is the number of bins on the first axis minus one times the number of bins on the second axis minus one divided by two times the number of samples times the natural log of two. That gives bits.

Let's check it. 8 bins and 500 samples. 7 times 7, 49, divided by about 693. 0.07 bits, exactly what the simulation gave. 32 bins, 31 squared, 961, divided by the same number. 1.39 bits, close to the 1.43 we measured.

The formula tells the whole story in one line. The bias grows with the number of cells in the grid and shrinks with the number of samples. What matters is not the number of samples alone, but the number of samples per cell. With 32 bins per axis, there are 1024 cells for 500 samples. Most cells hold zero or one sample, and the estimate is meaningless.

The formula is an approximation. It assumes every cell could be occupied, and it holds when there are enough samples per cell. With equal-width bins, the nearly empty tail cells break that assumption, and the real bias comes out different from the formula. Treat it as a guide, not as an exact correction.

So why not just use very few bins? Because coarse bins have the opposite problem. They throw information away.

Take two Gaussian signals with a correlation of 0.9. Their true mutual information is 1.2 bits. Cut each one into just two bins, above or below the median. Now each signal is a single yes or no. Even with an unlimited amount of data, the mutual information of these two binary signals is only 0.41 bits. A third of the truth. The fine structure that carried the rest of the information has been melted down.

With 500 samples, here is the whole picture for this correlated case. 2 bins, 0.41 bits, far too low. 8 bins, about 1.06 bits, a little low. 32 bins, almost 2 bits, far too high.

This is the bin trade-off. Too few bins, you lose information and the estimate is biased downward, no matter how much data you have. Too many bins, the cells starve, and the estimate is biased upward. Somewhere in between, the two errors partly cancel, but the location of that sweet spot depends on the data, on the sample size, and on the strength of the dependence. There is no universal number.

There is a third family, adaptive partitions. Instead of fixing a grid in advance, the algorithm keeps splitting the plane where the data are dense and stops splitting where a cell looks statistically uniform. Fraser and Swinney introduced this idea in 1986, and the values in the table of episode 4.2 came from an adaptive algorithm of the same spirit by Cellucci, Albano, and Rapp. Adaptive partitions reduce the problem, but they do not remove it. The stopping rule is still a choice, and it still interacts with the sample size.

What about the familiar rules like the square root of the number of samples, or the rules of Sturges and of Freedman and Diaconis? They were designed to draw a nice histogram of one variable. They were not designed to estimate mutual information, and they give no guarantee about its bias.

There is one case where binning is natural, data that are already discrete. A choice between two buttons. A neuron that fires or stays silent in a time bin. A stimulus drawn from four categories. Then the categories are given by the experiment, and the remaining question is only whether you have enough samples per cell.

Let's bring this back to an EEG recording. Two electrodes, two seconds of signal, sampled at 250 Hz. That is 500 samples, exactly our example. With 16 bins per axis, you are already in the zone where the bias is several tenths of a bit. And it is worse than it looks. Neighboring samples of an EEG are strongly correlated, so the effective number of independent samples is much smaller than 500. We come back to this in Chapter 6.

Now add a third variable, as conditional mutual information and transfer entropy require. 8 bins per axis was comfortable in 2 dimensions, 64 cells. In 3 dimensions, it is 512 cells. With 500 samples, about 1 sample per cell. The same grid that worked for a pair becomes useless for a triplet. Many practitioners therefore use very few bins, sometimes just two or three, for conditional measures, and accept the loss of information as the price of a stable estimate.

One practical detail about equal count bins. They assume that the values can be sorted without ties. Real signals are often quantized by the amplifier or contain many identical values, for example a reaction time rounded to the millisecond. Then some bins cannot be filled exactly, and the marginals are no longer flat. A common fix is to break ties by adding a tiny amount of noise, or by ranking with a random tie-breaker. Whatever you choose, do it the same way everywhere.

What is a binned estimate sensitive to? The number of bins on each axis. The way bins are placed, equal width or equal count. The number of samples. And in higher dimensions the number of variables because every variable multiplies the number of cells.

Here is the classic trap. Try a few bin counts, keep the one that gives the nicest result. With bias this strong, you can produce almost any number you like. The defense is simple, fix the binning before you look at the results, use the same binning for every condition and every participant, report it, and compare against a null computed with exactly the same bins.

A second trap is quieter, binning each condition separately. If condition A and condition B get their own bin edges, the two estimates are not computed with the same sieve, and their difference may come from the edges rather than from the brain.

In the notebook for this episode, you will reproduce these numbers. Independent noise and a correlated pair, equal width and equal count bins from 2 to 32 bins per axis. You will compare the simulated bias with the formula of Treves and Panzeri and see where it holds and where it breaks.

To recap, binning turns signals into categories and the choice of bins is part of the measurement. Few bins lose information. Many bins create information. The first-order bias grows with the number of cells and shrinks with the number of samples. Choose the bins in advance, keep them fixed, and always compare to a null.

Miller–Madow

The Miller–Madow correction adds back the first-order entropy bias, the number of occupied categories minus one over twice the sample size. It works when samples are plentiful compared with bins but undercorrects with scarce data: with 32 by 32 bins on 500 samples, most phantom information survives. It reduces bias, not variance, so labelling a result bias-corrected does not make it correct.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the river. In the last two episodes the gold pans found gold in an empty river. The plug-in estimator invented information, and finer bins invented more. Today the oldest and simplest repair. It is a single line of arithmetic, it is in almost every toolbox, and it is called the Miller–Madow correction.

Start with intuition. Remember why the plug-in estimate of entropy is wrong. It replaces each probability by the frequency observed in the sample. With a finite sample, rare categories are often missed entirely, and the frequencies look more uneven than the true probabilities. A distribution that looks more uneven looks more predictable. So the plug-in entropy is too low on average. It is biased downwards. And because mutual information is a sum and difference of entropies, those downward errors combine into the upward bias we met in episode 5.1.

How large is the error? In 1955, the psychologist George Miller worked it out to first order. The plug-in entropy in nats falls short of the true entropy by about the number of categories minus 1, divided by twice the number of samples. M minus 1 over 2N. The fix follows immediately, add that amount back. That is the whole correction. Count the categories, subtract 1, divide by twice the sample size and add.

There is one practical twist. You do not know how many categories the true distribution has. So the usual recipe counts the categories that actually appear in the sample, the non-empty bins, and uses that number, m hat. The correction is m hat minus 1 over 2n in nats. Divide by the natural log of 2 to get bits.

Here is the calculation in one picture. A simulated source with 20 categories, uneven, the first one common, and the last ones rare. Its true entropy is about 3.65 bits. Now draw samples of different sizes, 300 times each, and average the estimates. With 50 samples, the plug-in estimate averages about 3.32 bits, a third of a bit too low. The Miller–Madow estimate averages about 3.52. With 100 samples, the plug-in gives about 3.5, and the corrected estimate about 3.62, already close to the truth. From 500 samples on, all 20 categories appear in almost every sample, and the corrected curve sits on the true value.

So the corrected curve climbs back towards the truth, much faster than the plug-in curve. But notice what happens at the smallest sample sizes. With 10 samples, the corrected estimate averages about 2.85 bits, still 8 tenths of a bit too low. With only 10 draws, about 6 of the 20 categories appear on average. The correction counts 6, not 20, and adds far too little.

That is the central limitation. Miller–Madow corrects the first-order term of an expansion that assumes every category is well sampled. It works when the sample is large compared with the number of categories. When the data are severely insufficient, it does not fail loudly. It simply undercorrects.

Here is a starker example. A source with 1,000 equally likely categories. A true entropy of about 9.97 bits. With 100 samples, the plug-in estimate gives about 6.54 bits. Miller–Madow lifts it to about 7.22. Better but almost 3 bits short. Even with 1,000 samples, as many samples as categories, the corrected estimate is still about 4 tenths of a bit too low. No first-order correction can recover categories that were never seen.

Now the same correction for mutual information. Apply it to each of the three entropies of X, of Y, and of the joint, and combine them the way mutual information combines entropies. When every cell of the table is occupied, the three corrections add up to the number of rows minus 1 times the number of columns minus 1 over 2n. That is exactly the Treves–Panzeri bias from episode 5.2, now subtracted instead of suffered.

On 500 samples of independent noise, binned into 8 by 8 equal-count bins, the plug-in mutual information averages about 0.073 bits of phantom information. After the correction, only a few thousandths of a bit. Close to zero, as it should be, with a spread of about 0.015 bits from one sample to the next.

But go back to the dangerous case of episode 5.2, 32 by 32 bins on the same 500 samples. The plug-in estimate invents about 1.43 bits. After the correction, a little over 9 tenths of a bit remain. Most of the phantom information survives. Why? Because only about 400 of the 1,024 joint cells contain any sample at all. The correction counts the occupied cells, and with that many empty cells, the first-order expansion no longer describes the bias.

What is the correction sensitive to? First, to the ratio of samples to categories. A useful rough rule, the correction helps when the number of samples is several times the number of occupied cells, and it cannot be trusted when the two are comparable. Second, to how m hat is counted. Count all possible bins instead of the occupied ones, and you get a different correction. Both choices appear in software, so read the documentation. Third to dependence between samples. The formula assumes independent draws. For autocorrelated signals, the effective sample size from episode 1.7 is smaller than n, and the true bias is larger than the correction assumes. And note what the correction does not do. It reduces bias, but it does not reduce variance. At small n, the corrected estimates scatter just as widely as the plug-in ones, sometimes a little more.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports mutual information between an EEG feature and a stimulus, finely binned, on a few hundred trials, with the words bias-corrected in the methods. The reader relaxes. But with 16 bins on each side and 300 trials, there are 256 joint cells for 300 samples. The correction was applied in exactly the regime where it does not work. Bias-corrected says which formula was used, not that the bias is gone. The honest check is the one from episode 5.1. Run the same pipeline on shuffled data or on simulated data with a known answer and look at what comes out.

The companion notebook implements the plug-in and Miller–Madow estimators in a few lines, reproduces the 20-category curves and the 1,000-category failure, applies the correction to mutual information on independent noise with 8 and 32 bins, and checks the result against the Treves–Panzeri formula.

To recap, the plug-in entropy is biased downwards by about m-1 over 2 n nats to first order. The Miller–Madow correction adds that amount back using the number of occupied bins. It works well when samples are plentiful compared with bins. It undercorrects when data are scarce because unseen categories cannot be counted. For mutual information, it subtracts the Treves–Panzeri bias, and it fails in the same regime of many empty cells. And it fixes bias, not variance.

PLATE 5.410:00

Beyond Miller–Madow · Shuffle subtraction, extrapolation, NSB

Four corrections to the plug-in mutual information estimate are raced on a simulated channel whose true value is known: Panzeri–Treves, shuffle subtraction, quadratic extrapolation and NSB. Shuffle subtraction is exact on independent data but overcorrects when real information exists, while NSB stays closest with scarce data. No method is unbiased everywhere, so the full curve of the estimate against sample size should be reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the river, where the prospectors have learned a hard lesson, every pan finds a little gold in an empty river, and the Miller–Madow correction from the last episode only takes back part of it when the data are scarce. Today we look at four methods that try to do better. Each one has its own idea, its own strength, and its own blind spot. At the end, we race them against each other on a simulation where we know the true answer.

Here is the test bench used for every method, a simulated experiment with four stimuli shown equally often. For each stimulus, the response falls into one of 16 bins following a bell-shaped profile whose centre shifts with the stimulus. Because we built the channel ourselves, we can compute the true mutual information exactly, about 0.78 bits. Then we simulate experiments of different sizes, from 32 trials to 2,048, 200 times each, and average what each method reports.

First, the baseline. The plug-in estimate with 32 trials averages about 1.32 bits, more than half a bit of invented information, with 256 trials, about 0.87. It only reaches the truth when trials are counted in thousands. Miller–Madow helps a little. 1.22 bits at 32 trials, 0.81 at 256.

Method 1, the Panzeri-Trieves correction. In 1996, Stefano Panzeri and Alessandro Treves wrote down the first-order bias of mutual information for exactly this kind of stimulus and response table. The bias is the sum, over stimuli, of the number of response bins used by each stimulus minus 1, minus the number of bins used overall minus 1, all divided by twice the number of trials, times the natural log of 2 to get bits. If you count bins simply as the bins that happen to be occupied, this correction turns out to be identical to Miller–Madow applied to each entropy. Their real contribution is a smarter count, a Bayesian procedure that estimates how many response bins are genuinely relevant for each stimulus, including bins that should be occupied but were missed. In our notebook we only implement the simple count, so on the race chart it shares the Miller–Madow curve.

Method 2. Shuffle subtraction. The idea is direct. Shuffle the stimulus labels across trials, which destroys any real relationship between stimulus and response, and compute the plug-in mutual information again. Repeat many times. The average is what the estimator invents from nothing at this sample size with these marginal distributions. Subtract it from the real estimate.

On independent data, this works beautifully. In our simulation without any dependence with 64 trials, the plug-in estimate invents about 0.6 bits after shuffle subtraction, essentially zero. But look what happens when there is real information. With 32 trials, shuffle subtraction reports about 0.33 bits, less than half the true 0.78. With 128 trials, about 0.66. It now errs in the other direction.

Why? The bias of the plug-in estimator depends on how many cells of the table are occupied. When stimulus and response are really related, each stimulus uses only part of the response range, fewer cells are filled, and the bias is smaller. The shuffled data spread every stimulus over the whole response range, fill more cells, and have a larger bias. So the amount subtracted is too large. Shuffle subtraction is exact under the null hypothesis and conservative everywhere else. That makes it a fine tool for testing whether there is any information at all, and a poor tool for measuring how much.

Panzeri's group has proposed a different shuffling procedure, which shuffles responses within each stimulus to target one particular piece of the bias, and recommends combining it with another correction. Do not confuse the two, the word shuffle covers several distinct methods.

Method 3. Quadratic extrapolation. The plug-in bias shrinks as 1 over N plus smaller terms. So measure the bias's dependence on N directly. Compute the plug-in estimate on all your data. Then split the data in two halves, compute it on each and average. Then in four quarters, you now have three points, the estimate at N, at N over 2 and at N over 4. Fit a curve of the form true value plus over N plus b over N squared. And read off the value at infinite N. This idea was popularised in neuroscience by Steven Strong, William Bialek, and colleagues in 1998 for the entropy of spike trains.

On our bench, it does well from about 128 trials, 0.785 bits, essentially the truth. At 32 trials, it averages about 1.03, better than Miller–Madow, but still a quarter of a bit too high. And its spread is the largest of all methods, about a quarter of a bit at 32 trials, because estimates on quarters of an already small dataset are very noisy, and extrapolation amplifies the noise.

Method 4, NSB, named after Ilya Nemenman, Fariel Shafee, and William Bialek, who published it in 2002. It is Bayesian. Instead of plugging in frequencies, it asks, Given these counts, what entropies are plausible? A Bayesian estimate needs a prior, and the obvious priors on the probabilities the Dirichlet family turn out to be strongly informative about entropy. Each one quietly decides the answer before the data arrive. NSB's trick is to average over a whole family of Dirichlet priors, weighted so that the resulting prior on the entropy itself is nearly flat. The data then decide.

On our bench, NSB is by far the closest at 32 trials, about 0.75 bits. At 64 trials, about 0.71, a little low, roughly as far from the truth as quadratic extrapolation is above it. From 256 trials on, it is within a hundredth of a bit. But two warnings. NSB needs to know the alphabet size, here 16 response bins. And on independent data, it is not perfect either. With 64 trials, it still reports about 0.2 bits where the truth is zero. No method is unbiased everywhere.

Here is the race in one picture. Estimated information against the number of trials on a logarithmic axis with the true value as a dashed line. The plug-in curve comes down from above. Miller–Madow follows it a little lower. Quadratic extrapolation dives faster with wide error bars. Shuffle subtraction climbs from below. NSB stays close to the truth from the start. And by 2,000 trials, every curve has converged on 0.78. With enough data, the choice of correction barely matters. With scarce data, it can matter more than the effect you are trying to measure.

What are these methods sensitive to? Shuffle subtraction depends on the amount of real dependence. The more information, the more it overcorrects. Quadratic extrapolation depends on N being large enough that the three subsamples are all in the regime where the 1 over N expansion holds. Below that, it extrapolates noise. Panzeri–Treves depends on its estimate of relevant bins. NSB depends on the alphabet size you give it, and on the assumption that the prior family is suitable. For distributions with very long tails, it can be wrong with confidence. And none of them repairs dependence between trials. Autocorrelated samples carry less information about the distribution than their number suggests, and every correction here assumes independent draws.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study tries three corrections, gets 0.4, 0.6, and 0.9 bits, and reports the one that best fits the hypothesis. Each number came from a legitimate method. The choice did not. Pick the correction before seeing the data, or report all of them. Better still, report the full curve of the estimate against N, obtained by subsampling your own data. If the curve has not flattened at your sample size, no correction can tell you the answer with confidence, and the honest report says so.

The companion notebook implements all five estimators, including NSB from the formulas of the original paper, reproduces the race on the simulated channel, and shows the behaviour of each method on independent data.

To recap, the Panzeri–Treves correction is the first-order bias for stimulus and response tables. With a simple count of bins, it equals Miller–Madow. Shuffle subtraction is exact on independent data and overcorrects when there is real information. Useful for testing, not for measuring. Quadratic extrapolation fits the estimate as a function of 1 over N and reads the limit. It is noisy when data are scarce. NSB averages over priors to stay neutral about entropy. It was the best here at the smallest N, but it needs the alphabet size and it is not unbiased on independent data. With enough data, all agree. With scarce data, report the curve against N.

Kernel density estimators

A kernel density estimator puts a smooth bump on every sample instead of counting samples in bins, so edges disappear but the bandwidth becomes the free parameter. A narrow bandwidth invents information even on independent data, and a wide one smooths dependence away, especially curved dependence. Conditions can only be compared with the same reported bandwidth.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the river, so far every estimator dropped the samples into boxes and counted them. Boxes have edges and the edges cause trouble. Where they fall how many there are, how many stay empty. Today we put the boxes away. Instead of counting samples in bins, we place a small, smooth bump on top of every sample and add the bumps. These are kernel density estimators and they bring their own free parameter.

Start with intuition. You met kernels in episode 1.6 as piping bags of cream. Each sample gets the same small bump. A kernel, usually a Gaussian, centred on the sample with an area of exactly 1. Add up all the bumps and divide by the number of samples, and you have a smooth estimate of the density. Where samples crowd together, the bumps pile up high. Where samples are sparse, the estimate is low. No edges, no empty bins.

The width of each bump is called the bandwidth. It decides everything. A narrow bandwidth gives a spiky estimate, with a separate peak on every sample. A wide bandwidth gives a smooth estimate that blurs all the detail away.

Now turn this into mutual information. Build three density estimates from the same samples, one for x alone, one for y alone, and one for the pair, using two-dimensional bumps. Then, at each sample, compute the log of the joint density divided by the product of the two marginal densities, and average over all samples. That average is the kernel estimate of mutual information. It was proposed for time series analysis by Young-Il Moon, Balaji Rajagopalan, and Upmanu Lall in 1995. It is simply the formula of mutual information, the average of the log ratio with kernel estimates plugged in.

Here is the calculation in one picture. 500 samples of a simulated Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.6. The true mutual information is minus one-half log base 2 of 1 minus 0.36, about 0.32 bits. Each signal is standardised, and we use the same bandwidth for the marginal and joint estimates. We start from a standard rule of thumb, Scott's rule for two dimensions, the number of samples to the power minus one-sixth, and multiply it by a factor that we slide from one-tenth to five. Each setting is repeated 60 times.

At Scott's bandwidth, the estimate averages about 0.34 bits, close to the truth. Now shrink the bandwidth. At half of Scott's value, about 0.51 bits. At three-tenths, about 0.77. At one-tenth more than two bits. Every sample sits on its own spike, the joint density at each sample is dominated by its own bump, and the estimator reports an enormous dependence that is not there.

Look at independent data, where the truth is zero. At Scott's bandwidth, the estimate averages about 0.06 bits, a small phantom. At one-tenth of Scott's bandwidth, about 2.19 bits of pure invention. Narrow kernels do what fine bins did in episode 5.2, they manufacture information.

Now widen the bandwidth instead. At twice Scott's value, the Gaussian pair gives about 0.22 bits. At three times, about 0.13. At five times, about 0.04. The bumps become so wide that the joint density looks like the product of the marginals, and the dependence is smoothed away. Over-smoothing means under-estimating the information.

The damage is worse when the dependence is curved. Take the parabola of episode 4.2, a signal and its square, plus noise. At Scott's bandwidth, the kernel estimate gives about 0.86 bits. At three times Scott's bandwidth, about 0.13. Wide bumps flatten the curve, and most of the information disappears.

So the bandwidth has replaced the number of bins as the free parameter. The estimator has no edges, but it has a dial, and the answer moves wherever the dial goes.

What is it sensitive to? First and above all, the bandwidth as we have seen. Rules of thumb like Scott's or Silverman's are designed for estimating a single Gaussian-like density well. They are not designed to give the best estimate of mutual information, and they can over-smooth narrow or curved structure. Cross-validation can choose a bandwidth from the data, but it optimises the density, not the information.

Second, a technical detail that matters, each sample's own bump. In the simple version, the density at a sample includes the bump centred on that very sample. With narrow kernels, that self-contribution dominates and inflates the estimate. Leaving it out, the so-called leave-one-out version removes that inflation, but at narrow bandwidths it overshoots the other way. In our simulation it gives clearly negative values minus one bit and below. Neither version is safe when the bandwidth is too small.

Third, dimension. The bumps must fill a space with one dimension per variable. In two dimensions, 500 samples cover the plane reasonably. In six dimensions, as needed for conditional measures with histories they do not, and the estimate degrades quickly.

Fourth, cost. Every sample is compared with every other sample. The computation grows with the square of the number of samples, which becomes slow for long recordings.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports that kernel-estimated mutual information between two brain signals is larger in one condition than in another. The bandwidth was set by a rule of thumb separately for each condition. But the rule of thumb depends on the spread and the number of samples in each condition, so the two conditions were estimated with different dials. Part of the difference may come from the bandwidths, not from the brain. Use the same bandwidth when comparing conditions, report it, and show how the result changes when it is varied.

To recap, a kernel density estimator places a smooth bump on each sample and adds them up. Plugged into the formula of mutual information, it gives an estimator without bins. Too wide and it smooths the dependence away, especially when it is curved. Rules of thumb are a starting point, not an answer. Fix the bandwidth across conditions, report it and vary it.

Nearest neighbours I · Kozachenko–Leonenko

The nearest neighbour entropy estimator lets the data set the scale: the distance to the k-th neighbour acts as a local thermometer of density. It is nearly unbiased in low dimensions, but its bias grows quickly with dimension, and it fails on repeated values and on time series. Summing three such entropies gives a poor mutual information estimate, because their biases do not cancel.

Back at the river with a new kind of prospector. The last estimators asked us to choose a scale before looking at the data, a bin width or a kernel bandwidth. The answer moved with that choice. Today's estimator lets the data choose the scale, point by point. Where samples are crowded, it looks closely. Where they are sparse, it looks far. It is the nearest neighbour estimator of entropy, published by Kozachenko and Leonenko in 1987, and it is the foundation of the most widely used mutual information estimator in neuroscience.

Start with intuition. Stand on one sample and look for its closest neighbour. If the neighbour is very close, you are standing in a crowded region. The density there is high. If the nearest neighbour is far away, you are in an empty region. The density is low. So the distance to the nearest neighbour is a local thermometer of density. Small distance, high density. Large distance, low density. And entropy is an average of surprise, minus the log of the density averaged over samples. High density means low surprise. Low density means high surprise. So if each sample can tell us its local density from the distance to its neighbour, we can average the surprise directly, without ever building a histogram.

Here is the reasoning made precise. Around a sample, draw a ball whose radius is the distance to its k-th nearest neighbour. That ball contains k other samples out of N. So the probability mass inside the ball is roughly k over N. Divide by the volume of the ball and you have a local estimate of the density. Take minus the log and average over all samples.

The result is the Kozachenko–Leonenko estimator. In words, the entropy in nats equals the digamma function of N minus the digamma function of k plus the log of the volume of a unit ball in d dimensions plus d times the average of the log of the distances to the kth neighbour. The digamma function is a close cousin of the logarithm. Using it instead of the plain log of N over k removes a bias that the simple reasoning would leave behind. The original paper used the single nearest neighbour. The version with the k-th neighbour is the one used today.

Notice the last term, d times the average log distance. It is the only term that depends on the data. Spread the data out and the distances grow and the entropy grows. Squeeze them together and it shrinks. And because this is a differential entropy, it carries every trap of episode 2.7. It depends on units and it can be negative.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Draw a cloud of simulated samples from a standard Gaussian in one dimension. Its true differential entropy is one half log of 2 pi e, about 1.42 nats. With 100 samples and k equal to 1, the estimator averages about 1.41 nats over 300 repetitions. With 1000 samples, 1.41 again. Almost no bias with no bins and no bandwidth.

The spread is another matter. With 100 samples and k equal to 1, the estimates scatter with a standard deviation of about 0.16 nats. With k equal to 4, about 0.10. A larger k averages over more neighbours, and the estimate becomes steadier at the price of looking at a coarser scale. That is the bias-variance trade-off again, now controlled by k. A common choice is a small k between 2 and 4 with no strong rule.

A useful check, samples drawn uniformly between 0 and 1 have a differential entropy of exactly 0. With 1000 samples, the estimator gives about 0.003 nats. Good.

Now the price. Remember the plan for this chapter. No more bins to choose but a bias that grows quickly with dimension. Let's see it. Take a standard Gaussian in d dimensions, 1000 samples, k equal to 4, and compare the estimate with the true entropy, which is d over 2 times log of 2 pi e.

In one dimension, the error is less than a hundredth of a nat. In two dimensions, about minus two hundredths. In five dimensions, about minus eight hundredths. In ten dimensions, the error has changed sign and reached about plus 0.14 nats. In 20 dimensions, about plus 1.3 nats.

Why? The estimator assumes that the density is roughly constant inside each ball. In one dimension, the k-th neighbour is close, and the assumption holds. In many dimensions, a thousand samples are scattered thinly over an enormous space. The k-th neighbour is far away, the ball is huge, and the density changes a lot within it. One thousand samples in 20 dimensions are, in a sense, very few. This is the curse of dimensionality, and it matters because the measures in the rest of this series are built from entropies of joint spaces. A transfer entropy with a few samples of history for each of two signals already lives in six or eight dimensions.

There is a second lesson hiding here, and it leads straight to the next episode. Suppose we estimate mutual information as the sum of two Kozachenko–Leonenko entropies of X and of Y, minus the entropy of the pair. Each entropy has its own bias, and the joint entropy in two dimensions has a different bias from the two marginals in one dimension. Nothing makes these biases cancel. On a simulated Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.6, 500 samples and k equal to 4, the true mutual information is about 0.223 nats. The sum of three estimates gives about 0.24, with a spread of about 6 hundredths. On independent data, about 0.02. Not a disaster in two dimensions. But the mismatch grows with dimension, because each term looks at the data at a different scale. In 2004, Kraskov, Stögbauer, and Grassberger found a way to force the same scale on all three terms. That is episode 5.7.

What is this estimator sensitive to? First, to dimension, as we have just seen, the bias grows quickly and its sign can even change. Second, to k, which trades bias for variance. Third, to the metric. Distances can be measured with the ordinary Euclidean norm or with the maximum norm, and the unit ball volume in the formula must match the norm used. Fourth, to units and scaling. Like every differential entropy, the estimate shifts by d times the log of any rescaling factor. If the dimensions of X have different units, standardise them first. Otherwise, the neighbour search is dominated by whichever variable has the largest numbers.

Fifth, to repeated values. If your signal is rounded, say to the nearest tenth of a microvolt, many samples are identical. Their distance to the nearest neighbour is exactly zero, and the log of zero is minus infinity. In our simulation, a Gaussian rounded to one decimal place makes the estimator with k equal to one return minus infinity. The usual fix is to add a little noise before searching for neighbours. For an entropy, noise spread uniformly over one rounding step, which in our simulation brings the estimate back to about 1.4 nats, close to the truth. A tiny jitter far below the resolution would only measure the jitter itself, so the size of the added noise must be reported.

And sixth, to time. In a time series, a sample's nearest neighbours are often simply the samples just before and after it in time, which are near copies, as episode 1.7 showed. The estimator then measures how smooth the signal is, rather than the shape of its distribution. Chapter six introduces the Theiler window, which excludes temporal neighbours from the search.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study compares the entropy of EEG segments between two groups using a nearest neighbour estimator on 10-dimensional delay vectors with a different recording length in each group. Both the dimension and the sample size change the bias. A group difference of a tenth of a nat could be entirely produced by the estimator. When comparing entropies, keep the dimension, the sample size, the scaling, and k the same across conditions, and check the bias with simulated data of the same dimension and length.

To recap, the distance to the k-th nearest neighbour gives a local estimate of density, and averaging minus its log gives the entropy, the Kozachenko–Leonenko estimator. No bins, no bandwidth, the scale adapts to the data. It is nearly unbiased in low dimensions, and k trades bias against variance. But the bias grows quickly with dimension, it inherits every trap of differential entropy, it breaks on repeated values, and in time series it needs protection from temporal neighbours. And three separate entropy estimates do not make a good mutual information estimate, because their biases do not cancel.

Nearest neighbours II · KSG

The KSG estimator fixes the scale once, as the distance to the k-th neighbour in the joint space, and reuses it in each marginal space, so the biases of the three entropy terms largely cancel. On Gaussian pairs with known answers it is nearly unbiased, and the remaining problem is variance. It fails with unstandardised variables, very strong dependence, autocorrelation and ties.

Part 1

Back at the river for the most famous tool on the bank. If you have read a paper that estimates mutual information between continuous brain signals, there is a good chance it used this estimator directly or inside a toolbox. It was published in 2004 by Alexander Kraskov, Harald Stögbauer, and Peter Grassberger, and everyone calls it KSG. This is part one of two, how it works. Part two puts it to the test.

Start with the problem it solves. Last episode, we estimated mutual information as three separate Kozachenko–Leonenko entropies, X plus Y minus the pair. Each term looked at the data at its own scale. The joint entropy in two dimensions measured distances to neighbours in the plane. The marginal entropies in one dimension measured distances along each axis. Those distances are different, so the biases of the three terms are different, and nothing makes them cancel.

The KSG idea fits in one sentence. Choose the scale once in the joint space and use that same scale in the marginals. If all three terms look at the data through the same window, their errors are of the same kind, and they largely cancel in the sum.

Here is the recipe for one sample. First stand on the sample in the joint space, the plane of X and Y. Find its k-th nearest neighbour. Distances here are measured with the maximum norm, the larger of the distance along X and the distance along Y. So the neighbourhood is not a circle but a square centred on the sample. Call half the side of that square epsilon. The square contains exactly k other samples.

Second, project that same epsilon onto each axis. Along the X-axis, draw a strip of half-width epsilon around the sample's X value and count how many other samples fall inside it. Call that count n x, do the same along the Y-axis, and call the count n y.

Third, think about what those counts mean. If X and Y were independent, knowing a sample is close to you in x would say nothing about whether it is close in y. Many samples would fall in each strip, but few would fall in both strips at once. If X and Y are strongly dependent, samples close in x tend to be close in y too. The strips contain fewer samples relative to the square. So small marginal counts for a fixed number of joint neighbours signal strong dependence.

Here is the formula, the first KSG algorithm. The mutual information in nats equals the digamma of k plus the digamma of N minus the average over all samples of the digamma of n x plus 1 plus the digamma of n y plus 1. No bins. No bandwidth. 1 integer k and 2 counts per sample.

Let's read it with numbers. Take a simulated Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.6, 60 samples, and k equal to 3. Pick one sample. Its third neighbour in the plane sets a square of half-width epsilon. Along X, the strip of the same half-width contains 11 other samples. Along Y, 12. This sample contributes the digamma of 12 plus the digamma of 13. Do this for every sample, average and subtract from the digamma of 3 plus the digamma of 60. The smaller the counts in the strips, the larger the information.

Why does this cancel the biases? Kraskov and colleagues started from the Kozachenko–Leonenko formula, which is valid for any choice of k. In the marginal spaces, instead of fixing k, they fixed the distance, epsilon, inherited from the joint space, and let the number of neighbours n x and n y be whatever it is. With that choice, the terms that carry the local density errors are evaluated at the same scale in all three spaces. Most of the error cancels. The authors showed numerically that the estimator vanishes up to statistical fluctuations when the variables are independent, and that its remaining systematic errors are small for many distributions.

There is a second KSG algorithm. Instead of a square, it uses the rectangle spanned by the k nearest neighbours, its own half-width along X and a different one along Y. The counts change accordingly, and the formula gains a small correction of minus 1 over k. The authors found that the two versions perform very similarly. The first is the most common in practice.

Three details before the test. First, the answer is in nats, because the digamma function behaves like the natural logarithm. Divide by the natural log of 2 for bits. Second, X and Y can each be vectors, several channels or a signal in its past. The maximum norm handles any dimension. Third, the counts use strict inequalities and ties change them. If several samples sit at exactly the same distance, the recipe is ambiguous, which is why implementations add a tiny amount of noise to the data before counting.

To recap part 1, KSG fixes the scale once, as the distance to the k-th neighbour in the joint space with the maximum norm. It reuses that distance in each marginal space and counts the neighbours that fall inside. Small marginal counts means strong dependence. Using the same scale everywhere makes the errors of the three entropy terms largely cancel. The result is in nats.

In part two, does it actually work? We test it on Gaussians with known answers, turn the dial of k, and find the ways it can still fail.

KSG gives 0.84. The second algorithm gives almost the same numbers. The bias is a few thousandths of a nat in most cases, and about a hundredth at the strongest correlation. The spread is the honest limit. At zero correlation, the estimates scatter with a standard deviation of about 0.03 nats. So, on 500 samples, a single KSG value of 0.05 nats on its own says very little. Bias has been tamed, variance has not.

KSG averages 0.227, with a spread of about 0.047. Closer to the truth and steadier. In this two-dimensional example, the gain is modest. A value between 2 and 4 is the usual recommendation. Whatever you choose, report it. And the sample size at 0.6. With 100 samples, the spread is about 0.09 nats. With 500, about 0.04. With 2000, about 0.02. The average stays close to the truth throughout.

Now the ways it breaks.

First, scaling in the multivariate case. Suppose Y is a pair of channels recorded in different units. One in volts, the other in millivolts, so its numbers are a thousand times larger. The maximum norm takes the larger of the two distances, so the large number channel dominates every neighbourhood and the informative channel is ignored. In our simulation, the true mutual information is about 0.51 nats. With raw units, KSG reports 0.002. After standardising each channel to unit variance, 0.51. Standardise every dimension before estimating.

Second, very strong dependence. Push the correlation to 0.99999. The true mutual information is about 5.41 nats. With 500 samples, KSG reports about 4.58, nearly a whole nat too low. With 5,000 samples, about 5.37. Weihao Gao, Greg Ver Steeg, and Aram Galstyan showed in 2015 that nearest neighbour estimators of this kind need a number of samples that grows exponentially with the true mutual information. Large values are underestimated unless data are plentiful.

Third, autocorrelation. Take two independent signals, each smoothed so that neighbouring samples are nearly copies, 2,000 samples long. The truth is zero. KSG reports about 0.39 nats. The nearest neighbours of each sample in the joint space are simply its neighbours in time, which are close in X and close in Y at the same moment. The estimator mistakes smoothness for dependence. Chapter 6 introduces the Theiler window, which excludes temporal neighbours from the search and the surrogate tests that go with it.

Fourth, ties and discrete values. Rounded data or a variable that only takes a few values produce zero distances and ambiguous counts. Add a tiny noise below the resolution and report it. For genuinely discrete variables, use a discrete estimator instead.

Fifth, dimension. KSG handles vectors, and that is its great strength for conditional measures and transfer entropy. But every added dimension spreads the samples thinner, and the bias returns. Episode 5.8 measures that cost. Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports a KSG mutual information of 0.04 nats between two EEG channels on a few hundred samples of a filtered, oversampled signal with no surrogate test. Every warning applies at once. The value is inside the spread of the estimator on independent data. The signals are autocorrelated, so even the bias on independent data is positive. The two channels may differ in scale if they were not standardised. A small KSG value means nothing without a null distribution built from surrogates that preserve the autocorrelation. That is Chapter 6.

Small k is noisy, large k is biased, 2 to 4 is the usual choice. Back at the river, the KSG estimator gave us mutual information between two variables, with little bias. But many of the measures ahead in this series are not plain mutual informations. They are conditional ones. Conditional mutual information from episode 4.4, transfer entropy in chapter 8, which is a conditional mutual information between the past of one signal and the present of another, given the past of the second. Partial information decompositions built from conditional terms. So we need KSG with a condition. Today, how it works and what every added dimension costs.

Start with intuition. Conditional mutual information asks how much X tells you about Y once Z is known. Think of it as many small mutual informations, one inside each thin slice of Z, averaged. For a continuous Z, there are no slices, so the estimator must decide, for each sample, which other samples count as having nearly the same Z. Nearest neighbours do that naturally, the samples near you in Z are your slice.

The extension was published by Stefan Frenzel and Bernd Pompe in 2007, under the name partial mutual information. It follows the KSG recipe exactly. First, find each sample's k-th nearest neighbour in the full joint space of X, Y, and Z, with the maximum norm. That distance sets the scale epsilon. Second, reuse that same epsilon in three smaller spaces, the space of X and Z together, the space of Y and Z together, and the space of Z alone. Count the neighbours that fall within epsilon in each. Call them n X Z, n Y Z and n Z.

The formula, the conditional mutual information in nats equals the digamma of k minus the average over samples of three terms. The digamma of n X Z plus 1 plus the digamma of n Y Z plus 1 minus the digamma of n Z plus 1. As before, one scale for everything so that the errors of the different terms largely cancel.

Read it like this. Within the Z neighbourhood, if knowing X narrows down Y, then samples close in both X and Z are rarer than samples close in Z alone would suggest, and similarly for Y. Small counts in the X Z and Y Z spaces relative to the Z space mean that X and Y still share information after Z.

Here is the calculation in one picture. We return to the two electrodes of episode 4.4 driven by a common stimulus. Each electrode records the stimulus plus its own noise with a standard deviation of one half. 500 samples k equal to 3 100 repetitions. The plain KSG mutual information between the electrodes averages about 0.52 nats. The true value is 0.51. Now condition on the stimulus. The true conditional mutual information is exactly 0. The estimator averages about minus 0.01 nats, with a spread of about 0.03.

Minus. Yes. In 67 of the 100 repetitions, the estimate came out below zero. Mutual information can never be negative, but an estimate of it can be. Near zero, the estimator's errors fall on both sides of the truth, and a negative value is simply what that noise looks like. Do not set it to zero. Truncating every negative value at zero turns an unbiased cloud of estimates into a biased one, systematically above the truth. Report the values as they are and judge them against a null distribution. There is a useful side effect. The spread of these near-zero estimates, negative ones included, is itself a picture of the estimator's noise at this sample size. If the value you care about sits inside that cloud, you have not detected anything.

KSG with the Frenzel–Pompe recipe gives about 0.105 nats from Y to X, and about 0.003 from X to Y. The coupling is found in the right direction. Chapter 8 builds on exactly this.

Now the price. Every conditioning variable adds a dimension to the joint space. 500 samples that densely fill a plane are thinly spread in a space of 10 dimensions. Let's measure it. We build a case where the true conditional mutual information between X and Y is fixed at about 0.144 nats, while both X and Y depend on a conditioning vector Z whose dimension we increase. With one dimension of Z, KSG gives about 0.155. With two about 0.18. With four about 0.24. With eight about 0.3, more than twice the truth. Each added dimension pushes the estimate up because the neighbourhoods become large and the density changes within them.

Notice what drives this. When the extra dimensions of Z are pure noise unrelated to X and Y, the estimate barely moves, about 0.14 to 0.15 in our simulation. The bias grows when the conditioning variables really shape X and Y, which is exactly the case that matters, since we condition on things because we think they matter. In transfer entropy, the conditioning set is the past of the target, often several samples long and sometimes other signals too. A history of five samples for two signals already puts the estimator in 10 or more dimensions.

What is it sensitive to? First, to dimension, as we just saw. Choose history lengths and conditioning sets as small as the question allows. Small true values, which is what most conditional measures in the brain are, sit inside the noise of the estimator. A single estimate is almost meaningless without a surrogate distribution. The estimator computes faithfully whatever conditional quantity you ask for. It cannot tell you whether you asked the right one.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study estimates transfer entropy between 10 brain regions, conditioning each pair on the past of all 8 others, with a history of 4 samples each. Gaussian copula MI · GCMI

The Gaussian copula estimator replaces each signal by its ranks, maps them onto a Gaussian, and applies the closed-form Gaussian formula. It is fast, steady, invariant to monotonic transformations and works well with many dimensions. Its price is blindness to non-monotonic dependence such as a parabola, so it is a lower bound: when it finds information it is real, but finding none proves nothing.

Part 1

Back at the river, for a tool with a very different philosophy, every estimator so far has tried to capture any possible shape of dependence and paid for it in bias, variance, or computing time. Today's estimator makes one deliberate assumption, and in exchange becomes fast, steady, and easy to use with many variables. It is the Gaussian copula estimator of mutual information, GCMI, introduced to neuroimaging by Robin Ince and colleagues in 2017. It is the engine inside toolboxes such as frites, and you will meet it again and again.

Start with intuition and with an idea from episode 4.3. Mutual information does not change under any invertible transformation of each variable. You can stretch, squash, take logarithms or replace values by their ranks, and the information between two signals stays exactly the same. So we are free to transform each signal into whatever shape is most convenient before estimating anything.

What shape is most convenient? A Gaussian. For Gaussian variables mutual information has a closed form from the determinants of covariance matrices with no bins, no bandwidth, and no neighbours. In one dimension it is just minus one half log of one minus the squared correlation.

So here is the recipe in three steps. First, replace each signal by its ranks. The smallest value becomes one, the next two, and so on. Second, push those ranks through the inverse of the Gaussian cumulative distribution, so that each signal now has an exactly Gaussian histogram. Ince and colleagues call this copula normalisation. Third, compute the Gaussian formula on the transformed signals. That is all.

The name comes from the copula which we met in episode 4.3. The part of a joint distribution that remains once the individual distributions have been removed, the pure way the variables are tied together. GCMI keeps each signal's ranks which is all of the copula, but then estimates the dependence as if that copula were Gaussian.

Here is the calculation in one picture. 500 samples of a simulated Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.6, a true mutual information of about 0.32 bits. Repeated 200 times, GCMI averages 0.32, with a standard deviation of about 0.04 bits. KSG on the same data averages 0.33, with a standard deviation of about 0.06. With only 100 samples, GCMI scatters by about 0.08 bits and KSG by about 0.12. When the assumption holds, GCMI is the steadier of the two.

Now apply wild monotonic transformations to the same data, the exponential of the first signal, the cube of the second. The histograms become heavily skewed. GCMI gives exactly the same answer as before to every decimal place because the ranks did not change. Monotonic transformations are invisible to it by construction.

And the speed? On 20,000 samples, GCMI takes a few thousandths of a second on our machine. KSG with all its neighbour searches takes more than a second and a half, about 200 times longer. For a single number, that difference hardly matters. When you need to compute mutual information at every time point, every channel and every frequency of an EEG study, and then repeat it a thousand times for surrogates, that difference decides what is feasible.

Multivariate data are where GCMI shines most. Take one signal and 10 channels that each carry a little of it, plus their own noise. The true mutual information between the signal and the 10 channels together is about 0.46 bits. GCMI on 500 samples gives about 0.47. KSG in 11 dimensions gives about 0.26, barely more than half. Nearest neighbours drown in high dimensions, covariance matrices do not.

The same closed form extends to conditional mutual information. Conditioning on a Gaussian variable amounts to removing its linear influence through partial covariances, so a conditional estimate costs little more than a plain one. That is why Ince and colleagues used the same machinery for conditional measures and for directed information between MEG and EEG signals, and why it is a common choice for transfer entropy when many channels are involved. The assumption comes along, of course, the conditioning too is only as good as a Gaussian copula.

Part 2

Now the price. The Gaussian formula only sees what a Gaussian copula can express — dependence that is captured by correlations between the copula-normalised signals. Roughly monotonic relationships. Anything else is invisible.

Take the parabola from episode 4.2, a signal and its square plus noise. On 500 samples, KSG finds about 1.52 bits, consistent with a reference estimate of about 1.49 bits on 20,000 samples. GCMI finds 0.002. Essentially nothing. After ranking, the left half and the right half of the parabola cancel each other, the correlation between the transformed signals is near zero, and the Gaussian formula reports no information at all. A strong, obvious, deterministic relationship completely missed.

A subtler case, a monotonic relationship but with heavy-tailed noise, drawn from a student distribution. The reference estimate is about 0.43 bits. KSG on 500 samples gives about 0.44. GCMI gives about 0.27. It sees the dependence but not all of it.

This is not a bug. It follows from a property we met in episode 2.8. For a fixed covariance, the Gaussian has the largest possible entropy. After copula normalisation, each one-dimensional signal is exactly Gaussian, and that property implies that the Gaussian formula can only underestimate the true mutual information. For one-dimensional variables, GCMI is a lower bound on the true value, up to estimation error. For multivariate variables, the guarantee no longer holds exactly, because normalising each dimension separately does not make the vector jointly Gaussian. The authors describe it as an approximate lower bound.

So GCMI is honest in a specific sense. When it finds information, the information is there. When it finds none, there may still be plenty, in a form it cannot see. That asymmetry is useful in practice. For a statistical test, a lower bound is conservative. If even the lower bound exceeds what surrogates produce, the dependence is real. For an effect size, it is less comfortable because two conditions can differ in how much of their information GCMI can see, rather than in how much information they carry.

What is GCMI sensitive to? First, to the shape of the dependence as we have just seen. Monotonic relationships are captured well, non-monotonic ones poorly or not at all. Second, to ties. Ranks are ambiguous when values repeat, so discrete or heavily rounded variables need care. For a discrete variable such as a stimulus category, the framework provides a separate version, which models each class with its own Gaussian, rather than ranking the labels. Third, to bias, which is small but not zero. The Gaussian formula estimated on N samples is biased upward, especially with many dimensions, the authors provide an analytic correction and surrogate tests make it unnecessary. Fourth, it can return small negative values when the bias correction overshoots on data with no dependence. As with KSG, report them, do not set them to zero. And fifth, to autocorrelation like every estimator, the samples of a time series are not independent and the null distribution must respect that.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study compares the information that two brain regions carry about a continuous stimulus feature, using GCMI, and finds that region A carries information and region B carries none. But region B responds to the feature in a U-shaped way, strongly to low and high values, weakly to intermediate ones. GCMI cannot see a U-shape. The conclusion that region B carries no information is wrong. It carries information of a kind the estimator ignores. Before concluding absence, look at the scatterplot. If the relationship could be non-monotonic, check with an estimator that can see it, such as KSG, or include a transformed version of the feature, such as its distance from the middle.

To recap, GCMI ranks each signal, maps the ranks onto a Gaussian, and applies the closed-form Gaussian formula. It is invariant to monotonic transformations, fast, steady, and works well with many dimensions, including conditional measures. For one-dimensional variables, it is a lower bound on the true mutual information, for multivariate ones, an approximate lower bound. It captures monotonic dependence and misses non-monotonic dependence, such as the parabola, entirely. When GCMI finds information, believe it. When it finds none, look at the data before concluding there is none.

Ordinal and symbolic estimators

Ordinal estimators keep only the ranking of values in short windows and apply discrete tools to the resulting symbols. They are immune to monotonic transformations, units and gains, but blind to anything carried by amplitude, such as shared power fluctuations. Their alphabet grows factorially with pattern length, so longer patterns invent large amounts of information from independent noise unless data are plentiful.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the river, with an estimator that refuses to look at the size of anything. Not the amplitude, not the units, not the scale. Only the order. Is the next value higher or lower than this one? Which of these four values is the largest? This idea, ordinal patterns, was introduced by Christoph Bandt and Bernd Pompe in 2002, and it gives a family of estimators that are robust, simple, and fast, with two very particular blind spots.

Start with intuition. Take a short window of a signal, say three consecutive samples. Forget their values and keep only their ranking. Maybe the middle one is the highest and the first one the lowest. That ranking is the window's ordinal pattern. With three samples, there are only six possible rankings, three factorial. Slide the window along the signal, one sample at a time, and the signal becomes a sequence of symbols drawn from an alphabet of six.

Now every tool for discrete variables applies. The entropy of the pattern sequence is the permutation entropy, which chapter 10 examines as a complexity measure. And between two signals, the mutual information between their pattern sequences is an ordinal mutual information. Counting six symbols is easy, so the plug-in estimator from episode 5.1 becomes usable, with its bias under control as long as the alphabet stays small.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Two simulated signals. The first is a slowly correlated noise. The second is the first plus independent noise. 2,000 samples each, patterns of three, 100 repetitions. The ordinal mutual information between the two pattern sequences averages about 0.55 bits.

Now transform the signals: take the exponential of the first and the cube of the second. Every amplitude changes, every histogram is distorted. The ordinal mutual information is exactly the same, 0.55 bits to every decimal place. Any increasing transformation preserves every ranking, so the patterns do not change at all. Like the Gaussian copula of the last episode, ordinal estimators are immune to monotonic distortions, to changes of gain between electrodes and to the choice of units, and largely robust to slow drifts.

Now the first blind spot. Because ordinal patterns ignore amplitude, they ignore any information carried by amplitude. Let's build a case where two signals share nothing but their loudness. A slowly varying volume, the same for both, multiplies two independent noises. When the volume is high, both signals are large, when it is low, both are small. Their moment-to-moment ups and downs are independent. The mutual information between their absolute values, binned into eight levels, averages about 0.17 bits, against 0.02 for unrelated signals. The ordinal mutual information averages 0.009 bits, exactly what it gives for unrelated signals. The shared volume is invisible. In brain data, that is not an exotic case. Amplitude envelopes of oscillations, co-fluctuating power between regions are a classic form of coupling, and an ordinal estimator cannot see them.

The second blind spot is the alphabet. Patterns of three samples give six symbols. Patterns of four give 24. Five give 120. Six give 720. The number of possible patterns grows as m factorial, and the data needed to fill the table grow with it. For the mutual information between two pattern sequences, the joint table has m factorial squared cells.

Let's see the damage with two independent white noise signals of 1,000 samples, where the truth is exactly zero. With patterns of 3, the plug-in ordinal mutual information invents about 0.02 bits, with patterns of 4 about 0.45, with 5 about 3.75 bits, with 6 about 7.8 bits from nothing. That is the binning problem of episode 5.2 again with an alphabet that explodes factorially.

So the choice of the pattern length m is a real decision. Longer patterns capture more of the signal shape but demand far more data. A common rule of thumb asks for many times more samples than possible patterns, and for mutual information the joint alphabet is what counts. The time delay between the samples of a pattern is a second choice. Patterns of samples one millisecond apart describe very fast wiggles, while samples spaced further apart describe slower shapes.

What else is it sensitive to? Ties. If two samples in a window have the same value, the ranking is ambiguous. Rounded or saturated signals produce many ties, and the rule used to break them changes the patterns so it should be reported. Overlap. Consecutive windows share samples, so successive patterns are not independent, and the effective number of patterns is smaller than their count. And noise, with fine time resolution, a small amount of noise can flip the order of nearly equal neighbours and fill the table with patterns that describe the noise rather than the signal.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study computes ordinal mutual information between EEG channels with patterns of six samples on 10-second windows at 250 Hz, 2,500 samples. The joint alphabet has more than half a million cells. Every window shows several bits of shared information, and differences between conditions are interpreted as changes in coupling. Most of that is bias, and a difference in window length or signal smoothness between conditions would be enough to change it. Keep m small, compare against surrogates, and remember that a null result says nothing about amplitude coupling.

To recap, ordinal estimators replace each window of m samples by its ranking, an alphabet of m factorial symbols introduced by Bandt and Pompe. They are immune to monotonic transformations, units, and gains, and largely robust to slow drifts. They are blind to everything carried by amplitude, including shared power fluctuations. Their alphabet grows factorially, and with it the data they need and the bias they create when data are short. Choose m and the delay deliberately, report how ties are handled, and test against surrogates.

Variational bounds I · Donsker–Varadhan and NWJ

Any critic gives a lower bound in the Donsker–Varadhan form, and NWJ is a looser cousin with no logarithm. A critic family that is too narrow gives a valid but loose bound, even zero for a parabola, and scores need held-out data.

Part 1

Back at the river, where the prospectors are about to change trade. Until now, every estimator counted something. Samples in bins, bumps on samples, neighbours in a ball. Today we stop counting and start optimising. Mutual information becomes the answer to a contest. The best score a judge can reach when asked to tell real pairs from shuffled ones. This idea is behind most of the neural network estimators of the last decade. This is part 1 of 2, the principle. Part 2 puts it to work.

Start with intuition. Mutual information is a KL divergence, as we saw in episode 4.1. The divergence between the joint distribution of X and Y, and the product of their marginals, what the joint would be if they were independent. Samples from the joint are easy, they are your real paired data. Samples from the product are easy too. Shuffle one of the two signals so that every X is paired with a random Y.

So imagine a judge, a function T of X and Y, that gives a score to any pair. A good judge gives high scores to real pairs and low scores to shuffled ones. The more different the two kinds of pairs are, the better a judge can separate them. And how different they are is exactly what the KL divergence measures. The variational idea makes that precise. The KL divergence is the best possible score that any judge can achieve in a particular game.

Here is the game in the version of Monroe Donsker and Srinivasa Varadhan. The score of a judge T has two parts. First, the average of T over real pairs. Second, subtract the logarithm of the average of e to the power T over shuffled pairs. The Donsker–Varadhan representation says that the KL divergence equals the highest value of this score over all possible judges.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, any judge you can write down gives a lower bound. Pick some function T, compute the two averages, and you have a number that cannot exceed the true mutual information, apart from estimation noise. You do not need the best judge to get a valid bound, you only need it to get the true value. Second, the best judge is known in principle. It is the log of the ratio between the joint density and the product of the marginals, the same log ratio whose average is the mutual information. Any constant can be added to it without changing the score because the logarithm in the second term absorbs it.

Estimating mutual information has become an optimisation problem. Choose a family of judges. Search the family for the one with the highest score on your data. Report that score.

There is a close cousin derived from a more general family of divergences, the f-divergences of episode 3.4. It was proposed by XuanLong Nguyen, Martin Wainwright, and Michael Jordan in 2010, and it is usually called NWJ. Its score uses the same first term, the average of t on real pairs, but the second term is the average of e to the power t minus one on shuffled pairs, without a logarithm.

The NWJ score is also a lower bound for any judge, and also reaches the mutual information for the best judge. But the two differ in important ways. For the same judge, the Donsker–Varadhan score is always at least as high as the NWJ score. Donsker–Varadhan is the tighter bound. On the other hand, NWJ has no logarithm of an average, which makes it simpler to estimate without bias from a sample, a point that matters for neural network training in the next episode. And NWJ is not indifferent to constants. Its best judge is not just the log ratio, but the log ratio plus exactly one. Add any other constant, and the bound drops.

Why call the judge a critic? Because in machine learning, the same structure appears in generative adversarial networks and in contrastive learning, where a function is trained to tell real data from fake or mismatched data. The vocabulary was borrowed with the method.

Before we test it, one thing deserves to be said clearly. Each of these scores is a lower bound only for the true averages over infinitely many samples. On a finite dataset, the averages are themselves estimates. A judge searched and scored on the same data can overfit. It can learn the quirks of this particular sample and report a score above the truth. The remedy is the one machine learning always uses. Fit the judge on one part of the data and score it on another.

To recap part 1, mutual information is the KL divergence between real and shuffled pairs. The Donsker–Varadhan representation turns it into the best score of a judge or critic, T. The average of T on real pairs minus the log of the average of e to the T on shuffled pairs. Any critic gives a lower bound, and the best critic is the log density ratio. NWJ is a looser bound without the logarithm, whose best critic is the log ratio plus one. And the bound only holds for scores measured on data the critic has not been fitted to.

In part two, how good is the bound in practice? It depends entirely on the family of critics we allow.

Part 2

Back at the river for part two of the variational bounds. In part one, we turned mutual information into a contest. A critic scores real pairs against shuffled pairs, and the best possible score in the Donsker–Varadhan or NWJ game is the mutual information. Now we play the game and discover that the answer depends on which critics we allow on the field.

Here is the calculation in one picture. 4,000 samples of a simulated Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.8. The true mutual information is about 0.51 nats. We fit each critic on 2,000 samples, score it on the other 2,000, and repeat 30 times.

The first family of critics is the simplest. The score is a single number times the product of X and Y. One parameter, a straight-line notion of agreement. The best critic in this family reaches a Donsker–Varadhan bound of about 0.26 nats, barely half the truth. The NWJ bound, with a constant added, gives about the same.

The second family adds two more terms, a number times X squared and a number times Y squared. Now the Donsker–Varadhan bound reaches about 0.51 nats, the truth, and NWJ about 0.50. Why the jump? For a Gaussian pair, the optimal critic, the log density ratio is exactly a combination of X times Y, X squared and Y squared. The second family contains the optimal critic, the first does not. The bound is only as good as the family of critics you search.

Now the constant. Take the optimal critic, the log ratio and add different constants to it. The Donsker–Varadhan score does not move. On any given sample, it is the same number whatever the constant. The NWJ score changes completely. With a constant of plus one, it matches Donsker–Varadhan. With zero, it drops sharply to around a tenth or two of a nat. With plus two or minus one, it becomes negative. NWJ only reaches the truth when the critic learns the right offset as well as the right shape.

Now a harder case, the parabola again a signal and its square, plus noise. Its true mutual information can be computed numerically, about 1.03 nats. With the quadratic family that was perfect for the Gaussian, the bound is zero. Nothing. A combination of X times Y, X squared and Y squared cannot express Y is close to X squared. Add terms such as X squared times Y and X to the fourth, and the bound jumps to about 0.96 nats, close to the truth. Same data, same method, a different family of critics, zero or nearly everything.

This is the central lesson of variational estimation. The method itself is neutral and general. Everything depends on the family of critics. A family that is too narrow returns a valid but loose lower bound, possibly zero and gives no warning. The natural response is to use a very flexible family, a neural network which can approximate almost any function. That is the subject of the next episode and it comes with its own problems.

What is this sensitive to? First, the critic family as we have seen. A loose bound does not announce itself, the optimisation converges happily to a low value. Second. Overfitting. A flexible critic fitted and scored on the same data can exceed the truth. Hold out data for scoring always. Third. The shuffled pairs. The second term is an average over samples from the product of marginals obtained by shuffling. With time series, shuffling individual samples destroys autocorrelation, and the shuffled pairs no longer represent the right null, as Chapter 6 explains. Fourth. The estimator of the bound itself. The Donsker–Varadhan score contains the logarithm of an average. Estimated from a sample, the log of an average is biased downwards, and the variance of e to the t can be enormous when the critic takes large values. Both problems grow with the true mutual information, and they are the starting point of episode 5.14.

Fifth. Units. These bounds use the natural logarithm and the exponential, so they come out in nats. Divide by the natural log of 2 for bits.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study trains a flexible critic on a dataset, reports the final training score as the mutual information, and finds it larger than any classical estimate. The score was measured on the data the critic was fitted to. Part of it is the critic memorising the sample. Another study uses a very simple critic, finds a bound near zero, and concludes there is no dependence. A zero lower bound proves nothing. The dependence may simply lie outside the family. Score on held-out data and treat a low bound as a statement about your critics, not about your signals.

With narrower critics, the bound stays valid but can be loose, even zero, as on the parabola with quadratic critics. Donsker–Varadhan ignores constants, NWJ needs the right offset. MINE uses a neural network as the critic in the Donsker–Varadhan bound, with a moving average to reduce the bias of the gradient. On an easy Gaussian pair it approaches the truth from below. Back at the river. Last episode we learned that a variational bound is only as good as its family of critics. A narrow family can miss the dependence entirely, as the quadratic critics did on the parabola. The obvious answer is to use the most flexible family we know — a neural network. In 2018, Ishmael Belghazi and colleagues did exactly that and called the result MINE the mutual information neural estimator. Today we train one, watch it learn, and watch where it misbehaves.

Start with intuition. A neural network takes a pair, X and Y, and outputs a single number, the critic's score. Its weights are the parameters of the critic. We feed it batches of real pairs and shuffled pairs, compute the Donsker–Varadhan score, and adjust the weights by gradient ascent to make that score larger. As the network improves at telling real pairs from shuffled ones, the score climbs towards the mutual information. A small network with two hidden layers can approximate the log density ratio for most shapes of dependence we care about, including the parabola that defeated the quadratic critics.

There is one technical subtlety, and it gave MINE its signature trick. The Donsker–Varadhan score contains the logarithm of an average of e to the T over shuffled pairs. When we estimate that average on a small batch and take its logarithm, the gradient we compute is biased. On average, it does not point exactly in the direction that improves the true score. Belghazi and colleagues replace the batch average in the denominator of the gradient with a slowly updated running average, an exponential moving average over many batches. That reduces the bias of the gradient. The alternative is to train on the NWJ bound instead, which has no logarithm of an average. The authors called that variant MINE-f.

Here is the calculation in one picture. A simulated Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.9, a true mutual information of about 0.83 nats. 5,000 training samples, 5,000 held out samples, a network with two hidden layers of 64 units, batches of 256 pairs, 3,000 training steps, 4 random initialisations. We track the Donsker–Varadhan score on the held-out data as training goes on.

At the start, the score is near zero. An untrained critic cannot tell real pairs from shuffled ones. Within a few hundred steps, it climbs to about 0.75 nats. After 3,000 steps, the four runs end at 0.79, 0.80, 0.80, and 0.82 nats. Slightly below the truth, as a lower bound should be. With the moving average with the plain biased gradient or with the NWJ variant, the results in this easy case are almost the same. The bias of the gradient matters most with small batches. On this easy problem, it makes no visible difference.

One curious detail. Take a critic trained with the Donsker–Varadhan score and evaluate it with the NWJ formula instead. For one of our runs, the NWJ score is about minus 9 nats. The Donsker–Varadhan critic is free to drift by any constant, as we saw last episode, and NWJ punishes that drift severely. Critics are trained for one bound, do not score them with another.

Now a harder problem, five independent Gaussian pairs side by side, each with a correlation of 0.9. The true mutual information between the two five-dimensional vectors is 5 times 0.83, about 4.15 nats. The same network, the same data size, 4,000 steps.

The held-out score climbs quickly to about 3.6 or 3.7 nats. Then it wanders. Over the second half of training, across our four runs, it moves between about 3.5 and 4.24 nats. The final values are 3.75, 3.98, 3.98, and 3.75 depending on the run. Stop at a different step and you would report a different number with a spread of about 7 tenths of a nat. Nothing in the training curve tells you which step is right. Averaging the score over the last few hundred steps steadies the number, but it does not remove the deeper problem. With high information, the estimate itself has a large variance and a downward bias, which no stopping rule repairs.

And now the most important failure, small data. A Gaussian pair with a correlation of 0.6, a true mutual information of about 0.22 nats, but only 300 training samples and a larger network. On the training data, the score climbs steadily past the truth to between about 0.3 and 0.45 nats, depending on the run. On held-out data, the score rises to about 0.2 in the first few hundred steps and then falls, ending between about 0.12 and 0.15. The network is memorising the 300 training pairs. If you had reported the training score, you would have overestimated the information by up to a factor of two. If you had reported the held out score at the end, you would have underestimated it.

So MINE is powerful and it is also an optimisation procedure with every caveat of optimisation. The answer depends on the architecture, the learning rate, the batch size, the number of steps, the random initialisation, and the choice of the bound used for training and for evaluation. None of these choices is wrong in itself. The problem is that each of them moves the answer, and a reader cannot tell by how much unless they are all reported.

What is MINE sensitive to? First, the stopping point. There is no natural end to training. Use held-out data and decide in advance how to choose the step, for example by early stopping on a separate validation set, not on the data you report. Second, sample size. Neural critics overfit small datasets readily. Held-out evaluation is not optional. Third, the size of the true information. As the information grows, the term with e to the T becomes dominated by rare shuffled pairs with huge scores, and the estimate becomes noisy. Episode 5.14 shows that this is a fundamental limit, not a tuning problem. Fourth, the shuffled pairs. With time series, shuffling individual samples creates the wrong null, as chapter 6 explains. And fifth, reproducibility. Report the architecture, optimiser, learning rate, batch size, number of steps, seeds, an evaluation protocol. Without them, a MINE value cannot be checked.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study estimates mutual information between EEG features and behaviour with a neural estimator on a few hundred trials and reports the maximum of the training curve. Every problem we have seen applies at once. The maximum of a noisy curve is biased upward. The training data were used to fit the critic. The number of trials is small for a flexible network. A classical estimator on the same data with a surrogate test would have been both cheaper and more trustworthy. Neural estimators earn their place with large datasets and complex, high-dimensional dependence. They are not a remedy for small samples.

Variational bounds III · InfoNCE

InfoNCE turns estimation into a recognition game: pick the true partner among N candidates, and read the classifier's log loss as nats saved over guessing. The result is steady but can never exceed the logarithm of the batch size. With a perfect critic it falls far below a large true information, while NWJ stays centred but extremely variable.

Back at the river for the third variational estimator and the one that won in machine learning. MINE gave us a flexible critic and an unstable estimate. Today's bound trades some of that flexibility for steadiness and pays with a hard ceiling. It comes from contrastive learning, it is called InfoNCE, and it was introduced by Aaron van den Oord, Yazhe Li, and Oriol Vinyals in 2018 in their work on contrastive predictive coding.

Start with intuition. Instead of scoring real pairs and shuffled pairs separately, InfoNCE plays a recognition game. Take a batch of N real pairs. For the first X in the batch, line up all N candidate Ys, its true partner, and the N minus 1 Ys that belong to other pairs. The critic gives a score to each candidate. Then turn the scores into probabilities with a softmax and ask, how much probability did the critic put on the true partner? Repeat for every X in the batch.

If X and Y are independent, no critic can do better than guessing, one chance in N. If X tells you a lot about Y, a good critic can pick out the true partner almost every time. The InfoNCE estimate is the logarithm of N, minus the average cross-entropy of this recognition game. In words, how much better than chance the critic recognises true partners, measured in nats.

If this sounds familiar, it should. It is the cross-entropy loss of episode 3.3 applied to a classification problem with N classes, where the classes are the candidates in the batch. Training a critic with InfoNCE is training a classifier to recognise partners, and the estimate reads the classifier's log loss as a number of nats saved compared with guessing.

Here is the important property. The InfoNCE score is a lower bound on the mutual information, like Donsker–Varadhan and NWJ. But it has a second bound built in, it can never exceed the logarithm of the batch size. The reason is simple. The best possible performance in the recognition game is to put all the probability on the true partner, which gives a cross-entropy of 0 and an estimate of exactly log N. A critic cannot do better than perfect.

That ceiling follows directly from the formula. Ben Poole and colleagues made the point explicit in their comparison of variational bounds in 2019. With a batch of 128 pairs, the ceiling is the natural log of 128, about 4.85 nats. With a batch of 1024, about 6.93.

Here is the calculation in one picture. To separate the ceiling from any training problem, we give InfoNCE the best possible critic, the exact log density ratio which we know analytically for Gaussian data. Then we vary the true mutual information by stacking more and more independent Gaussian pairs, each with a correlation of 0.9 and we vary the batch size.

With one pair, the true information is about 0.83 nats. With batches of 128, InfoNCE gives about 0.82. With two pairs, the truth is 1.66, and InfoNCE gives about 1.61. So far close.

With six pairs, the truth is about 4.98 nats, just above the ceiling of 4.85. InfoNCE gives about 4.02. With 12 pairs, the truth is about 10 nats, and InfoNCE with batches of 128 gives about 4.81, pressed against its ceiling, less than half the truth. With the perfect critic. The ceiling, not the critic, is the problem. Now increase the batch size at 12 pairs. With batches of 16, about 2.77 nats. 64, about 4.13. 512, about 6.1. 1024, about 6.72, just under its ceiling of 6.93. Every time, the estimate climbs with the batch but stays below the log of the batch size. To measure 10 nats you would need batches of tens of thousands of pairs because e to the 10 is about 22,000.

There is an intuitive way to see this. A mutual information of I nats means, roughly, that knowing X narrows the plausible Ys down by a factor of e to the I. To demonstrate that narrowing, the recognition game must offer at least that many candidates. A batch of 128 can only demonstrate a narrowing by a factor of 128.

And yet InfoNCE has a great virtue, steadiness. With the same perfect critic in batches of 128, the NWJ bound at one pair gives about 0.82 nats, but with a spread of about 0.14 nats against 0.08 for InfoNCE. At six pairs, the NWJ estimates scatter over hundreds of nats, and their average is meaningless. InfoNCE is low variance and biased low. NWJ is nearly unbiased and wildly variable. Episode 5.14 turns this into a general trade-off.

Why does InfoNCE dominate in machine learning, then? Because in representation learning, the goal is usually not to measure mutual information. It is to train an encoder whose representations keep the information that matters, and InfoNCE gives stable gradients that do exactly that. A loose but steady lower bound is a fine training signal. It is a poor measuring instrument when the information is large. For measurement, the ceiling has to be kept in view at all times.

What is InfoNCE sensitive to? First, the batch size which sets the ceiling. Any estimate close to log N is a warning sign, the true value may be much larger. Second, the critic, as for every variational bound, an imperfect critic lowers the estimate further. Third, what counts as a negative. The N minus 1 other Ys in the batch must behave like independent draws. In time series, neighbouring samples are near copies of the true partner, and using them as negatives makes the recognition game artificially hard and the estimate artificially low. Fourth, the direction of the game. Some implementations average the recognition of Y given X and of X given Y. The ceiling is still log N, but the numbers differ slightly, so report which version you used. Fifth, units: nats, from the natural logarithm. Divide by the natural log of 2 for bits.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study compares the information that two neural network models extract from brain recordings, using InfoNCE with batches of 256. Both models score about 5.4 nats, and the study concludes that they capture the same amount of information. But the natural log of 256 is 5.54. Both estimates are pressed against the ceiling. The true values could be 6 nats and 20 nats, the measurement cannot tell. Before comparing InfoNCE values, check their distance from log N and repeat with a larger batch to see whether they move. An estimate that follows the log N line as the batch grows is measuring the batch, not the brain.

To recap, InfoNCE scores a critic by how well it recognises the true partner of each X among N candidates. It equals log N minus the cross entropy of that game, and it is a lower bound on the mutual information. It can never exceed log N: 4.85 nats for a batch of 128, even when the truth is 10. It is far steadier than NWJ or Donsker–Varadhan. An excellent training signal, a measuring instrument only when the information is well below log N.

The limits of variational estimators

Even with a perfect critic, variational bounds face a trade-off: NWJ and Donsker–Varadhan stay centred but their variance explodes with the information, while InfoNCE is steady but capped at log N. Three published results show this is a limit rather than an engineering problem. No distribution-free lower bound can certify more than order log N, and typical brain values are small enough to avoid it.

Back at the river, for the episode that tempers the enthusiasm. Over the last three episodes, we built variational estimators. Donsker–Varadhan, NWJ, MINE, InfoNCE. Each had its own problem, a loose critic family, a wandering training curve, a hard ceiling. A natural hope is that these are engineering problems, and that a better network, a bigger batch, or a cleverer bound will fix them. Three results published between 2019 and 2020 say otherwise. Today we look at all three and at what they mean for brain data.

Start with intuition and an experiment that removes every engineering excuse. We give each bound the perfect critic, the exact log density ratio which we know analytically for Gaussian data. No training, no network, no tuning. Then we stack more and more independent Gaussian pairs, each with a correlation of 0.9, to raise the true mutual information from under 1 nat to about 10. 1000 samples each time, 40 repetitions. The true value is known exactly in every case because the pairs are independent of one another, and their informations simply add. Whatever goes wrong now is not the critic's fault.

Here is the calculation in one picture. With one pair, the true information is about 0.83 nats. All three bounds get it right. Donsker–Varadhan, NWJ, and InfoNCE all average about 0.83. The spread differs. About 0.05 nats for Donsker–Varadhan and NWJ. About 0.03 for InfoNCE.

With four pairs, the truth is about 3.32 nats. Donsker–Varadhan averages 3.35 with a spread of 0.32. NWJ 3.3 spreads 0.35. InfoNCE 3.25 with a spread of only 0.06.

With six pairs about 4.98 nats, Donsker–Varadhan scatters by almost nine-tenths of a nat. NWJ by more than two nats. InfoNCE stays steady within six hundredths, at about 4.67 already pulled down by its ceiling the natural log of 1,000, about 6.9.

With 12 pairs about 10 nats, Donsker–Varadhan averages 8.8 with a spread of nearly 4 nats. NWJ produces estimates ranging over hundreds of nats and an average of minus 87. InfoNCE gives 6.7, with a spread of two hundredths pressed against its ceiling.

So with a perfect critic, there are two kinds of failure. One family of bounds, NWJ and Donsker–Varadhan, stays roughly centred on the truth, but its variance explodes as the information grows. The other, InfoNCE, keeps a tiny variance, but its bias grows without limit once the information approaches log N.

That is the first result. Ben Poole and colleagues in 2019 put the variational bounds side by side and described exactly this bias variance trade-off. They also built bounds that interpolate between NWJ and InfoNCE, trading a little of one for a little of the other. None escapes the trade-off, they only choose where to sit on it.

The second result explains the explosion. Jiaming Song and Stefano Ermon in 2020 showed that for estimators of the NWJ and MINE kind, the variance grows exponentially with the true mutual information. The reason is in the second term, the average of e to the T over shuffled pairs. When the information is large, the critic gives enormous scores to the rare shuffled pairs that happen to look like real ones, and those rare pairs dominate the average. One sample more or less changes the answer by nats. Song and Ermon proposed clipping the critics' scores to tame the variance at the price of a known bias. Their estimator, called SMILE, caps e to the T inside a fixed interval before averaging. The tighter the cap, the smaller the variance and the larger the bias. Once again a trade-off, not an escape.

The third result is the deepest. David McAllester and Karl Stratos in 2020 asked a general question. Is there any way to obtain, from N samples, a lower bound on mutual information that holds with high confidence, without assuming anything about the distribution? Their answer is no unless the bound is small. Any such bound is limited to order log N. The intuition is the one we met with InfoNCE. A mutual information of I nats means that real pairs are, roughly, e to the I times more likely than shuffled ones. To prove that from data, you need to observe events with probability around e to the minus I, and with N samples you cannot reliably observe events much rarer than one in N.

So log N is not a weakness of one estimator. It is a limit on what any distribution-free lower bound can certify. With a thousand samples, about 7 nats. With a million samples, about 14. To go beyond, you must assume something about the distribution, as the Gaussian copula does and live with the assumption.

What does this mean for brain data? Many information values reported between brain signals or between brain signals and stimuli are a fraction of a bit. For those values, the variational machinery is rarely needed in the first place. In that regime, the limits we have just seen are not the problem. The problem is the variance at small N and the same surrogate tests as for any estimator. The limits matter when someone reports large values, several nats, from neural estimators on high-dimensional recordings, or uses those estimators to compare representations whose information is large. There, a bound pressed against log N, or an estimate with a huge spread between runs, is exactly what the theory predicts.

What are these results sensitive to? To the size of the true information, above all, everything is fine below a nat or two and deteriorates exponentially above. To the sample size through log N. To the bound, NWJ and Donsker–Varadhan pay in variance, InfoNCE pays in bias. And to assumptions, a parametric estimator can exceed log N because it is not distribution-free. Its accuracy then depends on whether the assumption is right.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study trains a large neural estimator on recordings from a few thousand trials and reports 12 nats of information between neural activity and a stimulus. The natural log of a few thousand is about 8. A distribution-free lower bound above that cannot be certified from these data. Either the estimate has a huge variance or it relies on an implicit assumption or it is an artefact of training on the evaluation data. Any of the three should be checked before believing the number.

In practice, a few habits protect you. Report N and log N next to every variational estimate. Run several random initialisations and report their spread, not the best one. Repeat with a different batch size. An InfoNCE value that moves with the batch was at its ceiling. In low dimensions, compare with a classical estimator such as KSG or GCMI. And remember that a lower bound is a natural ally of a statistical test. If even a conservative bound beats the surrogates, the dependence is real, whatever its exact size.

To recap, even with a perfect critic, variational bounds face a trade-off. NWJ and Donsker–Varadhan stay centred but their variance explodes with the information. InfoNCE stays steady but is capped at log N. Poole and colleagues described the trade-off. Song and Ermon showed the exponential variance. McAllester and Stratos proved that no distribution-free high-confidence lower-bound can exceed order log N. Neural networks do not replace missing data. They are tools for large datasets and complex dependence, used with care. For typical brain values below a nat, the practical issues are variance and surrogates. For large values, the limits decide.

Round 4, the stand-in for our running example. A simulated EEG alpha power, positive and skewed, and a simulated heart rate, weakly and monotonically related to it, plus noise. Now the build. On our machine, for one estimate on 1,000 samples, the binned estimators take about a millisecond. GCMI 2 milliseconds. KSG about 50. The kernel estimator about 300. MINE about two and a half seconds. A factor of more than a thousand between the fastest and the slowest. One estimate is affordable with any method. A study with 64 channels, 100 time points, and a thousand surrogates per test needs millions of estimates, and then the bill decides.

So there is no best estimator. There is a good estimator for a question and a dataset. Here is a decision tree to make the choice explicit before seeing the result.

First question, are your variables discrete? Stimulus categories, response choices, spike counts in a few bins. Then use the plug-in estimator with a bias correction, Miller–Madow, or NSB when samples are scarce, and keep the number of cells well below the number of samples.

If the variables are continuous, second question, how many dimensions and how much data. For low dimensions up to three or four in total and hundreds to thousands of samples, KSG is the general purpose choice. It sees any shape of dependence with little bias. Standardise every dimension, choose k between two and four, and protect it from autocorrelation.

Third question, do you expect the dependence to be monotonic or do you need many dimensions or many thousands of estimates. Then GCMI, fast, steady multivariate and a lower bound for one dimensional variables. Check the scatter plot first and cross-check with KSG on a subset if a non-monotonic relationship is plausible.

Fourth question, do you have very large datasets and complex high-dimensional dependence such as representations from a neural network? Then variational estimators can be worth their cost. Train and score on separate data, fix the stopping rule in advance, report log N, and remember its ceiling.

And at every leaf of the tree the same three obligations. Test against surrogates that respect the structure of your data, which is chapter 6. Report the estimator and every setting with units. And when the result matters, check it with a second estimator that makes different assumptions.
