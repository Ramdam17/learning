PLATE 17.17:08

Every measurement has an error

Every measurement is true value plus noise, like a trembling dial. Reliability is the share of the variance that is true: 226 over 251 gives 0.90 for blood pressure. It describes a group, not one reading. Narrow the group to a true spread of 5 and it falls to 0.50. A reliable measure can still track the wrong thing, like shoe length.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the watchmaker's shop, where nobody trusts a single look at a dial. Measure the same person twice, and you do not get the same number twice. A new chapter begins: measurement reliability. Today's question is simple. How much of a score belongs to the person, and how much is noise?

Here is a doctor's office, simulated. Twelve patients have their systolic blood pressure taken twice, a few minutes apart, with the same device. Think of each patient as a dial. The needle trembles. A flash freezes it: that is the first reading. A second flash: the second reading. Look at the first patient. One hundred fifteen, then one hundred twenty-four. Same person, same device, nine apart.

The device is not broken. In a large Yale study of clinic records, people with normal pressure had a systolic reading that varied by an average of 6.3 millimetres of mercury between two consecutive visits. Every real measurement wobbles.

In a simulation, we can do what no clinic can: we can see the truth. Guess before you see. For each patient, does the true pressure sit between the two readings? Here are the true values, one tick per patient. For five patients out of twelve. Over ten thousand simulated patients, 50.4 percent: about half. Two noisy readings do not trap the truth.

So here is the simplest model of a measurement. What we see is the true value, plus noise. The noise has no favourite direction, and it does not know who the patient is. The idea goes back to Charles Spearman, in 1904, and classical test theory is built on it.

Now take ten thousand patients, each measured once. Their true pressures are spread out. The variance of the truth is 226. The noise has a spread of 5, so a variance of 25. And the readings we actually see have a variance of 251. Look: 226 plus 25 is 251. The variance of what we see is the variance of the truth, plus the variance of the noise.

Reliability is the share of what we see that is true. 226 over 251 is 0.90. So ninety percent of the differences between these patients are real differences, and ten percent is noise.

A common misreading: "reliability is 0.90, so each reading is ninety percent right." No. Reliability says nothing about one reading. How far can one reading be off? The noise says it: about 5 either way, for any patient. Reliability is about a group. It says what share of the differences between its members is real.

Now the strong idea. Keep the same device, the same noise of 5. But suppose a trial enrols only patients whose pressure is already in a narrow band. The true spread is 5, instead of 15. Guess before you see. What is the reliability now?

The variance of the truth is 25. The variance of the noise is still 25. The variance we see is 50. And 25 over 50 is 0.50. Half of what we see is noise. The device did not change. The group did.

Turn the knob slowly. Make the group more alike, and reliability falls: 0.80 at a true spread of 10, 0.50 at a spread of 5, 0.14 at a spread of 2. Make the group more varied, and it rises, but it never reaches one. Each dot is ten thousand simulated patients. The curve is the formula: the variance of the truth, over the variance of the truth plus the variance of the noise.

So reliability is not a property of the device alone. It is a property of the device in a group of people. A reliability in a paper is true for the people in that paper. Another group, another number.

The same story happens in other fields. Each time: one noise, two groups.

A bathroom scale wobbles by about one kilogram, because of the carpet and where you stand. Among adults, weights spread by 14 kilograms: reliability 0.99. In a team that all trains at one weight, they spread by 1.5: reliability 0.69.

A reaction time, as the average of sixteen trials. The noise is lapses of attention: 20 milliseconds on the average. Among general adults, 0.79. Among expert players who all react alike, 0.14. A single trial is much noisier: in 11.2, only one fifth of the variance sat between people. Averaging helps, and 17.2 shows how.

A well-being questionnaire. The noise is the mood of the day, and how each item is read: 3 points. Among students, 0.87. Among a group who are all doing very well, 0.31.

A heart rate read from a wrist watch, with movement and coffee as noise: 3 beats. Among adults, 0.91. Among runners whose resting rates are all low, 0.41.

A teacher grades essays twice, a week apart. The noise is mood, tiredness and the order of the pile: 4 points. In a class with strong and weak essays, 0.90. Among the ten best essays, 0.35.

And a kitchen thermometer, off by a quarter of a degree. Among children in a clinic, some with a fever, 0.95. Among healthy children only, 0.60. It tells fever from health, and it is poor at ranking healthy children.

Before you trust a score to rank people, ask: among which people?

Reliability answers one question: is the number repeatable? A stopped clock shows the same time every time you look. Perfectly repeatable, and useless. Validity is another question: does the measure track what we think it tracks?

Here are two thousand simulated children, aged five to eleven. We measure each shoe twice, to measure reading skill. The two lengths agree almost perfectly: reliability 0.98. And shoe length correlates with the reading score: 0.90. It looks like a wonderful test.

But shoe length tracks age. Look only at children of the same age: six, eight, ten. The correlations are 0.06, minus 0.03 and 0.02. Nothing. A reliable measure of the wrong thing. In this simulation both depend on age and on nothing else; that is how we built it.

In real life, we never see the true values. So how do we estimate reliability? By measuring each person twice. That is for the next video.

Remember: reliability is the share of the variance that is true, and it belongs to the instrument in a group of people, never to the instrument alone.

PLATE 17.27:20

Test–retest and the intraclass correlation

The intraclass correlation puts one number on the share of variance between people. Pearson's r ignores a ten-point practice lift, staying at 0.76, while absolute agreement falls to 0.54. The form must be named: consistency or agreement, single or average. Averaging trials raises reliability by the Spearman-Brown formula, from 0.20 for one trial to 0.80 for sixteen.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the watchmaker's shop. Last time we saw that every score is a true value plus noise. Today: how do we put one number on the share that is true? The answer is the intraclass correlation. First, we must see where the ordinary correlation falls short.

Here is a simulated study. Thirty people take the same vocabulary test twice, a week apart. This is a test and retest design. Each dot is one person. Their first score runs across, their second score runs up. The diagonal marks perfect repeatability: the same score both times. The dots hug it, but not perfectly. Pearson's r is 0.76. The intraclass correlation has several versions, and here two of them nearly agree: 0.75 for consistency, and 0.76 for absolute agreement.

Now a twist. Between the sessions, everyone practised, so every second score is ten points higher. Guess before you see. What happens to Pearson's r?

The whole cloud lifts, and r does not move. It is still 0.76, exactly. Pearson asks only whether the dots follow a straight line, any straight line, so a shifted streak is still a perfect streak. It is not wrong: it answers another question, whether the order holds. But look at the people: not one of them scored the same twice. The consistency version does not move either: still 0.75. It asks only whether the order is kept. Absolute agreement asks whether the scores themselves are the same, and it drops from 0.76 to 0.54.

The same thing happens in a clinic. Forty people have their blood pressure taken at two visits, and the first visit reads high, because people are tense. Pearson's r is 0.88. Consistency: 0.88. Absolute agreement: 0.72. A systematic shift is invisible to a correlation, and visible to agreement.

Where does the number come from? From the levels of a study, in 11.2, read as a ratio. Split the variance of the scores into pieces. Between people: 87.77. That is the part we want. Error, what changes inside a person from one session to the next: 28.64. Consistency is the part between people, over the part between people plus the error. 87.77 over 87.77 plus 28.64 is 0.75. This is the reliability of 17.1: the share of the variance that is true differences between people.

Now the shifted data. The lift adds a third piece, the gap between sessions: 44.81. The other two are untouched. Absolute agreement counts the gap as error too: 87.77 over 87.77 plus 44.81 plus 28.64 is 0.54.

So there are several forms. Shrout and Fleiss described them in 1979, and McGraw and Wong extended the list in 1996. Two questions choose the form. Do we ask for consistency, or for absolute agreement? And will we use a single measurement, or the average of several?

A new study. Four raters score the same thirty essays, and they differ in severity: we built the last one to be twelve points harsher than the first. For one rater, consistency is 0.59 and absolute agreement is 0.48. For the average of the four raters, consistency is 0.85 and absolute agreement is 0.79. Same essays, same scores, four numbers, from 0.48 to 0.85.

Here is the strong moment. "The intraclass correlation was 0.8" says nothing until we know the form. Which form? If scores only rank people, as with a well-being questionnaire, consistency is enough. If the score itself matters, as with an essay and a pass mark, ask for agreement. If the study averages sessions, use the average form.

How do we read a value? A popular guide, from Koo and Li in 2016: below 0.5 is poor, 0.5 to 0.75 moderate, 0.75 to 0.9 good, above 0.9 excellent. But the same authors say to judge the interval around the value, not the value itself. Why? Because thirty people give a wobbly estimate. We simulated ten thousand studies like the first one, with a true value of 0.74. In 95 percent of them, the estimate landed between 0.51 and 0.87. That runs from moderate to good. One small study cannot tell them apart. So a paper should report the form, the number of people, and the interval, not just a value.

Now the good news: averaging helps. A reaction-time task, as in 11.2, with 40 milliseconds between people and 80 within a person. One trial has a reliability of 0.20. Averaging two trials: 0.33. Four: 0.50. Eight: 0.67. Sixteen: 0.80. Each value is measured on ten thousand simulated people.

There is a formula, the Spearman–Brown prophecy formula. For m trials: m times the single-trial value, over 1 plus m minus 1 times that value. For sixteen trials: 16 times 0.20, over 1 plus 15 times 0.20. That is 3.2 over 4, which is 0.80. The formula and the simulation agree. The noise averages out, and the person stays.

Brain measures behave the same way. Take a resting connectivity value, simulated, from runs of five minutes. One run: 0.30. Eight runs, forty minutes: 0.78. In real neuroimaging, reliability depends strongly on how much data we collect per person, as Zuo, Xu and Milham wrote in 2019.

One line from 17.1: reliability belongs to the group. A grip-strength dynamometer, with the same noise of 2.5 kilograms. Among adults of all kinds, 0.93. Among national rowers, who are all alike, 0.59.

And one more caution: what counts as noise depends on the question. A step counter is worn on two days. The counter is fine, but people really do walk differently from one day to the next. If we call that change error, the intraclass correlation is only 0.30. It says how stable a person's walking is, not how good the counter is.

Remember: the intraclass correlation is the share of the variance that sits between people, and it comes in forms. Always say which one: consistency or agreement, single or average.

PLATE 17.37:26

Agreement and internal consistency

Two wall clocks can correlate perfectly yet never agree. A Bland-Altman plot shows the bias, here 7.56 for a wrist blood-pressure cuff, with limits of agreement judged against a width chosen beforehand. Two EEG pipelines correlate at 0.99999 yet never match. Cronbach's alpha rewards length: forty barely related items still give 0.81, and a high alpha does not prove one dimension.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the watchmaker's shop. Two wall clocks hang side by side. They tick in step, but one is always a quarter of an hour fast. Ask for their correlation and you get a perfect one. Ask them for the time, and they never agree. Today: a high correlation is not agreement. Then the same question for a questionnaire.

Here is a simulated study. A new wrist blood-pressure cuff, and the reference cuff on the arm. Fifty people wear both. Each dot is one person. The arm reading runs across, the wrist reading runs up. The dashed diagonal is agreement: the same number from both cuffs. The dots make a tight streak, and Pearson's r is 0.96. But the streak sits above the diagonal. The wrist cuff reads higher for 43 of the 50 people. On average the arm cuff gives 126.28, and the wrist cuff 133.84.

In 1986, Bland and Altman proposed a plot that shows this. Take one person. Their two readings differ by some amount: that is the difference. Their two readings also have an average. Now give each person one dot. The average runs across, and the difference runs up. Fifty dots, and the whole cloud floats above zero.

The mean of the differences is the bias: 7.56. Their standard deviation is 5.86. The limits of agreement are the bias, plus or minus two standard deviations. Here is the arithmetic. 7.56 plus 2 times 5.86 is 19.28. And 7.56 minus 11.72 is minus 4.16. Bland and Altman wrote two as the working value, and 1.96 as the more precise one, when the differences follow a bell curve. Here the two choices differ by about 0.2 at each end. All 50 differences fall inside the limits. A little better than the 95 percent promise: that happens with fifty people.

The wrist cuff reads 7.56 higher on average, and for one person it may be anywhere from 4 below to 19 above. Is that good enough? That is a clinical question, and the width must be chosen before the study. Suppose we decided, beforehand, that readings may differ by at most 10. The upper limit, 19.28, is beyond it. Only 31 of the 50 people differ by 10 or less. These two cuffs cannot be swapped. Bland and Altman wrote it plainly: how far apart measurements can be is a matter of judgement.

A second pair: a forehead thermometer against an oral one. Sixty people, simulated. Pearson's r is 0.94. That is lower than for the cuffs. But look at the plot. The bias is 0.07 degrees, and the limits run from minus 0.25 to 0.40. Before the study, the clinic said that half a degree is fine. Both limits are inside it. All 60 differences are within half a degree. These two can be swapped.

A third case. A smartwatch against a chest strap, during a workout. Sixty people, with heart rates from 60 to 180 beats per minute, simulated. Pearson's r is 0.99. The bias is 6.23 beats. But look at the plot: the difference climbs with the heart rate. For the twenty people with the lowest heart rates, the watch reads only 1.20 higher. For the twenty highest, 10.55. The line has a slope of 0.11 beats per beat. This is called proportional bias. No single number describes it.

Now the strong moment. Two EEG pipelines measure the same evoked amplitude, in microvolts, in forty people. Pipeline B always gives exactly 1.2 times pipeline A, plus 5. This is exact, by construction, with no noise at all. The correlation is 0.99999. And yet the two never give the same number. Out of forty people, none has equal readings. The smallest gap is 5.8 microvolts, and the largest is 9.6. A reading of 10 becomes 17. Correlation asks whether the dots follow a line, any line. Agreement asks whether they sit on the diagonal.

Now the second question. Not two instruments, but the ten items of one questionnaire. All ten are meant to measure anxiety. Do they agree with each other? The usual answer is Cronbach's alpha, from 1951. A simulated study: 300 people answer ten items. Each row of tiles is an item, each column a person, sorted by total score. The high scorers are dark all the way down: the items move together. The average correlation between two items is 0.27, and alpha is 0.78.

Alpha rises with the number of items, and with how strongly the items correlate. For k items with an average correlation r, alpha is k times r, over 1 plus k minus 1 times r. This is the Spearman–Brown formula of 17.2: more items, like more trials, average out the noise. Exact arithmetic for ten items and r of 0.30: 10 times 0.30 is 3.0, over 1 plus 9 times 0.30, which is 3.7. That gives 0.81. Simulated, over 2,000 studies of 200 people, we get 0.81. The simulation lands close to the formula, within about 0.01 in every cell. And our single sample had 0.27, not 0.30, so it gave 0.78: that is only sampling. Twenty items correlated at 0.50 give 0.95.

Now two traps. First, a long questionnaire of weakly related items. Forty items, each correlating only 0.10 with the others, so each shares just ten percent. Alpha is 0.81. A high alpha from items that barely agree. Alpha rewards length.

Second, a well-being scale with two sub-scales: five items on mood, five on social life. Inside a sub-scale, items correlate at 0.50. Across them, 0.20. The heat map shows two blocks, so there are two things. Alpha is 0.83. A scale of one dimension with the same average correlation gets 0.82. Alpha cannot tell them apart. Sijtsma, in 2009, showed that alpha says very little about whether a test measures one thing. A high alpha is not proof of one dimension.

Alpha also assumes that every item measures the thing equally well, and that their errors are unrelated. McNeish, in 2018, argued that these assumptions are often false, so that alpha can be too low, and he recommended other coefficients, such as omega.

Remember: a high correlation is not agreement. Plot the differences, report the bias and the limits, and judge them against a width chosen before. And a high alpha is not proof of one dimension.

PLATE 17.47:05

What noise does to results

Noise weakens results. With reliabilities of 0.6, a true correlation of 0.50 shrinks to 0.30. Robust tasks like Stroop can rank people poorly. Patients picked for a high first blood pressure drop from 148.7 to 140.3 with no treatment, so a placebo pill gets the credit. In small studies, results that pass the threshold are the ones luck inflated.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the watchmaker's shop. Last time we saw that every score is the true value plus noise. Today's question is what that noise does to the results we report. Reliability is the share of a score's variance that comes from real differences between people; measuring people twice is how it is estimated, and that is for another video.

Take two scores: an anxiety questionnaire, and a heart-rate response in the lab. In a simulation we know the truth, so suppose that among one thousand people, the true scores correlate at 0.50. Here is that cloud. But nobody ever sees true scores. We see them measured, and say each measure has a reliability of 0.6: sixty percent of what we see is real, forty percent is noise. Watch every dot shake.

Guess before you see. After the shake, is the correlation 0.50, 0.40, 0.30, or 0.20?

It is 0.30. The cloud grew fatter, and the correlation fell by two fifths, although nothing about the people changed. Charles Spearman worked this out in 1904. The observed correlation is the true one, times the square root of the two reliabilities multiplied. Here: 0.5 times the square root of 0.6 times 0.6. That is 0.5 times 0.6, which is 0.30. A million simulated people give 0.30 as well.

Now turn the knob of reliability. With no noise, 0.50. At 0.8, it is 0.40. At 0.6, 0.30. At 0.4, only 0.20. The weaker instrument rules: a very good questionnaire, 0.9, paired with a poor measure, 0.4, still gives 0.30. A team that expected 0.5 may call that a failure. Spearman's cure is to divide back: 0.30 divided by 0.60 gives 0.50. But it needs the reliabilities, and those are estimates too.

Now a surprise. Some of the most solid results in psychology make poor measures of people. Take a Stroop-style task. Everyone is slower when the word and its colour disagree. In 200 simulated people, the average cost is 62 milliseconds, with a standard error of 1.8. Nobody doubts that effect.

But who has the big cost? We simulate people who differ little, a true spread of 10 milliseconds, and the noise of one session is 25. Two sessions correlate at 0.14. The cloud is nearly round. Give the same noise to a task where people differ a lot, a true spread of 30, and the sessions correlate at 0.59.

A robust effect is one that everybody shows. A score that ranks people needs people to differ. Hedge, Powell and Sumner tested seven classic tasks twice, three weeks apart. Reliabilities ran from 0 to 0.82, and a flanker effect scored 0.40 in their first study. They called it the reliability paradox. Correlate such a task with a symptom score, and the result will be small whatever the truth.

Noise has a second effect, and it needs only one score. Pick people because their first score is extreme, and measure them again. A simulated clinic: 4,000 patients, blood pressure read at two visits, reliability 0.60. We enrol the 191 whose first reading is 145 or more. Their average is 148.7. Guess before you see: at the second visit, with nothing done, will they be higher, the same, or lower? They are lower: 140.3. Down by 8.4. The 203 patients with a first reading of 115 or less go the other way: from 111.6 up to 118.5.

Francis Galton saw this in 1886, in heights. Children of very tall parents were tall, but on average only about two thirds as far above the average as their parents were. The first reading was partly luck, and luck does not repeat.

Now give half of the enrolled patients a pill that does nothing. Their pressure falls by 8.9. A headline! But the other half, with no pill, falls by 8.0. Without a comparison group, the pill gets the credit. Barnett and colleagues wrote, in 2005, that the effect grows with measurement error, and when follow-up is limited to people picked for a high first value.

A school after its worst year: among 200 simulated schools, the 20 lowest receive extra help that does nothing. Their average rises from 454 to 482. And Daniel Kahneman tells of flight instructors who praised cadets after a clean manoeuvre, and saw the next one get worse. They shouted after a poor one, and saw it improve. They concluded that shouting works. Kahneman saw regression to the mean.

So does noise only weaken results? Not quite. An EEG marker and a symptom score: the true correlation is again 0.5, with reliability 0.6 each, in studies of just 20 people. Here are 24 such studies. Over 10,000 studies, the average correlation is 0.29: noise pulls toward zero. A study passes p below 0.05 only if r reaches 0.44. That happens in 26 percent of them.

Guess before you see. Among those that pass, is the average correlation below 0.30, near 0.30, near 0.50, or above?

It is 0.55. Above the true 0.50. Is that a contradiction? No, these are two questions. On average, noise pulls every study toward zero. But the threshold keeps only the studies that luck pushed up. With 50 people, the studies that pass average 0.39; with 200, they average 0.30.

So "the effect survived the noise" is no argument. In small studies, what survives is what luck inflated. Loken and Gelman, in 2017, called it a fallacy: what does not kill statistical significance does not make it stronger.

Remember: noise pulls correlations toward zero, makes extreme scores slide back to the average, and, in small studies, the results that pass the threshold are the lucky ones. Ask how reliable the measures were, and how many people.
