PCNtoolkit, end to end

A hands-on walk through PCNtoolkit version 1.4. Fit a warped Bayesian regression with B-splines on simulated data, with the outlier switch off and a test split locked away, then read the metrics and a QQ plot. Then load a pretrained model, read its label, match new data to it and adapt it, watching for zero-filled columns and subjects in both files.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we closed the book with a checklist. Nine lines, and every line points back to an episode. Today we leave the bench and open the editor. We will write the analysis, line by line, and watch each line of the checklist come out of the code.

Welcome to the veranda. LAPPY is here, with two screens. The first one runs PCNtoolkit. The second one, in the next episode, runs another library. Today is the first screen.

A word on versions, because code ages fast. PCNtoolkit was rewritten in twenty twenty-five, as a new generation, one point x. Models and scripts from the old zero point x line do not work with it. As of November twenty twenty-six, the latest release on the Python package index is version one point four point zero, published on the twenty-seventh of October. We read the documentation of that release, and the version is printed on the screen. This is a bonus episode. It is built to be replaced when the library moves.

Part one of two. Fit a model of our own.

The data are the simulated cortical index from earlier in the series. Four hundred and fifty fictional people, aged eight to eighty, from three sites. Site A has two hundred and forty people, site B a hundred and fifty, and the small site C only sixty, all of them over forty-five. Every number you see in this part comes from that simulation, and the screen says so.

Because we made the data, we know the truth. The spread grows with age. The noise leans to one side, more and more with age. And we can draw the true centiles. Keep them in mind. They are our answer key.

First line of code. We install the library with pip, and import it. Then we build a data object. In PCNtoolkit, it is called NormData. We hand it a table, and we say three things. Which column is the covariate. Here, age. Which column is the batch effect, the thing that differs between sources. Here, site. And which column is the response. Here, the index.

Pause on one option. When we build the data object, there is a switch to remove outliers. It is off by default. If you turn it on, people beyond a cut on the z-score are dropped before the model ever sees them. But the tails are exactly what a normative model is for. Cutting them first is a trap from the chapter on pitfalls, in its most innocent form. We leave it off.

Second line. The split. The data object can split itself. By default it keeps eighty percent for training and sets twenty percent aside for testing, and it does so site by site, so every site appears on both sides. On our data, that is three hundred and sixty people for training and ninety for testing. From this moment, the test people are locked away. They will not touch the model until we score it. That is the leakage rule, enforced by one line of code.

Now the model. In PCNtoolkit, it has two layers. Inside, a Bayesian linear regression. Outside, a wrapper that scales the inputs and the outputs, fits the model, and predicts.

We build the inside layer in three choices. A B-spline expansion of age, so that the mean is a smooth curve and not a line. A variance that depends on age, because our spread grows. And a sinh-arcsinh warp, so that the noise may lean and have heavier tails. These are the pieces of episodes four point three, three point four and four point four, now as three arguments.

One more switch, for the sites. The library can give each site its own offset, and its own slopes. We turn it on, because sites differ. Site C is the one to watch. It has only sixty people, and none of them is young.

Then one call. Fit, and predict on the test set. The console prints what it does, step by step, and we leave it on screen. In one run, the library fits the model on the training people, and it computes, for each test person, a prediction, a z-score, and centiles.

Now the picture. The library draws the centile plot. Grey dots are the training people, and the curves are the fitted centiles. We lay our answer key over it, the true centiles of the simulation. Look at the curves site by site. And look at site C, below age forty-five. There are no people there. Whatever curve the plot shows in that corner, the model did not learn it from site C. That is the extrapolation trap, and the model label has a line for it, the valid range. We write it down: from forty-five. And look at the young end of the warped curves. Only a handful of people are young, and the outer curves flare there, far above anything the simulation can produce. Sparse data, and a flexible warp, make a bad pair.

So far we have a picture. Now numbers. The library keeps an evaluation table, one row per response, with the same names that this series has taught.

EXPV and R squared say how well the mean is predicted. MSLL compares the model with a plain Gaussian. Below zero, the model beats that Gaussian. MACE, the mean absolute centile error, asks whether the centiles hold. If the model says twenty-five percent of people are below this line, is it so? And for the z-scores, the table gives the Shapiro-Wilk statistic, the skewness and the kurtosis. For a well calibrated model, the z-scores should look like a standard bell, with skewness near zero.

We do it twice. Once with a plain Gaussian version of the model, once with the warp. We put the two tables side by side, and we read them with those rules. The tables come out of the code, not out of the slides. On this run, the warp brings the skewness of the test z-scores from zero point seven four down to zero point three three, and the kurtosis from one point one five to zero point four nine. But MSLL and MACE barely move. MSLL is minus zero point five six for the Gaussian and minus zero point five four for the warp, and MACE is zero point zero seven one for both. With only ninety test people, we do not over-read small differences. Here, on simulated data, the warp has an unfair advantage. We built the noise with the very same family. A good fit here is a test of the code, not a proof about real brains.

And the table exists for two sets. The training set, and the test set. We read the test set. The training numbers can be kinder, because the model has seen those people. Here they are not: the test numbers come out a little better, which tells you how noisy an exam of ninety people is.

Last, the z-scores. They are already in the test object. We draw a QQ plot, which is the long version of the calibration number. If the points follow the diagonal, the z-scores behave like a standard bell. Where they bend away, we see where calibration breaks, in the middle or in the tails. That is also where we will compare this library with another one, in the next episode.

Part one is done. One model, one table, one QQ plot. The first lines of the model label are filled, from the code. Take a breath.

Part two of two. Use a model that someone else built.

You may not want to fit a lifespan model from scratch. You can borrow one. The PCN lab shares a collection of pretrained models, hosted on a file sharing service called SURFdrive, as of November twenty twenty-six. Their file names are small labels. A name starting with B-L-R-w is a Bayesian linear regression with a warp, the method we just used. A name starting with H-B-R is the hierarchical Bayesian version with a sinh-arcsinh likelihood. Then the measure, cortical thickness, surface area, subcortical volume, fractional anisotropy, or functional connectivity. Then the parcellation. Then the number of people, and the number of sites. The documentation says that all of these models were estimated on healthy people only.

We pick the cortical thickness model with the Destrieux parcellation. In its name, sixty-seven thousand people, from eighty-nine sites. We download it, unzip it, and load it with one call.

The library prints a warning, saying the model was saved with an older release. That is expected. It converts the old file to the current format as it loads.

Now, before anything else, we ask the model what it expects. Its covariate is age. Its batch effects are site and sex. It knows a hundred and fifty measures, and its age range runs from two to one hundred. This is the model label, read from the model itself. Our data must match it, line by line.

The data are the braincharts transfer set, from the braincharts project, built from open OpenNeuro data. Six sites. A hundred and ten rows to adapt the model, and four hundred and thirty-six rows to test it. Ages from five point four to seventy-three.

Matching has three traps, and they are the quiet ones. The names of the measures must be the same as the model's. The documentation tells us to keep only the measures that both sides know. All hundred and fifty names of the model are in the braincharts files, but that check is not enough: for ten of them, the column with the model's exact spelling holds only zeros, and the measured values sit next to it, under a slightly different spelling. The codes of the batch effects must be the same. The pretrained models write sex as F and M, and the braincharts data files write zero and one, and do not say which is which. A comment in the demo script that comes with them says zero is female and one is male. We use that, and we say on screen that it is an assumption, read from a comment. We also ran it both ways, and after the adaptation, swapping the two codes changed no z-score, to four decimals, so here the guess is harmless, but we only know it because we measured it. And last, the people. The model is built on healthy participants, and the braincharts files have no diagnosis column. Whether everyone in them is a control is not written down. That line of the label stays open.

Now the adaptation. A new site has its own scale, and its own offset. The documentation recommends twenty to a hundred healthy controls per new site. Our adaptation file has between sixteen and twenty-three rows per site. Four of the six sites are under twenty. We say it on screen, and we go on.

We adapt with one call. It is called transfer, and we use the version that also predicts. It uses the adaptation people to learn the offsets of the new sites, and then it predicts on the test people. The result is an ordinary PCNtoolkit model. We can draw its centiles, but they only span the ages of the new data, not the ages of the reference. That is the extrapolation line again. On the six sites, the mean absolute z-score, over all people and all measures, is one point zero three, and twelve percent of the z-scores lie beyond one point nine six, where five percent are expected. We cannot say why from these files. It may be the sites, the preprocessing, or people who are not controls, but not the sex coding, which we ruled out.

Look at the files once more. The test file has four hundred and thirty-six rows, but only four hundred and seventeen distinct identifiers. And nine identifiers appear in both the adaptation file and the test file. If those are the same people, they are used twice, once to adapt and once to be scored. That is leakage, caught in the data and not in the code. We flag it. We do not fix it by guessing.

Then the output we came for. The library gives a z-score for every person and every measure. We take one person from the test file, and we colour the brain by z-score. A pink region is above the norm, a blue region is below it, and the threshold and the scale are on screen. This is a map of one person from a public dataset, whose diagnosis, if there is one, the file does not say. It is a picture of the method, not a diagnosis. The last trap is the quickest to fall into. A coloured map looks like an answer. It is only a distance to a reference, and the reference has a label with an open line.

Here is the key moment. Every number of the model label came out of a line of code. The reference, from the loaded model. The covariates and sites, from the data object. The model, from its arguments. The metrics, from the table. The valid range, from the plot. And each trap of the pitfalls chapter had a place in the script where it would have slid in. The outlier switch. The split. The empty ages of site C. The sex codes. The identifiers in two files.

Two bridges, before we finish. The thicknesses in the file come out of a FreeSurfer segmentation chain, which you can follow in the fMRI series. And the log loss behind MSLL is the surprise of the Information series, minus the log of a probability.

So, to sum up. Fit with a split locked away, read the table and the QQ plot, and when you borrow a model, load its label first and match your data to it. Next time, same analysis, another screen. B point two. PyNM, end to end.

PyNM, end to end

PyNM offers LOESS, centiles, Gaussian process and GAMLSS behind one interface, and every z-score is a residual divided by a spread. Run on the same simulated people as PCNtoolkit, the libraries agree in the middle and part in the tails because their spreads differ. Released PyNM uses too small a spread for centiles and the wrong sign for GAMLSS.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ran PCNtoolkit from start to finish. We loaded data, fitted a warped Bayesian linear regression with B-splines, and read the scores. Today we turn to the other screen on the veranda. Same kind of question, another library. PyNM.

LAPPY has two screens now. On the left, PCNtoolkit. On the right, a lighter tool, written in Python by Annabelle Harvey and Guillaume Dumas, and published in the Journal of Open Source Software, in December twenty twenty-two.

Its idea is simple. One interface, several models. Local regression, called LOESS. Plain centiles, counted in bins of the covariate. Gaussian process regression. And GAMLSS, which PyNM reaches by calling the R package, because, say the authors, it was not yet available in Python. They describe PyNM as lightweight, with fewer advanced features than PCNtoolkit, and a different set of models.

Now a fact that can change. As of November twenty twenty-six, the latest release on the Python Package Index is version one point zero point one, from December twenty twenty-two. But it is published only as an old-style egg file, so a normal install gives version one point zero point zero, and the code we used is identical in both. We installed it fresh. With the newest versions of the libraries it relies on, the LOESS step stopped with an error. With an older version, it ran. Write that on the model label, on the line for software. A tool that is not updated is a fact about your analysis.

Now the data. This is the simulated cortical index again, in arbitrary units. Not real people. We keep one site, site A, with two hundred and forty people. And one covariate, age. That is not a limit of our choice. LOESS and the centiles accept a single covariate, and the Gaussian process accepts several. One covariate keeps the models comparable. GAMLSS needs R. We installed it, so this time GAMLSS runs too.

Here is the code, in the editor. It is short.

First, build the object. You give it a table, the name of the score column, the name of the group column, and the list of covariates. The group column says who is a control. Only controls are used to fit. Here, everyone is labelled a control, because everyone is simulated. In a real study, patients get the other label, and they never enter the fit.

Second, the split. By default, PyNM fits on all the controls, and then predicts the same people. The model scores people it has already seen. That is the leak of chapter eleven, sitting in the default. So we ask for seventy percent of the controls to fit, and keep thirty percent aside. Seventy-two people, never seen.

Third, one method per model. Each one writes new columns in the same table. A prediction. A spread. A residual. And a z-score. The GAMLSS method sends the table to R, and reads the answer back.

Fourth, a report method prints three metrics for each model, on the people used to fit. Two of them are errors, the other is the mean standardised log loss. In the Information series, that loss is called surprise. The two binned models printed a not-available. The Gaussian process printed a negative loss, which is good. GAMLSS printed a positive one, worse than predicting the plain mean for everybody. Keep that in mind.

We hit three small snags, and they are worth knowing. With the default bins, which are very narrow, thirty-six of the two hundred and forty people got no score at all from LOESS or from the centiles. Their bin held too few controls. We widened the bins to a window of five years on each side. Second, the Gaussian process, on the raw score and with its default settings, gave everybody the same prediction. The mean. Standardising the score first fixed it. Third, changing the random seed we passed changed nothing. The reason is in the code. PyNM draws its split with a seed fixed at one, whatever you pass. To get another split, you draw it yourself and hand it over.

Now fill the model label from this code, line by line, as we did last time. Reference population: simulated, two hundred and forty people, from site A. Preprocessing: the score, standardised. Covariates: age. Models: LOESS, centiles, the Gaussian process, and GAMLSS. Software: PyNM, version one point zero point zero, with its caveat, and the R package gamlss. Valid range: ages from about nine to about seventy-seven, with only three people younger than fourteen. So the youngest ages are thin, and a score there deserves caution. An empty line would stay empty on the label, and we would say so.

Now the key moment. Two libraries, the same data, the same people. We hand PCNtoolkit the split that PyNM drew. The same one hundred and sixty-eight people to fit, and the same seventy-two to score. On one side, PCNtoolkit, with the warped regression of last time. On the other, PyNM, with its Gaussian process.

Every model ends with the same recipe. A z-score is a residual, divided by a spread. What changes is the spread.

Take the forty-six held-out people near the middle, whose true score lies within one of zero. There, the two libraries almost agree. Their z-scores differ by about zero point one on average. Now take the eleven people far out, beyond one and a half. The difference grows to about zero point five. For the youngest person, nine years old, it passes two. The libraries agree in the middle. They part in the tails. It is the lesson of episode four point nine, seen again across two libraries. And the tails are where we read a patient.

Is that one lucky split? We drew twenty other splits ourselves, and ran both libraries on each. In every one of them, the tails differed more than the middle. In the typical split, about zero point two in the middle, and about zero point four in the tails.

Why do they part? Look at what each one divides by. The Gaussian process of PyNM uses almost the same spread at every age. In this simulation, the true spread more than doubles between eight and eighty. The model of PCNtoolkit lets the spread change with age. In the middle, a small error in the divisor hardly shows. In the tails, it divides a large residual, and the scores pull apart.

Inside PyNM, the divisors differ even more. For LOESS, the spread is estimated locally, around each age. For the centiles, here is a surprise. In the code of version one point zero, the spread is half the distance between the thirty-second and the sixty-eighth centiles. For a Gaussian bell, those two centiles sit about zero point four seven standard deviations on each side of the median. So half the distance is only about half a standard deviation. The divisor is too small, and the z-scores come out about twice as large. As of November twenty twenty-six, that line is still in the code on GitHub.

And GAMLSS. In the released version, PyNM reads the GAMLSS spread on a log scale. So the divisor is negative. Minus zero point four nine, where it should be zero point six one. Every GAMLSS z-score comes out with the wrong sign. Against the true score, the correlation is minus zero point nine four. There is a second problem. For a skewed curve, a residual over a spread is not a z-score anyway. The right conversion goes through each person's centile. Both were fixed on GitHub in November twenty twenty-five. As of November twenty twenty-six, no release contains the fix. With the fix, GAMLSS gives the z-scores closest to the truth in our run. One more line for the label. With the default settings of PyNM, the GAMLSS fit stopped after twenty cycles without converging, in about half of our splits.

Now count the people beyond plus or minus one point nine six, among the seventy-two. The simulation says two are truly out there. PCNtoolkit flags four. LOESS flags five, and so does the Gaussian process. The released GAMLSS flags sixteen. The centiles flag twenty-one.

So do not compare two libraries, or two models, by their z-score columns alone. Compare what each one divides by, and how it turns a residual into a score. Remember that these numbers come from one simulated site and two hundred and forty people. They are a demonstration, not a benchmark. And a simulated patient is still not a patient.

So, to sum up. PyNM puts several models behind one interface, and every z-score is a residual over a spread. Look at the spread before you trust the score.

This was the last episode of the series. You can now read a centile curve, and ask what it was built from.
