PLATE 8.14:51

Energy as compatibility between x and y

An energy is a function of two things, an observation x and a candidate answer y, returning a low number when they are compatible and a high one when they are not. Picture a tray with dents, one per possible future: inference drops a marble that rolls into the deepest dent by minimizing over y. Only the ranking matters, so no normalized probability is required.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the marble park. A lawn of hills and hollows, and a marble that always rolls downhill. In this chapter, that marble becomes a way of thinking about learning. Today's question is simple. How can a model say that one future fits better than another?

Look at a photo of a ball, caught in the air a moment after someone threw it. Call the photo x. What happens next? Call a possible answer y. Here are five. The ball lands very short. It lands a little short. It lands right where the arc leads. It lands a bit far. It flies off into the trees. Several answers, not one.

In episode one point one, a model was a function with knobs: one input in, one output out. That is not enough here. The photo allows many continuations, and we want a verdict on each of them. So we build a different kind of function. It takes two things at once, the photo x and a candidate answer y, and it returns one number. We call that number the energy, written E of x and y.

The rule is easy to remember. A low energy means that x and y are compatible. A high energy means they are not. Energy is a score for how badly they fit.

Let's build a toy one, and it is illustrative. Physics predicts where this ball will land. We define the energy as the squared distance between a candidate and that prediction, times one hundred. The compatible landing gets an energy of zero. The little-short one gets one. The bit-far one gets about three. The very short one gets about six. And the ball in the trees gets sixteen.

Now picture a tray with five dents, one for each answer. The deeper the dent, the lower the energy. This is the dented tray. Look at the floor of the tray and you can read the verdict at a glance: the deepest dent is the best continuation.

Let the photo change too, and the tray changes with it. Put x on one axis and y on the other, and we get a map in contour lines, with a trench where the pairs fit. This is the landscape from chapter two, the very same drawing. The only difference is what the axes mean. They are no longer the knobs of a model. They are what we observe, and what we predict.

Now, inference. Fix the photo. Drop the marble anywhere on the tray, at any answer, even an absurd one. It rolls downhill, and it stops in the deepest dent. Finding the best answer is minimising the energy over y.

You may think: this is gradient descent, from episodes two point three and two point four. It is the same rule, but something else moves. There, we turned the knobs, the parameters, to lower the loss. Here, the knobs and the photo stay put. It is the answer y that slides. In our toy, the marble starts at nine tenths of the way along, and after ten small steps it is within about a hundredth of the bottom.

Now the key moment. Notice what we never needed. We never asked for a probability. The energy only has to rank: this answer fits better, that one fits worse. Stretch the tray. Multiply every depth by three and add one. The numbers change, but the order does not, and the marble still rolls into the same dent.

What if you do want probabilities? There is a recipe, from the physicist Gibbs. Take the exponential of minus the energy, and divide by the sum of that quantity over every possible answer. It is the softmax of episode two point five, fed with minus the energies, and the sum at the bottom is exactly its normaliser, called Z. For our five answers, Z is easy: about one point four, and the probabilities come out near seventy, twenty-six, and four percent, with the other two close to zero. But for a video, or a picture, the possible answers are astronomically many. The tutorial on energy-based learning calls this sum intractable in many practical cases. Energies skip it.

That tutorial is by LeCun, Chopra, Hadsell, Ranzato and Huang, written in two thousand six for the book Predicting Structured Data, from MIT Press. It defines the energy as a measure of compatibility, and inference as finding the answer that minimises it. In his two thousand twenty-two position paper, LeCun describes JEPA in this language: a prediction error, measured in the space of representations, is the energy. We will build that in chapter eleven.

To sum up. An energy gives each pair of observation and answer a number: low if they fit, high if they do not. Inference rolls the marble to the bottom, over y. And we only need the ranking, not a normalised probability. Next time: training an energy, by pushing the true pairs down and pulling the false ones up.

PLATE 8.26:40

Training an energy: push down, pull up

An energy landscape is learned by sculpting it like modelling clay with two gestures: push down the energy of the true answer, then either pull up the wrong ones with negatives, or limit the total volume of low-energy hollows. Contrastive methods use hooks, regularised methods use a budget jar. With only the first gesture, the clay goes flat and every answer fits everything.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the marble park. Last time we gave every pair of an observation x and an answer y a number, the energy, and we let a marble roll to the lowest point. Today's question is the one we left open. Where does that landscape come from? Nobody draws it by hand. It has to be learned, from examples. So how do you sculpt a landscape?

Here is the picture to keep in mind. The landscape is a slab of modelling clay. We cut through it along one line and look from the side. The observation x stays fixed, and the answer y runs from left to right. The top of the clay is the energy: the higher the clay, the higher the energy. Everything on this slab is a toy, and it is illustrative.

At the start, the surface is bumpy for no good reason. The true answer sits at y equal to point four, and the clay is at height one point zero there. Worse, there are three hollows in places where no true answer lives. The model finds those wrong answers compatible. Our job is to reshape the clay.

The recipe is old. The tutorial on energy-based learning, by LeCun, Chopra, Hadsell, Ranzato and Huang, was written in two thousand six for the book Predicting Structured Data. It says that learning should have the effect of pushing down on the energy of the correct answer, and pulling up on the energies of the incorrect ones. Push down, pull up. Two gestures. Our clay slab has a thumb for the first, and a hook for the second.

Start with the contrastive way. The thumb presses into the clay at the true answer, and a hollow opens there. Then we choose wrong answers, called negatives or contrastive samples, and the hook lifts the clay under each of them.

The hinge loss says how far to lift, in plain words. Take the true answer and one wrong answer. If the wrong one is already higher than the true one by at least a margin, the loss is zero, and nobody touches anything. If not, the loss is the shortfall, so the thumb presses and the hook lifts until the gap is wide enough. Our margin is point five.

Watch the toy. We hook two of the three hollows, the ones at y equal to point one five and point six two. After thirty-two rounds, the true answer has sunk from one point zero to point four two. The two hooked hollows have been lifted to point nine seven and point nine three. But look at the third hollow, at point eight eight. Nobody hooked it, so it sits at point seven two, only point three above the true answer. A hollow that no negative visited is still there.

You have already met this in chapter seven. In episode seven point two, SimCLR pulled the two views of an image together and pushed strangers apart. In our language, the twin pair is the thumb, and every other image in the batch is a hook. With a batch of eight thousand one hundred ninety-two, the SimCLR paper counts sixteen thousand three hundred eighty-two negatives for each positive pair.

That is a lot of hooks, and this is exactly where LeCun objects. In lecture notes from twenty twenty-three, written with a co-author, he says that contrastive methods work for any architecture, but scale badly with the dimension of the data, even exponentially. The need to generate contrastive samples is the source. This is his argument, not a theorem we prove here. The tutorial of two thousand six adds a nuance. Only the wrong answers that inference actually finds need to be pulled up.

Now the second way, the regularised one. Keep the thumb, and drop the hooks. Instead, put a jar beside the slab. Every bit of clay you scoop out goes into the jar, and the jar has a mark that you may not pass. In the notes, regularised methods limit the volume of space that the low-energy region can take.

Press the thumb at the true answer again. The hollow deepens, the jar rises to its mark, and now something has to give. The other hollows are refilled, little by little. In our toy, after a hundred presses, the true answer is down at point two nine. The three wrong hollows have come up from point seven zero, point six two and point seven two, to point eight six, point eight two and point eight seven. No hook touched them. The budget did it.

So the split is this. Contrastive methods push down the true pairs and pull up the false ones. Regularised methods push down the true pairs and limit the total volume of the hollows. This is how LeCun frames it: his taxonomy, not a law of nature.

Now, what if you do neither? Only the thumb, no hooks, no jar. The tutorial warns about it. Pushing down on the correct answer alone pulls up nothing, and with some architectures the solution collapses: the energy is flat and equal to zero everywhere.

Our toy shows how. Start from a clean bowl: the energy is a squared distance between two necklaces, whose size is set by a scale s. The easiest way to shrink that distance for the true pairs is to shrink everything, so the scale goes down and down, like a rolling pin over the clay. After ten steps s is point one seven. After twenty-five it is point zero one. The energy at the far edge, y equal to point zero five, has fallen from point eight one to point zero zero zero one. The slab is flat. Every answer is compatible with everything. This is the collapse of episode seven point four, seen from the side, and from above it is a map with no contour lines at all.

Now we can read chapter seven again. SimCLR is the hook school: it pulls up the negatives. VICReg is on the other side. Its variance term keeps every bead of the necklace from freezing, and its covariance term keeps one bead from copying another. Together they limit how much of the space can sit at low energy, and the same lecture notes file VICReg under regularisation.

BYOL is the careful case. It uses no negatives, and a slow-moving average of the network as its target. The same lecture notes list it among the regularisation approaches, but how it avoids collapse is an interpretation, and episode seven point five told you that the explanation was long misunderstood. So hold that reading lightly.

To sum up. Learning an energy means sculpting it with two gestures: push down the true pairs, then either pull up the false ones, or limit the total volume of the hollows. Do neither, and the landscape goes flat. Next time: latent variables and many possible futures.

PLATE 8.36:45

Latent variables and many possible futures

When one past allows two futures, a deterministic model predicts their average, which is not a future: its energy has a single hollow in the middle. A hidden lever, the latent variable z, picks the branch, and keeping the lowest energy over z gives one hollow per future. The lever must carry little information, otherwise every answer fits and the landscape goes flat.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the marble park. Last episode we learned to sculpt an energy: push the true pairs down, then pull the false ones up, or limit the volume of the hollows. Today's problem is one that sculpting alone cannot fix. What if the same past allows two different futures? By the end of this video, you will know why one prediction fails there, how a hidden switch repairs it, and what that switch must never be allowed to do.

A marble rolls along a track and reaches a fork. Left, or right? Call the photo of the marble at the fork x. Nothing in the photo tells us which way it will go. We watched many marbles, and half went left, half went right. To keep the numbers simple, a marble that goes left lands at minus one, and one that goes right lands at plus one. This is a toy, and it is illustrative.

Now ask a deterministic model for the future. One photo in, one answer out, trained with squared error, as in episode 6.5. What does it say? The average of minus one and plus one: zero. Right between the two tracks, on bare ground, where no marble has ever gone. Betting on the average costs one, on average. Betting on one branch costs two: nothing when you are right, four when you are wrong. It is the blur of episode 6.5, with two futures instead of five. The average of two futures is not a future.

In the language of episode 8.1, this model has an energy with a single hollow. Take the energy to be y squared. At minus one it is one. At plus one it is one. At zero it is zero. So the marble of inference rolls to the middle, the one place that least resembles what really happens.

Here is the repair. Beside the track stands a signal box, and in it a points lever. Pull it one way, and the rails send the marble left. Pull it the other way, and they send it right. This lever is a latent variable, and we call it z. The photo does not show where the lever is, and during training nobody tells the model either. The tutorial of 2006 puts it plainly: such hidden variables are ones whose correct value is never, or rarely, given to us. In lecture notes by Dawid and LeCun, from 2023, z captures the information in y that is not readily available in x.

The energy now takes three arguments: the photo x, the answer y, and the lever z. Our lever has two positions. With the lever on left, the energy is the squared distance to minus one. With the lever on right, it is the squared distance to plus one. At y equal to minus one, the energy is zero with the lever on left, and four with it on right. At zero, it is one either way. At plus one, it is four, and zero.

But we do not want a verdict that depends on a lever nobody sees. So inference lets the lever settle by itself. For each answer y, keep the lowest energy over z. This is the free energy, F of x and y, the minimum over z of E of x, y and z. Here, F is zero at minus one, one at zero, and zero at plus one. Two hollows, one on each branch, and a hill in the middle. Drop the marble anywhere, and it rolls into the nearer hollow, left or right. One past, two low-energy futures.

There is a softer version, also from the tutorial. Instead of the minimum, add up the contribution of every lever position: F is minus one over beta, times the log of the sum of e to the minus beta E. With beta equal to four, F is zero at both branches and about zero point eight three in the middle. The lecture notes add that summing is expensive in high dimensions, so people usually settle for the minimum.

The lever lives outside the model, so the model becomes non-deterministic. Choose z from a list, and you get a set of futures. Draw z from a distribution, as the variational autoencoder of episode 6.2 does, and you get a probability over futures. The notes say that z can play the role of a switch. Ours is one, literally.

Now the catch, and it matters as much as the idea. What if the lever has too many positions? Say it slides smoothly, and for every answer there is a position that lands exactly on it. Then the energy is zero for every y. Every answer fits. The landscape is flat. This is the collapse of episodes 7.4 and 8.2, seen from the side. The notes say it directly: the information capacity of the latent variable must be limited, otherwise training may put all the information needed for the prediction into it.

We can measure it by counting the notches of the lever. Call an answer low-energy when its free energy is below zero point zero five. With two notches, that covers thirty-five percent of the range from minus one point two to plus one point two. With three notches, fifty-four percent. With five, ninety-one. With nine notches, all of it. More notches, and more of the landscape sinks. The remedies in the notes: make z discrete, sparse, stochastic or low-dimensional. Two notches carry one bit.

Where does JEPA stand? A JEPA predicts the representation of y, not its pixels, and its predictor can take an extra variable z. The I-JEPA paper, from 2023, describes the predictor as conditioned on an additional, possibly latent, variable z. Then comes what it actually does. In I-JEPA, z is the set of position tokens that tell the predictor where the target block sits. In V-JEPA, from 2024, z carries the position of the target in space and time. These are known signals, not a hidden switch that picks among futures.

So, as of November 2026, in the two published JEPAs I checked, the lever is missing. A preprint of the sixth of November makes the same observation, and proposes a stochastic z for masked image prediction. Another, from August, replaces the predictor with a mixture, one component per future. Both are preprints, so read them as proposals, not settled results. We build the basic JEPA in episode 11.2.

To sum up. One prediction aims at the average, and the average of two futures is not a future. A latent variable z, our lever, picks the branch, and the free energy keeps the lowest energy over z, so one past keeps several hollows. But z must carry little information, or every answer fits.
