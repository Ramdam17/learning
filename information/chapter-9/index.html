Welcome to the stained glass workshop. This is chapter 9, beyond pairs.

Every measure so far looked at two variables at a time or at one target in its past. Brains, bodies, and conversations involve many variables at once. This chapter asks what can only be seen when three or more are considered together. And it starts with the simplest system where pairs see nothing at all.

Here is the intuition. In the workshop, the glazier keeps three panes of tinted glass in a drawer. Hold any one of them up to the light, a uniform haze. Hold any two of them together, still a uniform haze. Stack all three, a sharp picture appears.

The picture is not in any pane, and not in any pair of panes. It exists only in the stack of three. Any inspection that looks at panes two by two will report correctly that nothing is there.

Here is the system in bits computed exactly. Two fair coins, A and B. A third variable C is their exclusive or C is one when A and B differ, zero when they agree.

Compute the mutual information between every pair. A and B zero they are independent coins. A and C zero. Knowing A alone tells you nothing about C because B is still a fair coin. B and C zero by the same argument.

So every pairwise mutual information is exactly zero. A connectivity matrix built from pairs would be completely empty.

Yet the system is perfectly determined. Knowing A and B together tells you C exactly. The mutual information between the pair A and B and C is one full bit. The total correlation, which we meet properly in the next episode, is also one bit. All of it lives at the level of the triplet.

The co-information of chapter 4, episode 4.7 is minus one bit, the signature of synergy.

Now make the system noisy. Flip C with probability 1 in 10. The pairwise mutual information stay exactly 0. The triplet information drops to 0.53 bits. With a flip probability of 1 in 4, the triplet information is 0.19 bits, and every pair is still exactly 0. The pairs are not just weak, they are blind. This is the opposite of the usual worry. In chapter 8, pairwise measures saw too much, relays and common drivers. Here they see too little.

That is the strong point of today. A pairwise matrix can be entirely empty while the system is perfectly determined.

Does this happen in the brain? We should be careful here. Clean exclusive or relations are rare in raw signals, but partial versions are common in principle wherever a response depends on a conjunction. A neuron that fires for one stimulus feature only in a particular context. A region whose activity depends on the agreement or disagreement of two inputs. A behavioural choice that depends on two cues together. In each case, part of the dependence can only be seen at the level of three variables. Schneidman, Bialek, and Berry in 2003 gave a careful account of synergy and redundancy in neural population codes and showed that both can be present in recordings. Chapter 12 looks at what has and has not been found in real data.

What does this sensitivity to triplets depend on?

First, the measure. Pairwise correlation and pairwise mutual information are both blind here. Only measures of the joint dependence of three or more variables can see it.

Second, the estimator. Triplet measures need joint densities in more dimensions, with all the biases of chapter 5. A real synergy can be underestimated, and a spurious one can appear from bias.

Third, the number of possible triplets. With 64 channels, there are about 41,000 triplets and far more quadruplets. Episode 9.5 deals with that explosion.

And fourth, noise. As we saw, noise shrinks the triplet information smoothly, but the pairs stay at zero. There is no warning from the pairs that something was missed. A weak synergy and no synergy look identical from below.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study builds a functional connectivity matrix from pairwise correlations or pairwise mutual information, finds no significant links between three regions and concludes that the three regions do not interact during the task.

The conclusion does not follow. Pairwise measures can only rule out pairwise dependence. Whether the three regions share information jointly is a different question, which needs a different measure.

The fix is to state the conclusion at the level that was tested, no pairwise dependence was found, and, where the question matters, to compute a multivariate measure on the triplets of interest, with surrogates that destroy the joint structure while keeping each variable's distribution, for example by shuffling one variable relative to the other two. In our exclusive or, that shuffle drops the triplet information to zero, while the pairs, already at zero, do not move.

Total correlation. All the dependence in a set in one number.

Last time, three variables shared one full bit that no pair could see. We need a measure that sees all the dependence in a set of variables, whatever its form. The oldest one is total correlation.

Here is the intuition. The glazier keeps a catalogue of the workshop's panes. One way to write it is pane by pane. Describe each pane on its own as if it had nothing to do with the others. Another way is to describe the whole set at once, noting what the panes have in common.

If the panes are unrelated, the two catalogues are the same length. If they share patterns, colours that always come together, or a picture that only appears in the stack, the joint catalogue is shorter. The number of bits saved by describing the set jointly instead of pane by pane is the total correlation.

Here is the definition. The total correlation of a set of variables is the sum of their individual entropies minus their joint entropy. Satosi Watanabe introduced it in 1960 as a measure of multivariate correlation. It is also called multi-information.

Equivalently, it is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the joint distribution and the product of the marginals, how far the set is from complete independence. That is the same construction as mutual information in episode 4.1, generalised to any number of variables. For two variables, total correlation is exactly their mutual information.

So it inherits two properties. It is never negative. And it is zero if and only if all the variables are mutually independent, not just pairwise independent, completely independent.

Total correlation can also be built one variable at a time. Take the mutual information of the second variable with the first, add the mutual information of the third with the first two, and so on. Each new variable contributes what it shares with all those before it. For the exclusive or that is zero for the second coin, then one full bit for the third, the whole bit arrives with the last variable.

Three binary variables, three systems, computed exactly in bits.

First, the exclusive or of last episode. Each variable has one bit of entropy, the three together have two bits because the third is determined by the first two. Total correlation, 3 minus 2, one bit. And every pairwise mutual information is zero.

Second, a copy, C is a copy of A, and B is an independent coin. Again, each variable has one bit and the set has two bits. Total correlation one bit. But here the whole bit lives in a pair. The mutual information between A and C is one bit.

Same total correlation, completely different structure. In the copy, the dependence is a shared bit between two variables redundancy. In the exclusive or, it exists only in the triplet synergy. Total correlation adds them up and does not say which.

That is the strong point of today. Total correlation measures all the dependence in a set without saying what kind.

Third, three copies of the same coin. The set has one bit of entropy. The sum of entropies is three. Total correlation, two bits. More shared, more saved.

Now continuous Gaussian variables. For a Gaussian set, total correlation has a closed form, minus one-half the log of the determinant of the correlation matrix in nats. Like every Gaussian formula, it only sees linear dependence.

We take sets of variables that all correlate with each other at 0.3 and grow the set. Two variables 0.047 nats, 3 0.12, 8 0.68, 16 1.82, 64 9.74 nats.

Two lessons. First, total correlation grows with the number of variables, even when every pair is identical. A set of 64 channels and a set of 8 are not comparable without accounting for size.

Second, total correlation is not the sum of the pairwise mutual informations. For 64 variables, the pairwise mutual informations add up to 95 nats, 10 times the total correlation. Pairwise sums count the same shared information over and over, once for every pair that sees it. Total correlation counts it once.

What is total correlation sensitive to?

First, the number of variables as we just saw. Compare total correlations only between sets of the same size or normalise with care.

Second, the estimator. Total correlation needs the joint entropy of all the variables at once. With nearest neighbour or binned estimators, that is a density in as many dimensions as there are variables, and the bias grows fast. With a Gaussian estimator, it is a single determinant, cheap and stable, but blind to nonlinear dependence.

Third, the marginal distributions for continuous variables. Total correlation, like mutual information, does not change under invertible transformations of each variable separately. That is good news, units and rescaling do not matter. It also means that a Gaussian copula transformation, as in episode 5.9, can be used before the Gaussian formula.

And fourth, what it cannot tell. It does not say how many variables share a given piece of information, nor whether the sharing is redundant or synergistic. The next two episodes build measures that start to separate those.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study computes the total correlation of a large set of EEG channels in two conditions, finds it higher in one, and concludes that the brain is more integrated or more redundant in that condition.

But a higher total correlation could come from more redundancy, more synergy, or simply stronger pairwise correlations, for example from a shared reference, a common artefact, or volume conduction which raise every pairwise dependence at once. And if the two conditions use different numbers of channels, the comparison is meaningless.

The fix is to compare equal set sizes, to check whether the difference is explained by the pairwise correlations alone, for instance by comparing with the Gaussian value from the correlation matrix, and to look at the measures of the next episodes which split the total into finer parts.

Dual total correlation. The information shared by at least two.

Total correlation measured how far a set of variables is from independence. Today we meet its twin, which looks at the same dependence from the other side, and the comparison between the two will become a measure of its own.

Here is the intuition. A pane breaks in the workshop. Can the glazier repaint it exactly from the panes that are left?

For some sets of panes, yes. Everything on the broken pane was also somewhere in the others. For other sets, no, part of the broken pane was its own, found nowhere else and it is lost.

Do this for every pane in turn. Whatever cannot be recovered from the others is private to a single pane. Everything else in the set is shared by at least two panes. That shared part is the dual total correlation.

Here is the definition. The dual total correlation is the joint entropy of the set minus the sum over each variable of its entropy given all the others. Te Sun Han introduced it in 1978, among the non-negative measures of multivariate correlation. It is sometimes called the binding information.

Each conditional entropy of one variable given all the others is exactly the private part of that variable, what nobody else knows. Subtract all the private parts from the total, and what remains is the information that at least two variables share.

Like total correlation, it is never negative, and it is zero if and only if the variables are independent. For two variables, it equals their mutual information, so total correlation and dual total correlation coincide. They only start to differ from three variables on.

O-information and S-information

The O-information, total correlation minus dual total correlation, is positive when redundancy dominates and negative when synergy does. The S-information, their sum, measures the total load of dependence. Redundancy and synergy can cancel to a zero O-information, so both numbers should be reported, with care about noise and estimators for higher order measures.

What are O and S information sensitive to?

First, the estimator. They inherit everything from total and dual-total correlation, joint entropies in many dimensions, and conditional entropies of each variable given all others. With continuous data, the Gaussian estimator or the Gaussian copula estimator of episode 5.9 is the common choice cheap and stable, blind to nonlinear dependence.

Second, the set size. The O information of a large set is a sum over many interactions, and its sign can change as variables are added. Episode 9.5 shows how to track that, with gradients that measure each variable's contribution.

Third compensation as we just saw. A zero or a small value is not evidence of no higher order structure. Always report the S information or the total and dual total correlations alongside.

Higher-order measures in practice. Estimators, explosions, gradients, and the null you test against.

The last episodes define measures of dependence beyond pairs. Today we use them on a set of many signals the way a study would and meet three practical problems, which estimator how many multiplets and against what to test.

Here is the intuition. The glazier now has 12 panes on the stacking table, not three. Somewhere among them, a few panes form a picture only when stacked together, and a few others share the same tint. To find them, the glazier would have to try every stack of 3, 220 stacks. Every stack of 4, 495. And so on.

With 64 panes, there are 41,664 stacks of 3, more than 600,000 stacks of 4, and more than 7.5 million stacks of 5. Nobody tries them all by hand. That is the combinatorial explosion.

So practice comes down to three choices.

First, the estimator. For continuous brain signals, the usual choice is the Gaussian estimator, or the Gaussian copula estimator of episode 5.9, which first transforms each signal to a Gaussian by ranks. Both reduce every O information to a function of a correlation matrix: a few determinants computed in microseconds. That is what makes scanning tens of thousands of multiplets feasible. The price is that only dependence visible in the correlation matrix after the copula transformation is seen.

Second, which multiplets? Either choose them in advance from a hypothesis, which keeps the number of tests small, or scan systematically and correct heavily for multiple comparisons. The HOI Python toolbox implements efficient scans of multiplets of increasing size for exactly this purpose.

Third, the null hypothesis. And this is where most of the damage happens, as we are about to see.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate 12 channels, 2,000 samples each, labelled simulated. Channels 0, 1, and 2 form a synergistic triplet. Channel 2 is the sum of channels 0 and 1, plus noise. Channels 3, 4, and 5 form a redundant triplet. Each is a copy of a common hidden signal plus its own noise. The six other channels are independent noise.

We compute the Gaussian O information of all 220 triplets.

The planted triplets stand out, minus 0.52 nats for the synergistic one, plus 0.10 for the redundant one. Every other triplet is within about 1,000th of a nat of zero. That includes triplets such as channels 0, 2, and a noise channel. Two strongly correlated signals and an independent one share no higher order information at all, so their true O information is exactly 0. The whole scan takes a fraction of a second.

Now which values are significant? Here are two ways to build a null.

The first is tempting. Shuffle each channel independently in time which destroys all dependence and see how large O information gets by chance. With a maximum statistic over all triplets to correct for the 220 tests, that null declares 27 triplets significant. Without correction, 37.

But only two triplets were planted. Why so many false positives? Because shuffling everything destroys the pairwise dependences too. A triplet made of channel 0, channel 2 and a noise channel has a true O information of exactly 0, but channel 0 and channel 2 are strongly correlated, and that correlation makes the estimate fluctuate more than it ever would for three independent channels. Compared with a null of complete independence, those fluctuations look significant. The null was too easy to beat.

The second way asks the right question, how uncertain is each triplet's own estimate? We resample time points with replacement, recompute every O information 200 times, and divide each value by its bootstrap standard deviation. With a Bonferroni correction over the 220 triplets, exactly two triplets pass, the synergistic one at 28 standard deviations below 0 and the redundant one at 14 above. Even without correction, only those two.

That is the strong point of today. A null hypothesis that destroys pairwise structure tests for any dependence, not for higher-order dependence. The surrogate must keep what you are not testing.

For time series, plain bootstrap resampling ignores autocorrelation, so block bootstraps or circular shifts that keep each channel's memory, as in episode 6.3, are the safer versions.

Now the gradients. Instead of scanning multiplets, Tomas Scagliarini, Sebastiano Stramaglia and colleagues proposed in 2023 to compute the O information of the whole set and then ask how much it changes when each variable is removed. That change is the gradient of the O information with respect to that variable, the O information of the full set minus the O information of the set without it. It costs one extra determinant per variable. A negative gradient means the variable mainly brings synergy, a positive one mainly redundancy.

On our 12 channels, the O information of the whole set is minus 0.41 nats. Synergy dominates overall, partly hiding the redundant triplet, the compensation of last episode. But the gradients separate them cleanly. Channels 0, 1 and 2 each have a gradient of about minus 0.52 nats. Channels 3, 4, and 5 about plus 0.10. The 6 noise channels within 1,000th of 0.

12 numbers, 1 per channel instead of 220 triplets, and they point straight at the planted structure. Second order gradients for pairs of variables refine this further.

What does higher order analysis in practice depend on?

First, the estimator. Gaussian and copula estimators see only what the correlation matrix shows. A nonlinear synergy like the continuous exclusive or of episode 9.1 is invisible to them.

Second, the null as we just saw. Keep the pairwise structure in the surrogates and keep each signal's autocorrelation.

Third, the number of tests. Even with correct surrogates, thousands of multiplets need correction or a prior selection.

Welcome to the stained glass workshop. Today we hold two panes of colored glass up to the light, and we ask a question that pairwise measures cannot answer. When two sources tell us about the same thing, how do their messages fit together?

Here is the setting. We have a target T. It could be a stimulus, a choice, or the activity of a third brain region. And we have two sources, S1 and S2. Two electrodes, two neurons, or one brain and one heart.

Mutual information already gives us three numbers. What S1 tells about T. What S2 tells about T. And what the two sources tell about T when we read them together.

The question is how the joint message is built from the parts. And it turns out there are four different kinds of pieces.

The first piece is redundancy. Information that either source can give you on its own. Hold up two pink panes one behind the other. The light is still pink. Adding the second pane tells you nothing new.

The simplest redundant system is a copy. Flip a fair coin and send the result to both sources and to the target. Each source alone tells you the target perfectly, one bit. Together they still tell you one bit. All of it is redundant.

The second piece is unique information. Information that only one of the sources carries. One pink pane on the left, nothing matching on the right.

The simplest example, the target copies S1, and S2 is an independent coin that has nothing to do with it. S1 carries one bit, and that bit is unique to S1. S2 carries nothing.

The third piece is the most surprising. Synergy. Information that neither source carries on its own, and that appears only when you read them together. This is the riso trick. Hold a pink pane over a blue pane, and a violet appears that neither pane contains.

The classic example is exclusive or, XOR. Two independent fair coins, and the target says whether they agree or differ. Look at S1 alone, it tells you nothing about T, 0 bits. S2 alone, also 0 bits. Both together, you know T exactly 1 full bit. All of that bit is synergy.

So we have four pieces of glass. Redundancy R. Unique information from S1, U1. Unique information from S2, U2. And synergy, S.

Here is the whole idea in one image. The joint mutual information is the sum of all four pieces. R plus U1 plus U2 plus S. What S1 tells about T is its redundant part plus its unique part, R plus U1. And what S2 tells is R plus U2.

This is the partial information decomposition proposed by Paul Williams and Randall Beer in 2010.

Now count. We have three equations, one for each mutual information we can measure. But we have four unknowns. One equation is missing. Mutual information alone cannot tell us how much is redundancy and how much is synergy.

What about the conditional mutual information? What S1 tells about T once S2 is known is its unique part plus the synergy, U1 plus S. Likewise, S2 given S1 gives U2 plus S. These are real, measurable numbers. But they are not new equations. They follow from the three we already have by the chain rule. We are still one equation short.

You can see the problem with a quantity we met in chapter 4, the co-information. With two sources and one target, the co-information equals redundancy minus synergy. It is a net balance. A positive value says redundancy wins. A negative value says synergy wins. A value near zero could mean nothing is happening or that a lot of redundancy and a lot of synergy cancel each other out.

Take the AND gate. The target is one only when both sources are 1. Its co-information is about minus 0.19 bits. With the original measure of Williams and Beer, that splits into 0.31 bits of redundancy and 0.5 bits of synergy. The net hides both.

So we need one more ingredient, a definition of redundancy. Once redundancy is fixed, the other three pieces follow from the equations. Williams and Beer propose the first definition. Since then, researchers have proposed many others.

What should a good redundancy measure respect? Williams and Beer asked for three properties. Symmetry, the order of the sources should not matter. Self-redundancy, a single source is fully redundant with itself, so its redundancy is simply its mutual information with the target. And monotonicity, adding a source can only shrink what is common to all of them. Later, Harder, Salge, and Polani proposed a fourth property, called identity, about exactly the kind of system we look at next.

And here the field disagrees. Take a target that simply copies both sources, two independent coins. The target carries two bits. With the original definition, this splits into one bit of redundancy and one bit of synergy. With another popular definition known as BROJA, it is one bit unique to each source, with no redundancy and no synergy at all. Same system, two very different stories.

The intuition of the second story is easy. The two coins are independent, so how could they be redundant? The identity property makes this precise. When the target is just a copy of two independent sources, redundancy should be zero. The original measure breaks that property while BROJA respects it. This kind of example is exactly why new definitions keep appearing. We will open this zoo of measures in episodes 9.8 and 9.9.

What is a decomposition sensitive to? First, the definition of redundancy you choose. Second, what you call the target and what you call the sources. Swap their roles and you get a different decomposition. Third, everything we learned about estimation. Every piece is built from estimated mutual informations, so their errors add up, and small samples can produce pieces that look meaningful but are only noise.

Where does this matter in the brain? Imagine two electrodes and a stimulus. If their information is redundant, either electrode would have been enough. If it is unique, each one sees a different aspect. If it is synergistic, the information lives only in the joint pattern, for instance in whether the two signals agree. Pairwise measures one electrode at a time would miss that last case entirely.

And here is the classic trap. A negative co-information or a negative O-information which we met in episode 9.4 is not a measure of synergy. It is a net balance, synergy minus redundancy. In Episode 9.6, we split the information that two sources carry about a target into four pieces of stained glass. Redundancy, the unique information of each source and synergy. Today, we see the structure behind those pieces, how it grows with more sources, and why everything rests on a single choice.

Here is the intuition. The glazier draws a plan before cutting any glass. At the bottom of the plan sits the piece that every source shares, the colour all panes have in common. Above it, pieces shared by fewer sources. At the top, the piece that only appears when all sources are combined. Every piece sits above the pieces it contains. That plan is a lattice, a family tree of overlaps.

Once the glazier knows how much glass belongs to the shared region at each level of the plan, every individual piece can be cut. Take a region, subtract everything already cut below it, and what remains is the new piece. The whole decomposition follows from one ingredient, a way to measure what a group of sources has in common.

This construction is due to Paul Williams and Randall Beer in 2010. They called it partial information decomposition.

Here is how the plan is built. Each node of the lattice is a collection of groups of sources, where no group contains another. For two sources there are four such collections. Both sources separately, which stands for their redundancy. Source 1 alone, its unique information. Source 2 alone. And the two sources taken together, which stands for synergy.

For each node, a redundancy function says how much information about the target is shared by all the groups in the collection. Then a step called Möbius inversion assigns each node its own atom, its redundancy minus all the atoms below it. The atoms add up to the total mutual information between all the sources and the target.

For two sources, this is the familiar picture. The information from source 1 is redundancy plus unique 1. From source 2, redundancy plus unique 2. From both together, all four atoms. Three known quantities, four unknowns, define the redundancy and the other three follow.

Let's run the inversion once by hand with the classic AND gate from episode 9.6. Two fair coins and the target is one when both are one. Each coin alone carries about 0.31 bits about the target. And synergy is what remains of the total, 0.81 minus 0.31, which is half a bit. Change the redundancy value, and every other atom moves with it.

A zoo of redundancy measures Part 1 — I-min, Minimum Mutual Information, and BROJA.

In Episode 9.7, we saw that the whole redundancy lattice follows from one choice — how to measure what sources have in common. Today we meet the first three candidates, and we put them side by side on the same small systems.

Here is the intuition. Picture a glass auction. Three appraisers look at the same window made of two panes and a target pattern. Each appraiser must say how much of the pattern is shown by both panes at once. They do not use the same rule, so they do not give the same price. Our job is to understand each rule well enough to know when to trust it.

The first appraiser is the original one. Paul Williams and Randall Beer in 2010 proposed a measure called I-Min. For each possible value of the target, ask how much each source tells you about that particular value. That is the specific information. Take the smaller of the two because that much is certainly provided by both. Then average over the target values.

The second appraiser is even simpler. Minimum mutual information or MMI says the redundancy is the smaller of the two mutual informations. If source one carries half a bit about the target and source two carries one bit, the redundancy is half a bit. Adam Barrett studied this measure for Gaussian systems in 2015, and it became popular in neuroscience for that reason, as the next episode shows.

The third appraiser is BROJA, named after its authors Bertschinger, Rauh, Olbrich, Jost, and Ay, in 2014. Its rule starts from a different idea. Unique and redundant information should only depend on what each source can tell about the target on its own, the joint distribution of source 1 with the target and of source 2 with the target. Synergy is the only part that needs the full three-way distribution.

So BROJA looks at every distribution that keeps those two pairwise tables fixed and picks the one where the two sources together say the least about the target. The information that disappears in that choice is called synergy. The unique information of source one is what it still adds, given source two in that least synergistic world. Computing it is a convex optimisation problem.

And notice a general ordering. I-min takes the minimum inside the average, MMI takes it outside, so I-min is never larger than MMI.

What does each measure depend on and where is each sensitive?

I-min is cheap and works for any discrete system, but it confuses the amount of information with its content, as the 2-bit copy showed.

MMI is the cheapest of all, two mutual informations and a minimum. It is easy to estimate for continuous data, especially with a Gaussian model. But it forces one unique atom to zero, and it fails the identity property.

BROJA has a clear operational motivation and satisfies identity. Its atoms are non-negative for two sources, but it requires an optimisation over distributions, which grows heavy with many states, and numerical solvers can stop at poor solutions. In our own checks, two textbook systems gave answers that changed with the solver, so we left them out of this episode. Always check convergence.

And for more than two sources, there is a deeper limit. Johannes Rauh and colleagues showed in 2014 that no non-negative decomposition on the redundancy lattice can satisfy the identity property once there are three sources. Every measure must give something up.

So how do you choose? Start from the question. If you want unique information to mean what one source can tell you that the other cannot, whatever the full joint distribution, BROJA expresses that idea. So far, every decomposition in this chapter had one target. Two sources, one target, four atoms. But a brain is not a set of sources pointing at a target. It is a system whose present turns into its future. Today we fold the stained glass in two and decompose what the whole past says about the whole future.

Here is the intuition. Take the four-pane window from episode 9.6 and imagine it hinged down the middle like a folding screen. On the left leaf the past, two regions X1 and X2 at time t. On the right leaf the future, the same two regions Y1 and Y2 one step later. Information flows across the hinge. Some of it stays in the same region. Some crosses to the other region. Some is shared by both regions on one side and split on the other. A single target cannot describe all of that because the future has two parts, and each part can receive information in its own way.

Why is the ordinary decomposition not enough? Take two binary regions and two toy systems. In the first, each region simply keeps its own bit, Y1 equals X1 and Y2 equals X2. Call it copy. In the second, the regions exchange their bits, Y1 equals X2 and Y2 equals X1. Call it swap. Copy is pure storage. Swap is pure transfer. They are opposite dynamics.

Now run a partial information decomposition with the two past regions as sources and the whole future as the target. In both systems, each source carries one bit about the future, and together they carry two bits. The decomposition is identical for copy and for swap for every standard redundancy measure. Seen as a single target, the two futures differ only in how their four values are labelled, and nothing records where each bit lands. The difference between storage and transfer lives in how the information is distributed among the future parts, and a single target throws that away.

The fix is to decompose both sides at once. This is integrated information decomposition, written ΦID, introduced by Pedro Mediano, Fernando Rosas and colleagues, and published in its full form in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2025.

Here is how the folded lattice is built. On the past side, the familiar four-node lattice, redundancy of the two regions, unique to region 1, unique to region 2, and synergy. On the future side, the same four nodes. Each atom of the new decomposition is a pair, a node on the past side and a node on the future side. 4 times 4, 16 atoms. Mathematically, the double redundancy lattice is the product of the two four-node lattices, ordered side by side.

Read an atom as a journey across the hinge. Unique 1 to unique 1, information that was only in region 1 and is still only in region 1. That is storage. Unique 1 to unique 2, information that leaves region 1 and arrives only in region 2. That is transfer. Redundancy to redundancy, information that both regions shared and both still share. Synergy to synergy, information that only the pair carried and only the pair still carries. And so on for all 16 pairs.

And the atoms add up to one familiar number. The time delayed mutual information, the information that the whole past carries about the whole future.

How do we compute the atoms? Exactly as in episode 9.7, with one extra ingredient. Many of the 16 cumulative values are ordinary quantities, mutual informations between groups of past and future variables, and redundancies from ordinary decompositions with one target, forward in time or backward in time. But one corner is new, the double redundancy, the information that is redundant in the past and also redundant in the future. That needs its own definition. Once it is chosen, Möbius inversion on the 16-node lattice gives every atom.

Mediano and colleagues proposed two such definitions, extending common change in surprisal and minimum mutual information. The minimum mutual information version is the simplest. The double redundancy is the smallest of the four mutual informations between one past region and one future region.

That is the strong point of today. A partial information decomposition only decomposes information about one target, while integrated information decomposition decomposes what the whole past says about the whole future. So storage and transfer, invisible to a single target, become separate atoms.

How is it computed in practice? The most common recipe in brain applications goes like this.

That is the strong point of today. Under the minimum mutual information double redundancy, persistent synergy tracks each region's own autocorrelation, not only interaction. Thomas Varley described this limitation in 2024. The measure cannot distinguish truly integrated systems from disintegrated ones with first-order autocorrelation, and some systems even become more synergistic when they are disintegrated.

Why does it happen? It is the identity problem of episode 9.8, folded in two. Each past region carries information about the joint future. Minimum mutual information calls that redundancy, then has to compensate elsewhere, and the bookkeeping pushes the whole time-delayed mutual information into the synergy corner.

What does this mean for brain maps? Signals with strong autocorrelation, like slow BOLD signals, are exactly where this effect is largest. A region with a slower or smoother signal can score higher on persistent synergy for that reason alone. This does not show that the published findings are wrong. It shows that a synergy map on its own cannot be read as a map of integration without controls.

So what can the decomposition say and what can it not?

It can describe how information is distributed between regions over time under a given redundancy definition and compare those distributions across regions, conditions, or species, as long as the comparison controls for the factors that drive the atoms.

It cannot, on its own, show a mechanism, identify causal influence, or measure integration independently of the redundancy definition. The choice of redundancy still matters here, as it did for ordinary decompositions.

What does it depend on? The redundancy definition above all, then the autocorrelation and sampling rate of the signals, the one-step Markov assumption, the Gaussian model, and the parcellation that defines the regions. In the summary, averaging or ranking over thousands of pairs can turn a small bias shared by every pair into a clean-looking map.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study finds higher persistent synergy in one group of regions or one group of participants and concludes that they integrate more information. But the groups also differ in signal autocorrelation, for example because of different haemodynamics, motion, or filtering. The synergy difference may come from the autocorrelation alone.

The fix, build a null model for each pair such as two independent autoregressive processes fitted to each region's own autocorrelation, and report synergy in excess of that null. Report the double redundancy definition, show the full set of atoms including negative ones, and where possible, check the conclusion with another definition, such as the common change in surprisal version.

The standard recipe uses a Gaussian model, the minimum mutual information double redundancy, one step of the past, and the persistent redundancy and synergy atoms.

Two regions that never interact get persistent synergy equal to their whole time delayed mutual information, 2.4 bits at memory 0.9, about four times more than a strongly coupled pair.

Persistent synergy under this recipe tracks autocorrelation as well as interaction, as Varley pointed out.

The decomposition describes distributions of information, it does not by itself prove integration. The redundancy choice still matters.

Synergy in the brain — What we know and what we don't.

This chapter built the tools, total correlation, O information, partial and integrated information decompositions. Today we look at what they have found in brain data, which confusions can produce a finding without any real synergy, and how to sort solid results from results that still need confirmation.

Here is the intuition. A museum of stained glass has two display cases. In the first, pieces whose origin has been checked several ways. In the second, beautiful pieces still waiting for their certificate. Both cases are worth visiting. The mistake is to mix up the labels.

First, a short tour of what has been reported.

Ageing. Marilyn Gatica, Fernando Rosas, Pedro Mediano, and colleagues applied the O information to resting state functional MRI from 164 healthy people aged 10 to 80 in Brain Connectivity in 2021. Older participants from 60 to 80 showed a higher predominance of redundant interdependencies than younger participants at every order of interaction they studied with a redundancy core in prefrontal and motor cortices. A follow-up whole brain model by the same group in 2022, reproduced the age differences from changes in the structural connectome.

Brain organisation. Andrea Luppi and colleagues in 2022 used integrated information decomposition on functional MRI. As in episode 9.13, redundancy in sensory and motor regions, synergy in association regions, and convergent evidence from evolution, positron emission tomography and genetics.

Consciousness. Luppi and colleagues reported in eLife in 2024, a synergistic workspace in the brain, whose synergy is disrupted when consciousness is lost through anaesthesia or brain injury.

Neurodegeneration. Rubén Herzog, Fernando Rosas, Agustín Ibáñez and colleagues, in Neurobiology of Disease in 2022, used high-order interactions in resting EEG and functional MRI to characterise Alzheimer's disease and behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia, with large effect sizes and classification accuracy comparable with standard biomarkers.

That is a rich picture. Now the confusions.

Now the calculation in one picture.

Confusion 1 correlated noise. Take a triplet that is truly synergistic, two independent Gaussian sources and a third channel that is their sum plus noise. Its O information is minus 0.21 bits, synergy dominated as expected. Now add the same noise to all three channels as volume conduction or a common reference would in EEG. With common noise of standard deviation 0.5, the O information is almost zero. With 0.75, it turns positive, plus 0.09 bits redundancy dominated. With 1, plus 0.21, the same synergistic system now looks redundant. Shared noise pushes every measure toward redundancy.

In EEG, every electrode sees a blurred mixture of sources, and the reference adds the same signal to every channel. Both are textbook ways to inject shared noise. Source reconstruction, a careful choice of reference, or measures that discount zero lag dependence reduce the problem, as chapter 8 discussed for transfer entropy. They do not remove it entirely.

Confusion 2. The Estimator. As episode 9.13 showed, the minimum mutual information version of the integrated decomposition gives persistent synergy that tracks each signal's autocorrelation, even with no interaction. Gaussian estimators also miss nonlinear synergy, like the continuous exclusive OR of episode 9.1. A synergy value is always a value under an estimator.

Confusion 3. The number of variables. Take groups of channels that all share the same common source, with the same pairwise correlation of one-third. Three channels, O information 0.04 bits, 6, 0.33, 12, 1.34. Even divided by the number of channels, it keeps growing. Comparing O information between groups of different sizes or between parcellations with different numbers of regions compares different things.

So which results belong in which display case?

In the first case, converging evidence. Older adults showing more redundant interdependencies in resting functional MRI reported across all orders of interaction and reproduced in a structural model. Sensory and motor regions dominated by redundancy and association regions by synergy, supported by several independent kinds of data in the same study, and loss of consciousness disrupting synergy reported across anaesthesia and brain injury.

In the second case results to confirm, most findings so far come from a small number of closely connected groups and independent replications with different estimators and redundancy definitions are still rare. Synergy maps computed with the minimum mutual information version need autocorrelation matched nulls. EEG results need controls for volume conduction and reference, and claims that compare synergy across species, ages, or patient groups need matched numbers of variables and matched signal properties.

What would move a result from the second case to the first, an independent group with its own data reproducing it, a second estimator or a second redundancy definition giving the same direction of effect, and a null model that keeps everything except the structure being tested, showing that the effect is not produced by autocorrelation, shared noise, or group size. None of these is exotic. They are the same checks we asked of transfer entropy in Chapter 8.

That is the strong point of today. A two-column table with converging results on one side and results to confirm on the other is more honest than a single list of discoveries.

What do brain synergy results depend on? The measure and its estimator, shared noise from volume conduction, reference or global signals, autocorrelation and filtering, the number and definition of regions, and the null model used to decide what counts as significant.

Now the classic pitfall.

A paper reports that a patient group has less synergy and more redundancy than controls. But the patients moved more, and motion adds shared signal across channels. As our triplet showed, shared signal alone turns synergy into redundancy.

The fix, match, or regress the shared components first, compare groups with the same number of variables, use nulls that keep each signal's autocorrelation and the pairwise structure, and check the result under a second estimator.
