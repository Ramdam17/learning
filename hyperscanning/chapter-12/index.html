Robustness I · A hyperscanning study that can fail

A hyperscanning study that can fail fixes seven things before the data: hypothesis, measure, regions, controls, number of dyads, analysis and the result that would refute the idea. In a null simulation, one pre-chosen analysis gives 3.4 percent false positives, while freedom to pick among 24 gives 70.6 percent. The three questions asked before recording become the skeleton of a preregistration.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A ship is never built first and tested afterwards. Before the hull exists, you draw it, and you test a small model in a basin where you control the waves.

Today we are in that basin. The ship is a hyperscanning study. And the question is simple. Can your study fail?

A study that cannot fail teaches nothing. If every result can be explained afterwards, no result ever counts against you. So here is the drawing, made before the water. Seven things are fixed in advance.

The hypothesis. The main measure. The regions of interest. The controls. The number of dyads. The analysis. And the result that would refute the hypothesis.

Why fix them? Because a hyperscanning dataset offers many roads. Several measures. Several frequency bands. Several region pairs. Each choice looks reasonable, and each is another chance to find something. In 2011, Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed that this kind of flexibility, in collecting, analysing and reporting data, dramatically increases the real rate of false positives.

Here is a simulation. Twenty dyads, two conditions, and no coupling at all. Every person is independent noise. We give the analyst twenty-four roads: two measures, four frequency bands, and three region pairs. Each road is a paired test between the two conditions. These data are simulated.

In one null study, two roads fall below point zero five. Over a thousand null studies, a single road fixed in advance gave a false positive three point four percent of the time. Free to pick any of the twenty-four, the analyst found something seventy point six percent of the time. Same noise. Only the freedom changed.

Preregistration closes that garden. Nosek and colleagues wrote in 2018 that it distinguishes analyses that result from predictions from those that result from postdictions. A plan written before the data is a test. A choice made after seeing the data is a hypothesis for the next study. Some journals go further. In 2013, Chambers described Registered Reports at the journal Cortex, where a substantial part of the submission is reviewed before the data are collected.

Now, the moment that ties the series together. In episode 2.6, we asked three questions before recording. What do the two people share besides each other? Did I record it? And what comparison would remove the interaction, and keep everything else? Those three questions are the skeleton of a preregistration.

The first lists the rival explanations in advance: the stimulus, the rhythm, the body. The second turns each one into a recording you promise to make. The third becomes the control: pseudo-pairs, a subtraction of the evoked response, a regression of the behaviour you recorded. Write each one as a decision, with the exact analysis attached.

Then comes the line that makes the study able to fail. A waterline, drawn in advance. Write it as a sentence. If the real dyads do not exceed the pseudo-pairs on the main measure, in this band and these regions, the hypothesis is refuted. If the synchrony disappears once the recorded behaviour is removed, the hypothesis is not supported.

A hyperscanning study that can fail is a study where you know, in advance, which result would refute the hypothesis.

Two numbers must also be on the drawing. How many dyads: that is power, in episode 8.4. And how the analysis avoids using the data twice: that is double dipping, in episode 8.3. Choose both before you record.

Two honest limits. A preregistration does not make a weak design strong. It only makes the weakness visible. And plans meet reality, so when you must deviate, report the deviation and say why. Whatever you find outside the plan is still welcome, if it is labelled exploratory.

Seven things fixed in advance. One waterline. Then the model can go in the water.

Robustness II · Report it so others can redo it

A hyperscanning report needs ten lines so another team can rebuild the study, among them the clock, preprocessing, measure, null, number of dyads and code. In a simulation with only a shared metronome, a team missing the epoch length and the null gets a different value and wrongly reads synchrony, while the pseudo-pair comparison shows nothing. The sheet is the series' own synthesis.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Morning at the test basin. A ship model floats on the water, and CHECK is holding a clipboard. Two teams will work from this model today, and one of them will fail. A result that nobody can rebuild is a toy, not a result.

Last time, we wrote down what would refute the idea, before the data. Now the study is done. How do we write it up, so that another team can redo it?

Here is the technical sheet of the model. Every line is something a builder needs, to make the same hull. CHECK ticks them one by one. For a hyperscanning study, there are ten lines.

One. The clock. How the two recordings were synchronized, and the latency that was measured, not assumed. That is episode four point two.

Two. The hardware and the montage, for each person. Episodes four point three to four point five.

Three. The preprocessing, identical for both people. Episode five point one.

Four. The measure, with its parameters: the frequency band, and the length of the epochs.

Five. The null. Pseudo-pairs or surrogates, and which kind exactly. Episodes seven point five and seven point six.

Six. The number of dyads, because the dyad is the unit.

Seven. The statistics: the model, the correction, the threshold.

Eight. Which sources of synchrony the design excludes, and which remain. That is the compass of episode two point six.

Nine, the code. And ten, the data, in a standard layout such as the Brain Imaging Data Structure.

Now the second team. They hold the same recordings and the same sheet, except that two lines were torn off. The epoch length, and the null.

The recordings are twenty simulated dyads, forty seconds each. There is no coupling at all, only a metronome that everybody follows. The first team cuts them into epochs of four seconds, and gets a phase locking value of zero point eight three. The second team does not know the epoch length. They pick one second, and get zero point nine one. Same data, a different number.

Is that just noise? No. On simulated pairs with no coupling and no metronome, the same pipeline gives zero point eight two with one second epochs, and zero point four one with four second epochs. Short epochs inflate the value. Zimmermann and colleagues, in Imaging Neuroscience in 2024, warn of exactly this in hyperscanning EEG, and suggest at least three seconds for alpha.

Then the null. The second team does not know which null was used, so they read their number against zero. Zero point eight three is far from zero. Synchrony! The first team compares it with pseudo-pairs, where each person is matched with a stranger. The pseudo-pairs also reach zero point eight three. The metronome is shared by everybody, so the number says nothing about these two brains in particular. The missing line changed the conclusion.

So which lines belong on the sheet? Others have written such lists, one modality at a time. For EEG and MEG, Keil and colleagues, in Psychophysiology in 2014, give a checklist of the key information to include. The committee of the Organization for Human Brain Mapping, Pernet and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience in 2020, adds recommendations on acquisition, analysis, reporting, and sharing of data and code. For fNIRS, Yücel and colleagues, in Neurophotonics in 2021, provide a checklist for authors and reviewers. For MRI, Nichols and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience in 2017, set out best practices for analysis and sharing. And for the files themselves, there is the Brain Imaging Data Structure, by Gorgolewski and colleagues, in Scientific Data in 2016.

Hyperscanning itself has methods papers. Czeszumski and colleagues, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience in 2020, review twenty years of methods and analyses. Zamm and colleagues, in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience in 2024, give a practical guide for EEG. Holroyd, in Trends in Neurosciences in 2022, argues that the flexibility of tasks and methods undermines the field, and recommends measures to address it.

But we did not find a published checklist written for hyperscanning itself, as of November 2026. So the sheet you just saw is this series' own synthesis. It is not a standard. It takes what the modality lists and the methods papers raise, and adds one line for each trap of this series.

Use it as a test. Hand your sheet to someone who was not in the room. If they can rebuild your model, the report is complete. Wherever they have to guess, a line is missing.

Next time: simulations with known truth. Episode twelve point three, DyadSim.

PLATE 12.35:45

Simulations with known truth · DyadSim

A simulator turns the question around: choose the truth first, then ask the pipeline. Two noisy oscillators with dials for coupling, shared stimulus, rhythm, breathing and noise test a band-pass, phase locking and pseudo-pair pipeline. It finds strong coupling and most weak coupling, while plain correlation fails when the null lacks the shared input, and passing only shows the pipeline recovers these models.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the test basin. Before anyone builds a ship, they build a model, and they launch it in water where the waves are chosen. The shape of the model is known. So when it sinks, they know why.

Hyperscanning has no such basin. In real data, nobody knows which dyads are truly coupled. A significant result could be coupling, a shared stimulus, a shared rhythm, speech, breathing, or luck. A simulator turns this around. We choose the truth first, and ask the pipeline afterwards.

That is the idea behind Dyad Sim, the simulator of this series. It builds on an idea from the Information series. Two noisy oscillators stand for the two people, and every ingredient is a dial. The strength of the coupling, with an optional delay, and an n to m ratio. A shared stimulus. A shared rhythm. A shared breathing component. And noise. Episode eleven point one showed the coupling dial and the Arnold tongue, so we will not redo it here.

Two things the simulator does not have. Its coupling is mutual: both oscillators pull on each other with the same strength, so it cannot say who leads. And it has no speech. For this episode only, we add both in a separate script: one-way coupling, and a burst of speech artefact shared by the two signals.

Testing a pipeline on simulated truth is not new. Haufe and Ewald built a public benchmark for EEG connectivity, where simulated alpha sources either interact in one direction or do not interact, and a method must say whether they interact and which way. Burgess used simulated data, and pseudo-pairs of real recordings, to show that common hyperscanning measures can find connections where none exist. Our version is smaller, and made for the pipelines of this series.

Here is the pipeline under test. Band-pass from eight to twelve hertz. Hilbert phase. Phase locking value. Then we compare each real dyad with the pseudo-pairs, every A paired with every B who was not their partner. A dyad is flagged when it beats ninety-five percent of the pseudo-pairs. The same steps, for every dyad.

The grid has three couplings: none, weak and strong. Each is run with the shared stimulus off, then on. Forty simulated dyads per cell, twenty seconds each, and a fixed seed. That gives fifteen hundred and sixty pseudo-pairs per cell.

Strong coupling first. Forty dyads out of forty are found, with or without the stimulus. Weak coupling: twenty-nine of forty are found without the stimulus, and thirty-four with it. The misses, eleven and six, show the power of this pipeline for an effect of this size. With no coupling at all, two dyads in forty are flagged without the stimulus, and five with it. The threshold promises about one in twenty, so five is a little high. We did not test whether that is chance or a small leak.

Across the four coupled cells, that is one hundred and forty-three hits out of one hundred and sixty. The seventeen misses are all weak couplings. Across the two uncoupled cells, seven false alarms out of eighty.

Now the trap. Switch the measure to a plain correlation of the raw signals. In dyads that share only the stimulus, the correlation is zero point five four. It looks like synchrony. Take a threshold from dyads without any stimulus, and all forty dyads are flagged. But the pseudo-pairs share the same stimulus, so their correlation is also zero point five four. Against that null, four dyads in forty are flagged. Same data. Only the null changed, because it now contains the shared input.

The models also show something we did not plan. Correlation barely sees weak coupling: one dyad in forty without the stimulus, three with it. The phase locking value finds twenty-nine and thirty-four. A blind spot only appears when the truth is known. And with strong coupling, strangers look alike. Locked dyads tick at one common frequency, so even pseudo-pairs reach about zero point six. The real dyads, at zero point nine six, still clear that bar.

Next, the speech burst. Its timing differs from dyad to dyad, so the pseudo-pairs do not share it. On raw correlation, all forty uncoupled dyads are flagged. With the band-pass and the phase locking value, three are. The filter helped here, but that is one burst shape and one size, both chosen by us.

Finally, direction. Here A drives B, with strength three and a delay of a tenth of a second. The index is a Granger measure: how much A's past improves the prediction of B, minus the reverse. We compare it with the same pseudo-pair interval. When A drives B, thirty-three dyads are called A to B, none are called B to A, and seven stay undetermined. When B drives A, twenty-nine are called correctly, none are reversed, and eleven stay undetermined. With no coupling, thirty-seven are undetermined and three get a false direction. That is the easy case, made of oscillators with a known delay. Real signals will be harder.

What does a pass mean? The pipeline recovers these models. It does not prove that real brains look like them. But a pipeline that fails here cannot be trusted on real data. So run the bench before the data arrives, change one ingredient at a time, and write down hits, misses and false alarms. Later episodes of the series use the same bench.

Let's recap. Choose the truth first. Change one ingredient at a time. Count hits, misses and false alarms. Build a null that contains the shared input.

Open hyperscanning datasets

Open hyperscanning datasets form a shelf: fMRI, EEG with groups of three, fNIRS and physiology, each checked on its repository page and dated. A dataset is used to test a pipeline on real artefacts, estimate effect sizes as a range, and build pseudo-pairs from different dyads. Licences such as CC0 and CC BY should be read on the repository page.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the test basin. Before anyone builds a ship, they study models. And a good yard does not build every model alone. It borrows the models of other yards, each one with its data sheet.

Hyperscanning has a shelf like that now. Open datasets, where two or more people were recorded at the same time, and the files are free to download. This episode walks along it. Datasets come and go, so every fact on screen is dated: as of November 2026.

First, fMRI. Wang and colleagues, in Scientific Data in 2025. Thirty-three dyads played a sender and receiver game, in cooperation and in competition. The two scanners stood three hundred and five kilometres apart. The data sit on OpenNeuro, with the licence CC0, in the BIDS format.

Next, EEG. Kim, Jun and Nam, in Data in Brief in 2026. Here the group is three people, not two: eleven groups of three, playing an iterated prisoner's dilemma. This one is also on OpenNeuro, also CC0, also BIDS. Three heads still give you three pairs.

Then fNIRS. Chen and colleagues, in Scientific Data in 2025. Forty-seven female dyads, friends or strangers, looking at pictures together, with or without holding hands. The data are on OSF, with the licence CC BY. The files are in the native format of the recorder, not BIDS, but MNE can read them.

And physiology. Chung and colleagues, in Royal Society Open Science in 2024. Sixty-one dyads of strangers watched videos side by side, while heart, breathing and skin conductance were recorded. The data are on Dryad, CC0, as one large MATLAB file and some tables. This one is the data of a study, not a data paper.

That is one shelf per modality, and no more. We did not find a bigger list that we could check ourselves, so we show only what we checked on the repository pages.

A word on the licence. CC0 means the authors give up their rights: you may reuse the data for anything, and you owe no credit, though citing is good practice. CC BY lets you reuse, share and adapt the data, as long as you credit the authors and say what you changed. This is not legal advice. Read the licence on the repository page, not only in the article. For one parent and child fNIRS dataset, the article says non commercial use, while the repository page says CC BY.

Now the key moment. What can you do with an open dataset before you launch your own study?

First, test your pipeline. In twelve point three, Dyad Sim gave us dyads with a known truth. Real data gives you no truth, but it gives you what the simulator cannot: real artefacts, real timing, and real files. If your code cannot read the data and produce a clean result from beginning to end, you learn it now, and not after a year of recording.

Second, estimate effect sizes. Run your measure on every dyad, and look at the spread. That spread feeds the power analysis of episode eight point four. One caution: an estimate from thirty-three dyads is itself uncertain, so treat it as a range, not a number.

Third, build pseudo-pairs. Take the A person of one dyad, and the B person of another. With thirty-three dyads, that makes one thousand and fifty-six pairs who never met. They share the same task and the same recording set-up, so they are the natural control of episode seven point five.

Getting a dataset is short work. Two lines of Python download an OpenNeuro dataset, and three more read one recording.

The real data shown in this series come only from datasets like these, so you can rerun every figure yourself.

To recap. Open datasets are models borrowed from other yards. Check the modality, the paradigm, the number of dyads, the licence and the format on the repository page. Use them to test the pipeline, to size the effect, and to build the pseudo-pairs.

Reading a hyperscanning paper

A fictional abstract is read against a grid of eight questions, one per chapter: ruled-out source, clock, preprocessing, measure, null band, number of dyads, link to behaviour and body. Two buoys light and six stay dark. Reading the verbs matters, since show and couple are claims that must be earned, and a dark buoy is a question for the methods section, not a verdict.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A bottle drifts into the test basin. Inside is a rolled note: the abstract of a hyperscanning paper. It is fictional, written for this episode, and it has gaps on purpose. Papers like it will cross your desk. How do you read one?

In episodes twelve point one and twelve point two, we stood on the author's side. What to fix before the data, and what to write down afterwards. Today we stand on the reader's side. You hold a finished paper, and you decide how far to trust it.

For that, the series has a reading grid. Eight buoys mark the channel, one for each chapter that taught the question. A buoy lights up when the abstract answers it. When the answer is missing or vague, the buoy stays dark. The fNIRS series gave a first version of this grid in episode seven point five. This one holds for every modality.

One. Which source of synchrony is ruled out? That is chapter two, where does synchrony come from. Two. Which clock tied the two recordings together? Chapter four, recording together. Three. Was the preprocessing the same for both people? Chapter five, cleaning two brains. Four. Which measure, and why that one? Chapter six, measuring inter-brain coupling. Five. Which null band? Chapter seven, the traps of synchrony. Six. How many dyads, and was the number planned? Chapter eight, statistics for dyads. Seven. What link to behaviour, and in which words? Chapter nine, synchrony and behaviour. Eight. And the body? Chapter ten, beyond the brain.

This grid is the series' own synthesis. It is not a published standard. It is a reading aid, built from the chapters you have seen.

Now, the abstract. Its title is: brain-to-brain coupling during cooperative drawing.

Sentence one. Eighteen dyads of adults drew together while EEG was recorded from both partners. The number is there. But eighteen is small, and nothing says it was fixed in advance. Buoy six stays dark. Episode eight point four is the place to look.

Sentence two. The same preprocessing pipeline was applied to both partners. Buoy three lights up. One sentence, and the question is answered. The parameters will be in the methods, but the principle of chapter five is stated.

Sentence three. We computed the phase locking value in the alpha band. Buoy four lights up. The measure and the band are named. That does not make it the best choice, but it makes it checkable.

Sentence four. Coupling was stronger during joint drawing than during solo drawing. Joint and solo differ in many ways. The partners may share a canvas, a rhythm, a room. The abstract does not say, so none of the four routes is ruled out. Buoy one stays dark.

Sentence five. The difference was significant, p below zero point zero five, corrected. Compared with what? A joint versus solo contrast is not a null band. Burgess showed in 2013, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, that people who never interacted can still show spurious synchrony. A null band built from pseudo-pairs tells you what strangers doing the same task would reach. None is mentioned here. Buoy five stays dark.

Sentence six. Coupling correlated with a questionnaire of social closeness, r equals zero point five two. Is that a lot? Here is a simulation of twenty thousand studies with eighteen dyads, where the true link is exactly zero. One questionnaire reaches zero point five two, in either direction, in two point six percent of them. Now suppose the team tried ten questionnaires, and kept the best. Then twenty-four percent of the studies reach it. The abstract does not say how many were tried. Buoy seven stays dark.

Sentence seven. These results show that cooperating brains couple. Look at the verbs. Show promises more than a correlation can give. Couple names a mechanism, when the data give a synchrony value. Episode one point three kept the words apart: synchrony is a measure, coupling is a mechanism. And Hamilton, in Neuron in 2021, asked for behaviour and models of mutual prediction next to synchrony. Buoy seven, and its words, stay dark.

Two buoys never had a sentence at all. Which clock? Nothing says how the two recordings were aligned, and for phase, a few milliseconds matter. That is chapter four. And the body? Nothing about heartbeat or breathing, which can move both recordings together. That is chapter ten. Often these live in the methods section, not the abstract. That is where you look next.

Count the buoys. Two lit, six dark. That does not make the paper wrong. A dark buoy is a question, not a verdict. It tells you what to look for in the methods, the supplement and the preregistration, and what to hold loosely until you find it.

Let's recap. Eight questions, one for each chapter. A lit buoy is a question answered on the page. A dark buoy is a question for the methods. And read the verbs: show and couple are claims that must be earned.

Replication and meta-analysis

A meta-analysis pools studies with weights, but missing studies bias the result: in simulation, keeping only significant studies lifts the pooled estimate from 0.32 to about 0.52. Published pools for fNIRS, EEG and fMRI show interaction effects with large heterogeneity. Replications are rare and mostly inside original papers, and the closing map separates what is known, suspected and unknown.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A long test basin, with a wave paddle at one end. In the water floats a small model of a ship. Its shape is known. Today the same model goes into many basins. This is the last episode of the main series, and the question is simple. When other labs test the same idea, what comes back?

Every figure here is a published result, as of November 2026. They will move. Check the date before you quote them.

First, how do we gather the answers of many basins? A meta-analysis pools them. Each study gets a weight, and a study with many dyads weighs more than one with few. This forest plot is illustrative. It shows twelve simulated studies. Each square is one study, and its line is a confidence interval. The diamond is the pooled estimate: about 0.32 in this simulation.

The squares do not agree perfectly. A random-effects model allows each basin its own true value, and measures the spread between them. That spread is heterogeneity. Here, about half of the variation is more than chance would give.

Now a trap. Suppose only the six studies whose own interval excludes zero come back from the basins. The other six stay in the drawer. The pooled estimate climbs from 0.32 to about 0.52. Nothing was faked. Studies were only missing. That is publication bias.

What exists for hyperscanning? For fNIRS and cooperation, Czeszumski and colleagues pooled thirteen studies in 2022, in eNeuro. They report a Hedges g of 1.98, with a confidence interval from 1.47 to 2.49. They also report that the studies disagree far more than chance allows: I squared of 98.6. Their funnel plot was asymmetric, and they list a relatively low number of studies as a limit.

For teaching and learning, Zhang and colleagues, also in 2022, pooled sixteen studies, twenty-three samples. They found a correlation of 0.444 between synchrony and learning outcomes, with an interval from 0.34 to 0.54. Their heterogeneity was lower, and Egger's test was not significant.

In 2023, Lotter and colleagues pooled twenty-two fMRI and sixty-nine fNIRS experiments. The right temporo-parietal junction and the left ventral prefrontal cortex stood out.

For EEG, Jiang and colleagues, in 2026, included fifty-four studies. In the core analysis, fourteen studies gave a Hedges g of 0.887, with an interval from 0.533 to 1.241. Six studies linking synchrony to outcomes gave a Fisher z of 0.456, with an interval from 0.288 to 0.621. The authors ask us to consider task design, shared stimulation and behavioural synchrony.

These numbers cannot be lined up. The measures differ, the comparisons differ, the modalities differ. And a pool is only as clean as the studies inside it. Hakim and colleagues, in 2023, counted twenty-seven different methods for inter-brain coupling in two hundred and fifteen studies. Zimmermann and colleagues, in 2024, showed that epochs of one second or less can inflate synchrony when there is no strong interaction.

What about replications? We looked for them. Bilek and colleagues, in 2015, used fMRI in a joint attention task. They report information flow between the temporo-parietal junctions of sender and receiver, and they replicate it in an independent sample. Schwartz and colleagues, in 2025, tested mothers and adolescents with EEG, in a sample of one hundred. Their second study repeated the paradigm with a sample of forty-four unacquainted adults, and found beta synchrony between one and one and a half seconds after the stimulus.

Both replications sit inside the original paper, by the same team. We also found one registered report, by Hinvest and Taylor, in 2026. It is a protocol, and its results are not in it. In our search, we did not find a direct replication across several labs. That is what we found, not a proof that none exists.

So here is a map. It is the series' own synthesis, not a published result. Each line points back to a chapter.

What we know with confidence. Synchrony can appear without any interaction, because a shared input is enough: chapters two and seven. A number needs its null band, and the dyad is the unit: chapters seven and eight. And the method moves the number: chapter six.

What we suspect. Interaction conditions show more synchrony than control conditions, in fNIRS and in EEG, but the studies disagree widely. The right temporo-parietal junction and the prefrontal cortex come back often. And synchrony goes with outcomes, such as learning and teamwork: chapter nine.

What we do not know yet. Which of the four sources, stimulus, rhythm and movement, mutual prediction or body coupling, lies behind a pooled effect. Whether a result survives an independent lab with a fixed pipeline. And whether synchrony causes the outcome, or only goes with it.

Let's recap the series. Ask where synchrony comes from. Measure it against its null. Count dyads, not people. Read behaviour with care. Design a study that can fail. Report it, simulate it, replicate it. Two islands, one bridge: a claim crosses only if it can be checked.
