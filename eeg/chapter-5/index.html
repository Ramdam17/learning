From question to EEG measure

Experiment design starts from the question, as a cook picks the dish before the ingredients. Most questions are about when, rhythm, interaction or state, pointing to ERPs, spectra and time frequency, connectivity, or resting measures, each with its own needs for trials and recording length. Naming electrodes, band, window and comparison before recording makes results believable and keeps exploration labelled as such.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter five. We have learned where the EEG comes from, how to transform it, and what its rhythms look like. Now we step back to the moment before any electrode touches a head: designing the experiment. Everything decided here, no amount of processing can fix later.

Think of cooking. You do not walk into the kitchen, open every cupboard, and then decide what dinner will be. You choose the dish first. The dish tells you which ingredients to buy, which pan to use, and how long to cook. In an EEG study, the question is the dish, and the measure is the recipe.

So the first step is to say exactly what your question is about. Most EEG questions fall into four families.

The first family is about when. When does the brain register a sound, notice an error, or recognise a face? That points to event related potentials: averaged responses locked to an event, measured as amplitudes and latencies, which chapter eight covers.

The second family is about rhythm. Does attention change alpha power? Does a movement suppress beta? That points to spectra and time frequency analysis, from chapters four and nine.

The third family is about interaction. Do two regions, or two people, coordinate their activity? That points to connectivity measures, from chapter ten, with all their care about volume conduction.

The fourth family is about state. How does the brain at rest differ between groups, between sleep and wakefulness, or under a drug? That points to resting state spectra, the aperiodic slope, complexity and microstates, from chapters four and twelve.

Each family brings its own constraints. E R Ps need many repetitions of a precisely timed event. Spectral measures need long enough windows for the frequencies you care about. Phase connectivity needs many epochs and an analysis that resists zero lag mixing. State measures need long, stable recordings and careful control of vigilance.

Then make the question precise. Not just "does attention change alpha", but: posterior alpha power, eight to twelve hertz or individually adjusted, in the second before the target, higher when attention is directed to the other side. Now you know the electrodes, the frequency band, the time window, and the comparison. You also know how many trials you will need, and what could confound you.

Why insist on doing this before collecting data? Because EEG offers an enormous number of possible measures: channels, time points, frequencies, connectivity pairs, and many analysis choices. If you search through all of them after the fact, something will always look significant by chance. Choosing the measure first is what makes a result believable. We return to this with statistics in chapter fifteen, and with preregistration in episode five point seven.

Exploration is still welcome. Many great discoveries began as surprises. The point is to label exploration as exploration, and to confirm surprising findings in new data, rather than presenting them as if they had been predicted.

A last practical tip: write the analysis down before you record. One page is enough: the question, the measure, the electrodes and time windows, the comparison, and the statistical test. If you cannot write that page, the design is not ready yet.

Let's recap. Start from the question, not from the data. Is it about when, rhythm, interaction, or state? Each family points to a type of measure, with its own needs for trials, timing and recording length. Make the question precise enough to name the electrodes, frequencies, windows and comparison. And decide the measure before you collect data, while still leaving room for clearly labelled exploration.

PLATE 5.24:18

Trials, timing and jitter

A trial is a timeline of fixation, stimulus, response and interval. Randomised event related designs beat blocked ones, and jittering the interval, like a toaster that pops unpredictably, stops anticipation from contaminating the baseline. Intervals must outlast slow responses, epochs need padding for time frequency analysis, stimulus and response should be separable, and sessions need breaks for the participant.

TRANSCRIPTEN

An EEG experiment is built from trials: short, repeated sequences of events. How you time them shapes everything you will measure. Today, we design a trial.

Imagine a toaster that pops every exactly four seconds. After a few rounds, you start reaching for the toast before it pops. You have learned the rhythm. Brains do the same thing, and in an EEG experiment, that anticipation leaves a trace in the data.

Let's draw a typical trial on a timeline. It may start with a fixation cross. Then a stimulus appears: a picture, a sound, a word. The participant may respond with a button press. Then comes the inter trial interval, the pause before the next trial. The time between the onsets of two successive stimuli is called the stimulus onset asynchrony, or S O A.

First decision: blocked or event related. In a blocked design, trials of the same condition are grouped together, for example a minute of faces, then a minute of houses. Simple, but participants know what is coming, and slow changes like fatigue or electrode drift can line up with the conditions. In an event related design, conditions are mixed in a random order. Participants cannot predict the next trial, and slow drifts affect all conditions equally. For most EEG studies, randomised event related designs are the safer default.

Second decision: the timing between trials. If the interval is always the same, the brain learns it. Preparatory activity starts to build before each stimulus: slow negative shifts like the contingent negative variation, changes in alpha, and the readiness potential before a planned response. That activity falls in your baseline, the period just before the stimulus that you use as a zero point. A baseline that already contains anticipation distorts every measure that follows.

The remedy is jitter: varying the interval randomly within a range, for example between one and one and a half seconds. Jitter makes the exact moment unpredictable. It also spreads out the slow responses of the previous trial, so they do not sum in the same place on every trial.

Third decision: the length of the interval itself. Responses take time to fade. A late E R P component or an alpha change can last a second or more. If the next stimulus arrives too soon, its response overlaps the tail of the previous one. Overlap is not always a disaster, and regression methods from episode eight point five can separate overlapping responses, but it is easier to avoid when you can. As a rule, make the interval longer than the longest response you want to measure, plus the baseline you need.

Fourth decision: what goes into the epoch. For an E R P, you might keep from two hundred milliseconds before the stimulus to eight hundred after. For time frequency analysis, you need more: wavelets and filters smear in time, so add padding of a few cycles of the lowest frequency on each side, which will be cut away later. If you are interested in four hertz, a few cycles is close to a second on each side.

Fifth: separate events that you want to measure separately. If the response follows the stimulus by three hundred milliseconds every time, stimulus related and response related activity will be mixed. A variable reaction time helps. So can a delay between stimulus and response cue, when the task allows it.

And finally, do not forget the participant. Long sessions bring fatigue, drowsiness and movement. Plan short blocks with breaks, keep the task engaging, and count the total duration honestly, including capping and instructions.

Let's recap. A trial is a timeline: fixation, stimulus, response, interval. Prefer randomised event related designs. Jitter the intervals so anticipation does not contaminate the baseline. Leave enough time for responses to fade, and enough padding for time frequency analysis. Separate stimulus and response when you can. And design for a human being, with breaks.

Next time: how many trials, and how many participants?

PLATE 5.34:08

How many trials, how many participants?

Trials are spoonfuls of soup and participants are pots, and both are needed. Noise shrinks with the square root of trial count, and phase measures are biased upward with few trials, since random unit arrows still average to a nonzero length. Power depends jointly on trials, participants and effect size, as power contours show, so plan for losses and realistic effects.

TRANSCRIPTEN

How many trials should each participant do? And how many participants should you test? These are two of the most important design questions, and there is no single magic number. Today, we see why, and how to decide.

Imagine you are tasting soup. One spoonful tells you something, but it might have been the spoonful with all the salt. Ten spoonfuls give a better idea of the whole pot. But tasting ten spoonfuls from one pot tells you nothing about the other cooks' pots. Trials are spoonfuls. Participants are pots. You need enough of both.

Start with trials. Each EEG trial contains your signal of interest plus noise: ongoing brain activity, muscle, small artifacts. Averaging N trials keeps the signal and reduces random noise, which shrinks in proportion to the square root of N. To halve the noise, you need four times as many trials.

How many is enough depends on the measure, and on how big the effect is compared to the noise.

Large E R P components, like the P three, can be estimated reasonably with a few dozen clean trials. Small components and small differences between conditions need more.

Spectral power is usually estimated from longer stretches of data, so the question becomes how many seconds you have, not just how many trials.

Phase based measures are the hungriest. The phase locking value, the inter trial coherence, and other measures built from the length of an average of unit arrows, are biased upwards with few trials. Even with random phases, the average of N unit arrows does not have length zero: its expected length is roughly the square root of pi divided by four N. With ten trials, pure noise already gives about zero point two eight. With a hundred, about zero point zero nine. So phase measures need many trials, and conditions must be compared with equal numbers of trials, or with unbiased versions of the measure.

Now participants. In 2018, Megan Boudewyn, Steven Luck and colleagues simulated E R P experiments while varying three things at once: the number of trials, the number of participants, and the size of the effect. Their message was in the title: it depends. Doubling the number of trials often more than doubled statistical power. More trials helped most for within participant comparisons. For differences between groups, adding participants mattered more.

In 2021, Daniel Baker and colleagues proposed a useful picture: power contours. Put participants on one axis, trials on the other, and draw lines of equal statistical power. The lines are curved. You can reach the same power with many participants and few trials, or fewer participants and more trials, within limits. Where the trade off is worth it depends on how variable responses are within each person, compared to how much people differ from each other.

Two practical warnings. First, plan for losses. Some trials will be rejected for artifacts, and some participants will be excluded entirely. Decide the exclusion rules in advance, and recruit enough people that your final sample still has the power you planned.

Second, use realistic effect sizes. Effects reported in small published studies tend to be inflated. Base your planning on large studies, meta analyses, or a conservative guess, and run a simulation when you can. Episode fifteen point ten shows how.

Let's recap. Trials are spoonfuls, participants are pots, and you need enough of both. Noise shrinks with the square root of the number of trials. E R Ps, spectra and phase measures need very different amounts of data, and phase measures are biased with few trials. Power depends jointly on trials, participants, and effect size, as power contours show. Plan for losses, and use realistic effects.

Next time: controls and confounds, or how not to fool yourself.

PLATE 5.44:00

Controls and confounds

A confound is a hidden difference between conditions, like a cake that also won because of a newer oven and a prettier plate. The layers are stimulus brightness or loudness, eye movements, motor responses, fatigue over time, event rarity, difficulty, and group differences. The remedies are to match, counterbalance, randomise, record the eyes, and check the boring explanations first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

An EEG difference between two conditions is only interesting if the conditions differ in the way you think they do. Today, we hunt for confounds: hidden differences that can create an effect your hypothesis never predicted.

Imagine a cake competition between two recipes. One cake wins. But it was baked in the newer oven, tasted first, while the judges were hungry, and served on a prettier plate. Was it the recipe? You cannot tell. Every extra difference is a layer on top of the one you care about.

Let's go through the most common layers in EEG.

The first layer is physical. Brains respond strongly to low level properties of stimuli. Brighter images produce larger early visual responses. Louder sounds produce larger auditory responses. Larger, more contrasted or more cluttered pictures differ too. If your angry faces happen to be brighter than your neutral faces, an early difference may have nothing to do with emotion. Match luminance, contrast, size, loudness and duration across conditions, or measure them and include them in the analysis.

The second layer is the eyes. Different conditions can make people move their eyes differently. A more interesting picture attracts more saccades. A harder task changes blink rates. Eye movements and blinks produce large electrical signals, and as we saw for gamma in episode four point six, even tiny saccades can mimic brain responses. Keep fixation, use short presentations where possible, record the eyes with electrodes or an eye tracker, and check that eye behaviour does not differ between conditions.

The third layer is the hands. If one condition requires a button press and the other does not, motor preparation and execution appear in one and not the other: the readiness potential, mu and beta suppression. Make responses equal across conditions, counterbalance which hand is used, or separate the decision from the response in time.

The fourth layer is time. Fatigue, drowsiness, learning, electrode drying and slow drifts all change over a session. If one condition always comes first, these changes line up with your conditions. Randomise trial order, counterbalance block order across participants, and keep an eye on vigilance.

The fifth layer is expectation and frequency. Rare events produce their own responses, such as the P three, simply because they are rare. If your target condition is less frequent than your control, part of the difference is rarity. Match probabilities, or make rarity part of the design deliberately.

The sixth layer is difficulty and effort. A harder condition changes arousal, attention, and often reaction times. Frontal theta and alpha change with effort itself. When conditions differ in difficulty, think about whether difficulty is part of your question or a confound.

And a seventh layer between groups: when comparing patients and controls, or children and adults, groups can differ in medication, sleep, caffeine, head size, hair, artifact rates and the number of clean trials. Report these, and match or model them.

How do you find confounds you have not thought of? Ask someone else to read your design and play the sceptic. Pilot the experiment on yourself and colleagues. And look at the data for the boring explanations first: eye channels, reaction times, number of accepted trials per condition.

Let's recap. A confound is a hidden difference between conditions. The classic layers are physical stimulus properties, eye movements, motor responses, time and fatigue, event probability, and difficulty, plus group differences. Match, counterbalance, randomise, record the eyes, and check the boring explanations first.

Next time: resting state, done right.

PLATE 5.54:08

Resting state, done right

Rest is a condition needing as much control as a task, like a pot left unattended on the stove. Eyes open and eyes closed are different states, so alternating short blocks gives a built-in alpha check. Many people drift toward sleep within minutes, and drowsiness reshapes the whole spectrum, so vigilance, instructions, order, time of day and caffeine must be controlled and reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Resting state recordings look like the easiest experiment in the world: no task, no stimuli, just sit still. In practice, rest is one of the hardest conditions to control. Today, we see how to do it right.

Imagine leaving a pot of soup on the stove while you step out of the kitchen. You come back ten minutes later, and it might be gently simmering, boiling over, or already cold. You did nothing to it, yet a lot happened. A resting brain is that pot: unattended, and changing all the time.

The first decision is eyes open or eyes closed. With the eyes closed, posterior alpha is large, as we saw in episode four point two. With the eyes open, alpha drops, and the visual system is engaged with whatever is in front of the participant. These are two different states, not two versions of the same one. Record both if you can, and say which you analysed.

Many protocols alternate them. In the open LEMON dataset from Leipzig, for example, resting EEG lasted sixteen minutes, in blocks of one minute, alternating eyes closed and eyes open, with half of the participants starting with each. Alternating blocks spreads slow changes over both conditions, and gives you a built in check: alpha should rise each time the eyes close.

The second decision is what the participant sees and does. With eyes open, give them a fixation cross, ideally low contrast, so the visual input is the same for everyone. Ask them to relax, keep still, avoid clenching the jaw, and not to think about anything in particular. Write the instructions down and read them the same way every time. Small differences in wording can change what people do in their heads.

The third decision is duration, and here comes the twist. Rest is not stable. In 2014, Enzo Tagliazucchi and Helmut Laufs trained a classifier on recordings with simultaneous EEG and functional M R I, then applied it to more than a thousand resting state scans. About a third of participants lost wakefulness within three minutes. Many people drift into light sleep without noticing, and without the experimenter noticing either.

Why does it matter? Because drowsiness changes the whole spectrum. Alpha fades and slows, theta rises, slow waves appear, and connectivity and complexity measures change with them. If one group is sleepier than the other, perhaps because of medication, age, or a long session before the rest block, your group difference may simply be a difference in sleepiness.

So what can you do? Keep rest blocks short and alternate eyes open and closed. Record rest early in the session, before fatigue builds. Ask the participant afterwards whether they felt drowsy or fell asleep. Look at the data for signs of drowsiness: alpha dropping out in eyes closed blocks, slow rolling eye movements, the first spindles. Tools exist to estimate vigilance from the EEG itself, and simply scoring the recording in thirty second pages, as in episode four point seven, is already informative.

The fourth decision is everything around the recording. Time of day, caffeine, sleep the night before, and even the temperature of the room affect resting EEG. Keep them consistent, and write them down.

And finally, the order. If rest comes after a demanding task, it is not the same rest as before the task. Fix the order, report it, and keep it the same for everyone, unless the order is itself part of your question.

Let's recap. Rest is a condition, and it needs as much control as any task. Eyes open and eyes closed are different states. Standardise the fixation, instructions and order. Keep blocks short, because many people drift towards sleep within minutes, and drowsiness changes the whole spectrum. Check vigilance, ask the participant, and document time of day, caffeine and sleep.

Next time: special populations, from infants to patients.

PLATE 5.64:15

Special populations

Infants, older adults and patients need adapted sessions, paradigms and analyses, like a dinner party with toddlers and guests with allergies. Infant studies lose about half of participants to artifacts and fussiness, rhythms and components change with age, and medications alter the EEG. Rejection rates are planned, groups compared like with like, and every adaptation justified in advance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Most EEG methods were developed with healthy young adults, usually students. But many of the most important questions concern infants, children, older adults, and patients. Today, we see what changes when you record them.

Imagine cooking for a dinner party. Some guests are toddlers, some are elderly, one has an allergy. You do not change the whole menu, but you adapt portions, timing and preparation. And you plan for some plates coming back untouched.

Start with infants and young children. Sessions must be short, because attention is short. Instructions cannot be followed, so the paradigm itself must hold attention: varied, colourful, engaging stimuli. Infants move, suck, look away and cry, which brings many artifacts. Nets of sponge electrodes soaked in saline are often used because they are quick to apply, but they dry out over time.

The infant brain is also different. The skull is thinner and has soft spots, the fontanelles. Rhythms are slower: the dominant posterior rhythm starts at a few hertz in infancy and accelerates through childhood towards the adult alpha frequency. And E R P components change in latency, shape and even polarity with age. Adult bands and adult component windows cannot be copied directly.

The biggest practical issue is attrition. In 2012, Manuela Stets, Daniel Stahl and Vincent Reid published a meta analysis of infant E R P studies. The average attrition rate was about forty nine percent. Roughly half of the infants tested did not contribute usable data. If you need thirty infants in the final analysis, you may need to test sixty.

Now older adults. Recordings are usually easier, but signals change. Alpha slows, the aperiodic slope flattens, as episode four point ten showed, and E R P latencies lengthen. Hearing and vision may need correction or matched stimulus levels. Sessions should include more breaks, and people may be more sensitive to the discomfort of a long cap fitting.

Clinical populations bring their own needs. Patients may take medications that change the EEG: benzodiazepines, for example, increase beta activity, and many drugs affect slow rhythms. Some conditions bring more movement, tics, or difficulty staying still. Some participants find the sensations of the cap and gel, or the sounds of the lab, hard to tolerate. Plan desensitisation, visits to the lab before the recording, and flexible pauses. And keep the session focused: one essential measure recorded well is better than five measures recorded badly.

Across all these groups, three design principles help.

First, plan the rejection rate from the beginning. Estimate how many trials and participants you will lose, from pilot data or published studies, and recruit and design accordingly. Decide the inclusion criteria, such as a minimum number of clean trials, before you see the results.

Second, compare like with like. Groups that differ in age or diagnosis also tend to differ in artifacts, number of accepted trials, and head size. These differences can create group effects by themselves. Report them, match them where possible, and use measures that are robust to unequal trial numbers.

Third, adapt analysis parameters thoughtfully, and justify them. Using an individual alpha frequency, age appropriate component windows, or a different reference may be necessary. But every adaptation should be decided in advance and explained, not tuned after looking at group differences.

Let's recap. Special populations need adapted sessions, paradigms and analyses. Infants bring short attention, many artifacts, developing rhythms and components, and attrition around one in two. Older adults bring slower rhythms and sensory changes. Patients bring medications and specific needs. Plan rejection rates from the start, compare like with like, and justify every adaptation in advance.

Next time: pilots and preregistration.

PLATE 5.74:27

Pilot and preregistration

A pilot is a full rehearsal that finds trigger, timing, instruction, duration and analysis problems while they are cheap to fix, like cooking a recipe once before the dinner. It is not a reliable source of effect sizes. A preregistration is the recipe written down: a time stamped plan of design, preprocessing and statistics that labels exploration rather than forbidding it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Before the real data collection begins, two habits save countless studies: running a pilot, and writing a preregistration. Today, we see what each one is for, and what it is not for.

Think of a new recipe before a big dinner. You cook it once for yourself first. You discover that the oven runs hot and that one ingredient is missing from the shop. Then you write the recipe down exactly as you will cook it on the night. The trial run is the pilot. The written recipe is the preregistration.

Start with the pilot. A pilot is a full rehearsal of the experiment, on a few people, often colleagues, sometimes yourself. Its purpose is to find problems while they are still cheap to fix.

What can a pilot reveal? Triggers that are missing, duplicated or wrongly coded. A delay between the trigger and what actually appears on the screen, which you can measure with a photodiode, as episode six point three explains. Instructions that participants misunderstand. A task that is too easy, too hard, or too long. A session that runs forty minutes over schedule. And, importantly, whether you can actually run your planned analysis on the recorded files from start to finish.

That last point deserves emphasis. Run the whole analysis pipeline on the pilot data: import, events, epochs, artifact handling, the measure, and the statistical model. Many design flaws only appear when you try to analyse.

What a pilot is not for: estimating your effect size. Pilots are small, so their effect estimates are very noisy. In 2018, Casper Albers and Daniël Lakens showed that planning a main study from a small pilot's effect size often leaves it underpowered, partly because researchers tend to follow up only the pilots that happened to look promising. Use pilots for feasibility. Plan sample size from larger studies, meta analyses, or the smallest effect you would care about.

And keep pilot data separate. If you change the procedure after the pilot, which is the whole point, those data no longer belong with the main study.

Now preregistration. A preregistration is a time stamped, public research plan, written before the data are collected or seen. It states the hypotheses, the design, the sample size and stopping rule, the exclusion criteria, the preprocessing steps, the measures, and the statistical tests.

Why does EEG need this so much? Because, as episode five point one showed, the number of possible choices is huge: filters, references, artifact methods, electrodes, time windows, frequency bands, and statistics. In 2021, Mariella Paul, Gisela Govaart and Antonio Schettino published guidelines for preregistering E R P studies, with concrete examples for each preprocessing and analysis step, and a community template followed.

A good preregistration is specific. Not "we will analyse the P three", but: the mean amplitude at Pz, between three hundred and five hundred milliseconds, with a mastoid reference, compared between conditions with a paired test.

Some studies go one step further with a registered report: the introduction and methods are peer reviewed before data collection, and the journal commits to publishing the result, whatever it turns out to be.

A common worry is that preregistration forbids exploration. It does not. You can still explore everything you like. Preregistration simply separates what was planned from what was discovered, so readers know which results confirm a prediction and which ones generate new hypotheses. And if something must change, because a plan turns out to be impossible, you report the deviation and explain why.

Let's recap. A pilot is a full rehearsal that finds problems with triggers, timing, instructions, duration and analysis, while they are cheap to fix. It is not a reliable source of effect sizes. A preregistration is a time stamped plan of hypotheses, design, preprocessing and statistics. It does not forbid exploration; it labels it. And deviations are fine, as long as they are reported.

Next time, the last episode of this chapter: a zoo of classic paradigms.

PLATE 5.84:46

A paradigm zoo

A cookbook of classic EEG paradigms spares a new task: each recipe is tuned to one brain response and arrives with known pitfalls. The oddball yields the P300, the mismatch negativity indexes automatic change detection, steady-state tagging gives sharp spectral peaks, go/no-go probes inhibition, motor imagery modulates mu and beta, and naturalistic listening is modelled with temporal response functions instead of averaging.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Many EEG questions do not need a brand new task. Over decades, researchers have refined a set of classic paradigms, each tuned to produce a particular brain response. Today, we tour the zoo.

Think of a cookbook of classic recipes. Each one is reliable because many cooks have made it before you, and their notes in the margins tell you what goes wrong. Choosing a classic paradigm is the same: you inherit its strengths, its known measures, and its known pitfalls.

First, the oddball. A stream of frequent standard stimuli, with occasional rare targets that the participant must detect or count. Rare, task relevant targets produce a large positive wave around three hundred milliseconds or later, maximal over parietal sites: the P three. It was first reported in 1965 by Samuel Sutton and colleagues. It is robust and large, which makes it a favourite for clinical studies and brain computer interfaces. The pitfalls: its amplitude depends on probability, attention and task, so conditions must be matched on all three.

Second, the mismatch negativity, or M M N, described by Risto Näätänen and colleagues in 1978. Again a stream of standard sounds with occasional deviants, but this time the participant ignores the sounds, often while watching a silent film. The deviant response minus the standard response shows a negativity at fronto central sites, roughly one to two hundred and fifty milliseconds after the change. It indexes automatic change detection, and works even in infants and in patients who cannot follow instructions. The pitfall: deviant and standard sounds differ physically, so a careful design compares the same sound as deviant in one block and standard in another.

Third, steady state responses. A stimulus that flickers or is modulated at a fixed rate, say twelve hertz, drives a response at exactly that frequency and its harmonics: the steady state visual evoked potential, or S S V E P, and its auditory cousins. The response appears as a sharp, narrow peak in the spectrum, with an excellent signal to noise ratio after a few minutes. Tagging different stimuli with different frequencies lets you follow attention to each of them, which is called frequency tagging. The pitfall: the response depends strongly on the frequency chosen and on low level stimulus properties.

Fourth, go and no go. Participants respond quickly to frequent go stimuli and must withhold the response to rare no go stimuli. No go trials produce a fronto central N two and P three complex, linked to conflict and inhibition. The pitfalls: the rare no go is also rare, so probability is confounded with inhibition unless controlled, and motor activity differs by design between go and no go trials.

Fifth, motor imagery. Participants imagine moving their left or right hand without moving. Mu and beta rhythms decrease over the opposite motor cortex, as episode four point three showed. This is the basis of many brain computer interfaces, and of the common spatial patterns method in chapter fourteen. The pitfalls: real micro movements and muscle tension, and large differences between people in how well they can produce the effect.

Sixth, the resting state, which we covered in episode five point five: simple to run, and hard to control.

Seventh, naturalistic paradigms: listening to a story, watching a film. There are no repeated trials, so instead of averaging, you model how the EEG follows features of the stimulus over time, such as the sound envelope, with temporal response functions. In Python, M N E provides a receptive field estimator for this. The pitfalls: a stimulus is many things at once, so features must be chosen carefully, and you need enough data to fit the model.

Let's recap. The oddball gives the P three; the M M N indexes automatic change detection without a task; steady state paradigms give sharp spectral peaks and frequency tagging; go and no go probe inhibition; motor imagery modulates mu and beta; rest needs vigilance control; and naturalistic paradigms use encoding models instead of averaging. Each comes with a favourite measure, and with known limitations that you inherit when you copy it.
