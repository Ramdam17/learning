PLATE 12.17:09

Why is it missing?

Whether holes in data are safe depends on why values are missing. A dropped tube is completely at random, older people skipping a session is at random and repairable with the recorded age, and depressed people skipping the mood questionnaire is not at random. A small bad hole biased the mean more than a large innocent one, and more people never shrink bias.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the jigsaw table. Chapter twelve is about holes. A puzzle lies on the table, and some pieces are missing. The first question is not how many. It is why those ones.

When a table of data has holes, we usually compute the mean of what is left and carry on. Is that safe? It depends on one thing only: why the values are missing.

Here are three stories for the same hole. In the first, the value is gone by accident. In the second, it is gone because of something we wrote down. In the third, it is gone because of the very value we never got. Statisticians call them missing completely at random, missing at random, and missing not at random, or M C A R, M A R, and M N A R. This classification starts with Rubin, in nineteen seventy-six. Today we use the plain words, and a machine will show why they matter.

Here is one simulated study. Two hundred people, and each dot is a person: age across, score up. The score could be a memory test or a symptom scale. Older people tend to score higher. Everyone's age is recorded. The mean of all two hundred scores is forty-nine point six. In real life we never see that. A simulation does.

Story one: a tube dropped on the floor. A technician knocks over a rack, and some tubes are lost. Which ones? Anyone's. The dice choose. Fifty-three scores vanish, and the mean of the rest is forty-nine point seven. Close. But is that a rule, or luck?

Repeat the study five thousand times. The pile of means sits on fifty point zero. The bias is zero point zero. The spread is point eight, a little wider than with nothing missing, because fewer people are left. This is missing completely at random. The reason has nothing to do with anything.

Story two: older participants skip the last session. Age is on the sheet for everyone. Now the holes crowd to the right. Sixty-six scores vanish. The missing people average sixty-four years old, against forty-four for those who stayed. Older people score higher, so the mean of the rest is forty-six point seven, too low.

Five thousand studies. The pile sits on forty-seven point nine, a bias of minus two point one.

But here we know the reason, and it is written on the sheet. So look inside age bands. Cut the ages into five bands. Inside a band, the people who stayed look like the people who left. Take each band's mean, and weight the bands by how many people were really there. In this study, the estimate is forty-eight point five. Over five thousand studies, the pile sits on forty-nine point nine: a bias of minus point one. Doing this properly is the job of the last episode of this chapter.

This is missing at random. Careful: here, at random does not mean by accident. It means that once we account for what we recorded, the holes do not depend on the missing value.

Story three: the most depressed people stop filling in the mood questionnaire. Now the holes crowd at the top, where the scores are highest. Sixty-two scores vanish. The mean of the rest is forty-six point three. Over five thousand studies, the pile sits on forty-six point seven, a bias of minus three point three.

Try the age trick. The pile only moves to forty-seven point seven. The bias is still minus two point three. Why? Inside every age band, the people who answered are still the less depressed ones. Age does not hold the reason. The reason is the value we do not have. This is missing not at random.

Now the uncomfortable part. Guess before you see. From the observed data alone, can we tell story two from story three? Take the study of story two, and keep its hundred and thirty-four observed people. Now imagine two worlds for the sixty-six who left. In world A, they scored what their age predicts. In world B, each scored eight points higher. Both are simulated.

The observed dots are the same in both worlds, by construction. The mean of the rest is forty-six point seven in both, and so is every number we could compute from them. Yet the true mean is forty-eight point nine in world A, and fifty-one point six in world B. The age-band estimate, forty-eight point five, is close in world A, and about three points short in world B.

Nothing in the table says which world we are in. In two thousand and eight, Molenberghs and colleagues showed this in general: every not-at-random model has an at-random counterpart that fits the observed data equally well. The reason has to come from outside the table, from how the data were collected.

So the question is not how much is missing, but why. Guess before you see: ten percent missing for a bad reason, or forty percent missing by accident. Which gives the worse mean?

Ten percent missing in the not-at-random way: the bias is minus one point two, the spread point seven, and the total error one point four. Forty percent missing by accident: the bias is zero point zero, the spread point nine, and the total error point nine. The small bad hole did more damage than the big innocent one.

Now grow the study. With fifty people, the two are close: one point nine against one point eight. With a thousand, the accident costs point four, and the bad hole one point three. With five thousand, the accident costs point two, and the bad hole still one point two. More people shrink the spread. They never shrink a bias.

Six more holes, and for each, ask why.

One: a sample tube slips from a gloved hand. Nothing to do with the sample. Completely at random.

Two: a field sensor runs out of battery on cold nights, and a second sensor records the temperature. The reason is recorded. At random.

Three: patients leave a trial because of side effects, and their last scores are never taken. If the side effects are written down at each visit and explain the leaving, at random. If it is how they feel, and nobody wrote it down, not at random.

Four: people with high incomes skip the income question. Not at random.

Five: an EEG study rejects trials with movement artefacts, more often in children. Age is recorded, so this is at random, as long as age explains the rejections.

Six: a phone survey never reaches people without a phone. If what makes them different is recorded, at random. If not, not at random.

The same variable can fall in any of the three boxes. The label belongs to the reason, not to the variable.

Remember: the question is not how much is missing, but why. Completely at random is harmless, at random can be repaired with what we recorded, and not at random cannot be fixed from the data alone.

Next time, what software does by default when it meets a hole: it throws away the whole row. Is that a safe choice? See you in episode twelve point two.

PLATE 12.27:19

Deleting rows

Software drops every row with a hole, but a questionnaire of 20 items with 5 percent holes keeps only 35.8 percent of rows. If holes are by accident, this costs width and power. If older people skip a session, the interval covering the truth falls to 29.8 percent. Deleting rows is an analysis choice, not the absence of one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the jigsaw table. Last time we asked why a value is missing: by accident, because of something we wrote down, or because of the missing value itself. Today, what happens next. A table has holes, and the software acts before you ask. It throws away every row that has a hole.

This is the default. In R, the linear model function omits incomplete rows, and its documentation calls this the factory-fresh default. The name is listwise deletion, or complete-case analysis. Harmless? Count.

Picture a questionnaire of 20 items, simulated. Each item has 5 percent of its answers missing, chosen by dice. Five in a hundred. That is small. But a row survives only if all 20 answers are there. Guess before you see: out of 100 rows, how many survive?

The chance is 0.95 times itself, 20 times. This is exact arithmetic. 0.95 to the power 20 is 35.8 percent. Barely a third of the rows.

The same arithmetic for other table widths. With 5 percent holes, one variable keeps 95.0 percent of the rows. Five variables keep 77.4. Ten keep 59.9. Twenty keep 35.8. Forty keep 12.9. With 10 percent holes, they keep 1.5 percent. It is the arithmetic of the repeated measures episode, where one empty cell dropped the whole person. Here a row has 20 chances to fail, not 3.

Now watch it happen. A simulated table: 1,000 people, 20 items, 5 percent of the cells emptied by dice. Here are 24 rows. Each hole sends its whole row into the shredder. Only 10 of these 24 rows are complete. In all 1,000 rows, 379 survive, 37.9 percent, though only 4.9 percent of the cells are empty. Repeat the table 5,000 times, and the share of complete rows averages 35.8 percent, as the arithmetic said.

So deleting rows costs people. Is that all? If the holes are placed by accident, almost. If not, the rows that remain no longer resemble the people we started with. Let us measure, with last time's study. 200 people, age recorded, a score whose true mean is 50. This time, 30 percent of the scores are lost. We keep the complete rows, take their mean, and draw a 95 percent interval around it. Repeat 5,000 times.

First reason: a tube falls off the bench. Dice again. The bias is zero, and the interval covers the truth in 94.8 percent of studies. With nothing lost, it was 95.2. Safe here, but not free. The interval is about 20 percent wider, 3.3 points instead of 2.8.

That width is power. Two groups of 100 people, a true difference of 4 points. With every row, the test finds it in 80.3 percent of studies. Lose 30 percent of the rows by accident, and it finds it in 66.0 percent.

Second reason: older people skip the last session. Age is written on the sheet, but the rows go anyway. The bias is minus 2.1. The interval that promised 95 percent covers the truth in 29.8 percent of studies.

Third reason: in a clinical trial, sicker patients drop out, and their last score is never taken. The bias is minus 3.3. The interval covers the truth in 0.7 percent of studies. A narrow interval, around the wrong number.

Now a surprise, which I measured before saying it. A survey where high earners skip the income question. Income predicts a well-being score, illustrative. The true slope is 0.6. Delete the rows with no income. The missing values are the high ones, so the holes depend on the missing value itself. Guess before you see: is the slope biased?

Over 5,000 studies, the slope averages 0.600. No bias, and its interval covers the truth in 95.1 percent of studies. But the mean well-being score is 48.1, not 50. Rows were removed by income, and a slope compares people with the same income. The people who stay still follow the same line. They only sit on its lower part. Paul Allison has written about this. It needs a correct line, and holes in the predictor, not the outcome. If instead the high well-being scores go missing, the slope falls to 0.52, and its interval covers the truth in only 65.9 percent.

The same shape, in EEG. Trials with blinks are rejected, and blinks are commoner in one condition. Simulated: two conditions, 100 trials each, no true difference. First, both lose 20 percent of their trials, the largest deflections first. The difference stays at zero. Second, condition B loses 30 percent and condition A 10 percent, by dice. The difference is still zero. B just has fewer trials. Third, B loses 30 percent, A loses 10 percent, and the largest deflections go. Now B falls 0.8 below A, and the test announces a difference in 29.2 percent of studies, where there is none. It should be 5.

Pairwise deletion loses less: for each pair of variables, use every row where both are present. Sensors that fail in the cold: three sensors, 30 rows, each reading lost with chance 30 percent, by dice. On average only 10 rows are complete, but each pair keeps about 15. So every correlation has its own n.

Worse, the table can become impossible. Here is the first such study. The correlations are 0.78, 0.79 and 0.03. Two sensors both strongly tied to the middle one cannot be nearly unrelated to each other. A valid table needs 1 plus 2abc minus a squared, b squared and c squared to be at least zero. Here it is minus 0.20. With these true correlations, close to the edge of what is possible, 29.4 percent of simulated studies give an impossible table.

So here is the point. Deleting incomplete rows is an analysis choice, not the absence of one. It assumes that the rows that remain are as good as the rows that left, for the question you ask. Sometimes that is true. When the holes depend on the missing value, it is false, and nothing warns you. The interval looks tight. The software has made that assumption for you.

Better choices exist: fill the hole once, fill it many times, or fit a model that copes with gaps. They come next. The classification we used starts with Rubin, in 1976.

Remember: deleting incomplete rows is an analysis, and it assumes that the remaining rows still look like the ones we started with. Next time, filling in one value. See you in episode twelve point three.

PLATE 12.37:27

Filling in one value

Single imputation fills each hole with putty and pretends it was measured. Filling with the mean shrinks the standard deviation from 10.0 to 8.3 and the correlation from 0.60 to 0.50. A regression fill tightens the link, and last-observation-carried-forward bias grows when scores change. The table looks complete, so standard errors lie: intervals cover only 84.2 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the jigsaw table. Last time we threw away every row that had one. Today, the opposite instinct: fill the hole, once, with a single guess, and carry on as if nothing had happened.

Think of a restorer. A hole in a painted wall is filled with putty, tinted to match. From across the room, the wall looks whole. But under an ultraviolet lamp, the putty glows.

Statisticians call this imputation. To impute a value is to fill it in. With single imputation, each hole gets one value. Our ultraviolet lamp is the simulation, because it remembers which values were real.

First putty: the mean. A blood-pressure diary, illustrative. Fourteen mornings, three of them skipped. Fill each gap with the average of the days that were written down. The three skipped days now look ordinary. The same happens in a school. One pupil missed the test. Fill the score with the class average, and that pupil becomes average, whoever they are.

Now at scale. A simulated study: 200 people, age across, score up. The correlation between age and score is 0.56. By dice, 58 scores are lost, about 30 percent. Fill every hole with the mean of the 142 scores that remain: 50.2.

In the histogram, 35 people sat in the 50 to 55 column. Now 78 do, and 58 of them are one invented number. The spread is the first victim. The standard deviation was 10.1. Now it is 8.2. The correlation with age was 0.56. Now it is 0.45. Over 5,000 such studies, the standard deviation goes from 10.0 to 8.3, and the correlation from 0.60 to 0.50. The mean did not move. Everything else did.

A questionnaire does the same. A person skips one item, and we write in that item's average. They now have an answer nobody gave, sitting exactly in the middle.

Second putty: a smarter one. Age is written down for everybody, and older people score higher. So fit a line of score on age, using the 142 kept rows, and fill each hole with the point of the line at that person's age. This is regression imputation. Guess before you see: what happens to the correlation?

All 58 filled points sit exactly on the line, by construction. They have no scatter at all. The correlation rises from 0.56 to 0.62. Over 5,000 studies, the true value is 0.60, and after the line fill it is 0.67. The fill makes the link look tighter than it is.

Picture a growth chart. A child's height at age eight is missing, and a line through the other visits fills it in. The curve looks smooth. A real child does not grow along a ruler.

Third putty: carry the last value forward. In a trial, people are measured at weeks 0, 4, 8 and 12, and some leave. A missing score is replaced by the last one taken. This is last observation carried forward, or LOCF.

John Lachin, in Clinical Trials in 2016, explained why this is fragile. It is unbiased only if the missing scores would look exactly like the last ones taken. In other words, only if nothing changes any more.

A simulated trial. Scores improve, from 40 to 46 on average. Before each visit, every person still in the study leaves with a chance of 15 percent, by dice. By week 12, 79 of the 200 are gone. If nobody had left, the mean at week 12 would be 47.3 in this group. The people who stayed average 47.2. The LOCF table says 45.4.

One group is only one group. Over 5,000 studies the true mean at week 12 is 46.0, and LOCF falls 1.6 points short. Its interval covers the truth in 30.4 percent of studies. The dice were fair, but the last score taken was an earlier, worse one.

Now let people worsen, from 52 down to 46. Same truth, same dice. LOCF carries forward the better, earlier scores, and the bias is plus 1.6. The interval covers the truth in 30.1 percent.

A sensor does it too. It drops out for an hour, and the last reading is repeated across the gap: a flat line, as if the world had stopped.

If nothing changes, LOCF is fine: the bias is 0.0, and the interval covers 94.8 percent. That is exactly what it assumes. In 2010, a National Research Council panel advised that single imputation methods like LOCF should not be the primary approach, unless the assumptions behind them are scientifically justified.

Now the question that matters. The table looks complete: 200 rows. But only 142 people said anything. Does the standard error know? Repeat the study 5,000 times, holes by dice, true mean 50, and compare with deleting the incomplete rows.

Deleting rows is honest. The standard error reports 0.84, the estimates really spread by 0.83, and the interval covers the truth in 95.2 percent of studies. Fill with the mean, and the table reports 0.59, against a real spread of 0.83. The interval covers 84.2 percent. Fill along the line, and the table reports 0.63, against 0.79. It covers 88.5 percent.

Can random noise repair it? Add to each line guess a random amount, as large as the usual scatter. The standard deviation comes back to 10.0, and the correlation to 0.60. That helps the spread, but the standard error is 0.71, against a real 0.85, and the interval covers 90.0 percent. The table still believes it has 200 observations.

Now let the holes depend on age, which is written on the sheet. The mean fill is biased by minus 2.1, and covers 13.0 percent. The line fill uses age, so it is unbiased. Its interval still covers only 85.6 percent.

LOCF is the odd one. Its standard error is right: 0.65, against a real spread of 0.65. What it cannot see is the bias. A narrow interval, around the wrong number.

So here is the point. A single imputation pretends the guessed value was observed. The table looks complete, and the standard errors lie, or sit narrowly on the wrong number. The putty glows under our lamp. The software has no lamp.

The cure is not a better guess. It is to fill the hole many times, differently each time, so that the disagreement shows.

Remember: one filled value is a guess, and the table treats it as a measurement. Next time, multiple imputation, and models that handle gaps. See you in episode twelve point four.

PLATE 12.47:30

Multiple imputation, and models that handle gaps

Multiple imputation works like five carbon-copy forms: observed boxes show through on every sheet, while each sheet fills the empty boxes differently. Analyse each table, then pool with Rubin's rules, which add the disagreement between sheets. The standard error grows from 0.58 to 0.76, restoring 95.3 percent coverage. A mixed model gives the same result without filling anything.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the jigsaw table, for the last episode of chapter twelve. Last time, one guess made the standard errors lie. Today, two honest ways to handle holes.

Think of a stack of five carbon-copy forms. Whatever was printed in the boxes shows through on every sheet: those are the values we observed. The empty boxes are filled in by hand, and every sheet gets a different handwriting.

This is multiple imputation. Donald Rubin set it out in a book of 1987. The software called mice, described by van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn in 2011, does the filling.

Here is a simulated study: 200 people, age across, score up. 55 scores are lost, and the holes are linked to age, which is written on the sheet. The people who left average 65 years old, against 44 for those who stayed. That is the second story of episode twelve point one.

Step one: fill. Fit a line of score on age, using the people who answered. Do not take the line's value. Take a draw: the line's value plus random scatter, as large as the real scatter. And since the line itself is uncertain, redraw the line a little each time. Do it five times.

Look at the first twelve people. The observed scores are identical on every sheet. The holes are not. The first person aged 73 gets 59.0, 58.2, 59.1, 52.1, and 56.2. That disagreement is what a single guess hid.

Step two: analyse. Take the mean score of each completed table: 49.70, 50.10, 50.55, 49.76, and 50.03.

Step three: pool. The answer is the mean of the five means: 50.03. For its uncertainty, Rubin's rules add two parts. The variance within a table, averaged: 0.436. And the variance between tables: 0.114. The total is the within part, plus one plus one fifth, times the between part. 0.436 plus 1.2 times 0.114 is 0.573. Its square root, the standard error, is 0.76.

Guess before you see: will the pooled standard error be larger or smaller than the one a single guess reports? A single filled table said 0.58. The pooled one is 0.76. Larger, because the five sheets disagree.

Now repeat the study 5,000 times, holes linked to age, true mean 50. Deleting rows has a bias of minus 2.1, and its interval covers the truth in 29.0 percent of studies. One regression guess has no bias, but reports 0.64 against a real spread of 0.84, and covers 86.7 percent. Multiple imputation has no bias, reports 0.86 against a real 0.87, and covers 95.3 percent. These are averages over 5,000 studies, which is why they differ from our single study.

A survey does the same with income. Many people skip that question. Fill it five times, and one respondent becomes five plausible incomes, not one. These incomes are illustrative.

How many fills? Repeat the whole procedure on this one table. With 2 imputations, the standard error moves by about 0.19 between repeats. With 5, by 0.08. With 20, by 0.04. With 100, by 0.02. The answer barely changes. What shrinks is the noise in the standard error.

The other road needs no filling. In a trial, people are measured at weeks 0, 4, 8, and 12, and some leave. A mixed model, from episode eleven point four, fitted by maximum likelihood, uses every measurement each person gave, even a person who came once. Simulated: 200 people, and 78 are gone by week 12. The people who did well at their last visit are the ones who leave.

The people still in at week 12 average 42.4. Everyone, had nobody left, would average 45.3. The mixed model says 45.1, and multiple imputation says 45.3.

Over 5,000 such trials, the true mean at week 12 is 46.0. The completers have a bias of minus 3.0, and cover the truth in 3.0 percent. One regression guess reports 0.59 against a real 0.73, and covers 89.0 percent. Multiple imputation reports 0.75 against 0.75, and covers 95.7 percent. The mixed model reports 0.67 against 0.67, and covers 95.1 percent. Both roads recover the truth, and keep the promised coverage.

The same two roads fit other studies. A cohort with missing blood tests: fill them from the other tests. A school study with missing grades: fill them from earlier grades. An EEG study with missing sessions: a mixed model uses the sessions that exist. A hyperscanning dyad where one recording failed: fill that session from the dyad's others. All four are illustrative.

Now the hard case: people leave because of the score they would have had. Same trial, same methods. Every method is biased. Completers: minus 3.2. The regression guess, multiple imputation, and the mixed model: minus 0.8. They cover the truth in 1.1, 66.6, 80.4, and 77.0 percent.

What remains is a sensitivity analysis. Impute as before, then shift every imputed value by delta: the people who left scored delta points higher than their history predicts. In one study, delta 0 gives 44.4, delta 4 gives 46.5, and delta 8 gives 48.6. Only the simulation knows that the truth is 46.0. A real analyst reports the range, under stated assumptions.

So what did multiple imputation recover? Not the lost data. A simulation can compare the filled values with the hidden ones. Over 5,000 studies, a regression guess misses a hidden score by 8.1 points, typically. One imputation draw misses by 11.5. Worse. Yet the pooled interval is honest. Each filled value is a worse guess, and the answer is a better answer. In 2002, Schafer and Graham recommended maximum likelihood and multiple imputation over the older procedures.

Chapter twelve in one line: ask why, then delete, fill many times, or fit a model.

Remember: multiple imputation does not recover the lost data. It carries the uncertainty we have about them into the result.
