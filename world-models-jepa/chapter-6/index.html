Autoencoders

An autoencoder passes a picture through a narrow hatch of a few beads, and a decoder repaints it from that necklace alone, scored by average squared pixel difference with no labels. It learns whatever helps repainting, even useless detail like sky brightness. A hatch as wide as the picture just copies, and a sharp repainting can miss a faint mark that a one-bead code reads.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the painting studio. Every model in this chapter is a painter. The first painter has the simplest job there is: look at a picture, then paint it again from memory. By the end of this video, you will know how an autoencoder works, what it learns, and why a good copy can still teach you nothing.

Here is the game. Two painters stand on either side of a wall. In the wall there is a hatch, only a few beads wide. The first painter looks at a picture, and may pass a short necklace through the hatch. The second painter has never seen the picture. From the necklace alone, she must paint it again. Whatever fits through the hatch is the representation: the necklace of episode four point one, used as a code.

Now the architecture, in one image. The first painter is ENKO, our encoder, the blue trapezoid that narrows. The necklace is the bottleneck. The second painter is the decoder, the pink trapezoid that widens again. And the scale sits in the pixel panel. It weighs the original picture against the repainted one, pixel by pixel. The loss is the average of the squared differences. No labels are needed: the picture is its own answer. And the training is the one you know. Backpropagation, from episode three point three, sends the error back through the decoder, then through the encoder.

Let's try it on a toy. Pictures of eight by eight pixels, made up for the occasion: a sky of changing brightness, a sun that moves, a hill of changing height. Four hundred pictures to train on, two thousand new ones to test. We use the simplest autoencoder, a linear one. In nineteen eighty-nine, Baldi and Hornik showed that its best possible code is the one given by principal component analysis, so we can compute it exactly. Deep, nonlinear autoencoders do better: Hinton and Salakhutdinov reported it in two thousand six.

With one bead, forty-nine percent of the repainting is explained, and fifty-one percent of the picture's variance is left. With two beads, forty percent is left. With four, twenty-three. With eight, thirteen. Now look at what the first bead paints. It follows the brightness of the sky very closely. The sky is the biggest thing to repaint, so it takes the first bead, even though it says nothing about what is in the scene. That is what an autoencoder learns: what helps to repaint, including details that are useless for understanding.

Now the classic trap. What if the hatch is as wide as the picture? Sixty-four beads for sixty-four pixels, and the error falls to zero. Perfect. But the encoder can simply copy the pixels onto the necklace, and the decoder copy them back. Nothing is squeezed, so nothing has to be understood. The loss does not ask for more. The bottleneck is what forces the model to choose.

The second trap is about judging the result. In our toy, half of the pictures carry a faint mark in the top left corner. That is the class we would like to read. Train a probe on the code, as in episode four point three. With eight beads, the repainting leaves thirteen percent of the variance. The pictures look right. Yet the probe reads the mark in fifty percent of new pictures. That is chance. The mark is too faint to be worth a bead.

Now take another code, with a single bead: the corner, compared with its neighbour. It repaints almost nothing. Ninety-seven percent of the variance is left. Yet the probe reads the mark in ninety-one percent of new pictures. A good repainting is not a good representation, and a blurry one is not necessarily a bad one. What matters is what you need the code for.

To sum up. An autoencoder squeezes a picture through a narrow necklace and repaints it, and the scale weighs the repainting against the original. It learns what helps to repaint, useful or not. A hatch as wide as the picture learns to copy. And a sharp repainting does not prove a good code. Next time: variational autoencoders, where every bead becomes a small cloud, so that we can draw new pictures.

PLATE 6.27:35

Variational autoencoders

A variational autoencoder writes a mean and a width instead of one necklace, so each picture becomes a blot of ink pulled toward a shared round disc. The reparameterization trick, mu plus sigma times epsilon, lets error flow through the random draw. Reconstruction plus a KL price makes random draws decodable into new pictures, though results tend to blur and can collapse.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the painting studio. Last time, one painter passed a short necklace through a hatch, and a second painter repainted the picture from it. Today we do not only want to copy pictures. We want to paint new ones that nobody has shown us. By the end of this video, you will know how a variational autoencoder works, what its loss is made of, and what goes wrong when you train one.

Here is the problem. In an ordinary autoencoder, every picture lands on its own point of the latent space, the space of episode four point two. Between the points there are gaps. Pick a spot at random, and you are probably in a gap that the decoder never saw. So we change one thing. A picture no longer lands as a dot. It lands as a drop of ink on a round sheet of blotting paper, and the drop spreads into a blot. The middle of the blot is where the encoder thinks the picture belongs. Its size says how unsure the encoder is. Every picture now lives at a cloud, not at a dot.

And the sheet has one rule. All the blots are pulled toward the same shared shape: a round disc, centred in the middle, a standard bell curve in every direction. When the blots overlap and fill the disc, any spot you poke with a pencil falls inside a blot, so the decoder has seen it. That is how we will paint new pictures. The idea is by Kingma and Welling, posted in December two thousand thirteen and published in twenty fourteen. Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra found it independently, the same year.

Now the architecture, in one image. It is the diagram of the last episode. ENKO, the blue trapezoid, reads the picture. The pink trapezoid repaints it. The scale sits in the pixel panel. Only the middle changes. Instead of one necklace, the encoder writes two. The first is a mean: the centre of the blot, one bead per direction. The second is a width, called sigma: the size of the blot in each direction. Then we draw one point inside the blot, and that point is the necklace the decoder receives.

There is a catch. Drawing a point at random is not a calculation, so the error cannot flow backwards through it. Backpropagation, from episode three point three, needs a smooth road from the loss back to the encoder. The trick is to move the dice out of the way. We draw a number epsilon from a standard bell curve, with mean zero and width one. Then we compute the point as mu, plus sigma, times epsilon. The luck now comes from outside, and the road through mu and sigma is plain arithmetic. The gradient flows. This is the reparameterization trick.

Let's try a toy: pictures with a sun that moves and a hill that changes height, and a latent space of just two beads, so that we can draw it. Picture A lives at a mean of minus one point two and zero point eight, with a width of zero point five five in both directions. Draw epsilon three times, and you get three different points, all inside the same blot.

What does the model learn? Two things at once, so the loss has two terms. The first is reconstruction: how well the decoder repaints the picture from its point. With pixels that are on or off, this is the cross-entropy of episode two point five, the surprise of the true pixels. With a Gaussian decoder, it is the squared error of last time. The second term is a price on the blot. It is the K L divergence between the blot and the shared disc.

Here the Information series comes in: see its episodes on K L divergence and variational bounds. The K L divergence from the blot to the disc is the average extra surprise you pay for expecting the disc when the blot is what really happens. It is zero when the blot is the disc, and it grows when the blot is small or far from the middle.

Put the two together. The log probability of a picture is at least the expected reconstruction, minus the K L divergence. That lower bound is the ELBO.

For Gaussian blots, the K L divergence has a closed form, given in the appendix of the paper. In each direction, it is one half of mu squared, plus sigma squared, minus one, minus the log of sigma squared. Check it. A mean of zero and a width of one: zero, plus one, minus one, minus zero. Exactly zero. The blot is the disc. A mean of one and a width of one: one half. A mean of zero and a width of one half: zero point three two. A mean of two and a width of zero point one: three point eight one, in nats. A small blot, far from the middle, is expensive.

In our toy, the four blots cost between one and one and a half nats each. Shrink them into sharp dots, pushed further out, and each would cost between seven and ten and a half. Now you see the tug of war. Reconstruction wants small, precise blots, so that the decoder knows what to paint. The K L price wants every blot to look like the disc. The trained model is the compromise.

And here is the payoff. After training, we throw the encoder away. We draw a point from the standard bell curve, and we hand it to the decoder. Because the blots overlap, eighty-six percent of random draws in our toy fall inside a two-sigma blot of one of the four pictures. With sharp dots, fewer than one in a thousand would. A regular latent space is one you can sample.

Now the classic traps. The first is blur. With a Gaussian decoder of fixed width, the reconstruction term is a squared error. For a given point, the best answer under squared error is the average of all the pictures that could have come from it. The average of a sun on the left and a sun on the right is two faint suns. That is the usual explanation, and we meet the same effect in episode six point five. Larsen and colleagues, in twenty fifteen, reported lower visual quality for variational autoencoders trained with element-wise errors than with a learned similarity measure. Blur is a tendency, not a law.

The second trap is posterior collapse. Suppose the decoder is strong enough to explain the data alone, like a language model that predicts each word from the words before it. Then the cheapest way to pay the K L price is to make every blot equal to the disc. The K L is zero, the point carries nothing about the picture, and the decoder ignores it. Bowman and colleagues met this in twenty sixteen, training on sentences. Trained without both of the fixes they proposed, their models ended with a K L of zero. First, K L annealing: start with a weight of zero on the K L term, so that the model first learns to use the latent, then raise the weight slowly to one. Second, word dropout: weaken the decoder by hiding some of the words it sees.

The same knob is the beta V A E of Higgins and colleagues, in twenty seventeen: a weight beta on the K L term, with beta equal to one being the original.

To sum up. A variational autoencoder writes a mean and a width instead of a necklace, so every picture becomes a blot. The reparameterization trick, mu plus sigma times epsilon, lets the error flow through the dice. The loss is a reconstruction term plus a K L price that pulls every blot toward the shared disc, a lower bound on the log probability. The result can be sampled, but it tends to blur, and it can collapse when the decoder ignores the latent. Next time: autoregressive models.

PLATE 6.36:55

Autoregressive models

An autoregressive model paints one stroke at a time: the chain rule writes a sequence's probability as a product of next-piece probabilities, and a masked transformer scores every position at once. Generation loops, drawing a token and feeding it back, with temperature setting boldness. Errors can snowball because training uses true canvases, and generation takes one step after another.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the painting studio. Last time, a variational autoencoder painted a whole picture in one go, from a single point drawn at random. Today the painter works differently. She paints one stroke at a time, and every stroke is decided by everything already on the canvas. By the end of this video, you will know how an autoregressive model works, what it learns, and why it is both powerful and slow.

The idea rests on one rule of probability. The chance of a whole sequence is the chance of the first piece, times the chance of the second given the first, times the chance of the third given the first two, and so on. In symbols: p of x one to x n equals the product, over i, of p of x i given everything before x i. This is the chain rule. It is exact. It holds for any sequence, and nothing has been assumed. Take four words: the, cat, sat, down. Here are illustrative numbers. The chance of starting with the is zero point two. Of cat, after the: zero point zero five. Of sat, after the cat: zero point four. Of down, after the cat sat: zero point five. Multiply them, and the sentence has a chance of zero point zero zero two. One in five hundred.

So to build a generator, we need only one machine: given what is already there, give a probability for the next piece. For text, a piece is a word, or part of a word, called a token. For an image, a piece is a pixel, or a small patch. The painter picks an order, and keeps to it.

For images, the order of Pixel R N N and Pixel C N N is the order of reading: row by row, from the top left corner. The paper is by van den Oord, Kalchbrenner and Kavukcuoglu, presented at I C M L in twenty sixteen. Each pixel is predicted from the pixels above it and to its left. Even the three colours of one pixel come one after the other: red, then green, then blue. A small colour picture, thirty-two by thirty-two pixels, is three thousand seventy-two values, to be predicted in a row.

In twenty twenty, Chen and colleagues did the same with a transformer, under the name Image G P T. Three thousand seventy-two tokens was too long for them, so they grouped the colours into five hundred twelve. That gives a thousand twenty-four tokens for a thirty-two by thirty-two picture.

Now the architecture, in one image. It is a transformer, from episode three point five, but one that may only look at the past. When it reads stroke number five, a mask hides stroke six and everything after it. In the paper that introduced the transformer, Vaswani and colleagues, in twenty seventeen, describe this mask as preventing positions from attending to subsequent positions. Thanks to it, training is fast: every position of a sequence is scored at once, each one blind to its own future. The first G P T, by Radford and colleagues in twenty eighteen, is exactly that: twelve layers of masked transformer, trained to predict the next word on the text of over seven thousand unpublished books.

Then comes the loop. To generate, the model reads the canvas and gives a list of probabilities for the next token. We draw one token from that list, and write it down. That token goes straight back in as input, to help predict the one after it. Round and round, until we stop.

What does the model learn? Not one answer, but a full distribution over the next piece. Drawing from it gives a different painting each time. And we have a knob, from episode two point five. The model gives raw scores, and a softmax turns them into probabilities. Divide the scores by a number T, the temperature, before the softmax. Take illustrative scores for the word after the cat sat on the: mat, two. Sofa, one. Roof, a half. Moon, minus one. At temperature one, mat gets sixty-one percent. At a half, the distribution sharpens: eighty-four percent, and moon almost never. At two, it flattens: forty-three percent for mat, and ten percent for moon. Cold means safe and repetitive. Hot means surprising, and sometimes nonsense.

Now the classic traps. The first is about what the painter has seen. In training, every stroke is predicted from the true canvas: the real previous words, the real previous pixels. This is called teacher forcing. At generation time, the canvas is her own work, mistakes included. Ranzato and colleagues, at I C L R in twenty sixteen, named this mismatch exposure bias, and warned that errors may quickly accumulate. Bengio and colleagues, at NeurIPS in twenty fifteen, proposed scheduled sampling: during training, sometimes show the model its own guesses instead of the truth.

A toy shows the worry. Suppose each step is right ninety-nine times in a hundred, and one wrong step spoils the painting. After ten steps, ninety percent of paintings are still fine. After a hundred, thirty-seven percent. After a thousand, almost none. This is illustrative. Real mistakes are not all fatal, and steps are not independent. How much exposure bias hurts large modern models is still discussed, so hold it with care. But it is the same snowball as the imagined rollouts of episode nine point three.

The second trap is time. To write n tokens, the model must run n times, one after the other, because each step needs the previous one as input. Training is parallel, thanks to the mask. Generation is not. A trick called the key value cache keeps the work already done on earlier tokens, so each new step does not read the whole canvas again. It saves recomputation. It does not remove the line: still one step after another.

One more thing to notice. Every stroke this painter predicts lives in the space of the data itself: a word, a pixel. She must settle every detail, even those that do not matter. Whether that is the right space to predict in is a question we will push on in episode six point five, Predicting every pixel, and why it costs, and again in the J E P A chapters.

To sum up. The chain rule turns any sequence into a product of next-step predictions, so a model that predicts the next piece can generate. A masked transformer scores every position at once, and a loop feeds each draw back in. The model learns a full distribution, and temperature sets how boldly we draw from it. The traps: errors can snowball, because the model trains on true canvases and generates on its own, and generation takes one step after another. Next time: diffusion models, where the painter starts from a canvas of fog and clears it, step by step.

PLATE 6.47:16

Diffusion models

A diffusion model adds fog to a picture in many small steps until only static remains, then learns to undo them. One closed-form jump reaches any fog level, and the network is trained to name the added noise. Sampling walks down the staircase from pure noise, which is slow; faster samplers and latent-space diffusion make it cheaper.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the painting studio. Last time, in autoregressive models, the painter added one stroke at a time. Today she works the other way round. She starts from pure fog, and clears it away, a little at a time. By the end of this video, you will know how a diffusion model works, what it learns, and why it is slow.

Here is the idea. Take a picture, and add a little fog. Not much. You can still see everything. Add a little more, and again, and again. After many small steps, nothing is left but grey static. Destroying a picture is easy. The clever part is the way back. Suppose a network could learn to undo just one small step of fog. Then we could start from pure static and undo step after step, until a picture appears. Picture a long staircase. At the top, pure fog. With every step down, the fog is a little thinner. At the bottom, a clear picture. A diffusion model walks down that staircase.

The idea has a physical origin. In twenty fifteen, Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues, inspired by nonequilibrium thermodynamics, gradually destroyed the structure of data with a forward diffusion, and learned a reverse process to restore it. Five years later, in twenty twenty, Ho, Jain and Abbeel made it work well on images, in a paper called Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models. It is known as D D P M. They used a thousand steps, and a loss that fits on one line.

Let's make the fog precise. At each step, we shrink the picture a little and add a little Gaussian noise. The amount of noise, called beta, grows linearly: from one ten thousandth at the first step, to two hundredths at step one thousand. Now comes a useful trick. Because the noise is Gaussian, we do not need to walk through all those steps to reach step t. One jump is enough. In symbols: x t equals the square root of alpha bar t, times x zero, plus the square root of one minus alpha bar t, times epsilon. Here x zero is the clean picture, epsilon is one draw of random noise, and alpha bar t is the product of one minus beta, over the first t steps. Two weights: how much picture is left, and how much noise has come in.

Here are the numbers for this schedule. At step two hundred fifty, the picture weight is zero point seven two, and the noise weight is zero point six nine. At step five hundred, zero point two eight against zero point nine six. At step seven hundred fifty, zero point zero six against one. At step one thousand, the picture weight is under one hundredth. Nothing is left but noise.

Now a toy, labelled illustrative. Points that sit in two tight clusters. At step two hundred fifty, the clusters blur. At step five hundred, they overlap. At step one thousand, there is one round cloud, and the two clusters are gone. Walking back means pushing the points of that round cloud, step by step, toward the places where data lives.

What does the network learn? We give it a noisy picture, x t, and the step number t. We ask one question: which noise was added? It answers with epsilon theta, a guess of epsilon. The loss is the squared difference between the true noise and the guess. That is the simple loss of D D P M, and we minimise it by gradient descent, as in episode two point four. Training is a short loop. Pick a clean picture. Pick a random step. Jump there in one go, with the formula. Ask the network for the noise. Take a gradient step. The same network serves every step of the staircase, and the step number tells it how foggy the picture is.

Now the architecture, in one picture. At the heart sits a single network, the denoiser. During training, DICE adds the noise, and the denoiser tries to name it. To generate a picture, we start from pure noise, ask the denoiser what noise it sees, and remove a little of it. Then DICE adds a small fresh dose of noise, and we ask again. The D D P M sampling rule does this at every step except the last one. A thousand calls to the denoiser, a thousand steps down the staircase, and a picture appears.

What has it learned? To climb back from noise to plausible images. And since the starting noise is random, every run gives a different picture. It is a distribution again, like in the earlier episodes of this chapter. In twenty twenty one, Song and colleagues showed that learning to predict this noise is closely related to learning the score of the data, the direction in which data becomes more likely. They unified diffusion models and score-based models in one framework.

The price is the staircase. One picture costs a thousand passes through the network. There are two classic fixes. The first is a cleverer way of walking down. In twenty twenty one, Song, Meng and Ermon proposed D D I M. It reuses the same trained network, and changes only the sampling rule. They report samples ten to fifty times faster than D D P M in wall-clock time. In their experiments, samples comparable to a thousand step model come within twenty to a hundred steps.

The second fix is to stop painting in pixels. In twenty twenty two, Rombach and colleagues ran the whole diffusion process in the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder, the one from episode six point one. Remember the latent space of episode four point two: a small necklace instead of a big image. Fog on a small necklace is far cheaper than fog on every pixel. They call it latent diffusion.

Where do we meet these ideas today? Here are three dated examples, as of November twenty twenty six, whose method is described in a paper or a report. In April twenty twenty two, the D A L L E two paper describes a decoder built with diffusion models. In August twenty twenty two, Stable Diffusion was released publicly, a latent diffusion model. In February twenty twenty four, OpenAI's Sora report describes text-conditional diffusion models, with a transformer working on patches of video. When a product's method is not documented, we claim nothing.

Now the classic pitfall. Sharp, realistic images are not proof that a model understands the scene. The Sora report itself says that the model does not accurately model the physics of many basic interactions, like glass shattering. This is only one documented example, not a measurement of the whole field. But it reminds us of the logic. A model can learn which pixels usually go together, without learning why they do. Whether predicting every pixel is the right goal is exactly the question of episode six point five: Predicting every pixel, and why it costs.

To sum up. A diffusion model adds fog to data in many small steps, and learns to undo them. One jump gives any fog level, and the network learns to name the noise. Sampling means walking down the staircase, which is slow, and D D I M and latent diffusion make it cheaper. Next time: predicting every pixel, and why it costs.

PLATE 6.54:33

Predicting every pixel, and why it costs

Trained with squared error, a model asked for the next second of a tree in the wind outputs the average of possible futures, a leaf blurred everywhere, and for that loss the blur is the right answer. Pixel prediction also spends effort on unforeseeable detail. Masked autoencoders make pixels useful by hiding three quarters of patches, while LeCun argues for abstract prediction.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the painting studio. Every model in this chapter paints. Today we ask what it costs to predict every single pixel. By the end of this video, you will know why a model trained on pixels paints a blur, how masked autoencoders use pixels anyway, and what the case is for predicting something else.

Picture a video of a tree in the wind. We show the model one second, and ask for the next second. The leaf near the top could swing left, swing right, stay put, or drop. Say it has five equally likely places to go. Each possible future is a tracing sheet, with the leaf drawn in one spot. Five sheets, five futures, and the wind decides which one happens.

Now train the model with squared error, the loss from chapter one. It may hand in only one sheet. It is scored by how far its pixels are from the truth, squared. So what is the best single sheet? Stack the five tracing sheets and average them. We made a toy of this: a row of thirty pixels, and a leaf that is a small bright blob. The average has five faint bumps, none brighter than one fifth of a real leaf. That is the blur. A leaf that is a little bit everywhere, and it matches none of the five futures.

Here is the odd part. For this loss, the blur is the right answer. Its average error is zero point zero six one. Pick one sharp future and bet on it, and the average error doubles, to zero point one two two. The model is not failing. It is doing exactly what we asked. Squared error pays for the average, and the average of many futures is not a future. In episode 2.5, a model could say twenty percent for each answer. A single painted image cannot say that.

Predicting pixels also costs a lot of numbers. A small color image, two hundred and twenty-four pixels on a side, holds one hundred and fifty thousand, five hundred and twenty-eight of them. Three color values for each of fifty thousand pixels. Every one of those numbers gets a share of the loss, including the shimmer of a leaf that nobody could have foreseen.

Yet pixels are not useless. In November 2021, Kaiming He and colleagues posted masked autoencoders, published at CVPR in 2022. Cut the image into sixteen by sixteen patches. That gives one hundred and ninety-six of them. Now hide seventy-five percent. A hundred and forty-seven patches are gone, and forty-nine stay. The encoder, like the one in episode 6.1, sees only the visible patches. A decoder then repaints the hidden pixels, and the loss is squared error on those patches alone. Neighboring patches look so alike that hiding a few is too easy. Hide three quarters, and the model has to understand the scene to fill it in.

Now an argument, and it is an argument, not a settled fact. In June 2022, Yann LeCun posted a position paper called A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence. His case goes like this. A model that generates pixels is forced to account for details that are hard to predict, like the exact flutter of each leaf. A model that predicts an abstract description is free to ignore them. Think of episode 4.3: a good representation keeps what is needed, and throws the rest away. So instead of five sheets with a leaf on each, predict a few beads: a tree, wind, leaves moving.

The other side answers right away. Recent video generators produce sharp, coherent video, with no blur at all. In February 2024, OpenAI's Sora report called such models world simulators. Some researchers see them as world models. Others doubt that a sharp picture means understanding. Notice what these generators do differently: they draw one plausible future from the many, instead of painting the average. We will come back to this debate in episodes 10.5 and 13.1.

To sum up. Squared error rewards the average, and the average of many futures is a blur. Predicting every pixel spends effort on details no one can foresee. Masked autoencoders turn pixels into a useful game: hide three quarters, repaint them. LeCun argues for predicting an abstract description instead, and video generators give the sharp reply.
