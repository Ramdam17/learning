No common frame

Two brains, or a brain and a model, share no columns. Shuffling the twenty channels of one system puts it 64.41 from itself, farther than from a cloud of zeros, yet the 190 distances between stimuli are identical. Comparing tables of distances gives 0.973 for two systems reading the same hidden structure and minus 0.01 for unrelated ones, read between a floor and a ceiling.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every chapter so far shared one assumption. The two things we compared had the same columns. Column three on the left was column three on the right.

Two brains do not give you that. Neuron twelve in one brain is not neuron twelve in another. Channel seven of one headset is not channel seven of another montage. And a model has no voxels at all. Comparing two brains, or a brain and a model, means comparing two spaces with no common frame. This chapter is about what you can still do.

Here is the example we will use until episode 12.5. Twenty stimuli are shown to two systems. System A answers on twenty channels. System B answers on thirty. Each system gives a cloud of twenty points, one per stimulus. Everything here is simulated, with seed twelve.

Let us try the straight line of chapter two. We would subtract channel one of system A from channel one of system B. But nothing says that these two channels correspond. And system B has ten more channels than system A. You may think this is a detail. So let us take system A alone, and make the detail visible.

Shuffle the order of its twenty channels. It is the same system, and the same answers, written in another order. Now measure the straight line between system A and its shuffled copy: 64.41. For comparison, measure system A twice, with the same channels and new noise: 14.96. And the whole cloud of system A sits only 48.46 away from the cloud where every answer is zero. The same system is farther from itself than from nothing.

Why so large? When channels are paired up wrongly, each difference is the gap between two unrelated channels, so the errors add up instead of cancelling. The straight line was never measuring the system. It was measuring how well the columns lined up.

Yet something has not moved. Take the distance between two stimuli, in system A. Then take it again in the shuffled copy. There are 190 pairs of stimuli, and the 190 distances are identical before and after the shuffle. Not close: identical, to the precision of the computer.

Any rotation of the channels does the same. A shuffle is the simplest rotation: it only swaps axes. A turn by any other angle changes every number in the table of answers, and leaves every distance between two stimuli as it was. Distances belong to the cloud, not to the axes we chose to write it in.

That is the thread of this chapter. Without a common frame, what a system says is read in the relations between its points, not in its columns. The idea is old in psychology. In 1970, Shepard and Chipman argued that the match between an object and its internal representation should be sought in the relations among objects, not in a one-to-one copy. They called it second-order isomorphism.

Now the full example. Three hidden directions organise the twenty stimuli, with spreads of 2, 1.5 and 1. System A reads them through twenty channels, with some noise. Its second measurement, with new noise, we call A again. System B reads the same three hidden directions through thirty channels, with weights that have nothing to do with those of system A. And system C reads three other hidden directions, through twenty channels.

A good comparison must say three things. System A and A again: almost identical. System A and system B: close, because they read the same hidden structure. System A and system C: nothing in common.

First try, the one of episode 7.4. For each system, build the table of distances between its twenty answers. This is the second visual code of the series: the darker the cell, the farther the pair. Both tables are twenty by twenty, whatever the number of channels. System B has thirty channels, and its table is still twenty by twenty. That is the trick: the stimuli are the common frame we do have. Then correlate two tables, 190 numbers against 190 numbers. System A against A again: 0.988. System A against system B: 0.973. System A against system C: minus 0.01. The three answers are the right ones. And the shuffled copy of system A scores exactly one.

A caution about the word correlation. It is not a distance in the sense of chapter one. Here it is only a score of resemblance between two tables, and episode 12.3 will say how to read it properly.

Two reference points keep such a score honest. The ceiling is the same system measured twice. The floor is two unrelated systems of the same sizes. To see them, we repeat the whole example 2000 times with fresh clouds, simulated with seed 121. The ceiling averages 0.989. The floor averages zero, and nine times out of ten it falls between minus 0.19 and plus 0.21. So minus 0.01 is an ordinary floor value. And across the draws, system A against system B averages 0.927: below the ceiling, far above the floor.

What comes next is ordered by what the two systems still share. If the same stimuli were shown to both, we can fit one cloud onto the other, in episodes 12.2 and 12.4, or compare their tables, in episodes 12.3 and 12.5. If they share nothing, not even the stimuli, we must also find which row goes with which row: episodes 12.6 and 12.7. And if the object is a movement, the order of the points matters: episode 12.8. Each method decides what it forgives: a rotation of the channels, any mixing of the channels, or everything except the order of the distances.

One trap runs through all of them, the one of episode 10.5. The more a comparison forgives, the more resemblance it finds between systems that have nothing in common. That is why every score of this chapter comes with its floor and its ceiling.

So: without a common frame, do not compare answers, compare relations between answers. Shuffle the channels, and the straight line says 64.41, while the 190 distances say nothing has changed. Tables first. And a score is read between its floor and its ceiling.

Turn one onto the other

Two clouds of the same twenty stimuli can be centred, scaled and turned onto each other, leaving an angle: 10.2 degrees between systems A and B, 63.0 for unrelated C. But with a thousand channels, pure noise fits to 5.7 degrees. Fitting on ten stimuli and measuring on the other ten exposes it: noise gives 90.0. Hyperalignment recognised 70.6 percent of film segments.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Inside the booth, one interpreter listens and another speaks. Two voices, one message, and no common words.

Last time, we met two systems that saw the same twenty stimuli and share no frame. System A has twenty channels, system B has thirty. We saw that you cannot subtract their columns. We also saw what survives: the points, and the relations between them.

Today we use the points. If the two clouds have the same shape, one of them should fit onto the other after a turn. So let us turn one onto the other.

You have seen this recipe already, in episode 10.6, on landmarks. Here the points are the twenty stimuli, and the space we turn in is the space of channels.

Here is a picture with just two channels, made up for the occasion. Twenty stimuli, read by two systems. The second system reads them turned, bigger, and shifted.

Step one: centre. Slide each cloud so that its average sits at the origin.

Step two: scale. Divide each cloud by its size, so that both have size one.

Step three: turn. Rotate one cloud, in the space of channels, until it lies as close as possible to the other.

You do not search for this turn by trial and error. It has a direct answer. Multiply the two centred clouds into one small matrix, one row and one column for each channel, and take its singular value decomposition. Green solved a first case in nineteen fifty-two. Schönemann gave the general solution in nineteen sixty-six.

What if the second system has more channels? Then add columns of zeros to the first one, until both have the same number. Nothing else changes.

What is left is a gap, read as an angle. Zero degrees means two clouds that lie exactly on top of each other. Ninety degrees means two clouds with nothing in common. In our picture, the two systems start 105.1 degrees apart. After the fit, 4.6 degrees remain.

Now the real example, with twenty stimuli. First, take system A and a copy of itself, with its channels turned by a random rotation. Before the fit, 96.2 degrees. After the fit, zero point zero. The turn is found exactly.

The fit also accepts a mirror. Flip the sign of one channel, and the angle is still zero.

Now system A versus its own second measurement, with a new draw of noise: 9.3 degrees. System A versus system B: 10.2 degrees. System A versus system C, which reads other hidden directions: 63.0 degrees.

The order is right. The same system twice is closest, B is close, C is far.

But look at C. Sixty-three degrees is not ninety. These two systems have nothing in common, and the fit still found a good deal of resemblance. This is the trap of episode 10.5: a minimum taken over all rotations always finds something.

So how much does it find by chance? Take two clouds of twenty points drawn independently, with no structure at all. Fit one onto the other. Averaged over two hundred draws, simulated with seed one two two, the angle is 74.9 degrees with two channels. 65.9 with five. 41.0 with twenty. 18.1 with a hundred. And 5.7 with a thousand.

Read the last one again. With a thousand channels and twenty stimuli, pure noise does better than system A does with system B. Many channels leave a lot of room to turn, and twenty points are easy to place in that room.

With a hundred points, the floors are higher: 83.4 degrees for two channels, 12.9 for a thousand. More points leave less room.

So an angle after the fit means nothing on its own. It has to be read against this floor, which depends on the number of channels and on the number of stimuli.

Here is the remedy. Do not judge on the points that were used to fit.

Fit the turn on ten stimuli. Then measure the angle on the other ten, which the fit has never seen.

From A to B: 6.5 degrees on the stimuli that were used, and 20.7 on the others. From A to C: 54.0 and 76.2. The test separates the two cases much better.

Now the worst case. Two noise clouds, a thousand channels, twenty stimuli. Averaged over one hundred draws: 4.1 degrees on the fitted stimuli, and 90.0 on the others. The fit looks perfect, and the test says there is nothing there.

And now a true structure, read through a thousand channels by two systems, with noise. Averaged over one hundred draws: 2.1 degrees on the fitted stimuli, and 19.2 on the others. Only the second number speaks.

This is the principle of hyperalignment, by Haxby and colleagues, in 2011.

Twenty-one people watched the same film while an fMRI scanner recorded their brains. A voxel is a small cube of brain, where the scanner measures activity. Here each voxel plays the role of a channel. The fMRI series presents voxels in its episode 2.5.

For each person, the authors took one thousand voxels of the ventral temporal cortex, and turned that space towards a common space. Then they tested on what had not been used to fit: which eighteen-second segment of the other half of the film is this person watching, judging from the other people?

After the turn, 70.6 percent of the segments are recognised. With the anatomical alignment alone, 32.0 percent. Chance is below one percent.

On seven categories of images, from another experiment, it is 63.9 percent against 44.6.

A variant looks directly for a small common space. It was proposed by Chen and colleagues in 2015.

Now the card of this distance. The object: two clouds of the same stimuli, in two different spaces of channels. The distance: an angle, after centring, scaling and the best turn.

What it ignores: position, overall size, and any rotation or mirror of the channels. It does not ignore the stretching of a single channel. Multiply one channel of system B by ten, and the angle between system A and system B jumps from 10.2 to 25.7 degrees.

What it costs: one singular value decomposition, of a matrix as large as the number of channels, for each pair.

Its trap: the floor, which depends on the number of channels and of stimuli. An angle after the fit can only be read on stimuli that were kept apart.

So, here is the idea to keep. Fitting and measuring on the same points measures the fit. Keep some stimuli aside, and read the angle there.

Compare the tables, not the points

Systems with different channels can be compared through tables of distances between the same twenty stimuli, each holding 190 numbers. Correlating the ranks of those distances gives 0.964 for systems A and B and minus 0.015 for an unrelated one. Noise lowers these correlations, so a score is read between a floor from shuffled labels and a ceiling from the same system measured twice.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two interpreters sit in two glass booths. They share no vocabulary at all. How can you tell whether they mean the same thing?

We had the same problem last time, with two systems and no common frame. System A reads twenty stimuli through twenty channels. System B reads the same stimuli through thirty. Which channel of one matches which channel of the other? Nobody knows.

Episode 12.1 gave the way out. Shuffle the channels of system A, and the distances between its stimuli do not move. So today we look for no rotation at all. We compare tables.

For system A, take its twenty responses, one per stimulus. Measure the straight-line distance between every pair. Twenty stimuli make one hundred ninety pairs, so the table holds one hundred ninety numbers. The darker the cell, the farther the pair.

Do the same for system B. It has ten more channels, but its table has the same size: twenty by twenty. Whatever the number of channels, a table speaks only about stimuli. Now the two tables can sit side by side.

This is representational similarity analysis, by Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini, in 2008. The table is called a representational dissimilarity matrix, or R D M.

How do we compare two tables? Each of the one hundred ninety pairs has a distance in system A and a distance in system B. Draw one dot per pair. If the two systems agree, the dots climb together.

Episode 3.4 told us why we use ranks: often we trust only the order of the distances. So replace each distance by its rank, from the smallest to the largest. Then take the ordinary correlation of the ranks. That is the Spearman correlation.

A small example. Three distances: 2.1, 7.5 and 4.0. Their ranks are 1, 3 and 2. Multiply the distances by three, and you get 6.3, 22.5 and 12.0. The ranks are still 1, 3 and 2.

It differs a little from the correlation of episode 12.1, which used the raw distances. For system A against a second measurement of itself, that gave 0.988. With ranks, it gives 0.983.

Now the three comparisons. System A against its own second measurement: 0.983. System A against system B: 0.964. System A against the unrelated system C: minus 0.015.

What does this score ignore? Rotate the channels of B, and multiply everything by three. The score stays at 0.964. Square every distance, and the score stays the same. Any change that keeps the order of the distances is invisible.

But multiply one single channel of B by ten, and the score falls to 0.769. The table depends on the distance that fills it. Everything we learned in the earlier chapters applies to that choice.

The founding example compares two species. Ninety-two images are shown to humans in a scanner. F M R I measures small cubes of brain, called voxels. The fMRI series presents them in its episode 2.5. The same images are shown to monkeys, and six hundred seventy-four neurons are recorded in their inferior temporal cortex.

No voxel matches any neuron. Yet each species gives a table of ninety-two by ninety-two. The question is no longer which voxel matches which neuron. It is whether both systems put the same images close together, and the same images far apart. The two tables correlate at 0.49, say Kriegeskorte and colleagues in 2008.

Is 0.49 far from zero? Is 0.964? We need a reference. The one hundred ninety cells of a table are not independent: each stimulus sits in nineteen of them.

So shuffle the labels of the stimuli in one table, rows and columns together. Compute the score again. Repeat, thousands of times. This permutation test is known as the Mantel test, after a paper by Nathan Mantel in 1967.

Here are ten thousand shuffles. Between system A and system C, fifty-three percent of the shuffles do at least as well as the real pair. That means nothing. Between system A and system B, not one shuffle of the ten thousand comes close. The best reaches 0.41.

The real trap is noise, and it has two faces.

First, noise lowers the correlation between tables. Add noise with a standard deviation of 0.5, then 2, 4 and 8. This is simulated, a mean over five hundred draws.

The same system measured twice gives 0.98, 0.70, 0.27 and 0.04. System A against B gives 0.96, 0.73, 0.33 and 0.05.

So a correlation of 0.3 between two systems says nothing by itself. Read it between two reference points. The floor is what shuffled labels give. The ceiling is what the same system gives against itself, its noise ceiling, as Nili and colleagues called it in 2014.

At a noise of 4, system A against B reaches 0.33. That is no failure. It is more than the system reaches against itself. B has ten more channels, so its distances are a little less noisy. Without the ceiling, 0.3 could be a failure, or the best score possible.

The two references answer two different questions. Shuffled labels ask: could chance give this? The same system twice asks: how much could any system give, with this noise?

Second face: noise inflates the distances. Take two identical conditions, twenty channels, and a noise of 1. The distance we measure is about 6.2, instead of 0. A true distance of 3 is measured at about 6.9. Squared, that is about 40 and 49.

The remedy crosses two halves of the data. Multiply the difference measured in one half by the difference measured in the other. The two noises are independent, so they cancel on average.

The squared distance then averages 0.09 when the truth is 0, and 9.07 when the truth is 9. That is the cross-validated distance, from Walther and colleagues in 2016, and from Diedrichsen and colleagues in 2021. The estimate can be negative, and that is normal.

Here is the distance card. The object is two tables of distances, with the same stimuli in rows and columns. The score is the rank correlation of the tables, or one minus it, as a distance.

What does it ignore? Position, any rotation or mirror of the channels, and the number of channels. With ranks, it also ignores any change that keeps the order of the distances.

What does it cost? Two tables, so the square of the number of stimuli. Add thousands of shuffles for the test.

Its trap is noise. It raises the distances, and it lowers the correlations.

On the lights: it is never negative. It is the same both ways. Two different tables with the same order get zero. I leave the shortcut rule dashed, because I have not checked it.

Remember: a correlation between two tables is read between two references. The floor, given by shuffled labels. And the ceiling, given by the same system measured twice.

PLATE 12.47:03

The fit that forgives too much

Canonical correlation forgives any invertible mixing of channels, and that is too much. With twenty stimuli and twenty channels, all nineteen canonical correlations equal one even for an unrelated system, so floor and ceiling coincide. It also ignores variance: with three strong shared signals the Procrustes angle is 15.5 degrees, but with seventeen weak shared ones it is 81.6.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, one system was allowed to be turned to fit another. Only a turn. A rotation of the channels, and nothing else.

This time we forgive more. Any mixing of the channels is allowed. Stretches, squeezes, shears. And the question for this episode is simple: can a comparison forgive too much?

The idea is due to Harold Hotelling, in nineteen thirty-six. Two systems answered the same stimuli. In the first system, look for a mix of channels. In the second system, look for another mix. Choose both so that their values across the stimuli are as correlated as possible. That is the first canonical correlation. Plot it: each stimulus is a dot, with its value in the first mix across, and its value in the second mix up. A correlation of one means that all the dots sit on a line.

Then look for a second pair of mixes, uncorrelated with the first pair. Then a third, and so on. In chapter nine, we met these numbers as cosines of the angles between two subspaces. Here they are correlations between mixes.

A simulation. Two systems, two hundred stimuli, twenty channels each. Both read the same three strong hidden signals, which hold ninety-five percent of the variance. Each system also has seventeen weak signals of its own.

The first pair of mixes is perfectly correlated: one. So are the second and the third. The fourth pair gives a cloud of dots, not a line: zero point five four. the fifth zero point five zero, and the rest fall away. These weak ones are private noise, and we will see in a moment how much noise can fake. The mean of the twenty numbers is zero point three seven.

Now take the channels of the first system and mix them with a random matrix. Any invertible mixing. The twenty correlations do not move. The mean is still zero point three seven.

That is a very wide invariance. Rotations, yes, but also stretches and shears. And it is paid twice.

The first price is the floor. Take two independent noises, a hundred points each, with the same number of channels on both sides. Nothing is shared. How large is the best canonical correlation? These are means over two hundred simulated draws, seed one hundred twenty-four.

With five channels on each side, the largest correlation is zero point three seven, and the mean of all of them zero point one nine. With ten channels: zero point five five, and zero point two seven. With twenty-five: zero point eight five, and zero point four four. With forty: zero point nine eight, and zero point five six. With fifty, the largest is one, and the mean is zero point six four.

With sixty channels on each side, twenty-one correlations are exactly one.

There is a rule. Add the channels of both sides, subtract the number of points, and add one. Sixty plus sixty is a hundred and twenty. A hundred points leave ninety-nine directions. A hundred and twenty minus ninety-nine is twenty-one. In all our draws, the count matched this rule exactly.

So once the two systems together have more channels than there are points, you can always find mixes that coincide. Nothing is shared, and the match is perfect.

Now our running example. Twenty stimuli. System A has twenty channels. System B has thirty, and reads the same hidden signals. System C has twenty channels, and reads other hidden signals. It has nothing to do with the first two.

Centring means subtracting the mean of each channel. It costs one direction, so twenty stimuli leave nineteen. After centring, each system spans nineteen directions. Between systems A and B, there are nineteen canonical correlations. All of them are exactly one.

Between systems A and C, the unrelated pair? Nineteen correlations. All exactly one.

The ceiling is the same system measured twice. It is also one. The floor and the ceiling are the same number. The method can no longer see that system C has nothing to do with system A.

This is general. In twenty nineteen, Kornblith and colleagues proved that a measure unchanged by invertible linear mixing gives the same value for any representation whose width is at least the number of examples. With as many channels as stimuli, it forgives everything.

It is the trap of episode ten point five again. The more a comparison forgives, the more resemblance it finds between strangers. More forgiveness, less distinction.

The second price is that variance stops counting. Back to two hundred stimuli. Case one is the one you just saw: three strong shared signals, and seventeen weak private ones. In case two, we swap the roles. The seventeen weak signals are shared, and the three strong ones are private. The shared part now holds only five percent of the variance.

In case one, the mean canonical correlation is zero point three seven. The Procrustes angle of the last episode is fifteen point five degrees, a small angle. And the kernel alignment of the next episode is zero point nine nine nine five, almost one.

In case two, the mean canonical correlation is zero point eight six. Seventeen of the twenty correlations are above zero point nine nine. The Procrustes angle is eighty-one point six degrees. And the kernel alignment is zero point zero one three.

The two families of measures contradict each other, and neither is wrong. Both are computed on the very same clouds. One counts the shared directions. The other weighs the shared variance. You must decide which of the two questions you are asking.

Two remedies are common. Raghu and colleagues, in twenty seventeen, first keep only the principal components that carry ninety-nine percent of the variance, and then compute the correlations. Morcos, Raghu and Bengio, in twenty eighteen, weight each correlation by how much of the system it accounts for.

Now the card. The object: two sets of responses to the same stimuli. The score: the canonical correlations, usually summarised by their mean. It is a similarity score, and we leave the four rules unchecked. What it ignores: any invertible mixing of each system's channels, and with it the variance of every direction. What it costs: one decomposition for each pair. And the trap: the number of channels against the number of points. Before reading a canonical correlation, compute what two noises of the same sizes would give.

So, remember. Allowing every mixing is generous, and generosity has a floor. Before you trust a match, measure two unrelated systems of the same sizes, and the same system measured twice. If the channels are not fewer than the points, the answer is already one.

PLATE 12.57:35

One number for two tables

Centred kernel alignment compares two tables of dot products between stimuli, giving 0.982 for systems A and B against 0.164 for the unrelated one. It is biased: two unrelated noises over thirty stimuli score 0.971 with a thousand channels. One extreme point can decide it, it hears only high-variance directions, and one minus it breaks the triangle rule, 0.586 by a detour against 1.000 directly.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two glass booths, and one dial above them. Today we build the dial.

Episode 7.4 measured the dependence between two measurements taken on the same objects. It did so by comparing their two tables. Two systems that answer the same stimuli are exactly that case.

System A reads twenty stimuli through twenty channels. System B reads the same stimuli through thirty. Their channels do not match, so we leave the channels behind, and look only at how the stimuli relate to each other.

For each system, fill a table of dot products. One number for every pair of stimuli, twenty by twenty, whatever the number of channels.

Centre each table, so that every row and every column adds up to zero. Divide each table by its own size. Then multiply the two tables cell by cell, and add everything up. Where two stimuli look alike in both systems, the product is large and positive. Where one system says alike and the other says opposite, it is negative.

That sum is the centred kernel alignment. It is zero when the tables share nothing, and one when they are the same up to a scale. It comes from Cortes and colleagues, in 2012, and Kornblith and colleagues proposed it in 2019 to compare neural networks. With the plain dot product, it is an older coefficient from statistics, the R V coefficient, by Robert and Escoufier, in 1976.

On our example. System A against a second measurement of itself: 0.992. System A against system B: 0.982. System A against the unrelated system C: 0.164.

Every score needs two reference points. Measured twice, systems built like these give 0.992 on average over five hundred draws. That is the ceiling. Two unrelated systems of the same sizes give 0.114 on average. That is the floor. System A against system C sits near the floor, and system A against system B near the ceiling.

What does the alignment ignore? Shift every channel of B by a constant: 0.982. Multiply everything by five: 0.982. Rotate the channels of B: 0.982. The table does not care.

But multiply one single channel by ten, and the score falls to 0.826. That channel now dominates the table.

A random mixing of the channels can change the score too. In the first case of episode 12.4, it goes from 0.9995 to 0.834. The alignment forgives less than the canonical correlations, and that is on purpose.

What does it cost? Two tables, so the square of the number of stimuli. Twenty stimuli: four hundred cells each. A thousand stimuli: a million.

Now the traps. There are four.

The first is the bias. Draw two clouds of thirty points, independently, so they have nothing to do with each other. With one channel, the alignment is 0.029. With ten channels, 0.254. With a hundred, 0.770. With a thousand, 0.971. These are means over two hundred draws. With a hundred points, the same four values are 0.010, 0.090, 0.500 and 0.909.

Two unrelated noises look almost identical as soon as the channels outnumber the stimuli. Neural recordings often are in that case.

A corrected version exists. The correction is older, from Székely and Rizzo, in 2014, and Murphy and colleagues showed in 2024 that the corrected alignment stays near zero on random data. On our draws, it averages zero, give or take 0.01.

So a number between zero and one is not a distance, and 0.97 is not a resemblance. Two unrelated noises, read by a thousand channels over thirty stimuli, give 0.971.

The second trap is one single point. The alignment weighs variance, so a few extreme points can decide it. Two independent clouds, a hundred points, ten channels: 0.092 on average over two hundred draws.

Now move one point, the same one in both clouds, five standard deviations from the centre in every channel: 0.327. Ten: 0.772. Twenty: 0.947. Fifty: 0.992.

The third trap: the alignment hears only the directions that carry a lot of variance. Take case 2 of episode 12.4. Seventeen weak directions are shared, and three strong ones are private. The canonical correlations averaged 0.861. The alignment says 0.013. The shared directions carry only 5.4 percent of the variance.

Davari and colleagues, in 2023, pushed this to the limit. They moved part of a network's representations in a direction that a linear classifier does not care about. The alignment fell to zero, and the classifier stayed above 90 percent.

The opposite happens too. In the last layer of the language model Bert, 97 percent of the principal components had to be deleted before the alignment differed from what two training runs give. By then, a linear reading had dropped from 80 percent accuracy to 63, where chance is 50. That is from Ding, Denain and Steinhardt, in 2021.

The fourth trap: one minus the alignment is not a distance. Take three systems. The first reads one direction. The third reads another. The second reads both. The alignments are 0.707, 0.707, and zero.

One minus each: 0.293 and 0.293 by the detour, 1.000 directly. The detour adds up to 0.586. It is shorter than the direct way. The triangle rule is broken.

In angles, everything falls back in place: 45 degrees and 45 degrees by the detour, 90 directly. So Williams and colleagues built distances between representations that respect the rule, starting from the Procrustes angle of episode 12.2. With them, you can use clustering and nearest neighbours, which assume the rule.

And the violations do happen. With one minus the mean canonical correlation, 17 of the 1,128 pairs among 48 regions of the mouse brain had at least one. With the rank correlation of tables, 14.

Here is the card. The object: two tables of dot products. The measure: the centred cell-by-cell product, rescaled. It ignores position, rotation, mirror and global scale. It costs two tables. And its trap: a value near one can come from the number of channels, or from a single point.

Remember: read every alignment between its floor and its ceiling. A number between zero and one is not a distance.

When nothing is matched

When no point is matched across two clouds, the matching becomes part of the question. Gromov-Hausdorff takes half the smallest worst disagreement between distances over all correspondences: for zero and one against zero, one and five it is 2.0. It ignores position, rotation, mirror and names, but one stray point at distance five makes a square jump to 1.81.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, the same twenty stimuli were the common rows of both clouds. They told us which point went with which. Take them away.

What is left is two clouds of points. Each one is known only by its own table of distances. You do not know which point of the first cloud goes with which point of the second. You may not even know that they have the same number of points.

Episode three point five already has a distance for two sets of points: the Hausdorff distance. Take each point of one set, find its nearest neighbour in the other set, and keep the worst of these distances. Do the same the other way round, and keep the larger of the two.

But look at what it measures. It goes from a point of one set to a point of the other set, inside one common space. Here there is no common space. A point of the first cloud has no place in the second one. So that distance does not apply.

The idea is to look for the matching and the distance at the same time.

A correspondence is a list of pairs. Every point of each cloud appears in at least one pair, so nobody is left without a partner. A point may have several partners.

Now take one correspondence. Pick two points of the first cloud, and look at their two partners in the second. Each pair of points has a distance in its own cloud. Compare the two distances. The gap between them is a disagreement.

Do this for every pair of points, and keep the worst disagreement. That is the price of this correspondence. Then try other correspondences, and keep the one whose price is the smallest.

Half of that smallest price is the Gromov-Hausdorff distance. Edwards defined it in nineteen seventy-five, and Gromov in nineteen eighty-one.

Here is the small example of episode three point five. The first set is zero and one, on a line. The second set is zero, one and five. Hausdorff said four.

Here, the point five needs a partner. There are twenty-five correspondences to try. Pair the point five with the point one. In the first set, the point one is at distance zero from itself. In the second set, one and five are four apart. The disagreement is four. No correspondence does better, so the smallest price is four, and the distance is half of it: two point zero.

Why half? A disagreement of four means that the two clouds could be glued into one space, with every pair of partners placed two apart. Each cloud gives way by half.

Two numbers for the same sets. Hausdorff compared positions. This compares distances. It never needed the two clouds to meet.

Now a few more small examples. Each one was computed by trying every correspondence.

An equilateral triangle of side one, against one of side two: zero point five. A square of side one, against four points in a row, one apart: zero point seven nine. Look at the worst pair. Two opposite corners are one point four apart, and their partners are three apart.

The same square, against four points that are all one apart from each other: zero point two one. The square, and the same square with its centre added: zero point three five. The square against a single point: zero point seven one, half of its diagonal. The square against a rectangle of two by one: zero point five.

Now a triangle with three different sides, and its image in a mirror. The distance is zero.

So what does this distance ignore? Run the row of changes on that triangle. Move it: zero. Rotate it: zero. Flip it in a mirror: zero. Rename its points, in any order: zero. Only noise is seen. With simulated noise of zero point one five on each coordinate, it reads zero point one nine.

Everything that keeps the distances is ignored, mirror included, and nobody had to decide it, unlike the choice we made with Procrustes in episode ten point six. It also needs no common space at all.

It is a true distance between spaces: never negative, zero only when the two clouds have the same distances, the same both ways, and the triangle rule holds. And when two clouds do live in the same space, it is never larger than their Hausdorff distance. For the noisy triangle, Hausdorff says zero point three seven.

What does it cost? The search for the correspondence. Between two clouds of four points there are sixteen possible pairs. A correspondence keeps some of them, so there are sixty-five thousand five hundred thirty-six subsets to look at. Forty-one thousand five hundred and three of them leave nobody out.

If you only allow one-to-one matchings, ten points already give three million six hundred twenty-eight thousand eight hundred of them. Twenty points give about two point four billion billion. No fast general method is known. Even between two trees, getting within a factor of three of the answer is a hard problem in the computer-science sense, shown by Agarwal and colleagues in twenty eighteen.

Now the trap. It is the trap of Hausdorff: this distance hears only the worst disagreement. Take the square, and add one single point, placed at distance five. The distance jumps to one point eight one. One point decides everything.

So this distance is mostly a theoretical reference. It inspires methods that compare two surfaces that bend without stretching, an open hand and a closed hand, by the distances measured along each surface. Elad and Kimmel did this in two thousand three, and Mémoli and Sapiro in two thousand five. And in chapter fourteen, it will guarantee that a summary of shape moves little when the cloud moves little.

When you do not know which point goes with which, the matching is part of the question.

To remember. The object is a cloud known only by its table of distances. The distance is half the worst disagreement of the best correspondence. It ignores position, rotation, mirror, names, and any common space. It costs a search over correspondences. Its trap is one stray point.

Matching by mass

Giving every point a mass and paying on average gives the Gromov-Wasserstein distance, which also returns a matching. A spiral of 40 points is matched to its shuffled, rotated twin; with noise 0.05, 74 percent of points are correct. Only 7 percent of random starts reach the best cost, so several starts are needed, and an ellipse admits four exact matchings.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the matching became part of the question. Gromov-Hausdorff keeps the matching whose worst disagreement is smallest, and one far point can decide everything.

Chapter 6 had a cure for worst-case rules. Give every point a weight, and pay on average. Today we make that same gesture again, with no common frame.

Each cloud now comes with two things. A table of the distances between its own points, and a mass on each point. Here, equal masses. Nothing else. No coordinates, and no shared axes.

A plan of transport is a table. One row for each point of the first cloud, one column for each point of the second. Each cell says how much mass goes from that row to that column. The rows add up to the masses of the first cloud, and the columns to the masses of the second.

What changes is the price. In chapter 6, a trip cost its weight times its length, and that needs a common ground. Here, trips are priced in pairs.

Take two points of the first cloud, and the two points where the plan sends them. Compare the gap between the first two with the gap between the other two. Square the disagreement. Weigh it by the masses of both trips. Add up over every pair of trips. That is the cost of the plan.

The cheapest plan gives the distance. It is the Gromov-Wasserstein distance, defined by Mémoli in 2011, and by Sturm, from another angle, in 2006. Take the square root of the least cost, and halve it. Then it sits on the same footing as Gromov-Hausdorff, which is also a half.

Try it on the small clouds of last time. One cloud is the points 0 and 1 on a line. The other is 0, 1 and 5. Equal masses.

The cheapest plan is on the screen. The point at 5 receives a third of the mass, all of it from the point at 0. The least cost is 6.5, and the distance is 1.27. Gromov-Hausdorff said 2.

Now push the far point out to 50. With a third of the mass on it, the distance is 16.3. Gromov-Hausdorff is 24.5, because it pays for that single worst pair.

Shrink the mass on the far point to 1 percent. Gromov-Wasserstein falls to 3.4. Gromov-Hausdorff does not move, because it never heard of mass. The far point no longer decides alone.

The result is not only a number. It is a matching. Here is a spiral of 40 points in the plane. Its twin is the same spiral, turned at random in three dimensions, moved far away, with its 40 points shuffled. We keep only the two tables of distances, and we ask the plan who goes with whom.

Neighbours on the spiral are about 0.19 apart. We add noise to the twin, and we run 20 simulated trials at each level. Without noise, every point finds its partner in all 20 trials. With noise 0.02, again all of them.

With noise 0.05, 74 percent of the points are matched correctly on average. In the worst trial, only 10 percent. In one trial out of four, all of them. With noise 0.1, 43 percent.

How big is the distance itself? As in every episode of this chapter, we need two reference points. The ceiling is the spiral against its own noisy twin. At noise 0.02, the distance is 0.014. The floor is the spiral against 40 unrelated points with the same overall spread. On average over 20 draws, 0.19, and never below 0.17.

Even at noise 0.1, the distance is 0.09, about half the floor. The two clouds still look alike to the score, while fewer than half of the points are matched correctly. The score and the matching are two different questions.

Why do the matches fall apart? Because the search has more than one valley. It starts from a plan, walks downhill, and stops in the nearest valley. Above, it started from the flat plan, where every point spreads its mass evenly.

Start instead from 200 random plans on the same pair, at noise 0.02. Only 7 percent reach the best cost. On average, only 16 percent of the points are matched correctly.

So we start from several places and keep the cheapest. The cheapest run here has every point right. If each start has that 7 percent chance, 50 starts give about a 97 percent chance that at least one finds the best valley.

The second trap is symmetry. An ellipse of 40 points fits on itself four ways: as it is, flipped one way, flipped the other, or turned by half a turn. The table of distances cannot tell them apart.

From 400 random starts without noise, 80 reach a cost of zero. A fifth. Every one of them is perfect, and they land on all four matchings. The one hidden in the shuffle shows up in only 15. The others are just as exact, and just as different.

Now, three uses.

First, words. Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola aligned the word vectors of two languages with no dictionary, using only the geometry of the words. English to Spanish, 81.7 percent of the translations were right, the same as the best method without a dictionary that they compared. English to Russian, 45.1 percent, against 52.4.

Second, cells. Demetci and colleagues matched cells measured by two different techniques, with no cell known in advance.

Third, an old question. Is my red your red? Groups of 128 people judged how similar 93 colours look. We match the colours of one group to those of another, without using their names. Between two groups with typical colour vision, 51 percent of the colours land on themselves. Between two groups with atypical vision, 57 percent. Between a typical group and an atypical group, 3.8 percent, where chance is 1.1. These are averages over 20 random splits, from Kawakita and colleagues in 2025.

Here is the card. The object: two clouds, each with its own table of distances and its masses. The distance: the mismatch of the cheapest plan. What it ignores: everything that keeps the distances, so position, rotation, mirror, and the names of the points.

What it costs: for n points on each side, n to the fourth power terms in the cost. 2,560,000 for 40 points. One trillion for 1,000. A blurred version, like the blurred plan of episode 6.5, makes the computation practical. Peyré, Cuturi and Solomon showed it in 2016.

Its trap: the answer depends on the start, and a symmetric shape has several answers.

With no common frame and no labels, geometry alone can recover which point goes with which. It works when the shape is irregular enough to fit on itself only one way.

Two dynamics

Two systems can pass through the same circle, one turning forever and one settling at a point, with Hausdorff distance 0.13. Comparing the rule of passage instead, the eigenvalues of a fitted linear rule do not depend on the sensors: modulus 0.98 and angle 0.1 whatever the mixing. For an oscillator, eigenvalue gaps are 0.004 within one tuning, 0.046 across tunings.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two systems can pass through exactly the same points, and still do different things.

Here is a circle, with two systems on it. The first one turns, and never stops. The second one slides along the circle, and comes to rest at a single point. Each system leaves a cloud of 2,880 points. The two clouds cover the same circle. Their Hausdorff distance is 0.13.

Every method of this chapter would call them the same. They all look at where the points are. None of them looks at how you go from one point to the next.

We met this difference in episode 4.4. Hausdorff only asks where the curves pass. Fréchet asks how you walk along them. So to compare two dynamics, we compare the rule that takes you from one instant to the next.

Take the simplest rule. The next state is the present state, multiplied by a matrix.

Here is one. At each step it turns the state by 0.1 radian, and shrinks it by two percent. The state spirals slowly inwards.

Now observe the same system through other sensors. Say the readings are mixed and stretched, in a way that can be undone. The picture changes, and so does the matrix. Written as lists of numbers, the two matrices are 1.18 apart in straight line. The two trajectories are 35.7 degrees apart for Procrustes.

But look at the eigenvalues of the matrix. They have not moved. The modulus is 0.98, and the angle is 0.1.

The modulus says how fast the state shrinks. The angle says how far it turns at each step. Together they tell a story. A full turn takes 62.8 steps. The amplitude halves every 34.3 steps.

And this holds whatever the sensors are, as long as the change of sensors can be undone. The eigenvalues of the rule do not depend on the sensors. That is what we wanted.

Back to the circle. Fit the best linear rule to the first system. Its eigenvalues have modulus 1.0 and angle 0.1. A pure rotation.

Fit the best rule to the second system. It has two real eigenvalues, 1.002 and 0.971. No rotation at all. A system that settles.

Same points. A rotation on one side, a relaxation on the other.

Real systems are not linear. So take a classical nonlinear oscillator, the van der Pol oscillator, and tune it two ways. At the first tuning it oscillates at 0.150 cycles per unit of time. At the second, at 0.113.

We watch it through ten channels. Each channel mixes the two variables of the oscillator, then bends the result. We make three readings, with nothing in common. Alpha and beta watch the first tuning. Gamma watches the second.

Now the trick. Stack the signal with delayed copies of itself. A single reading cannot tell whether the state is going up or down. A reading and its past can. In that larger space, a linear rule is often a good enough description.

The idea that a nonlinear system becomes linear, when you look at enough functions of its state, goes back to Koopman, in 1931. Fitting such a rule to data is called dynamic mode decomposition, from Schmid, in 2010.

Here is what the fit finds. For alpha, the slowest oscillations are at 0.150, 0.300 and 0.450. For beta, the same. For gamma, they are at 0.113, 0.231 and 0.339.

Now the two reference points of the chapter. The ceiling is the same system measured twice. The gap between the two sets of eigenvalues, from alpha to beta, is 0.004. The other tuning is farther. From alpha to gamma, the gap is 0.046.

In this example, geometry already separated the two tunings. For Procrustes, alpha and beta are 22.9 degrees apart, and alpha and gamma 86.8 degrees. The two readings complement each other.

This is the principle of dynamical similarity analysis, from Ostrow and colleagues, in 2023. Stack delays. Fit a linear rule. Then run Procrustes between the two rules, not between the two clouds.

Remember Procrustes, from episode 12.2. You turn one cloud to fit the other, and you read the angle that is left. Here we turn the rule itself. A rotation of the sensors changes the matrix, and Procrustes undoes it.

Their demonstration has two parts. First, they trained 240 recurrent networks on the same task, a three-switch memory task. All of them solve it with the same structure, eight stable memory states. Yet Procrustes on the clouds sorts them by architecture. Comparing the rules does not.

Second, three noisy networks have the same average geometry, but different dynamics. Only the comparison of rules tells them apart.

Now the card of this distance. The object: two dynamics, each seen through its own sensors.

The distance: a fit per system, then Procrustes between the two rules.

What it ignores: the frame of the sensors. By default, only rotations of it. Allow any invertible change, and you lose the triangle rule.

What it costs: one fit per system, then one Procrustes.

Its trap: it has several. The number of delays, and the number of components you keep, both change the eigenvalues. On our oscillator, I tried nine settings. In two of them, the two readings of the same system were at least as far apart as the two tunings.

Here are two of the nine settings. With 20 delays and 5 components, the gap from alpha to beta is 0.032, and from alpha to gamma only 0.019. With 40 delays and 11 components, the numbers we used, it is 0.004 and 0.046.

The linear rule is only an approximation.

And equal eigenvalues do not prove that two systems are the same. Budišić, Mohr and Mezić proved that eigenvalues pass from a system to its conjugate. The other direction needs more, and a fitted rule with a few delays does not give it.

So here is the idea to keep. Two systems can pass through the same points and do different things. To compare two dynamics, compare their rules of passage. And the eigenvalues of that rule do not depend on the sensors.

Do two brains share a geometry? Two brains have no common frame. Neuron twelve of one brain is not neuron twelve of the other, so we cannot compare the columns. We can only compare the relations between points, and that is what this whole chapter has been about.

As in every ideas episode, the screen has two columns. On the left, what is established in the literature. On the right, what is only worth trying. And at the end, one question that separates them.

Established, first. In 2008, Kriegeskorte and colleagues showed the same ninety-two images to human volunteers and to monkeys. They built one table of distances between the images for each, from fMRI in people and from recorded cells in the monkey, and the two tables resembled each other. No neuron was matched to any voxel. Only the relations were compared.

Cichy, Pantazis and Oliva then did it across devices, in 2014, with the same ninety-two images. MEG gives one table per instant, fMRI gives one table per region, and comparing them showed early instants matching the first visual area, and later instants matching the inferior temporal cortex.

Third, the layers of trained networks line up with the visual stages of the brain. In 2014, Yamins and colleagues found that the top layer of the best network predicts inferior temporal cells, and its middle layers predict the area before. Khaligh-Razavi and Kriegeskorte, the same year, found that a deep supervised network explained that cortex best.

Public benchmarks followed. Schrimpf and colleagues, in 2018, opened a platform where networks are scored on how brain-like they are.

Fourth, turn each brain's space toward a shared one. This is hyperalignment, from Haxby and colleagues, in 2011, and again in 2020. A rotation, which keeps the distances, brings every person onto a common space built from the same film.

Fifth, the statistical side is now serious. Schütt and colleagues, in 2023, gave inference for comparing models of these geometries, with a bootstrap over both the stimuli and the people.

The warnings are established too. Soni and colleagues showed that conclusions about brain alignment depend strongly on the measure. Klabunde and colleagues found that different measures can give materially different results on the same pair.

And a high score does not tell you what produced it. Dujmović and colleagues, and Cloos and colleagues, showed that confounds in the stimuli, or in the data, can give a high score without the same information.

Now the other column. These are tracks, not results. And each time I say nothing was found, it is a limited search, not a proof.

First track. Tables in hyperscanning. Varlet and Grootswagers proposed it in 2024, tested on simulated pairs. Moerel and colleagues then recorded twenty-four real pairs with EEG, in 2025, and compared one partner's tables with the other's.

Here is a toy dyad, simulated with seed one two nine. Eight conditions, one person with thirty-two channels, the other with sixty-four. Their two tables agree at 0.94. When we shuffle the condition labels two thousand times, the ninety-fifth percentile of the floor is 0.34.

Second track. Rotate one partner onto the other, with hyperalignment or a shared response model. I found nothing for hyperscanning EEG.

The fit needs a shared, timed stimulus. A film, yes. A free interaction, no. See the Hyperscanning series.

Third track. Compare the dynamics by their rules of passage, from episode 12.8, and not instant by instant.

Fourth track. Match without labels. Kawakita and colleagues, in 2025, matched the colour judgments of two groups of people with no colour names given.

Fifth track. EEG and fNIRS, compared through their tables. I found nothing there either.

One question stands between the two columns. Between which landmarks do you read a score? The floor: two systems with nothing in common, of the same sizes. The ceiling: the same system, measured twice.

The ceiling is system A against its own second measurement, and the pair is system A against system B, one draw, as in episodes 12.1 to 12.5.
