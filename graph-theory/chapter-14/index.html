PLATE 14.16:03

Turning measurements into links

Mantegna's thirty Dow Jones stocks, turned from price series into distances and linked by closeness, form groups matching industries, though nobody told the computer what they sell. A graph from measurements needs one number per pair. On eight simulated signals, correlation, coherence and mutual information give three different networks. The measure decides which relations can become links.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Look at this board. Every row is a company, and every line is its price, day after day. It does not look like a graph.

In 1999, Rosario Mantegna took the daily prices of the thirty stocks of the Dow Jones index, from July 1989 to October 1995. Thirty stocks make four hundred thirty-five pairs. For each pair, he measured how closely the two prices move together. Then he turned that closeness into a distance, small for stocks that move together.

Now link the closest ones. The board becomes a graph, and groups appear. Chevron, Texaco and Exxon: three oil companies. Alcoa and International Paper: two suppliers of raw materials. Procter and Gamble and Coca-Cola: consumer goods.

Nobody drew these links. Nobody told the computer what each company sells. He kept only the cheapest set of links that still connects every stock, and the industries showed up anyway. He repeated it on four hundred forty-three stocks of the S and P five hundred, and with only a few exceptions the groups matched industries.

This network was never given. It was built from measurements, and every step of the building was a choice. Today we look at those choices.

The recipe starts with one time series for each element. A stock gives a price. An EEG electrode gives a voltage. A region in an fMRI scan gives a signal that rises and falls.

Take every pair of elements. Compute, for each pair, one number that says how much the two series go together. That number is the weight of the link between them, drawn as thickness, as before. Three common choices are correlation, coherence and mutual information.

Every pair gets a number, so the graph is complete. Eight elements make twenty-eight links. The thirty stocks give four hundred thirty-five. A brain atlas with ninety regions makes four thousand and five.

If the number is the same whichever series comes first, the link has no arrow, as in one point two. If the measure can be negative, so can the weight, and it is drawn as a dotted blue line, as in one point three.

Points in a space are different, because there is no series to compare. A common recipe links each point to its k nearest neighbours. Here k is two, and ten points give thirteen links.

Now the moment that matters. Same data, different measure, different network.

Here are eight simulated signals, two minutes each. Four pairs are related, in four different ways. All the other pairs are just noise.

A and B are plain. When A goes up, B goes up. Then C and D share a ten hertz rhythm, but D runs twenty-five milliseconds late, a quarter of a cycle, and both are buried in noise. Next, F is a noisy parabola of E, so F is large whenever E is far from zero, in either direction. Last, G and H are plain, but opposite. When G goes up, H goes down.

Now three measures. Correlation compares the two signals at the same moment, and runs from minus one to one. Coherence asks, frequency by frequency, whether the two signals keep a steady timing relationship. We average it from eight to twelve hertz, over two second windows, and it runs from zero to one. Mutual information asks how much knowing one signal shrinks your doubt about the other. It is counted in bits, and it is never negative. We estimate it with the nearest neighbour method of Kraskov and colleagues.

Here are the three networks. With correlation, A and B are strongly linked, at zero point eight five. The pair G and H is linked too, with a dotted blue line, at minus zero point eight five. The pair C and D vanishes: zero. Then E and F nearly vanish: zero point zero four.

Coherence keeps A and B, at zero point seven, and the pair G and H, at zero point seven three. But the pair C and D, invisible a moment ago, is now linked, at zero point six six. And coherence cannot say that the second pair is opposite, because it has no sign. The parabola pair still vanishes.

Mutual information links A and B, at zero point nine three bits, and the last pair at the same value, again with no sign. The rhythm pair vanishes: zero point zero one bits. But E and F, which correlation could not see, are linked at zero point eight seven.

Same signals, three networks. Only the plain pairs, A and B, and G and H, appear in all three.

None of these measures is wrong. Each one answers a different question. Correlation misses a delay, because it only looks at the same moment. Correlation and coherence both miss the parabola, because the up on one side cancels the down on the other. And here, mutual information misses the delayed rhythm, because at any single moment the two signals are independent.

A word about the estimates. On the twenty-four pairs that are pure noise, the three measures still return small numbers, never more than zero point zero two. That is estimator bias, not a link. A different estimator, or a different band, would give different numbers.

So, before any measure of the network, someone chose what a link is. Correlation or coherence or mutual information, the band, the window, the estimator: each choice decides which relations can become links. In two point one, a dictionary chose which word ladders were possible. Here, the measure chooses which relations are possible.

To sum up. One series per element, and one number per pair. The graph is complete. Whether it has arrows, and whether it has signs, depends on the measure. And the measure is a choice.

But a complete graph has a link for every pair, and it is too dense to read. Where do we cut? That is the question for next time.

PLATE 14.25:17

Where to cut: thresholds

A complete graph says nothing, so we keep only the strongest links. An absolute threshold keeps a value, a proportional threshold keeps a share, and the measured clustering and efficiency change as the cut slides. A share that looks fair can force a group with weaker correlations to keep noisier links. Neither threshold is innocent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended with a complete graph. Every pair of elements has a number, so every pair is linked.

Look at what that does to the measures. In a complete graph, every neighbourhood is fully linked, so clustering is one. Every pair sits one step apart, so efficiency is one. Nothing is left to measure.

So we keep only the strongest links. Picture a sieve. Each pair of elements is a pebble, as big as its weight. Shake the sieve, and the small pebbles fall through. What stays is the network.

Here is a simulated example. Twenty-four elements, in four blocks of six. Two elements in the same block are really linked. That is the true wiring: sixty links out of two hundred seventy-six pairs. Each element is a signal of sixty time points. The weights are correlations, and we keep their size, whatever their sign.

There are two ways to set the sieve. The absolute threshold fixes the mesh. Keep every link whose weight is above a value, say zero point four. The proportional threshold fixes the share. Keep the same share of links in every network, say the strongest ten percent.

Start with the mesh, and slide it up from zero. At first nothing falls through. The network is one block of two hundred seventy-six links.

At zero point two seven, the block breaks in two. Raise it to zero point four, and four islands appear, as in two point four. Forty-four links stay, and not one of them is false. Past that, at zero point five, ten islands. By zero point seven, nothing is left.

Now measure the network at every position of the sieve. Clustering, from seven point one, starts at one. With the mesh at a tenth, it has fallen to zero point six three. Then it climbs back to zero point eight, just as the blocks separate. Then it falls to zero point one two. Efficiency, from seven point two, only falls, down to zero point one nine by zero point four.

No value is hiding in these curves. The network you measure depends on where you cut.

Garrison and colleagues saw this on real data, in twenty fifteen. They took resting fMRI from a hundred adults, and tried every absolute threshold from zero to one, in steps of one hundredth. Many measures were unstable across thresholds. For some, the direction of a difference between men and women reversed as the threshold moved. They found the measures steadier across proportional thresholds.

That absolute mesh has a second trap. Take two groups with the same wiring, where the second has weaker correlations. Set the mesh at zero point four. The first network keeps forty-four links. The second keeps two.

Comparing networks with different numbers of links is itself a trap. Most measures depend on how many links a network has. The two networks differ before anything else is compared.

So fix the share instead. Keep the strongest ten percent in each network: twenty-eight links each. The same number of links. That looks fair. Is it?

Here is the catch. Look at where each cut falls. In the strong group, it falls at zero point four eight, so only strong links survive. In the weak group, it falls at zero point two five. There, the true links are barely taller than the noise.

The sieve cannot tell them apart. The strong group keeps no false link out of twenty-eight. The weak group keeps seven. Remember, both groups have exactly the same wiring.

Their measures differ anyway. Clustering is zero point four five against zero point one nine. Efficiency is zero point one two against zero point three four, because the false links join the blocks and shorten the paths.

That is one pair of subjects. With a hundred simulated subjects in each group, it is the same. Together, the strong group's hundred subjects keep one false link. Each weak subject keeps nearly ten. Average clustering is zero point four five against zero point one nine.

This is the argument of van den Heuvel and colleagues, in twenty seventeen. When one group has weaker correlations overall, as is often seen between patients and controls, a proportional threshold forces that group to keep weaker links, which are more likely to be spurious. With real and artificial data, they showed that lower overall connectivity will most often produce differences in efficiency and clustering.

And the gap depends on where you cut. At twenty-two percent of the links, our two groups differ in clustering by zero point five nine. At fifty percent, by zero point zero seven.

Neither threshold is innocent. The absolute one changes the number of links. The proportional one changes the quality of the links. Each fix creates another problem. So how do we compare networks fairly? We will come back to that.

To sum up. A complete graph says nothing, so we cut. One threshold keeps a value, and the other keeps a share. The measures change as the sieve slides. And a share that looks fair can force a group with weaker correlations to keep noisier links.

But there is another problem, before any cut. Some strong links are not real links at all. That is next time.

PLATE 14.36:54

Ghost links

A strong link can be a ghost: a path through a third element, a common source, or, in EEG, volume conduction. In a simulation, A and C correlate at 0.64 though A never drives C, while their partial correlation is minus 0.004. The graphical lasso estimates this when elements are many. Delay-based EEG measures remove ghosts but lose true links without delay.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. Is every strong link a real link?

Think of whispers in a long corridor. A whispers a message to B, and B whispers it on to C. A never speaks to C. They stand too far apart.

Yet whatever A said, C repeats a moment later. If you watch only A and C, they look tightly linked. That link does not exist. It is a ghost: a link created by a path through someone else.

Here it is in a simulation. Three signals, three hundred samples each, seed one hundred forty-three. A drives B, and B drives C. A has no direct input to C.

The correlation of A and B is zero point eight. Between B and C, the correlation is zero point eight. And the correlation of A and C is zero point six four. That is a strong link, between two elements that never touch.

To remove the ghost, ask a different question. Take B out of the picture. Remove from A whatever B explains, and from C whatever B explains. Then correlate what is left. This is the partial correlation.

For A and C, given B, it is minus zero point zero zero four. Close to zero, but not exactly zero. With a finite sample, it never is. We repeated the simulation two thousand times: leftovers of this kind have a typical size of zero point zero five, and ninety-five percent stay below zero point one one.

The real links survive. A and B, given C, stay at zero point six two. B and C, given A, stay at zero point six three.

A second ghost comes from a common source. Picture a loudspeaker in a square. Two listeners, A and B, never talk to each other. Both just follow the same announcement, S, so their signals rise and fall together.

In the simulation, the correlation of A and B is zero point six three. If S is among the measured elements, the partial correlation of A and B, given S, is minus zero point zero one. The ghost is gone.

But if nobody recorded S, nothing can be removed. Without S, the partial correlation is just the plain correlation, zero point six three, and the ghost stays. So a partial correlation erases only what you measured. And like a correlation, it has no arrow.

A real network has many elements, and that brings a problem. Computing a partial correlation needs the inverse of the covariance matrix, the table of how every pair varies together. With ninety regions and sixty time points, that table has rank fifty-nine, not ninety. It cannot be inverted.

Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani proposed the graphical lasso in two thousand eight. It estimates the inverse covariance matrix with a penalty that pushes small entries to exactly zero. A zero entry means no link. They also gave a fast algorithm, built on coordinate descent for the lasso.

Here are twelve simulated elements on a ring. Each is truly linked to its two neighbours. That makes twelve true links among sixty-six pairs. We simulate one hundred fifty samples.

Keep every correlation above zero point three. Twenty-seven links stay. Twelve are true, and fifteen are ghosts, between elements that are not neighbours.

Now the graphical lasso, from scikit-learn. Its penalty is a dial. At zero point one, it keeps twenty-eight links, and sixteen of them are false. Raise it to zero point three, and eighteen links stay, six of them false. Raise it again, to zero point five, and thirteen links stay, with one false.

Unlike a partial correlation, the lasso returns exact zeros: forty-eight of the sixty-six pairs at zero point three. The price is a choice. The penalty decides how many links survive, and a penalty that is too weak keeps ghosts.

Now the ghost that comes from physics. In EEG, a current in the brain spreads through the skull and the scalp. Several electrodes record the same source at the same instant, each with its own weight. There is no delay between them. Sound in a hall would be late. This is not.

We simulate two independent sources with an eight to twelve hertz rhythm, and four electrodes. Each electrode is a fixed mix of the two sources, plus its own noise. The sources do not interact at all.

Electrodes one and two sit over the first source. Their coherence is zero point nine five. It looks like a strong link, and it is a pure ghost.

Nolte and colleagues, in two thousand four, saw that signals mixed instantly from independent sources have a coherency with no imaginary part. They proposed to keep only that imaginary part. That value is zero point zero three.

In twenty eleven, Vinck and colleagues proposed the weighted phase lag index. It asks whether one signal leads or lags the other, and weights each lead or lag by the size of the imaginary part of the cross-spectrum. It comes to zero point two three. That is not zero. With only sixty epochs, this estimate is biased upward, and Vinck's paper also gives a debiased version.

Now make one interaction real. The second source follows the first, with a delay of twenty-five milliseconds. Take electrodes one and four, far apart. Coherence there is zero point six four. Their imaginary coherency is zero point five eight. Their weighted phase lag index is zero point nine three. Both measures see the real link.

But there is a cost. Now make the interaction real, with no delay at all. Source two is now a copy of the first, plus noise, at the same instant. Between electrodes one and four, coherence is zero point eight two. The imaginary coherency falls to zero point zero six. The weighted phase lag index is zero point two three.

A real link, thrown away. These measures cannot tell a true interaction without delay from volume conduction, so they discard both. A missing link is then unknown, not absent.

The EEG series follows these measures in chapter ten. The Information series, in episode eight point ten, shows how the same trap fools transfer entropy.

To sum up. A strong link can be a ghost. A path through a third element makes one, and so does a common source. Partial correlation removes what you measured, and the graphical lasso estimates it when elements are many. In EEG, volume conduction adds ghosts that only a delay can unmask, at the price of losing true links without delay.

One question remains. Networks built this way can have very different numbers of links. How do we compare them fairly? That is next time.

PLATE 14.46:45

Comparing networks of different densities

Four ways to compare networks of different density: trim to the same density, divide by a reference, integrate over densities, or keep the minimum spanning tree. On two simulated groups differing only in strength, each keeps something and loses something, and the tree has zero clustering. A result deserves trust when it survives several remedies, and all should be reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked how to compare networks that have very different numbers of links.

Today there are four answers. Each one is a station on an inspection line. At every station we read the same two groups, and we measure what the station keeps and what it throws away.

First, the groups. You met them in seven point five. Each has twenty simulated people, with thirty regions each, so every person has a matrix of correlations.

The patients have the controls' matrices, with every correlation multiplied by zero point eight. Nothing else differs. The mean correlation is zero point two eight for the controls, and zero point two three for the patients. The organisation is identical. Only the strength changes, and that difference is real.

Apply the rule of seven point five: keep a link above zero point three. The controls keep one hundred eighty-seven links, on average, and the patients one hundred nine. Clustering reads zero point four four against zero point three two. Efficiency, zero point seven one against zero point six zero. The two networks differ because their densities differ.

Station one trims every network to the same density. Keep the strongest twenty-five percent of the pairs. That is one hundred nine links, for every person.

Now look at the first person of each group. Their networks are one and the same drawing. Clustering is zero point three three in both groups, and efficiency is zero point six zero in both.

The reason is simple. Multiplying every correlation by zero point eight does not change which pairs are the strongest. The ranking is untouched, so the trimmed networks are identical.

So the false gap in clustering is gone. But a real gap went with it. The patients are weaker, and this binary network can no longer say so. Only the weights of the kept links still show it, zero point four seven against zero point three seven.

Station two keeps the absolute rule, and divides by a reference. Take each network and rewire it, keeping every degree. That is the reference of chapter ten. Do it ten times for each person, and average. Then divide the real clustering by the reference clustering.

For the controls, the real clustering is zero point four four, and the reference gives zero point four two. The ratio is one point zero six. For the patients, zero point three two against zero point two three, a ratio of one point four zero.

The reference did not remove the density. Two groups with the same organisation still get different ratios, and the patients now look more clustered than the controls. For efficiency it works, with a ratio of one point zero zero for the controls, and zero point nine nine for the patients.

Station three refuses to choose a single cut. Measure at every density from ten to fifty percent, in steps of five, and average. This is cost integration, from Ginestet, Nichols, Bullmore and Simmons, in twenty eleven. Their key result is that integrating over cost is equivalent to controlling for any monotonic transformation of the weights.

Multiplying by zero point eight is such a transformation. Against the threshold, the two efficiency curves sit far apart. Against the density, they lie on top of each other. The averages are identical. Both groups get zero point three six for clustering, and zero point six one for efficiency.

So this station is blind to strength too. That is why the authors recommend reporting two things. One is the cost-integrated measure. The other is the difference in weighted cost, which is the mean correlation: zero point two eight against zero point two three.

Station four keeps only the minimum spanning tree of two point six. Turn each correlation into a distance, and keep the cheapest links that join all thirty regions. That is twenty-nine links, for every person. Tewarie and colleagues, in twenty fifteen, argued for it in brain networks, because it does not mix the layout of a network with the strength of its links.

Again the first person of each group gives the same tree. The diameter, averaged over a group, is fourteen and a quarter steps in both. The tree keeps the backbone of the network.

But look at its clustering. It is zero. Exactly zero. A tree has no cycle, so it has no triangle. The same person, under the zero point three rule, has three hundred thirty-nine triangles. Here, all the clustering is thrown away.

So no station is free. But do they find a real difference when there is one? Take a third group. Its mean correlation is the same as the controls, zero point two eight, but its modules are blurred. Inside a module, the correlation falls from zero point four five to zero point three five. Between modules, it rises from zero point two five to zero point two seven.

The raw rule sees almost nothing. Its clustering reads zero point four four against zero point four five. Station one finds zero point three three against zero point two five. Station three finds zero point three six against zero point three zero. Station two gives a ratio of one point zero six against one point zero zero. The tree, zero against zero, cannot see it.

Now compare the answers. Take the claim that the patients are less clustered. Under the raw rule, yes. Stations one and three find no difference. Station two says the opposite. The tree cannot say. A claim that stands at one station only should not be believed, and this one is false by construction.

Another claim: the blurred group is less clustered. Three stations agree. That claim deserves more trust.

And the real gap in strength? The rule itself shows it, and so does station two, which keeps the same links. Station one, station three and the tree do not, unless you keep the weights.

So a result should hold under several remedies. Fix the density, divide by a reference, integrate, keep the tree. Then report all of them.

To sum up. Four stations: the same density, a reference, an integral over densities, and the spanning tree. Each keeps something and loses something. The ones that read only ranks cannot see a change of strength, and the tree has no clustering at all. Trust a result when it survives several.

Next, we have fair numbers for each person. But is a gap between two groups more than chance? And which links differ?

PLATE 14.56:49

Testing a difference

On simulated groups of thirty controls and thirty patients with 4,005 links each, one test per link gives 363 hits, 339 of them chance. The network-based statistic groups links above a threshold that touch, and compares the biggest group with shuffled labels, giving p of 0.002. It speaks about a sub-network, not single links. Shuffle labels between people, never links.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended with two questions. Is a gap between two groups more than chance? And which links differ?

Here are two groups of brain networks, simulated. Thirty controls and thirty patients. Each person has ninety regions, as in a common atlas. Ninety regions make four thousand and five links. Every link is the correlation between two regions.

We planted a difference, so we know the truth. In twenty-four links, the patients have a lower correlation. These links touch each other. They form one small network, on twelve regions. Everywhere else, the groups are the same.

Can the data find our twenty-four links?

The obvious test is one test per link. That is four thousand and five tests, each at the usual five percent.

Three hundred sixty-three links pass. Twenty-four are planted, and three hundred thirty-nine are chance. Chance alone gives about two hundred.

So we correct. The simplest correction divides five percent by four thousand and five. A link must now pass at about one chance in eighty thousand. Nine links pass, and all nine are planted.

But fifteen real links are missed. The correction treats every link as alone. Our twenty-four touch each other, and it ignores that.

In two thousand ten, Zalesky, Fornito and Bullmore proposed the network-based statistic. It starts from one idea. A real effect comes as a group of links that touch.

Step one. Test every link. Each gets a t value, a measure of the gap between the groups.

Step two. Choose a primary threshold, and keep the links above it. We take a t value above three point two four. That is one chance in a thousand, for a single link. Thirty-one links remain.

Step three. Group the links that touch. These are the connected components of episode two point four. We get three: twenty-four links, then four, then three.

Step four. Give each group a size, its number of links. The biggest has twenty-four.

How big can chance make a group?

So we shuffle. Every person keeps their whole network. We only redeal the labels, control and patient, among the sixty people. If the groups did not differ, any redealing was as likely as the real one.

Then we repeat the four steps, and write down the size of the biggest group.

We did this one thousand times. The biggest group of chance is usually two links. Its ninety-fifth percentile is ten. Only one shuffle in a thousand reached twenty-four. The corrected p-value is two over one thousand and one, or zero point zero zero two.

One comparison is enough, because each shuffle gives a single number, its biggest group. So the false alarm is counted once for the whole network, and not four thousand times.

Now read what this p-value says. It belongs to the group of links, taken as a whole. It says that this group is not chance. It does not say that each link inside differs.

Look inside. The group holds twenty-four links. Twenty-one are planted. Three are not. They are chance links that happen to touch the group. And three planted links are outside, below the threshold.

So the test cannot say which single link differs. We report a sub-network of twenty-four links, not twenty-four findings.

The result also depends on the primary threshold. Let us move it.

At one chance in twenty, the biggest group has three hundred sixty-three links, and three hundred thirty-nine are chance. The test is still significant, and the group is mostly noise. Tighten it to one chance in ten thousand, and the group has sixteen links, all planted. At one in a hundred thousand, it has nine.

So the threshold is a choice. Make it before looking.

Is the statistic better than the correction? We drew two hundred new datasets with the planted difference. The statistic rejected in one hundred ninety-five. The correction found at least one link in nearly all of them, but only a quarter of the planted links, on average. They answer different questions.

Now the part that matters most. What did we shuffle? The labels, between people. The question is about people, so the person is the unit, and each keeps all their links.

There is a tempting shortcut. Shuffle the links inside each network instead. It looks the same, but it is not valid.

The links of one brain are not independent. Links that share a region rise and fall together. Across people, two such links correlate at zero point three one. Two links that share nothing correlate at zero point zero zero.

Take one person. Their modules show as blocks. After the shuffle, the blocks are gone. The gap between inside and between modules falls from zero point three zero to minus zero point zero one.

Now ask what chance looks like when nothing differs. We simulated four hundred datasets with no difference at all, and measured the biggest group in each. The ninety-fifth percentile is thirteen.

Shuffling labels gives a similar curve, with a ninety-fifth percentile of ten. Shuffling links does not. Its biggest group is almost never above two.

So the alarms differ. Shuffling labels raised an alarm in twenty-five datasets out of four hundred, six percent. We asked for five. Shuffling links raised one in one hundred thirty datasets, thirty-two and a half percent. That is more than six times too many.

The same dependence explains our three hundred thirty-nine. Over the four hundred datasets, the count of chance links averaged two hundred, but ranged from fifty-two to four hundred thirty-two. Independent links would stay within about forty of two hundred.

A link shuffle tests something else. It tests that the links of a network are interchangeable, which is false for every brain. What you shuffle is what you test.

Chapter ten also shuffled links, for another question. Given its degrees, is one network more clustered than a reference? There, the network is the unit. The Statistics series builds the whole table of shuffles, in its chapter fourteen, called What do we shuffle.

To sum up. One test per link makes about two hundred false alarms, and a safe correction finds few links. The network-based statistic keeps the links above a primary threshold, groups those that touch, and compares the biggest group with shuffled labels. It speaks about the group, not about single links, and it depends on its threshold. Shuffle the labels between people, never the links inside a network.
