A dyad from scratch (EEG)

A code walkthrough builds an inter-brain matrix from open EEG of two players in a group of three. Both files are aligned on trials, preprocessed identically, and phase locking value, circular correlation and an adjusted version are written by hand. Pseudo-pairs from other groups give a null band and a cluster test: this dyad's 0.22 sits inside it, and conclusions vary by measure.

Part 1

Welcome back to the pair desk. Two laptops side by side, one for each member of the dyad. This is a code bonus, and this is part one of two.

We take a real recording of two people, and we build their inter-brain matrix cell by cell. Part one ends on the raw matrix, exactly as the code produces it. Part two puts the null band on it.

A bonus is built to be replaced. The run on screen used Python three point twelve, MNE one point thirteen, MNE Connectivity zero point nine, NumPy two point five, and SciPy one point eighteen. Those are the versions as of November 2026.

The data are open. They come from OpenNeuro, from Kim, Jun and Nam, in Data in Brief in 2026, under CC0. Eleven groups played an iterated prisoner's dilemma while their EEG was recorded: nineteen channels, three hundred hertz. But each group has three players, not two. So we take two of the three players of one group: group one, players one and two. They are our A and B.

Step one is to open and align the two files. Each file holds forty trials, already cut by the authors: one second before the decision, then four seconds of it. There is no continuous recording, so there is no trigger channel to find. What the two files share is the event table: the same trial numbers, the same onsets.

The paper explains why. The three computers received one common marker over the network, with no shared hardware clock. The authors set a tolerance of two milliseconds, and did not measure the real offset. So we align trial by trial, and we check. Every trial starts on the same sample in both files: an offset of zero samples, in all forty trials. That zero is built in. It is not a measured delay of the equipment, and we cannot measure that delay here.

Step two is the same preprocessing for both people. One function, applied to A and to B. The authors already filtered at one hertz, and removed the mains line. We re-reference to the average, and band-pass between eight and twelve hertz: the alpha band, the band of an early dual-EEG imitation study in 2010. We keep the first three and a half seconds of each decision, because the edge effect of the filter sits at the end. For the details of cleaning one person, see the EEG series, in the bonus episode From raw to clean.

One rule needs care. A trial with a huge swing in any channel is bad. In A, five trials are bad. In B, none. A bad trial is dropped for both people, so the pairs stay aligned. Thirty-five trials remain.

Step three puts the two heads in one array. Trials, then channels, then samples. The nineteen channels of A come first, then the nineteen of B. Thirty-five by thirty-eight by one thousand and fifty. From now on, a pair of channels is a pair of rows, inside a head or across.

Step four is three measures, written by hand. First, the phase of every signal, from the Hilbert transform. The phase locking value, PLV, is the length of the average arrow of the phase difference. One line of NumPy gives every pair of channels, trial by trial.

Second, the circular correlation. Subtract each signal's mean direction, take the sines, and correlate them. Third, the adjusted version, from Zimmermann and colleagues in 2024, equation one point two. It compares the length of the difference of phases with the length of their sum. Same arrays, a few more lines.

Can we trust them? We test the three functions against a plain loop, on random pairs and trials. The largest difference is below ten to the minus fifteen.

Take one pair: the midline parietal channel of A against the same channel of B. Averaged over the thirty-five trials, the PLV is zero point two three. CCorr is minus zero point zero seven. ACCorr is zero point two three.

Now the whole matrix. Thirty-eight by thirty-eight, one cell for every pair of channels. Four blocks. A with A, at the top left. B with B, at the bottom right. And between the heads, A with B, and B with A. These two blocks are mirror images, because the PLV treats both signals alike.

Look at the brightness. Inside a head, the average is zero point three one in A, and zero point four two in B. Between the heads, it is zero point two two. The ACCorr between the heads says zero point two one, close to the PLV. CCorr can be negative, and between the heads it averages zero point zero zero.

Is zero point two two a lot? Nobody can say without a null band. Inside a head, neighbouring channels share the same skull, so those two blocks are bright for a reason that has nothing to do with the dyad. Between the heads, we need to know what people who never met would give.

That is part two. Let's recap. Align the two files on their shared trial table. Preprocess both heads with one function, and drop a bad trial for both. Stack them into one array. Write the three measures in a few lines of NumPy.

Part 2

Welcome back to the pair desk. This is part two of the code bonus. Part one ended on a raw matrix, and on a question: is zero point two two a lot? Today we answer it.

The versions are the same as in part one, as of November 2026: Python three point twelve, MNE one point thirteen, MNE Connectivity zero point nine, NumPy two point five, and SciPy one point eighteen.

First, a fourth measure: the weighted phase lag index, wPLI. It ignores a phase difference of exactly zero, the signature of one source seen by two sensors, and it weights each trial by the size of the imaginary part of the cross-spectrum. Vinck and colleagues proposed it in 2011. This one we do not write by hand. MNE Connectivity computes it from the stacked array, between eight and twelve hertz, for the cells between the heads.

On our dyad, the average wPLI between the heads is zero point one nine. Inside a head, it is zero point two six in A, and zero point two eight in B. The plain wPLI is biased upward when there are few trials, so we also compute the debiased version, from the same paper. Between the heads, its average is zero point zero zero six.

Now the null band. The question is what two people who never met would give. So we build pseudo-pairs: player one of one group, with player two of another group. They played the same game, but never together. With eleven groups, there are one hundred and ten pseudo-pairs. For our dyad, we drop every pseudo-pair that contains group one, which leaves ninety. Each pseudo-pair goes through exactly the same steps: same filter, same trial rule, same measures.

Take the PLV. For each of the three hundred and sixty-one cells between the heads, the ninety pseudo-pair values give a band: its ninety-fifth percentile. In our dyad, twenty-two cells are above their band. But five percent of three hundred and sixty-one is about eighteen. Twenty-two is close to what chance gives.

A band, cell by cell, does not correct for the number of cells. So we add a cluster test, in the spirit of Maris and Oostenveld, 2007. Each cell gets a z score against the pseudo-pairs. Cells above one point six four five are kept, and neighbouring cells form clusters: cells whose channels are neighbours in A, and in B. The mass of a cluster is the sum of its z scores. We keep the biggest one.

Then we do the same for every pseudo-pair, scored against the others, and we count how often chance beats our cluster. Our biggest cluster has a mass of twenty point four. Among the ninety pseudo-pairs, the biggest cluster reaches up to one hundred and eighty-nine, and more than a third of them beat ours. The p-value is zero point three six.

Here is the matrix again, with its answer. Inside each head, the blocks are bright, but they are not tested: neighbouring channels share a skull. Between the heads, the cells above their band are circled. There are twenty-two of them, scattered, and no cluster survives. For this dyad, the PLV between the heads sits inside the null band. Zero point two two was not a lot.

The other measures say the same. For each, the real average between the heads, then the pseudo-pair average, then the cluster p-value. PLV: zero point two one seven, against zero point two one six, p zero point three six. ACCorr: zero point two one one, against zero point two one zero, p zero point five two. CCorr: zero point zero two three, against zero point zero two three, p zero point eight eight. wPLI: zero point one nine zero, against zero point one seven six, p zero point one one. Debiased wPLI: zero point zero zero six, against zero, p zero point three zero.

One dyad is an anecdote, so we ran the same test on all eleven groups, each against the pseudo-pairs that do not contain it. With PLV, seven of the eleven dyads have a cluster p-value below zero point zero five. With ACCorr, six. With CCorr, one. With wPLI, none, and none with the debiased version. With ninety pseudo-pairs, the smallest possible p-value is zero point zero one one, and two dyads reach it with PLV. Our dyad is not among them.

These tests are not corrected across measures or across dyads, and this is one dataset. What they show is that the answer depends on the measure, and that only a null band makes it visible.

Let's recap. One dyad, two of the three players. wPLI from a library, with its debiased version. Pseudo-pairs from the other groups. A band cell by cell, then a cluster test. And the matrix, read against its null band.

This time the data are real. They come from an open dataset called InterGenSynchrony, shared in the public domain on OpenNeuro by Moffat and Cross. Sixty-one dyads, one hundred and twenty-two adults, drawing together over six sessions, recorded with a Cortivision cap. Thirty-one of the dyads pair an older and a younger adult. We take one of those pairs, from session five, during the drawing task.

Step one is to open the two files. Each head has its own recording, and MNE reads the SNIRF file directly. Each head has fifty-two channels: twenty-two long pairs, about twenty to twenty-six millimeters apart, and four short pairs, ten millimeters apart. The two recordings did not start together, and they do not tick at the same rate: five point zero zero hertz for one head, five point one zero for the other. So we cut each head at its own triggers, and resample both to five hertz. The co-drawing task is two blocks of three hundred seconds.

Step two is quality. We compute the scalp coupling index of every channel on both heads, and keep those above zero point five. The dataset's own status flags said every channel was good. The index disagrees in four places: one short channel on the older head, and three long pairs on the younger head. Nineteen long pairs are good on both heads.

Step three is the same chain on both heads, with no setting that belongs to one person. Optical density. Motion correction with temporal derivative distribution repair. Then the modified Beer-Lambert law, which gives oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin.

Step four is the short channels. A short channel sees the scalp, not the brain. So each long channel gets a scaled copy of its nearest short channel subtracted, and we do it in hemoglobin space, with one weight for each of the two hemoglobins.

The library function, short channel regression in MNE-NIRS, works on optical density. We tried it first. Two details came out. Its default distance limit of ten millimeters misses one short channel, which sits a hair over ten, so we set twelve. And it picks the nearest short channel without looking at the wavelength: all twenty-two long channels at eight hundred and fifty nanometers were paired with a short channel at seven hundred and sixty. So we called it once per wavelength.

It gave the same picture as the hemoglobin version, which part two shows. But on this dataset it added variance to forty-four percent of the deoxygenated channels, against twenty-three percent for the hemoglobin version. That is a measurement on these data, not a rule. So the hemoglobin version is the one we keep.

Before any coherence is computed, we fix the band, and say why. Eight to fifty seconds. In twenty twelve, Cui and colleagues centered theirs on a trial of about seven seconds. Here there are no trials, only a block of three hundred seconds. Fifty seconds still leaves six cycles. Eight seconds leaves out the heartbeat and the breath. The slow oscillation near ten seconds stays on purpose, because it comes from the body, and part two asks what removing it does. This is a choice of this series, not a rule of the field. We write it down now, and we will not change it after the first result.

Part 2

Welcome back to the pair desk. This is part two of a dyad from scratch. In part one we opened a real dyad, cleaned both heads the same way, regressed the short channels, and fixed the band, eight to fifty seconds, before looking at anything. Now we compute the coherence.

Wavelet coherence, step by step. First, slide a small wave, a Morlet wavelet, along each signal. For every moment and every period, this gives an amplitude and a phase. Second, multiply the result of one head by the conjugate of the other. That product is large when the two phases stay locked together. Third, smooth it, in time and across periods. Without this smoothing, the coherence is exactly one everywhere, and tells us nothing. Fourth, divide by the smoothed power of each signal. The result lies between zero and one. We keep only what lies outside the cone of influence, where the edges of the block distort the wave. We wrote it with plain SciPy, and checked it on synthetic signals where the answer is known.

Then the band. For each pair of homologous channels, the same channel on both heads, we average the coherence over the periods from eight to fifty seconds, and over time. Nineteen channel pairs, two blocks, one number for the dyad.

Here is the map for the first block, averaged over the nineteen channel pairs. Before the regression on the left, after on the right.

They look nearly the same. The band mean is zero point three four four before, and zero point three four three after. The regression took variance out of single channels, but it did not take a visible slice of coherence out of this dyad. The optical density route gives zero point three four one.

Is zero point three four a lot? A coherence has no natural zero. Even two unrelated signals give a positive value. So we need a reference. Take an older adult from one dyad, and a younger adult from another, same session, same task. They never drew together. We run them through exactly the same chain, regression included. There are five hundred and six such pseudo-pairs.

Before the regression, the pseudo-pairs average zero point three three seven, and their ninety-fifth percentile is zero point three six zero. Our dyad sits at zero point three four four, inside the crowd, at the sixty-fourth percentile. Of the twenty-three real dyads, none rises above that line.

After the regression, the whole crowd moves. The pseudo-pairs now average zero point three four one, and they are much tighter. Our dyad is at zero point three four three, at the fifty-sixth percentile. Two real dyads out of twenty-three cross the line, where chance alone would give about one.

So the regression changed the reference more than it changed the dyad. We expected the cleaning to reshape the picture, and it barely did. That is why we check, even when we expect an effect. A map can look like synchrony, and a number can look big. Only the comparison with pseudo-pairs tells us whether it is more than two people drawing at the same time. This is one dyad, one band and one task: a demonstration of the method, not a finding about drawing together.

Let's recap. Compute the coherence by hand, once. Average the band over homologous pairs. Draw the map before and after. Build pseudo-pairs, processed the same way. Read the dyad against them.
