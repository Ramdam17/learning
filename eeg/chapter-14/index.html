Decoding over time

Decoding asks whether the pattern across all electrodes lets a classifier tell conditions apart, like a detective combining many weak clues. A classifier trained at each time point yields a decoding curve, and training at one time and testing at others gives a temporal generalization matrix. Cross-validation is mandatory, and above-chance decoding shows information is present, not that the brain uses it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter fourteen: multivariate analysis and decoding. First: decoding over time.

So far, most analyses looked at one electrode, or one region, at a time. Decoding turns the question around. Instead of asking "how does this electrode respond to condition A versus B?", it asks: "from the pattern across all electrodes, can a computer tell which condition the participant saw?"

Picture a detective's office. A single clue rarely solves a case. But a pattern of small clues, each weak on its own, can point clearly to one suspect. A decoder combines many weak clues across electrodes.

Here is how decoding over time works. Take your epochs, for example faces and houses. At one time point, say one hundred and seventy milliseconds after the stimulus, each epoch is described by sixty-four numbers: the voltage at each electrode. Train a classifier, such as logistic regression or linear discriminant analysis, to separate faces from houses using these numbers. Then repeat at every time point.

The result is a decoding curve: classification performance over time. Before the stimulus, it hovers around chance. When the brain starts to process the stimulus differently, the curve rises. When it rises, the electrodes carry information about the condition at that moment.

Performance is often measured with the area under the R-O-C curve, or A-U-C, where zero point five is chance and one is perfect. Accuracy can also be used, but it is misleading when the classes are unbalanced.

Now the most important rule in decoding: cross-validation. You must never evaluate a classifier on the data used to train it. A flexible classifier can memorize the training data, including its noise, and score perfectly on it, while knowing nothing useful. So split the epochs into folds. Train on all folds but one, test on the left-out fold, and rotate. The average test performance is the honest estimate.

Then you can go one step further, with temporal generalization, popularized by Jean-Remi King and Stanislas Dehaene in 2014. Train a classifier at one time point, and test it at every other time point. Repeat for all training times. The result is a matrix, with training time on one axis and testing time on the other.

The shape of this matrix tells a story. A narrow diagonal means that the brain's code keeps changing: a pattern at one moment is not reused later, like a chain of different processing stages. A square block means that the same code is sustained over time. Off-diagonal patterns can reveal a code that disappears and comes back.

What does an above-chance result mean? It means that information about the condition is present in the signals at that time. It does not tell you that the brain uses that information, or where it comes from. And it can come from unwanted sources, such as eye movements or muscle activity, if they differ between conditions. Always check what drives the decoder.

Statistics are usually done across participants: for each time point, is decoding above chance at the group level? Permutation tests and cluster corrections, from chapter fifteen, handle the many time points.

Tom Grootswagers, Susan Wardle and Thomas Carlson wrote a practical introduction to decoding for M-E-G and E-E-G in 2017.

In Python, M-N-E provides SlidingEstimator for decoding over time and GeneralizingEstimator for temporal generalization, built on scikit-learn pipelines and cross-validation.

To recap. Decoding asks whether the pattern across electrodes predicts the condition. Train a classifier at each time point to get a decoding curve. Always cross-validate: never test on training data. Temporal generalization trains at one time and tests at others, revealing whether codes change or persist. Above-chance decoding means information is present, not that the brain uses it, and artifacts can be decoded too.

Common Spatial Patterns

Common spatial patterns find spatial filters whose virtual channel has maximal power in one condition relative to the other, solved as a generalized eigenvalue problem on the two covariance matrices. Log-variance of a few filtered channels feeds a linear classifier, as in motor imagery. Spatial patterns, not filters, show the source on the scalp, and the method must be learned inside cross-validation.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Common spatial patterns. A filter that finds what differs in power between two conditions.

Motor imagery is a classic brain-computer interface task. The participant imagines moving the left hand or the right hand, without moving. As we saw in episode four point three, imagining a movement reduces the mu and beta power over the opposite motor cortex. So left-hand imagery lowers power over the right hemisphere, and right-hand imagery over the left.

The question is: which combination of electrodes best captures this difference in power? Common spatial patterns, or C-S-P, answers it directly.

Here is the idea. C-S-P looks for spatial filters, each one a set of weights across electrodes. Applied to the data, a filter gives a single virtual channel. The first C-S-P filter is chosen so that this virtual channel has the highest possible variance in condition A, relative to condition B. The last filter does the opposite: high variance in B, low in A. Since band-pass filtered signals have zero mean, variance is simply power.

Mathematically, this is a generalized eigenvalue problem, using the covariance matrices of the two conditions. Herbert Ramoser, Johannes Mueller-Gerking and Gert Pfurtscheller described its use for single-trial motor imagery classification in 2000.

Picture the detective with a pair of tinted glasses. One lens makes clues from suspect A stand out brightly, and fades those from suspect B. The other lens does the reverse.

In practice, the pipeline is simple. Band-pass filter the data, for example eight to thirty hertz. Learn a few C-S-P filters from the training data: usually two or three from each end. Apply them to each trial. Take the logarithm of the variance of each virtual channel. These few numbers become the features for a linear classifier.

Now the key point for interpretation: the filter is not the pattern. The filter weights tell you how to combine electrodes to extract the signal, while suppressing noise. They can have large weights on electrodes far from the source, just to cancel noise there. To see where the activity comes from, look at the corresponding spatial pattern, which shows how the extracted source projects onto the scalp. Stefan Haufe and colleagues explained this distinction in 2014, and it applies to any linear decoder.

C-S-P also has weaknesses. It is sensitive to outliers and artifacts, because covariance matrices are. A single noisy trial can dominate. It can overfit with few trials and many electrodes. Regularized versions shrink the covariance estimates, or combine data across participants, to make it more robust. It assumes that the relevant information is in power differences, and needs a well-chosen frequency band. Filter bank C-S-P runs it in several bands and selects the best features.

And the same leakage rule as always: the C-S-P filters must be learned inside the cross-validation loop, on training data only. Learning them on all trials and then cross-validating the classifier gives an optimistic result.

In Python, M-N-E provides a C-S-P class that fits directly into a scikit-learn pipeline, and can plot both filters and patterns.

To recap. Common spatial patterns find spatial filters that maximize power in one condition relative to the other, using the two covariance matrices. Log-variance of a few filtered channels feeds a linear classifier. Interpret the patterns, not the filters. C-S-P is sensitive to outliers and can overfit, so use regularization, enough trials, and learn it inside cross-validation.

Next time: Riemannian geometry of covariance matrices.

PLATE 14.34:20

Riemannian geometry of covariance matrices

Each trial is summarized by its covariance matrix, and such matrices lie on a curved manifold, like routes on a globe rather than a flat map. Geodesic distance respects that curvature. A classifier can assign a trial to the nearest Riemannian class mean, or flatten the manifold into a tangent space for standard classifiers; re-centering sessions helps transfer, with care for sample size and rank.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Riemannian geometry of covariance matrices. Measuring distances along a curved surface.

Common spatial patterns used covariance matrices as an intermediate step. Riemannian methods use them directly. Each trial is summarized by its covariance matrix: how every electrode varies with every other electrode during that trial. This matrix captures both power, on the diagonal, and the relationships between electrodes, off the diagonal.

Covariance matrices are special. They are symmetric, and positive definite: every combination of electrodes has positive variance. The set of all such matrices is not a flat space. It is a curved surface, a manifold.

Picture the detective planning a trip between two cities. On a flat map, the shortest path looks like a straight line. But the Earth is curved, and the true shortest route follows the curve, like a flight path over the pole. Using flat-map distances on a curved world gives the wrong answer.

The same happens with covariance matrices. Averaging them like ordinary numbers, or measuring straight-line distances between them, ignores the curvature, and can give strange results. For example, averaging two covariance matrices in the usual way can inflate the result's determinant, a distortion sometimes called the swelling effect. Riemannian geometry provides the right distance: the length of the shortest path along the curved surface, called the geodesic distance.

Alexandre Barachant and colleagues brought these tools to brain-computer interfaces around 2012. The simplest classifier is called minimum distance to mean. For each class, compute the Riemannian mean of its training covariance matrices. A new trial is assigned to the class whose mean is closest, along the curved surface.

A second approach is the tangent space. Choose a reference point on the manifold, usually the overall mean, and flatten the neighbourhood around it, like unrolling a small patch of the globe onto a map. In this flat tangent space, each covariance matrix becomes an ordinary vector. Then any standard classifier, such as logistic regression, can be used.

Why do these methods work so well? They use the full covariance structure, not just a few filters. They are invariant to many linear transformations of the data, including a change of reference or a mixing of channels. And they have few parameters to tune. Simple Riemannian classifiers have rivalled much more complex methods in motor imagery, evoked responses, and several machine-learning competitions.

They also help with a key problem in brain-computer interfaces: data from different sessions or participants often have different covariance structures. Re-centering each session around its own mean, an idea studied by Paolo Zanini and colleagues in 2018, aligns sessions and improves transfer from one to another.

Cautions remain. Covariance estimates need enough samples per trial relative to the number of electrodes, or they become unstable. Shrinkage helps. Rank deficiency, after average referencing or I-C-A, must be handled, for example by reducing dimensions first. Artifacts still distort covariance. And these methods are mostly tools for prediction: interpreting which brain features drive the decision takes extra work.

In Python, pyriemann, created by Alexandre Barachant and developed with others, implements covariance estimation, Riemannian means, minimum distance to mean, tangent space, and re-centering, all compatible with scikit-learn.

To recap. Each trial becomes a covariance matrix, which lives on a curved manifold. Riemannian geometry measures distances along that surface. Minimum distance to mean classifies by the nearest class mean, and the tangent space flattens the manifold for standard classifiers. These methods are simple, robust and transfer well across sessions, with care for sample size, rank and artifacts.

Next time: encoding models and temporal response functions.

PLATE 14.44:21

Encoding models · Temporal response functions

Encoding models predict continuous EEG from stimulus features such as the speech envelope. The temporal response function, an ERP-like kernel over time lags, is estimated by ridge regression on lagged copies of the stimulus, with the penalty chosen by cross-validation. Quality is the correlation between predicted and recorded held-out EEG, usually small but testable; backward models reconstruct the stimulus, for example to decode attention.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Encoding models: temporal response functions. Studying the brain while it listens to a story.

Classic E-R-P experiments need short, repeated, identical trials. But real life is continuous: a conversation, a story, music, a film. Encoding models let us study the brain during such natural, continuous stimuli.

The idea is to predict the E-E-G from a description of the stimulus. Take speech as an example. A useful description is its amplitude envelope: how loud the voice is from moment to moment. The brain's response at any moment depends on the envelope over the last few hundred milliseconds. We model this with a temporal response function, or T-R-F.

A T-R-F is like an E-R-P for a continuous stimulus. It says how the E-E-G responds, at each delay, to a unit change in the stimulus feature. Convolve the stimulus envelope with the T-R-F, and you get a prediction of the E-E-G.

Picture the detective reconstructing a timeline. Every noise in the street leaves traces at different delays: a door slams, and a few moments later the neighbours react. If you know the pattern of reactions, you can predict what happens after each new noise.

How is the T-R-F estimated? As in episode eight point five, with a regression. We build a design matrix with time-lagged copies of the stimulus, for example from minus one hundred to plus four hundred milliseconds, and solve for the weights. Because neighbouring lags are highly correlated, ordinary regression is unstable. So we use ridge regression, which adds a penalty on large weights. The strength of the penalty is chosen by cross-validation.

Edmund Lalor and colleagues developed this approach for E-E-G, and Michael Crosse and colleagues released the m-T-R-F toolbox in 2016, which made it widely used.

Now the key part: the model is evaluated by how well it predicts new data. Fit the T-R-F on part of the recording, predict the E-E-G on the held-out part, and compute the correlation between predicted and recorded signals. That prediction accuracy becomes the measure. You can compare it between conditions, groups, or stimulus features.

Prediction correlations for E-E-G are typically small, often below zero point one or zero point two, because the E-E-G contains much more than the response to the stimulus. That is expected. The question is whether they are reliably above what you would get by chance, for example by pairing the E-E-G with a mismatched segment of the stimulus.

Encoding models are flexible. The stimulus can be described by several features at once: the envelope, the onsets of words, phonetic features, or the surprise of each word given its context. Comparing models with and without a feature tells you whether that feature adds explanatory power.

The reverse direction is possible too: a backward model, or decoder, reconstructs the stimulus from all electrodes. It has been used to detect which of two speakers a listener is attending to, an approach introduced by James O'Sullivan and colleagues in 2015.

Cautions. Stimulus features are often correlated with each other, which makes their contributions hard to separate. The regularization must be chosen inside cross-validation, without touching the test data. And the T-R-F weights of a backward model are filters, not patterns: interpret them with the same care as in C-S-P.

In Python, M-N-E's ReceptiveField class estimates T-R-Fs with ridge regression, in a scikit-learn style.

To recap. Encoding models predict the E-E-G from continuous stimulus features. The temporal response function is estimated with time-lagged ridge regression, with the penalty chosen by cross-validation. Prediction accuracy on held-out data is the measure, usually small but testable. Several features can be compared. Backward models reconstruct the stimulus, for example to decode attention.

Next time: representational similarity analysis.

PLATE 14.54:16

Representational similarity analysis

Representational similarity analysis summarizes the response to many stimuli as a dissimilarity matrix: which stimuli the brain treats as alike. Matrices from models, behaviour, fMRI or neural network layers share the same stimuli and can be compared by rank correlation, giving time courses of when each kind of information appears. Noise biases dissimilarities, so cross-validated distances and controls for low-level features are needed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Representational similarity analysis. Comparing brains, models and machines in a common space.

Decoding asks whether two conditions can be told apart. Representational similarity analysis, or R-S-A, asks a richer question: how are many conditions arranged relative to each other in the brain's response?

Here is the idea. Take a set of stimuli, for example twenty-four images: faces, animals, tools, places. For each pair of stimuli, measure how different their E-E-G responses are, at a given time point. That difference is called a dissimilarity. One common choice is decoding accuracy between the pair; others are one minus the correlation between patterns, or a distance.

Put all these dissimilarities in a matrix, with stimuli as rows and columns. This is the representational dissimilarity matrix, or R-D-M. It describes the geometry of the brain's representation: which stimuli it treats as similar, and which as different.

Picture the detective's board of suspects. Instead of describing each suspect in isolation, you draw how similar every pair is. Two suspects with the same alibi and habits sit close together. The pattern of similarities says more than any single description.

Now the key move. You can build R-D-Ms from anything: a model of visual features, a semantic model, behavioural similarity judgments, f-M-R-I responses in a brain region, or the layers of an artificial neural network. Because each R-D-M is a matrix over the same stimuli, they can all be compared directly, even though they come from completely different measurements.

To compare two R-D-Ms, correlate their entries, usually with a rank correlation such as Spearman's or Kendall's. If the brain R-D-M at one hundred and fifty milliseconds resembles the R-D-M of a model of low-level visual features, and the brain R-D-M at four hundred milliseconds resembles a semantic model, you learn when each kind of information is represented.

Nikolaus Kriegeskorte, Marieke Mur and Peter Bandettini introduced R-S-A in 2008. With E-E-G and M-E-G, it can be computed at every time point, giving a time course of representational similarity. Radoslaw Cichy, Dimitrios Pantazis and Aude Oliva used this in 2014 to fuse M-E-G and f-M-R-I: matching R-D-Ms across modalities shows when and where a representation emerges.

R-S-A has become a common way to compare E-E-G with artificial neural networks. If the representations in successive layers of a vision network match brain R-D-Ms at successive times, that suggests a similar processing hierarchy, at least in terms of representational geometry.

Cautions. Dissimilarities from noisy data are biased: noise makes every pair look different. Cross-validated distances, computed between independent parts of the data, reduce this bias. Many model R-D-Ms are correlated with each other, so a match with one model does not exclude others. Low-level differences between stimuli, such as luminance or size, can drive E-E-G R-D-Ms, so they should be controlled or modelled. And statistical inference needs care: permutation tests over stimuli, and corrections over time points.

In Python, rsatoolbox and mne-rsa compute R-D-Ms, compare them with models, and integrate with M-N-E epochs.

To recap. R-S-A summarizes the brain's response to many stimuli as a representational dissimilarity matrix. Any model or measurement over the same stimuli can be turned into an R-D-M and compared, with rank correlations. With E-E-G, this gives time courses, and enables comparisons with f-M-R-I and neural networks. Use cross-validated dissimilarities, control low-level features, and test with permutations.

Next time: decoding pitfalls.

PLATE 14.64:21

Decoding pitfalls

A decoder exploits any difference between conditions, including leaks. Selecting features, standardizing or fitting ICA outside the cross-validation loop can yield high accuracy on pure noise, block designs let a classifier learn recording time, artifacts such as blinks are decodable, small samples make chance unreliable, and peak picking inflates results. Permutation tests and shuffled-label runs expose these problems.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Decoding pitfalls. How to get ninety-five percent accuracy on nothing.

Decoding is powerful, and that is exactly why it is easy to fool yourself. A classifier will exploit any difference between conditions, including the ones you did not intend. Here are the most common traps.

Picture the detective who solves every case instantly, until someone notices that the answer was written on the back of each file. The method was perfect. The evidence had leaked.

Trap one: data leakage through preprocessing. Suppose you standardize each feature using the mean and standard deviation of all trials, then cross-validate. Information about the test trials has already shaped the training data. The same happens if you select the best electrodes or time windows using all the data, or fit I-C-A, C-S-P, or any data-driven step before splitting. The fix: every step that learns from data must be fitted inside the cross-validation loop, on training data only. In scikit-learn, put all steps in a single pipeline, and pass the whole pipeline to the cross-validation.

Here is a dramatic example with pure noise. Generate random data for two fake conditions. Select the hundred features that differ most between conditions, using all trials. Then cross-validate a classifier on those features. Accuracy can soar well above chance, even though there is nothing to find. Do the selection inside each fold, and accuracy falls back to chance.

Trap two: temporal dependence. E-E-G trials close in time are similar: slow drifts, electrode changes, fatigue and drowsiness. If all trials of condition A come in one block and all trials of condition B in another, a classifier can learn the time of the recording rather than the condition. Ren Li and colleagues showed in 2021 that block designs can inflate decoding accuracy in this way. The fix: interleave conditions randomly during recording, and when blocks are unavoidable, keep whole blocks together in the same fold.

Trap three: artifacts. If conditions differ in eye movements, blinks or muscle tension, the classifier will happily decode them. Check decoding on E-O-G channels, inspect the patterns, and make sure the time course of decoding makes physiological sense.

Trap four: what counts as chance? With two balanced classes, chance is fifty percent in theory. With few trials, observed accuracy fluctuates a lot by chance alone. Etienne Combrisson and Karim Jerbi showed in 2015 that with small samples, accuracies well above fifty percent can occur on random data. Use permutation tests to build the real null distribution, and use balanced accuracy or A-U-C when classes are unbalanced.

Trap five: small samples and large error bars. Gael Varoquaux showed in 2018 that cross-validation estimates from small datasets have large uncertainties, often ten percent or more. Report the variability, not just the mean.

Trap six: double dipping in interpretation. Choosing the time window where decoding peaks, then reporting the peak accuracy as the result, inflates it. Choose analysis windows in advance, or use independent data to select them.

A simple habit catches many of these traps: run your entire pipeline on data with shuffled labels. If you get anything other than chance, something is leaking.

In Python, scikit-learn's Pipeline, cross-validation splitters such as GroupKFold for blocks, and permutation test score cover most of these safeguards, and M-N-E's decoding tools are built on them.

To recap. Every data-driven step must be fitted inside the cross-validation loop. Temporal dependence and block designs can create false decoding. Artifacts are decodable too. Chance is not fifty percent with small samples: use permutations. Report the uncertainty of cross-validation. Choose windows in advance. And always test the pipeline on shuffled labels.
