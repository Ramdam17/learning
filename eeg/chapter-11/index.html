Entropy and mutual information in one picture

Entropy measures uncertainty in bits, like not knowing which of a thousand shelves holds a book. Mutual information is the overlap of two signals' entropy circles: never negative, zero if independent, and unlike correlation it catches nonlinear dependence, such as Y equal to X squared. It is hard to estimate, is fooled by volume conduction, and has no direction.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter eleven: an information bridge. Entropy and mutual information, in one picture.

This short chapter is a bridge to our separate series on information theory, which covers the theory and the estimators in depth. Here, we only look at what these measures bring to EEG, and at their specific traps.

Start with uncertainty. Before you look at a signal, how unsure are you about its next value? If the value could be almost anything, you are very unsure. If it almost always takes the same value, you are not unsure at all. Claude Shannon gave this uncertainty a precise definition in 1948. He called it entropy, and measured it in bits.

Picture a library at night. A book could be on any of a thousand shelves. That is a lot of uncertainty. Now someone tells you the book's section. Your uncertainty shrinks. The amount it shrinks by is the information the hint gave you.

Now take two signals, X and Y. Draw the entropy of each as a circle. Where the circles overlap is the uncertainty they share: what knowing one tells you about the other. That overlap is the mutual information.

If the two signals are independent, the circles do not overlap, and the mutual information is zero. The more one signal tells you about the other, the larger the overlap. Mutual information is never negative.

Why bother, when we already have correlation and coherence? Because those measures only capture linear relationships. Here is a classic example. Let Y be the square of X, where X is symmetric around zero. Y is completely determined by X. And yet their correlation is zero: the positive and negative halves cancel. The mutual information, on the other hand, is clearly positive. It sees the dependence.

So mutual information captures any kind of statistical dependence, linear or not. For two Gaussian signals, it brings nothing new: it is a simple function of the correlation. Its value appears when the relationship is nonlinear.

Mutual information is also flexible. It can relate two electrodes, an electrode and a stimulus category, or a brain signal and a behavioural measure, all on the same scale, in bits.

Now, the traps.

First, estimation. Mutual information is easy to define, but hard to estimate from a limited number of samples. Simple methods, like cutting values into bins, are biased upwards, especially with few data points. The next episode shows a robust estimator.

Second, mutual information sees every kind of dependence. That includes the dependence created by volume conduction and by a shared reference. Two neighbouring electrodes that see the same source will share a lot of information, without any interaction. Everything from chapter ten still applies.

Third, mutual information has no direction and no timing on its own. It says that two signals share something, not who sent it. Episode eleven point three adds direction.

And fourth, a value in bits needs a reference. As with connectivity, compare it to surrogates or between conditions, not to zero.

In Python, the frites package, from Etienne Combrisson and colleagues, computes mutual information on electrophysiological data, and the information series covers other libraries.

To recap. Entropy measures uncertainty, in bits. Mutual information is the uncertainty two signals share. It is zero for independent signals, never negative, and it captures nonlinear dependence that correlation misses. But it is hard to estimate, it is fooled by volume conduction like any other measure, and it has no direction.

Gaussian-copula mutual information. A fast, robust way to estimate information between EEG signals.

Last time, we saw that mutual information is easy to define and hard to estimate. With a few hundred trials, the simplest estimators, based on histograms, give values that are inflated, and that depend on how you choose the bins.

In 2017, Robin Ince and colleagues proposed an estimator designed for neuroimaging. It is called the Gaussian-copula mutual information, or G-C-M-I.

It works in two steps.

Step one: the copula transform. Take all the values of signal X, and replace each value by its rank: the smallest becomes one, the next becomes two, and so on. Then map these ranks onto a standard Gaussian distribution. Do the same for signal Y. Now both signals have perfectly Gaussian distributions, whatever their original shape.

This step keeps the order of the values, and throws away their original distribution. A skewed signal, a signal with outliers, a signal in microvolts or in decibels: after the transform, they all look the same.

Picture the library again. Instead of measuring the exact thickness of each book, you just line them up from thinnest to thickest, and give each one its place in the line. Any rule that keeps the same order gives the same line.

Step two: use the formula for Gaussian variables. For Gaussian signals, mutual information has a simple closed form. For two single signals, it depends only on their correlation: minus one half times the logarithm of one minus the correlation squared. No bins, no tuning parameters, and it is very fast to compute.

The result has several useful properties. It is robust to outliers, because ranks ignore extreme values. It works with few samples. And it extends naturally to several dimensions at once. For example, you can describe a signal by its value and its temporal derivative together, or describe a phase by its cosine and its sine.

Now the honest part. The copula transform only keeps one kind of dependence: the kind a Gaussian copula can model. In one dimension, its sensitivity is similar to a rank correlation. So G-C-M-I gives a lower bound on the true mutual information. If the relationship is monotonic, the bound is good. If it is strongly non-monotonic, for example Y equal to X squared, G-C-M-I can stay close to zero, even though the true mutual information is large.

In other words, the estimator matters as much as the measure. G-C-M-I trades sensitivity to every possible dependence for robustness, speed, and good behaviour with small samples. For many neuroimaging questions, that is a good trade.

A practical point: like other estimators, G-C-M-I has a small bias with few samples, and an analytic correction exists for the Gaussian case. Even so, test against surrogates, for example by shuffling trials, before interpreting a value.

And the usual EEG caution applies: between two electrodes, shared sources and the reference create shared information. G-C-M-I measures dependence, not interaction.

Where is it used? To relate brain signals to stimuli, trial by trial. To compare electrodes or sources over time. And as a building block for conditional mutual information, transfer entropy, and higher-order measures, which we meet in the next two episodes.

In Python, the gcmi functions released with the paper, and the frites package, implement it.

To recap. G-C-M-I replaces values by ranks, maps them onto a Gaussian, and applies the closed-form Gaussian formula. It is fast, robust to outliers, works with few samples and in several dimensions. It is a lower bound on the true information, close to a rank correlation in one dimension, and can miss non-monotonic relationships.

Transfer entropy · and why it meets Granger

Transfer entropy asks whether the past of X reduces uncertainty about the future of Y beyond Y's own past, a conditional mutual information. For Gaussian signals it equals half the Granger causality, so it adds something only with nonlinear dependence. It requires choosing an embedding and a delay, and inherits the traps of directed EEG connectivity: instantaneous mixing, unequal signal-to-noise ratios and common drivers.

Transfer entropy, and why it meets Granger causality.

In episode ten point nine, Granger causality asked a simple question: does the past of X help predict the future of Y? Transfer entropy asks the same question, in the language of information.

Thomas Schreiber introduced transfer entropy in 2000. Here is the idea. Take the future of Y. How uncertain is it, once you know the past of Y? Now, how uncertain is it, once you know the past of Y and the past of X? If knowing the past of X reduces the uncertainty further, information is transferred from X to Y.

Formally, transfer entropy is a conditional mutual information: the information shared by the past of X and the future of Y, given the past of Y. Conditioning on Y's own past is essential. It removes what Y could already predict about itself.

Picture the library at night. A reader is about to pick the next book. Knowing the reader's own history already narrows the choice. Does knowing what the librarian recommended yesterday narrow it further? If so, something flows from the librarian to the reader.

Now the key result. In 2009, Lionel Barnett, Adam Barrett and Anil Seth showed that for Gaussian variables, Granger causality and transfer entropy are equivalent. Transfer entropy equals Granger causality divided by two, when information is measured in natural units.

That means that for signals well described by linear Gaussian models, transfer entropy tells you nothing that Granger causality does not. Transfer entropy only brings something new when the dependence is nonlinear, or the distributions are far from Gaussian.

That is the trade-off. Granger causality is fast, well understood, and comes with spectral versions. Transfer entropy is model-free, but needs more data, and careful estimators.

Transfer entropy also has its own choices to make. How much of the past do you use? This is called the embedding, the equivalent of the model order in Granger causality. And what delay do you allow between source and target? Interactions in the brain take time, and choosing the wrong delay can hide them. Michael Wibral and colleagues proposed scanning over delays and keeping the one that maximizes transfer.

And all the traps of directed measures from chapter ten are still here. Volume conduction mixes sources instantly. Two electrodes with unequal signal-to-noise ratios can create a false direction, the cleaner one seeming to drive the noisier one. A common driver with different delays can look like a transfer. Filtering can smear the past into the future. So in practice, use source space, test against surrogates that keep each signal's own structure, and check robustness.

Like mutual information, transfer entropy is a quantity, not an estimator. You can estimate it with the Gaussian copula from the last episode, which keeps it fast and robust. Or you can use nearest-neighbour estimators, which are more sensitive to nonlinear effects, but slower and more demanding in data.

In Python, IDTxl, from Patricia Wollstadt and colleagues, provides transfer entropy and network inference, with surrogate testing built in. Frites offers Gaussian-copula building blocks, like conditional mutual information.

To recap. Transfer entropy asks whether the past of X reduces the uncertainty about the future of Y, beyond Y's own past. It is a conditional mutual information. For Gaussian signals, it equals half the Granger causality, so it only adds something when relationships are nonlinear. It needs choices of embedding and delay, and it inherits every trap of directed connectivity in EEG.

Beyond pairs · Higher-order interactions

Some information exists only in groups of signals. With X and Y random bits and Z their exclusive or, every pairwise mutual information is zero, yet the trio shares one full bit: synergy. Redundancy is the same information copied across signals, as with electrodes seeing one source, and the O-information gives the balance, positive for redundancy and negative for synergy.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Beyond pairs: higher-order interactions. When three signals share something that no pair can show.

Every connectivity measure in the last two chapters looked at pairs: electrode A with electrode B. A network was a collection of pairs. But some information only exists when you look at three signals, or more, at the same time.

Here is the classic example. Take three binary signals. X and Y are random coin flips, independent of each other. Z is "exclusive or" of X and Y: Z is one when exactly one of them is one. Now look at the pairs. X and Y share no information. X and Z share none. Y and Z share none. Every pairwise mutual information is zero. And yet, if you know any two of the three, you know the third exactly. The whole trio shares one full bit.

A pairwise connectivity matrix of this system would be completely empty. The structure is invisible, unless you look at all three together.

Picture three readers in the library at night. Each one alone tells you nothing about the others' books. But put two of them together, and you know exactly what the third one is reading. The information lives in the group, not in any pair.

This is called synergy: information that is only available from several signals jointly. The opposite is redundancy: the same information copied in several signals, so that any one of them is enough. Imagine three electrodes that all see the same source, through volume conduction. They are highly redundant.

How can we summarize the balance between the two? In 2019, Fernando Rosas and colleagues proposed the O-information. It compares two ways of counting the dependencies in a group of signals. When it is positive, the group is dominated by redundancy. When it is negative, it is dominated by synergy.

In the exclusive or example, the O-information is negative: pure synergy. For three electrodes driven by the same source, it is positive: redundancy.

There are other frameworks, like the partial information decomposition, which tries to split information into unique, redundant and synergistic parts. These are covered in detail in the information series.

Now the cautions, which you can probably guess.

First, estimation. Higher-order measures combine many entropies, each with its own bias. The number of possible groups grows very fast with the number of channels. With sixty-four electrodes, there are more than forty thousand triplets. Fast estimators, like the Gaussian copula, and strong statistics are essential.

Second, volume conduction again. Mixing creates redundancy everywhere, especially between neighbouring electrodes. That can hide synergy, or make the network look redundant for reasons that have nothing to do with brain interactions. Source space helps.

Third, interpretation. The O-information is a balance. A value near zero can mean that nothing is happening, or that redundancy and synergy cancel. And the definitions of synergy are still debated.

Why is this exciting for EEG? Because brain function is likely to rely on interactions among many regions at once. A network that looks disconnected in pairs may still carry information in groups. Higher-order measures give us a way to look for it.

In Python, the hoi package, from Matteo Neri, Etienne Combrisson and colleagues, computes the O-information and related measures efficiently, using the Gaussian copula.

To recap. Some information exists only in groups of signals: that is synergy. Information copied across signals is redundancy. The exclusive or example has zero information in every pair, and one full bit in the trio. The O-information summarizes the balance: positive for redundancy, negative for synergy. Estimation, the number of groups, volume conduction and interpretation all need care.
