PLATE 7.15:15

Synchrony from nowhere

Two simulated people in separate rooms, with no contact, show a phase locking value of 0.59 simply because both produce a ten hertz alpha rhythm. Shift one frequency and the value collapses. Real partners score no higher than swapped pseudo-pairs, and a condition effect can come from rhythm strength alone, so a raw value always needs a pseudo-pair comparison.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The curtain rises on the conjurer's theatre. Tonight's trick has a name: synchrony from nowhere. Watch closely, because nothing is hidden, and still you will be fooled.

The conjurer holds up two top hats. They come from two different rooms. The two people who wore them have never met. From the first hat, he pulls a ribbon of signal. From the second, another. These are simulated EEG traces, one for Ava and one for Otto.

Each ribbon is a rhythm of about ten hertz, the alpha range. Each one wobbles a little as it goes, because no brain keeps perfect time. And that is all. There is no wire between them, no shared sound, no conversation. Every ingredient of each ribbon was drawn on its own.

Now the conjurer measures. He slides a two second window along both ribbons, and in each window he computes the phase locking value. It asks how steady the gap between the two phases is. For this pair, the average value is 0.59. The lamp on stage lights up. It looks like two brains in tune.

So where did the synchrony come from? Nowhere. Or rather, from the recipe. Both ribbons were made with the same dominant frequency. Two oscillators that share a frequency keep a steady phase relationship for a while, whether or not they know about each other. This is the point made by Adrian Burgess in 2013, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience. In his paper, a rhythm like alpha, in two people in two separate rooms, is the example.

Let's check that the frequency really is the ingredient. We repeat the trick sixty times, with new random ribbons each time. When both people peak at ten hertz, the average value is 0.57. Now change one thing. Let Otto peak at eight point three hertz. The average falls to 0.15. Same method, same windows, same filter. Only the shared frequency changed.

Now the conjurer reveals the trick. He swaps the hats.

Here are twelve Avas and twelve Ottos, all simulated, all independent. Each A is paired with each B. That is a grid of one hundred and forty four pairings. The twelve on the diagonal are the original partners. The other one hundred and thirty two are pseudo-pairs, people who never shared anything.

Look at the colours. The diagonal is not special. The original partners average 0.51. The pseudo-pairs average 0.50. If you had shown only the diagonal, you would have reported high synchrony. Once you see the grid, you know that the value was the same for any pairing.

That is why a raw value is not a result. A phase locking value tells you how aligned two phases are. It does not tell you why. A pseudo-pair has the same kind of signal, and no relationship, so it shows you what the measure gives for free.

Now a second, subtler trick. The conjurer gives each person two states: eyes open, with a weaker alpha rhythm, and eyes closed, with a stronger one. The people are still independent. In the simulation, the real dyads rise from 0.46 with eyes open to 0.54 with eyes closed. An abstract could say: more synchrony with eyes closed.

But look at the pseudo-pairs. They rise from 0.45 to 0.52. The same increase, with no partner at all. The condition did not change the link between people. It changed how strong each person's rhythm is, and a stronger shared rhythm gives a higher value.

This is not only a simulation. Burgess also recorded ten healthy adults, one at a time, and combined them into forty five pseudo-pairs. Between people, in the alpha band, one hundred and seventy of seven hundred and eighty four channel pairs showed a higher phase locking value with eyes closed than with eyes open, though no two of these people ever met. In his simulations with no coupling at all, he also obtained a phase locking value of 0.58 and a coherence of 0.65, for one setting of the phase spread.

So what do we do? Three habits.

First, never report a synchrony value alone. Compute the same measure on pseudo-pairs, in the same condition, and compare.

Second, when you compare two conditions, run the same comparison on the pseudo-pairs. Only the part that the real dyads have in addition counts.

Third, look at the spectra of both people, in both conditions. If the rhythm changes between conditions, expect the measure to change too. Burgess found that circular correlation was less prone to this problem. We meet it again in episode six point seven.

Let's recap. Two independent people with the same dominant frequency can show high synchrony. Change the frequency, and it disappears. Swap the hats, and the real partners look like everyone else. A condition effect can come from rhythm strength alone. Always ask what the pseudo-pairs do.

PLATE 7.24:59

Common sources: wires, rooms and references

Synchrony can arise from contamination rather than interaction. A shared mains hum drives the value at fifty hertz to one, and shared room noise lifts alpha synchrony even after a notch filter, while pseudo-pairs fail to reveal it. A phantom recording with no brains shows what the setup alone contributes, though that test is a suggestion, not a standard.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the conjurer's theatre. Today's trick needs no task, no story and no interaction. Two hats sit on two heads, far apart, and still their signals keep step. The conjurer says the secret is an invisible wire. Let us find the wire.

Episode four point three already covered the first one: a ground or a reference shared by two heads on one amplifier. We will not repeat it. Today is about everything else that connects two recordings, even with two amplifiers. The mains, the room, a neighbouring device, and the ground loop.

Start with the mains. The electrical grid alternates at fifty hertz in Europe, and sixty hertz in North America. Every cable in the walls, every power supply and every lamp gives off a little of it, and electrodes and their cables pick it up. Leske and Dalal, in NeuroImage in 2019, write that EEG and MEG signals are often exposed to strong power line interference at fifty or sixty hertz.

Here is the trick, in simulation. Two independent brains, each with its own alpha rhythm, in the same room, one channel each. Add a small hum, with the same phase in both, because there is one grid and one clock. At fifty hertz, the phase locking value between the two people is one point zero. Without the hum it is zero point zero seven. In the alpha band, nothing changes: zero point zero five, with or without the hum.

The hum is easy to remove. A notch filter at fifty hertz brings the value back to zero point zero nine. And a study that analyses only the frequencies below forty hertz would never see it. The mains is a polite intruder.

The rude intruder is the rest of the room. A power supply, a monitor, a charger, a projector: they give off broadband noise, not a clean tone, and that noise lands in the alpha band. In the simulation I add one shared room noise, reaching the first cap a little less than the second. The alpha value between the two people rises from zero point zero five to zero point four eight. A notch at fifty hertz does nothing. It stays at zero point four eight.

And here is the bad news. Pseudo-pairs do not catch it. Pair each person with someone from another session, and the value is zero point zero six, because that session had a different room noise. The real pairs sit far above the null, and the cause is a power supply. A shared contamination lives in the instant, and the null cannot see it.

Two more wires. Crosstalk is a signal leaking from one device, or one cable bundle, into the other. Suppose a fraction of Ava's signal leaks into Otto's channel. With five percent, the alpha value is zero point zero six. With ten percent, zero point zero eight. With thirty percent, zero point two two. The size of the leak is my assumption, not a measurement.

A ground loop is the same idea with a different path. Two grounds are connected twice, once through the equipment and once through the wall, and a current circulating in that loop carries the same hum into both recordings. This is general electronics knowledge. I did not find a hyperscanning paper that measures it.

So how do we make the wire visible? We show the trick, with a phantom. Replace the people with two things that are not brains: two resistors, or two phantom heads, plugged into the same setup. Same room, same cables, same positions, same analysis, and no brain. Whatever stays in step between them is the setup.

In the simulation, two empty channels give a value of zero point zero five in the alpha band. Switch on the shared hum and the room noise, and it becomes zero point nine nine. After a notch, and after regressing out a room reference channel, it falls to zero point two seven. The cleaning helps, but the phantom shows it is not enough. Fix the room: move the power supply, shorten and separate the cables.

Has anyone published this test? I searched for a phantom or dummy head protocol for hyperscanning, and for a published measurement of crosstalk between two EEG systems. I did not find them. The setup paper by Barraza and colleagues, in MethodsX in 2019, goes as far as calling a shared ground a possible confounding factor. So the phantom test is a suggestion, not a standard.

This trick has no stimulus, no rhythm in common, no prediction and no body. It belongs to none of the four sources of synchrony. It is contamination. Before recording, ask three things. What do the two setups share besides the people: a ground, a room, a power line? What does a recording with no brains say? And does the synchrony sit at the mains frequency, or in the band you analyse?

PLATE 7.34:45

The shared-stimulus trap

When a stimulus reaches both people at the same moment, their brains move together without influencing each other, and in simulation correlation peaks just after events. Three remedies address this: subtract the evoked response, compare with pseudo-pairs sharing the same timing, and add a no-interaction condition. A genuine interaction leaves a residue that these controls do not remove.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The curtain rises on the conjurer's theatre. Two people sit on the stage, far apart. They do not talk. They do not look at each other. Yet the synchrony between their brains lights up, and it follows the show.

This is the shared-stimulus trap. Behind the curtain, MAESTRO is running the whole trick. Every sound, every picture, every instruction reaches both people at the same moment. So both brains answer the same event, and both signals move together. The synchrony is real. It just is not between the two brains. It is between each brain and the experiment.

Here is the trick, in simulation. Forty dyads do the same task. An event arrives every two point two seconds, and some events are stronger than others. The two people never influence each other. The correlation between their signals is zero point four nine. These data are simulated.

Now look at when the synchrony happens. In windows that start just after an event, the correlation is zero point five eight. In the windows between events, it is zero point one two. The synchrony follows the timeline of the experiment. When you see that, suspect the stimulus first. This is route 1, the stimulus, or shared input.

The literature has warned about this. In 2013, Burgess built pseudo-pairs from people who never met. He still found spurious connections, because their EEG had been recorded under the same experimental conditions. In 2021, Hamilton argued that hyperscanning is hard to interpret on its own, and that behaviour and models of mutual prediction should be brought in. The trick is old. The remedies are three.

The first remedy removes the response to the event. Take each person's average response to the events, and subtract it. In the simulation, the correlation falls from zero point four nine to zero point one two. It does not fall to zero. Some events were stronger, and both brains answered them more strongly. So we go one step further, and regress the design. We model the response, and how it scales with the strength of each event. Now the correlation is zero. This remedy needs the timing and the strength of every event. You cannot remove what you did not record.

The second remedy uses pseudo-pairs. Take person A from one dyad, and person B from another dyad who lived exactly the same timing. They never met, but they heard the same events. Their correlation is zero point four nine, the same as the real dyads. A pseudo-pair keeps the stimulus, and removes the partner. So if the real dyads are no higher than their pseudo-pairs, the stimulus explains everything. Be careful, though. Give every dyad its own event timing, and the pseudo-pairs fall to zero point zero one, while the real dyads stay at zero point four eight. Now the control is too easy, and a study with no interaction at all looks as if it had one. Episode 7.5 comes back to this.

The third remedy is a condition without interaction, with the same stimulus. The two people do the same task, but a partition separates them, and they cannot influence each other. Their correlation is zero point four nine. The same as in the interacting condition. So the interaction added nothing.

Now a second study, with the same task and the same events, but with a real two-way influence between the two people. Its raw correlation is zero point five seven, not very different. Run the three remedies. The pseudo-pairs sit at zero point four eight, the people behind the partition at zero point four nine. After removing the evoked response, the pseudo-pairs and the partition both fall to zero, and the real dyads keep zero point one seven.

So the first study ends at zero. The second keeps something. That leftover is a candidate for route 3, mutual prediction.

Two honest limits. In a real study, responses vary from person to person and from event to event in ways you did not model, so a removal is never perfect. And removing the evoked response also removes any part of a real interaction that is locked to the events. These three remedies also answer only route 1. Rhythm and body must still be ruled out, as in episode 2.6. A survivor is a candidate, never a proof.

So, before recording, three questions. What do the two people share besides each other? Did I record its timing? And which comparison keeps the stimulus, and removes the interaction?

PLATE 7.45:38

The biases of phase measures

Phase measures sit on a false floor for unconnected signals. Narrow filters raise it, noise pulls coupled pairs down toward it, and unequal trial counts create fake condition effects: ten trials against sixty gave a false difference in ninety-five studies out of a hundred. Equalising trials or using pairwise phase consistency removes the bias, and every value needs its own floor.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the conjurer's theatre. On the stage stands a trunk. The conjurer opens the lid, and the trunk looks deep. It is not. It has a false floor, and the floor is higher than you think.

A phase measure has the same trick. Take two signals that have never met. Their phase locking value is not zero. It sits on a floor, and the height of the floor depends on how you measured. Episodes 5.5 and 6.3 showed it for the number of epochs and segments, and 6.6 for the sign based measures. Here is what they left out.

First, the width of the filter. Here are two independent noise signals, simulated, and the same noise filtered twice around ten hertz. Once through a band one hertz wide, once through a band sixteen hertz wide. The narrow band looks like a clean sine wave. The wide one wanders. A narrow filter makes neighbouring samples depend on each other, so a window holds only a few really different samples.

Now measure phase locking in a window of two seconds, on two thousand pairs of independent noise. With a band one hertz wide, the average value is zero point five five. Two hertz, zero point four oh. Four hertz, zero point two eight. Eight hertz, zero point two oh. Sixteen hertz, zero point one four. Nothing is coupled here.

As a rule of thumb, the floor is the square root of pi over four N, where N is the width times the duration. It overshoots a little: it gives zero point six three at one hertz, and zero point one six at sixteen. In white noise, Scheffer-Teixeira and Tort found the same trend for cross frequency phase locking, in eLife in 2016. The narrower the filter, the higher the value.

Second, the signal to noise ratio. Now each signal is an oscillation near ten hertz plus noise, filtered between eight and twelve hertz. Independent pairs stay near zero point three, whatever the noise. Zero point three two at twenty decibels, zero point three one at ten, zero point three oh at zero, zero point three one at minus ten.

Now a pair that really shares an oscillator. Its value is zero point eight three at twenty decibels, zero point eight three at ten, zero point seven six at zero, and only zero point five oh at minus ten. Noise does not create locking. It pulls every pair down toward the floor. So a noisy recording looks less synchronous than a clean one, even when the coupling is the same.

Third, the trap. You want to compare two conditions. One condition gave ten trials per dyad, the other gave sixty. The true locking is the same in both, zero point one five. This is a simulation of twenty four dyads.

Look at the value of each dyad. With ten trials, the average is zero point two eight. With sixty trials, zero point one seven. A paired test says the difference is real, with a p value below zero point zero one. There is your condition effect. But it is the false floor: ten trials sit on a higher floor than sixty.

How often does this happen? We simulated three thousand studies with the same true locking in both conditions. The raw comparison finds a difference in ninety five studies out of a hundred. The averages over the studies are zero point three one, and zero point one eight.

The first remedy is to equalise the trials. Take the longer condition, draw ten of its sixty trials at random, and repeat many times. Both conditions now stand on the same floor. The averages are zero point three one and zero point three one, and a false difference appears in five studies out of a hundred, as it should. The cost is that you throw data away. When the true locking really differs, zero point one five against zero point three, this analysis finds it in fifty eight studies out of a hundred.

The second remedy is the pairwise phase consistency, or PPC, introduced by Vinck and colleagues in NeuroImage in 2010. Instead of the length of the average arrow, it averages how well every pair of trials agrees, and leaves out each trial's agreement with itself. That self term is what builds the floor. Its expected value is the squared locking value, whatever the number of trials.

Here it is working. The squared true locking is zero point zero two two five. The pairwise phase consistency gives about zero point zero two at ten trials, at sixty trials, and at a hundred and sixty. The locking value falls from zero point three one, to zero point one eight, to zero point one six. For independent signals the pairwise measure is zero on average, and it can be slightly negative.

Used to compare the two conditions, it makes a false difference in seven studies out of a hundred. It keeps every trial, and finds the real difference in fifty studies out of a hundred. Both remedies remove the bias. Neither creates information. The real cure is more trials.

So, four rules. Report the window length, the filter width and the trial count next to every value. Never compare conditions with different counts using the raw value. Equalise the counts, or use a measure without this bias. And compare every value with its own floor, built from pseudo-pairs with the same counts and the same filter.

PLATE 7.55:56

Pseudo-pairs · What they control and what they don't

A pseudo-pair swaps in a partner from another dyad, removing the interaction but keeping the stimulus only when timing was shared. With identical timing it is a fair control, but with different timing the null collapses to zero and false alarms occur every time. Pair within the same schedule, condition and roles, since information is limited by the number of people, not pairings.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The curtain rises on the conjurer's theatre. Tonight's trick is a swap. On the stage stands a cabinet with two doors. Ava steps in through the left door. Out of the right door steps an Otto she has never met, from another dyad. The conjurer calls this a pseudo-pair.

You have met pseudo-pairs before. They were the first fork of a chalk tree, and a grey strip of three hundred and eighty. Today we ask a sharper question. What exactly does the swap control, and what does it leave behind?

A pseudo-pair is made of two real people who did the same task, in the same condition. So it keeps the task. It keeps the stimulus, if both people met it. And it removes one thing for sure: the interaction between these two people. That is its whole job. But notice the condition in the middle. It keeps the stimulus only if the timing was the same.

Case one: identical timing. Every dyad hears the same sounds at the same moments, like a fixed list played to everybody. We simulate twenty dyads. Nobody interacts. The only shared thing is the stimulus. The real dyads reach a mean correlation of 0.41. The pseudo-pairs reach 0.42. The real dyads sit inside the grey. The verdict is no synchrony beyond the stimulus, and that is the right answer.

Now we give the dyads a true interaction. The real dyads rise to 0.60. The pseudo-pairs stay at 0.30. The gap is what the interaction adds. This is a control working as intended.

Case two: different timing. Each dyad has its own schedule, so the sounds fall at different moments for different dyads. The simulated people are the same, and still nobody interacts. The real dyads still reach 0.41. But the pseudo-pairs now give 0.01. The swapped partners no longer hear anything at the same moment, so the grey strip collapses to zero. The real dyads tower above it. The swap removed the interaction, and the shared stimulus as well. The control was too easy.

We repeated each study four hundred times, with no interaction at all, and counted how often a test at five percent called it significant. With identical timing, it did so about five percent of the time, as promised. With different timing, it did so every single time.

The fix is to pair people who shared the timing. Suppose the dyads came in two schedules, ten dyads each. Pair only within a schedule, and the false alarms return to seven percent, close to the five we expect.

So how do we build pseudo-pairs, paradigm by paradigm? These are the series' working rules, not laws. With a fixed schedule, pair any A with any B from the same condition. If the order is randomised for each dyad, pair within the same order, or re-align both recordings on the stimulus onsets, so the same events line up. In a free conversation there is no shared schedule to protect, but match the condition and the recording length. If the roles are distinct, as in parent and child, keep them: a parent is only paired with a child. And always match the session type and the recording setup.

How many pseudo-pairs exist? With N dyads, each A can be paired with N minus one other B's. Twenty dyads give twenty times nineteen, three hundred and eighty. Ten dyads give ninety. If the two members are interchangeable, any person can meet anyone from another dyad, and twenty dyads give seven hundred and sixty.

Now suppose you reassign all the B's at once, so that nobody keeps their partner. For ten dyads there are more than one million ways. For twenty, almost nine hundred quadrillion. You will never run out of pairings.

But you can run out of information. Each person appears in nineteen pseudo-pairs. A person with a strong response gives a high value in every one of them, so the values are not independent draws. In our simulation, the mean of all three hundred and eighty values still wobbled by 0.024 from one study to the next. Independent draws would have wobbled by 0.006. The three hundred and eighty are worth about twenty-one independent ones. The people are the limit, not the pairs.

So how many should we draw? For a group test, draw whole reassignments, compute the group mean for each, and count how many reach the real value. The p-value is that count plus one, over the number of draws plus one, so it can never be zero. This is the advice of Phipson and Smyth, in 2010. More draws do not add information about people. They steady the p-value. On one simulated dataset, the p-value, near 0.14, wobbled by 0.03 with a hundred draws, by 0.011 with a thousand, and by 0.003 with ten thousand. As a rule of thumb, take at least a thousand draws, and ten thousand when you will report a small p.

One last caution. In 2013, Adrian Burgess combined separately recorded EEG into pseudo-pairs, and still found spurious hyper-connections between conditions. People in the same condition share rhythms. A same-condition pseudo-pair null contains those effects, which is exactly why the real dyads must beat it.

Let's recap. A pseudo-pair removes the interaction. It removes the shared stimulus too, unless the timing was shared. Match the condition, the roles and the timing. There are many pairings, but only about as much information as there are people. Draw enough reassignments to steady the p-value.

PLATE 7.65:57

Surrogates

A surrogate cuts one signal and glues it back differently, asking whether two signals align beyond chance. Circular shift, phase randomisation, IAAFT and block shuffle each preserve different properties, and a block shuffle with short blocks wrongly flagged shared rhythms. Surrogates cannot separate a shared stimulus from interaction, whereas pseudo-pairs ask whether the partner is special, so a solid study uses both.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The curtain rises on the conjurer's theatre, and tonight the conjurer has a workbench. On it lies a long paper strip, with a signal printed along its length. Scissors on one side, a glue stick on the other. Tonight's trick is to cut the strip into pieces, and glue it back together differently. The strip that comes out is called a surrogate.

Last time we met pseudo-pairs. A pseudo-pair swaps the partner, and asks one question: is this synchrony specific to this partner? A surrogate keeps the partner, and damages the signal. It asks a different question: is this synchrony more than these two signals would give by chance? A solid study asks both.

A surrogate must match a null hypothesis. We want a signal that looks like the real one in every way, except the one thing we test, which is the timing between the two people. So every method keeps something, and breaks something. Theiler and colleagues proposed the surrogate data method in 1992, in Physica D. Lancaster and colleagues reviewed the whole family in 2018, in Physics Reports. Let's look at four of them.

First, the circular shift. Slide one signal along the time axis, and wrap whatever falls off the end back to the start. Nothing inside the strip changes. So the spectrum, the values and the autocorrelation are all kept. Only the alignment with the other person is broken. Second, phase randomisation, from Theiler and colleagues. Take the Fourier transform. Keep every amplitude, and give every frequency a random phase. Transform back. The power spectrum is kept, and so is the autocorrelation. But the values change: their distribution is pushed toward a Gaussian, and any link between one frequency and another is gone.

Third, the IAAFT, from Schreiber and Schmitz, in 1996. The name stands for iterative amplitude adjusted Fourier transform. It repeats two steps. Put back the spectrum of the data. Then reorder the values, so that they are exactly the values of the data. After a few rounds, the surrogate has the same values, and nearly the same spectrum. It matters most when the signal is far from Gaussian.

Fourth, the block shuffle. Cut the strip into short blocks, and glue them back in a random order. Inside a block, everything is kept, and so are the values overall. But the slow rhythm is cut at every seam, so the spectrum and the long autocorrelation are gone. Make the blocks longer than the memory of the signal, or the surrogate will look too random.

Let's test them. We simulate three kinds of dyads, 100 of each, with 30 seconds of recording. In all of them, both people run a 10 hertz rhythm that wanders slowly. In the first kind, the dyads are uncoupled. They share a rhythm, and nothing else. In the second, they are truly coupled. In the third, nobody interacts, but every dyad hears the same sounds, and both brains answer each sound with a burst. For each dyad, we compute the phase locking value, and compare it with 99 surrogates. Then we count the dyads flagged at five percent.

First, the uncoupled dyads. Few should be flagged. The circular shift flags 10 out of 100. Phase randomisation flags 9, and the IAAFT flags 9. That is a little above five, but we only have 100 dyads. The block shuffle flags 37. Its blocks were half a second, shorter than the wandering of the rhythm, so its surrogates were too random, and it called a shared rhythm a connection. The pseudo-pairs flag 6.

Now the coupled dyads. Every method flags every dyad, 100 out of 100, pseudo-pairs included. When the interaction is real, both questions say yes.

Now the shared sounds. Nobody interacts. The surrogates flag 78, 83, 84 and 97 out of 100. They answer their question correctly: these two signals are more aligned than chance would give. But they say nothing about the partner. The pseudo-pairs, drawn among dyads who heard the same sounds, flag 4. They answer their question: not specific to this partner.

Two questions, two answers, and both are right. A surrogate cannot tell a shared stimulus from a shared interaction. A pseudo-pair cannot tell you the signals are aligned, only that the partner is not special. In code, each surrogate takes a few lines of NumPy. A random circular shift is numpy roll. Phase randomisation draws random phases for the real Fourier transform, and inverts it. The block shuffle reshapes the signal into blocks, and permutes them. The IAAFT is a short loop: put the spectrum back, then rank the values. Compute your measure on every surrogate exactly as on the data, with the same filter and the same window. Then the p-value is the number of surrogates at least as large as the real value, plus one, over the number of surrogates plus one.

How do we choose? Start from the null hypothesis, and ask what it must keep. For a narrow rhythm like alpha, the circular shift, phase randomisation and the IAAFT behaved well here. These are the series' working rules, not laws. Say which method you used, and how many surrogates. And put a pseudo-pair null beside it.

Let's recap. A surrogate cuts one signal and glues it back differently. The circular shift keeps everything but the alignment. Phase randomisation keeps the spectrum. The IAAFT keeps the values too. The block shuffle keeps only what is inside a block. Surrogates ask whether the signals are aligned beyond chance. Pseudo-pairs ask whether the partner matters. Ask both.
