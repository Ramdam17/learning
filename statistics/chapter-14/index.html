A shuffle is a null hypothesis

Every shuffle is a null hypothesis, so a permutation test says nothing until we say what was swapped, kept and destroyed. The same ten patients gave p of 0.007 when before and after were swapped within each patient, and 0.27 when twenty values were pooled. Wrong shuffles, like shuffling time points or trials, answer questions nobody asked.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cloakroom. A methods section says: we ran a permutation test, and the result was significant. It sounds careful. It says almost nothing. A permutation of what? Today's idea: every shuffle is a null hypothesis. Choose the shuffle, and you have chosen the question.

Remember the curtain of chance from chapter six: the histogram of a statistic in a world where nothing happens, with a flag on our result, and the p-value as the shaded tail. In 6.2, Nil built it by drawing from a pot. In real life there is no pot, so we build the curtain from our own data, by shuffling. But data can be shuffled in many ways, and each way builds a different curtain.

To keep track, every shuffle gets a card with four lines, always in this order. What do we swap? What do we keep? What do we destroy? And the null hypothesis that this defines.

A drug trial, to begin. Twenty patients, ten on the drug and ten on a placebo, each with a symptom score. We swap the group labels. We keep every score, and ten patients in each group. We destroy the link between group and score. What remains is the null hypothesis: the group does not matter.

Why does a shuffle define a null hypothesis? Because of exchangeability. If the null hypothesis is true, the labels can be redistributed without changing anything about what produced the data. The group names were only names. Fisher described tests built on this idea in 1935, and Pitman studied them in 1937. The sentence also works backwards: whatever we keep fixed, we are saying the null hypothesis does not touch.

Now a demonstration, by simulation. Ten patients had their resting heart rate measured before and after a breathing exercise. People differ a lot, from 65 to 82 beats a minute, but almost everyone went down. On average, minus 3.3. We will shuffle these same ten patients in two different ways. Guess before you see: will the two p-values be about the same?

Shuffle one. Inside each patient, swap before and after, at random. We swap the before and after labels, within each patient. We keep each patient's own level, and their two values together. We destroy the direction of each change. The null hypothesis: a change is as likely to go up as down.

Ten thousand shuffles build the curtain. It is narrow, because every patient's level cancels out. Our flag, minus 3.3, lands far out. Only 72 shuffles in ten thousand reach it, and p is 0.007.

Shuffle two, with the same data and the same statistic. Pool all twenty values, and deal them into two groups of ten. We swap the before and after labels, across everybody. We keep the twenty values. We destroy who belongs with whom. The null hypothesis: two unrelated groups from the same distribution.

Now the curtain is wide, because the differences between people are mixed in. Our flag lands inside it, and p is 0.27. The same ten patients, the same number: 0.007 against 0.27. Both are honest answers, to two different questions. The card says which question each one asks.

A tempting misreading: shuffle one is the good one, and shuffle two is merely weak. No. Had these been twenty different people in two separate groups, shuffle two would be the right card, and shuffle one would have no pairs to swap inside. The design decides.

Three more cards, in other fields. Reaction times in one participant: forty trials, in two conditions. We swap the condition labels between trials. We keep the participant, and all forty times. We destroy the link between condition and speed. The null hypothesis: for this person, the condition does not matter.

Classrooms. A teaching method is given to eight classrooms. We swap the method between whole classrooms. We keep the pupils of each classroom together. We destroy the link between method and classroom. The null hypothesis: the method does not matter for a classroom's average.

Hyperscanning. The slow alpha power of two people's EEG. We slide one recording along in time, wrapping around the end. We keep each person's own rhythm and memory. We destroy the alignment between the two. The null hypothesis: the two brains are unrelated in time.

Now two wrong shuffles, where the card reveals a null that nobody cares about. First, two slow signals, simulated, independent by construction. Their correlation is minus 0.50. Shuffle the time points of one signal, at random. We swap the moments. We keep the values of the signal. We destroy their order, which is the memory. The null hypothesis: the signal is white noise.

That curtain is very narrow. Our flag lands far outside it, and p is one in ten thousand and one. Now slide the signal instead. A series of 100 points has only 99 slides, so the curtain has 99 tiles. It is wide: nine slides reach our flag, and p is 0.10. Guess before you see: on fresh pairs of independent signals, how often will each shuffle cry wolf at the five percent line? The time shuffle: 50 percent of the studies. The slide: 4 percent. The first shuffle tests whether the signals are white noise, and 13.3 showed that the answer is always no.

Second wrong shuffle. Sixteen volunteers, eight against eight, forty reaction times each, and no effect at all. Pool all 640 trials, and shuffle the group labels trial by trial. The card says: we destroy the link between a trial and its volunteer. The null hypothesis: trials are independent. That is the pseudoreplication of 11.1. It rings in 57 percent of the studies. Swap whole volunteers instead, and it rings in 4 percent.

So the card is also a checklist. If this null hypothesis were true, could these things really be swapped without changing how the data were made? If not, the curtain answers a question nobody asked.

This chapter gives every shuffle its card, in one table of eight: labels, signs, trials, sessions, subjects, circular shift, random phases, and blocks. Today the table is empty. By 14.7, it will be full.

Back in the cloakroom, the sentence from the methods section: we ran a permutation test. Of what? Swapping what, keeping what, destroying what, testing which null hypothesis?

Remember: a shuffle is a null hypothesis. We ran a permutation test says nothing until we say what was permuted, and each shuffle builds its own curtain. Next time, in 14.2, the first shuffle: swapping labels.

Shuffling labels

Shuffling labels builds the curtain of chance from the data alone, like swapping tickets on forty coats in the tea study. After 10,000 shuffles, 560 reach the observed difference of minus 105, giving p of 0.056, using b plus one over m plus one. Any statistic works, but exchangeability is the price: unequal spreads gave 31 percent false alarms.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the cloakroom. Forty coats hang on the rail, and each one carries a ticket. Today we build the curtain of chance from the data alone, by swapping tickets.

Remember the tea study from episode 6.2. Twenty people drank tea, twenty drank water, and everyone took a reaction test. The tea group averaged 291 milliseconds, the water group 396. A difference of minus 105. In 6.2, NIL built the curtain by drawing ten thousand studies from the whole pot. In real life, there is no pot. There are only these forty people.

Before shuffling anything, CHECK fills in a test card. It has five lines, always the same. The question: does tea change reaction time? It compares the mean of the tea group with the mean of the water group. The other lines come as we go.

Here is the idea. Each person is a coat, and the coat is their reaction time. The ticket says tea or water. If tea does nothing, the ticket tells us nothing about the coat. Anyone could have carried either ticket, and their time would be the same. That is the null hypothesis, and it goes on the card: the tickets do not matter.

So let's swap them. Collect the forty tickets, mix them, and hang them back at random, twenty tea and twenty water. The coats do not move. Only the labels change. Now compute the difference again. This time, minus 40. Shuffle again: minus 15. Again: plus 14. Each shuffle is a study from the world where the tickets do not matter, built from our own forty people.

The eleventh shuffle lands at minus 108, already beyond our flag. Chance alone can do that.

Ten thousand shuffles later, the curtain is full. Its title says how it was built: by shuffling. Nobody drew a new person. Every bar comes from the same forty coats.

Now count. 560 shuffles land at least as far from zero as minus 105, on either side. The p-value is 560 plus one, divided by ten thousand plus one. That is 0.056. Why plus one? Because the real tickets are one of the possible shuffles too, so they count once, on both sides of the fraction. It also means that a shuffle p-value is never zero. With ten thousand shuffles, the smallest possible value is one in 10,001. Phipson and Smyth made this point in 2010.

A small study can be listed in full. Illustrative: ten pupils, five on a new revision method, five on the old one. Their marks are the coats. Five tickets on ten coats can hang in 252 ways. Guess before you see: how many land as far out as ours? Four. So p is 4 over 252, 0.016. No plus one here: the list holds our real tickets. The smallest possible p is 2 over 252.

With forty coats there are more than 137 billion splits, so we draw ten thousand. Run the whole test ten times on the same data. With 100 shuffles, p ranges from 0.04 to 0.10. With a thousand, from 0.05 to 0.08. With ten thousand, from 0.056 to 0.063. So ten thousand is a good habit, and the gap with 6.2 is not noise.

Wait. In 6.2, the same study gave 0.031. Same forty people, a different curtain. Why? The curtain of 6.2 came from the pot, whose spread is 153 milliseconds. This curtain comes from our forty, and they happen to be more spread out: 171 milliseconds. A wider crowd makes a wider curtain, about 56 milliseconds instead of 48, so more shuffles reach the flag. The formula of real life, the t-test, must also guess the spread from these forty people. It gives 0.060, close to the shuffle.

So one study gives two honest p-values, on either side of 0.05. The line at 0.05 is not a wall. Both say the same thing: a difference this large comes up by chance a few times in a hundred.

The shuffle has a second gift. The statistic can be anything. Take the medians instead of the means. The tea median is 283.5, the water median 358, a difference of minus 74.5. Same tickets, same shuffles, and the curtain of medians fills. 1,278 shuffles reach the flag, and the p-value is 0.128. A median, a ratio, a maximum: the recipe never changes. Just choose the statistic before you look at the data, not after.

In a clinic, an illustrative trial: twelve patients on a new drug, twelve on placebo. Ten and four improved: 83 percent against 33, a difference of 50 points. Same tickets, same shuffles: p is 0.031.

Now the price. Swapping tickets assumes that, if tea does nothing, the two groups are interchangeable. Not just the same mean: the same everything. This is called exchangeability, and it fills the line of the card that stayed empty until now: what the test assumes.

Here is what happens when it fails. Two groups with exactly the same mean, 400 milliseconds. A small group of eight people, very spread out. A large group of thirty-two, tightly packed. Two thousand such studies, each tested by shuffling the difference of means. With the line at 0.05, about 5 percent should cross it by chance. With equal spreads, 4.7 percent do. With these unequal spreads, 31 percent do. That is six times too many false alarms, while the means are identical.

Why? The shuffle mixes the wide group into the narrow one, and its curtain no longer belongs to our question. Shuffling labels tests whether the two groups come from the same distribution, not whether they have the same mean.

There is a repair. Before shuffling, divide the difference by its own uncertainty, measured in each group separately. Then shuffle that ratio instead. False alarms fall back to 6.3 percent, close to five. Chung and Romano studied this repair in 2013.

The card is now full. The question: does tea change reaction time? It compares the two means. The null: the tickets do not matter. It assumes the groups are exchangeable when the null is true. And we read the p-value as b plus one over m plus one: 0.056.

Back in the cloakroom. Remember: shuffling labels builds the curtain from your own data, with any statistic you like. The price is exchangeability. If the spreads differ, the shuffle answers a different question. Next time, the tickets stay with each person, and we flip signs instead.

PLATE 14.37:16

Flipping signs

For paired data, tickets stay with each person and signs are flipped, like reeds and their reflections on a pond. Ten runners give 1,024 patterns, and 18 reach the observed mean, so p is 0.018. Pooling the twenty times as strangers gives 0.73. The sign flip assumes symmetric differences; with skew, false alarms rose to 19.3 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cloakroom. Last time we swapped tickets between strangers. Today every ticket stays with its person, and we flip signs instead.

Remember episode 7.3: when the same people are measured twice, each person's difference removes the spread between people. Today's question is what to shuffle for such data. The design will answer.

Ten runners, simulated. Each ran four hundred metres twice, once in old shoes and once in new shoes. Runner nine took 67.7 seconds in the old pair and 63.5 in the new pair. Subtract, and the new shoes saved 4.2 seconds. Ten runners give ten numbers, the seconds saved. Eight are positive. Their mean is 0.95 seconds.

Picture a pond. The waterline is zero. Each runner is a reed, as tall as the seconds saved. A runner who lost time hangs below the line. And every reed has a reflection on the other side.

Now the null hypothesis. If the shoes do nothing, a runner's change is as likely to be a gain as a loss. So each reed is as likely to stand as to swap places with its reflection. That is a sign flip, and DICE writes it on the shuffle card. We swap the sign of each difference. We keep its size, and its runner. We destroy its direction. The null hypothesis: for each runner, a gain is as likely as a loss. It is the same as swapping old and new inside one runner.

NIL flips a coin for every reed. Each pattern of ten coins gives a new mean: 0.71, then 0.17, then minus 0.05. Ten runners have two to the power ten, 1,024 patterns, so we do not draw them. We list every one, and each drops a tile into the curtain.

The curtain is narrow: its spread is 0.49 seconds. Our flag, 0.95, lands far out. Count the patterns at least as far from zero as ours, on either side: 18 of 1,024. So p is 0.018. There is no plus one this time, because our real signs are one of the 1,024 patterns already.

How small could p be? If all ten reeds stand up, only that pattern and its mirror reach it. Two over 1,024, so 0.002. Counted on one side only, one over 1,024, about 0.001. Both are right. Two-sided simply doubles.

Now the wrong shuffle, on the same ten runners. Pool all twenty times, and deal ten to the old shoes and ten to the new, ignoring who is who. We swap labels across everybody. We keep the twenty values. We destroy who belongs with whom. The null hypothesis: two unrelated groups. Guess before you see: same data, same flag. Will p stay below 0.05?

There are 184,756 ways to deal the heap, and we list them all. The curtain is more than five times wider: 2.65 seconds, against 0.49. It still contains the differences between runners, one near 51 seconds, another near 69. Our flag sits deep inside. 134,620 deals reach it, so p is 0.73. The same ten runners give 0.018 and 0.73. Both answer their own question. Only one matches how the data were collected.

Is the wrong shuffle just unlucky here? Simulate 4,000 studies of ten runners, with a real gain of one second. The sign flip detects it in 68 percent. The wrong shuffle, in none. Now 4,000 studies where the shoes do nothing. The sign flip rings in 4.8 percent, the wrong shuffle in none. It does not cry wolf. It is deaf: its curtain is too wide for the signal.

The sign flip has a price, like the exchangeability of last time. Under the null, the differences must be symmetric around zero: a reed and its reflection equally likely. Take 20 people. Guess before you see: with skewed differences, does the sign flip keep its five percent? With symmetric differences, 5.0 percent. With skewed differences whose mean is exactly zero, 9.2. With skewed differences whose median is zero, and a long tail pulling the mean up, 19.3. It does not stay near five.

The same card fits many designs, all illustrative. Same patients, before and after a treatment: flip each change, keep the patient's level. Left and right hand: flip right minus left, keep the person's strength. Two conditions in one participant's EEG: flip the difference, keep the participant's baseline. Twins on different diets: flip within the pair, keep the family. Matched pairs of schools: flip within the pair, keep the match. One plot of land, with and without fertiliser: flip within the plot, keep the soil. Each time, the pairing removed the spread between people, so the shuffle must respect it.

A real case. Darwin grew pairs of maize plants in the same pots, one cross-fertilised, one self-fertilised. In 1935, Fisher used the fifteen pairs to show a test like this one. Thirteen differences are positive, two negative, and they add up to 314 eighths of an inch. Fifteen pairs give 32,768 patterns, and 1,726 land at least as far out: p is 0.0527. The t-test gives 0.0497. Two sides of 0.05, once more.

How small can p get? It depends on the number of people. With five, 32 patterns, and the smallest two-sided p is 2 over 32: 0.0625. Guess before you see: can five people ever give p below 0.05? No, never. With ten, 1,024 patterns, and 0.002. With twenty, 1,048,576, and under two in a million.

Twenty we can still list. With forty people there are more than a trillion patterns, so we draw, and the rule from 14.2 applies: p is b plus one, over m plus one. A twenty-person study, simulated. Listed in full, 28 of 1,048,576 patterns reach our flag: 27 in a million. Draw 10,000 random patterns, and none does, so p is one over 10,001. Ten thousand draws cannot see below that.

Back in the cloakroom, the table of eight shuffles gets its second row: signs.

Remember: the design dictates the shuffle. Paired data are shuffled inside each person, by flipping signs, and the test assumes the differences are symmetric around zero. Next time, in 14.4: which unit do we shuffle?

Which unit do we shuffle?

A shuffle must move the unit at the level of the conclusion. Swapping condition labels between trials inside twelve people gave p of 0.0008 and rang 18.4 percent of the time, while flipping whole people gave 0.154 and 4.9 percent. Blocks must be respected, shuffling within sites. A covariate like age needs the Freedman and Lane residual shuffle.

Welcome back to the cloakroom. Last time we flipped signs inside each person. Today the question climbs one floor: which unit do we shuffle? In 11.1, n counted the independent units at the level of the question. A shuffle follows the same rule.

Here is a simulated study. Twelve people do a reaction task in two conditions, A and B, twenty trials of each. The question is about people in general: is B slower than A? In this simulated world, the true average effect is exactly zero. But people differ: B slows some down and speeds others up, by about thirty milliseconds. Each person is a bunch of forty balloons on one string. A balloon is a trial, blue for A and pink for B, and its height is the reaction time.

Averaged over the twelve people, B comes out 23.9 milliseconds slower. We need a curtain. First shuffle: inside each bunch, swap the colours of the balloons at random. We swap labels between trials, keep each person's trials together, and destroy the link between condition and trial.

Ten thousand shuffles build the curtain, and it is narrow: its spread is 7.4 milliseconds. Only 7 shuffles reach our flag, so p is 8 over 10,001: 0.0008.

Second shuffle, same data. Now flip each bunch as a whole: every balloon of a person changes colour together, or none does. This is the sign flip of 14.3. We swap whole people's labels, keep each person's trials, and destroy which people are slower in B. Twelve people give 4,096 patterns, and we list them all. Guess before you see: will this curtain agree?

It does not. It is more than twice as wide: 16.5 milliseconds. And 632 of the 4,096 patterns reach our flag, so p is 0.154. The same data give 0.0008 and 0.154. Both are correct answers, to two different questions, and only one is about people. The first curtain never moves a person, so it holds the noise of trials and none of the spread between people.

Count the damage by simulation. Repeat this world 2,000 times, with no average effect at all. Guess before you see: how often does the first shuffle ring at the five percent line? 18.4 percent of the studies. The people shuffle: 4.9 percent.

Turn the first knob: trials per condition. With one trial, the two shuffles coincide, and both ring near five percent. With five trials, the first rings 8.8 percent. With twenty, 18.4. With eighty, 45.1. Second knob: how much people differ. With no difference, the first shuffle is honest: 4.7 percent. At thirty milliseconds, 18.4. At sixty, 41.7. The people shuffle stays between 4.4 and 5.5. A rate within half a point of five is normal: 2,000 studies cannot be more exact.

Is the first shuffle then simply wrong? No, it answers a smaller question. Take one person, shuffle only their forty trials, and ask: does this person respond differently in B? There are over 137 billion splits, so we draw ten thousand. For person nine, B is 119 milliseconds slower, and none of the ten thousand shuffles reach it: p is 0.0001. Four of our twelve people show an effect on their own. A person with no effect rings 4.8 percent of the time. That holds for those people, not the next one.

The same choice appears everywhere, and these examples are illustrative. Ask: what is the level of the conclusion, and what must be shuffled? Trials within participants: to speak about people, shuffle people. Neurons within animals: shuffle animals. Pupils within classes, with a method given to whole classes: shuffle classes. Sessions within patients: shuffle patients. Plots within fields, with a fertiliser given to whole fields: shuffle fields.

Sometimes units come in blocks. An exchangeability block is a group of units that may be swapped with each other, and not with units of another block. Here are three sites, eight people each, and a treatment that does nothing. People were randomised within each site, but unevenly: two treated at the first site, four at the second, six at the third. Site means are minus 33, zero, and 34, so the treated group holds more people from the high site. The raw difference is 28.1, with no effect at all.

Shuffle within sites: 54,880 patterns, all listed. The curtain is centred at 22.3, where the uneven allocation puts it, so we measure distance from its centre. Our flag is 5.8 above it, and p is 0.454. Now cross the blocks, and shuffle across all 24 people: 2,704,156 splits, centred on zero. Our flag is far out, and p is 0.038. Over 2,000 such studies, crossing the blocks rings 20.5 percent, against 5.4 within sites. With even allocation, four treated at each site, crossing rings 0.2 percent, and finds a real effect less often: 32.5 percent against 65.5. In 2015, Winkler and colleagues built rules for blocks inside blocks.

One more complication: a covariate. Thirty people, two groups, one score each. Age drives the score, and B is older: 49.8 years on average, against 40.1. The group itself does nothing. Shuffle the group labels and compare raw means, and p is 0.005. Over 2,000 null studies, this rings 61.3 percent, because age relates group and score.

Freedman and Lane proposed three steps, in 1983. Fit the score on age alone, and keep the residuals. Shuffle the residuals, and add the age line back. Refit with group and age, and read the group's t. This gives p of 0.755, and rings 5.1 percent. Here, shuffling labels and refitting with age also holds, at 5.2, so the gain comes from adjusting for age.

Hyperscanning uses the same idea. Illustrative: twelve dyads, one signal per person. We re-pair partners across dyads, so that no dyad keeps its own partner: we keep each person's signal, and destroy the real pairing. The real dyads correlate 0.13 on average. Over ten thousand re-pairings, the curtain is centred near zero, and only 42 reach our flag, so p is 0.004. In the cloakroom's table, three rows fill today: trials, subjects, and blocks.

Remember: shuffle at the level where you want to conclude. Trials answer about these people, people answer about people, and blocks must be respected. Next time, in 14.5: shuffling time.

Shuffling time

Two independent smooth series correlate by accident, so a time shuffle must keep what is not the hypothesis. Dealing samples in random order destroys memory and says significant 65 percent of the time. The circular shift, like turning a combination dial, and random phases keep memory and stay near 5 percent. Their limits are short series and drifts, where a seam appears.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cloakroom, with two long ribbons on the hooks. In 13.3, two smooth series with nothing to do with each other often correlated strongly, by accident. Today we shuffle time in three ways, and one rule tells which is right: a shuffle must keep what is not the hypothesis.

Here is a pair of series, simulated, independent by construction. Each has 200 points and a memory of about twenty points, so each one is smooth. Blue is the first, pink the second. Their correlation is 0.43. We need a curtain of chance, built from these very data.

First shuffle: deal the samples of the pink series in random order. This destroys the memory, so every shuffled pink series is jagged, and its correlation with blue sits near zero. Ten thousand shuffles build the curtain, and it is narrow: its spread is 0.07. Our flag lands outside all ten thousand, and p is one in ten thousand and one.

Second shuffle: the circular shift. Wrap the pink series onto a ring, the end joined to the start, like a combination dial. Turn the ring by one notch, and read the correlation. Every up and down of the pink series is still there, in the same order. Only its alignment with blue has moved. A series of 200 points has 199 turns away from the start, and each turn drops a tile.

The curtain is wide: its spread is 0.30, four times more. Twenty-eight turns out of 199 reach our flag, and p is 0.145. Pink keeps its slow swings, so its turned copies still line up with blue by chance, and often.

Guess before you see: on 2,000 fresh pairs of independent series, how often does each curtain say significant at the 5 percent line? The sample shuffle: 65 percent. The circular shift: 5.7 percent. The time shuffle of 14.1 gave 50, and the slide 4: those series were a little less smooth, and 2,000 studies cannot be exact.

Turn the knob of memory. With none, the sample shuffle behaves: 4.4 percent. At 0.5, 12 percent. At 0.9, 51. At 0.99, 77. For a random walk, 83 percent. The smoother the series, the more the sample shuffle lies.

Third shuffle: random phases. Any series is a sum of waves. Each wave has a size, and a phase, which is where it starts. The sizes together are the power spectrum, and the Fourier transform finds them. In 1992, Theiler and colleagues proposed keeping every size, drawing every phase at random, and adding the waves back up.

The sizes are the same, up to rounding. The texture is the same: the lag-one autocorrelation is 0.945 for the original and 0.948 for the surrogate. But it is a new series, correlated minus 0.25 with the original. Ten thousand surrogates build a curtain with a spread of 0.30, and p is 0.151. The circular shift said 0.145. Both estimate one answer: one has 199 tiles, the other ten thousand draws.

Both keep the memory, and both stay near the 5 percent we asked for: 5.7 and 6.3. A series of 20 points has only 19 turns, and the smallest p the circular shift can ever give is 0.05. With 200 points, it is 0.005. With 50 points, the false alarms reach 11 percent. And turns of a notch or two leave the pink series almost aligned: keeping only turns of 20 notches or more raised the power for a coupled pair a little, from 19.7 to 23.9 percent.

The ring also has a seam, where the end meets the start. If the series drifts, the seam is a jump: 1.3 standard deviations for our smooth series, 2.0 for a random walk. For a random walk, the circular shift says significant 21 percent of the time, and random phases 21 as well. At memory 0.99, 11 and 12. Both shuffles assume a series that stays put.

Now a side effect of random phases. Here is a simulated skewed series, with a few big values, like the power of a rhythm. Its skewness is 2.2. Guess before you see: after random phases? Minus 1.0. The surrogate dips below zero, which the original never did. The shuffle kept the spectrum, and lost the shape of the values.

In 1996, Schreiber and Schmitz proposed an iterative fix. Alternate: impose the spectrum, then put the original values back, in rank order. The surrogate has exactly the original values, and nearly the original spectrum: about 4 percent off. Does it change the verdict? On 1,000 independent skewed pairs, random phases say significant 5.3 percent of the time, and the amplitude-adjusted version 4.3. For a correlation, we see no difference. We do not force one.

Now the rule. A surrogate must keep everything that is not the hypothesis being tested. The sample shuffle keeps the values, and throws away the memory. So its curtain answers another question: is my signal white noise? Its spread, 0.07, is the spread of white-noise pairs. Of 2,000 independent smooth pairs, 30 percent are beyond 0.3 in size. Of white-noise pairs, none. For a smooth signal, the answer is always no. A 2018 review by Lancaster and colleagues compares many surrogate types by the property each can test.

The same logic, in other fields. All these pairs are illustrative. Two EEG channels of one person: keep each rhythm, destroy their alignment, so slide one. The heart rates of two people in conversation: keep each person's slow rhythm, destroy the real-time link. Two stock prices, nearly random walks, the hard case: remove the drift first. Temperature and ice-cream sales share the seasons, and sliding destroys that too: remove the season first.

Two fNIRS channels: keep each slow spectrum, scramble the phases. Two brains in hyperscanning: slide one recording. Rainfall and river level: the rain is skewed, with many zeros, so keep the values, and use the amplitude-adjusted version.

Back in the cloakroom, the table of eight shuffles has two more rows. The sessions row comes in 14.7. Two other cures for memory wait elsewhere: counting the points differently in 13.4, and whitening in 13.5.

Remember: a surrogate must keep everything but the hypothesis. Sample shuffles keep the values and lose the memory, so they test white noise. The circular shift and random phases keep the memory, and destroy the alignment. Next time: the bootstrap, in 14.6.

PLATE 14.67:29

The bootstrap

The bootstrap treats the sample as the pot and draws with replacement to show how far an estimate could move. For the median of thirty people, a 95 percent interval ran from 332 to 437.5 milliseconds, with no formula. Permutation is for testing, the bootstrap for estimating. It fails with tiny samples, maxima, very heavy tails, and samples that represent the population badly.

That is drawing with replacement, and the method is called the bootstrap. Bradley Efron introduced it in 1979.

Look at one resample. Some people come out twice, one comes out three times, and many never come out. Here, 11 of the thirty are missing, and its median is 356. Last time, the bootstrap redrew a sample with replacement, to see how far an estimate could move. It redrew rows one at a time, and that is only honest when the rows are independent. Often they are not. So today's rule is the rule of the whole chapter: redraw what was drawn when the data were produced, and respect the dependence, at every level.

Here is a simulated study. Ten people, forty reaction times each. People differ: their averages run from 293 to 542 milliseconds. We want the average of people in general, which in this simulated world is exactly 420. Our study gives 422.0.

First bootstrap: throw all four hundred trials into one heap and redraw them one at a time, ten thousand times. The interval is 409.2 to 434.6 milliseconds, narrow, as if four hundred people had spoken.

Second bootstrap, which follows how the data were made. First draw ten people with replacement, so some come twice and some not at all. Then, inside each drawn person, draw forty trials with replacement. The interval is 367.6 to 474.0, four times wider. Both hold 420 this time. Guess before you see: over three thousand such studies, how often does the narrow interval hold the truth? Ninety five percent? Eighty? It holds it 41.9 percent of the time. The two-stage interval: 90.6. Not 95, because with only ten people a percentile interval runs a little short, the small-sample trap of 14.6.

Turn a knob: trials per person, with ten people always. With ten trials, the narrow interval holds the truth 70.8 percent of the time. With forty, 41.9. With a hundred and sixty, 21.6. More trials only sharpen each person's own average. The spread between people stays, and the two-stage interval stays between 90.6 and 93.8.

Do we need the second stage? Redraw only the people, and keep each person's average: 89.2 percent, against 90.6. Here, drawing trials inside people changes little. In 2020, Saravanan, Berman and Sober applied the two-stage version, which they call the hierarchical bootstrap, to the songs of Bengalese finches and to the behaviour of flies. In their simulations, tests that ignored the nesting gave false positives over 45 percent of the time at a five percent level, while the hierarchical bootstrap gave error bars similar to summarising each person first. Mixed models, from chapter 11, are another honest route.

The same move works wherever data are nested, and these examples are illustrative. Trials within participants: draw participants. Neurons within animals: draw animals. Pupils within classes: draw classes. EEG epochs within participants: draw participants, then epochs.

Now time. A simulated series of two hundred points. Each point keeps 0.8 of the previous one's distance from the mean, plus a new nudge. Neighbours correlate 0.79, and by ten steps the correlation has faded to 0.03. The mean is 9.87, and the truth is 10.

Redraw the points one at a time, and the resample is white noise: the memory is gone. The interval is 9.67 to 10.06. Instead, cut the series into pieces of ten consecutive points, draw pieces with replacement, and tape them end to end. Neighbours stay neighbours inside each piece, and only the seams are false. This is the moving-block bootstrap, from Künsch in 1989. Its interval is 9.48 to 10.36, more than twice as wide.

Guess again: how often does the naive interval hold the truth, over three thousand fresh series? 47.7 percent. Blocks of ten: 82.0. The real spread of the mean is 0.36, and the naive bootstrap sees 0.10. Two hundred points with this memory carry the information of about twenty-two independent ones, the effective sample size of episode 13.4.

Turn the block-length knob. Blocks of five: 74.8 percent. Ten: 82.0. Twenty: 84.5. Then coverage falls: fifty, 76.4, and a hundred, only two pieces, 55.1. Too short, and every seam cuts the dependence. Too long, and too few pieces remain to shuffle. We never reach 95, because this much memory leaves little information, the same trap as a tiny sample.

In 1994, Politis and Romano removed the choice of one fixed length. In the stationary bootstrap, after each point we flip a biased coin. One time in ten we jump to a random place, otherwise we take the next point. Block lengths are random, ten on average, and at the end of the series we wrap around to the beginning. The resampled series is stationary too. Its coverage here: 82.7 percent, close to fixed blocks of ten.

More examples, illustrative. Daily temperatures: redraw runs of days. Heartbeats: redraw runs of beats. River levels: redraw stretches of days. A working rule: longer than the memory, and much shorter than the series.

Which three days are B can happen in twenty ways, so for one person the smallest possible one-sided p is one in twenty.
