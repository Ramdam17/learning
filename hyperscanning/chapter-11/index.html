PLATE 11.15:58

Coupled oscillators

The Kuramoto model describes fireflies as oscillators nudged by coupling K. Two of them lock when K is at least the gap between their natural frequencies, a region called an Arnold tongue. For 300 fireflies, an order parameter jumps once K passes a critical value, and because the coupling is known, the model tests whether a measure such as phase locking recovers it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Night falls on the firefly meadow. Thousands of small lights, and nobody conducting them. Yet some species of fireflies really do end up flashing in unison. John Buck reviewed the evidence in 1988, in the Quarterly Review of Biology.

This episode builds the simplest model of how that can happen. It is called the Kuramoto model. And it gives hyperscanning something precious: two signals whose coupling we know, because we set it ourselves.

Meet two lights in the grass. One is Ava's, one is Otto's. Each is an oscillator: a phase that turns round and round, and a flash each time it passes the top. Each has its own natural frequency. Ava's is six radians per second. Otto's is seven. With no link between them, Otto gains on Ava all the time. Their flashes drift in and out of step.

Now add a link. Each firefly nudges the other. It speeds up when the other is ahead, and slows down when the other is behind. The size of the nudge is the coupling, K.

In symbols, the rate of change of each phase is its natural frequency, plus K over two, times the sine of the other phase minus its own. That is the Kuramoto model, written for two oscillators. Kuramoto proposed it in 1975, and developed it in his 1984 book.

Subtract the two equations, and only the difference of phases is left. Call it phi. Its rate of change is delta omega, minus K times the sine of phi. Delta omega is the gap between the natural frequencies. Here it is one.

If K is small, phi can never stop. The lights slip past each other again and again. If K is large enough, phi can stop, at an angle where K times the sine of phi equals delta omega. Then both fireflies flash at the same frequency, with a fixed delay.

So the rule is simple. With this convention, the two fireflies lock if and only if K is at least the absolute value of delta omega. Another convention moves the factor of two, so always check which one you are reading.

Try it. At K equal to 0.4, the lights drift. At K equal to 1, exactly the gap, they just lock, a quarter of a turn apart. At K equal to 1.6, they lock with Otto about 39 degrees ahead of Ava.

Now turn both knobs. Detuning runs along the bottom, coupling runs upward. For each cell, we simulate the pair and ask: do they lock? Locked cells are yellow. The others are ink, darker where the drift is faster.

A wedge opens upward from zero. This is an Arnold tongue. Its edges are the lines K equals plus or minus delta omega. The simulation agrees with the rule in all 7,381 cells.

Now the whole meadow. Take 300 fireflies, with natural frequencies drawn from a bell curve, one radian per second wide. Each one is nudged by all the others, with a strength of K over 300.

We summarise the crowd with one number, the order parameter r. It is the length of the average of all the unit arrows, one per firefly. It is the same arrow trick as the phase locking value of episode 6.5, but taken across the fireflies at one instant, instead of across time. Near zero, the lights are scattered. At one, they flash as one.

Turn K up slowly. For small K, r hovers near 0.06, which is what chance gives for 300 fireflies: one over the square root of 300. Then, around K equal to 1.6, r takes off. At K equal to 2, it is 0.73. At 3.2, it is 0.94. The meadow lights up.

That onset is not a surprise. Kuramoto's theory predicts a critical coupling of two over pi times the density of natural frequencies at the centre. For this bell curve, it is about 1.6. Strogatz reviewed the theory in 2000, in Physica D. Acebrón and colleagues wrote the standard review in 2005, in Reviews of Modern Physics. With only 300 fireflies, the rise is a little rounded.

Why does this matter for hyperscanning? Because here we know the truth. We set K ourselves. So we can ask a measure from chapter 6 whether it recovers the coupling.

Record the two fireflies with noise. Filter around their frequency, take the phase, and compute the phase locking value. Each number is the mean of 20 simulated runs of two minutes. At K equal to zero, it is 0.05. At 0.5, it is 0.26. At K equal to 1, right on the edge of the tongue, it is 0.61. At 2, it is 0.95.

Two lessons. First, the measure does not jump at the edge. It climbs through it, because noise blurs the tongue. Second, it is a number between zero and one, not K itself. It follows the order of the coupling, not its value.

That is the use of this model. Before you trust a measure on real brains, run it on oscillators whose coupling you know.

And keep the limits in mind. A brain is not a phase oscillator. This model shows what coupling alone can do. It does not show that brains obey it.

Let's recap. Each firefly has its own frequency. Two of them lock when K is at least their frequency gap. That region is an Arnold tongue. For many, the order parameter r jumps once K passes a critical value. And a known coupling lets us test our measures.

Between two brains, there is no wire. Whatever carries the nudge, sight, sound or movement, is the next question. Next episode: Two brains, one dynamical system.

PLATE 11.26:04

Two brains, one dynamical system

Between two brains there is no wire, so the question is whether a link made only of behaviour can synchronise them. The HKB model describes limbs snapping into step as tempo rises, and the same law appears between two people who only see each other. In a toy brain model, a few links from one person's motor regions to the other's visual regions raise synchrony.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back on the firefly meadow. In the last episode, two fireflies were linked by a dial, K, and we turned it ourselves. Between two brains, there is no dial and no wire.

So what can link them? Only what passes through the world: what one person does, and what the other sees and hears. This episode asks one question. In a model, is a link made of behaviour alone enough to make two brains synchronise?

We start with the movements themselves. In 1985, Haken, Kelso and Bunz published a model of coordinated hand movements, in Biological Cybernetics. People move two limbs rhythmically, either together or in opposition. At slow speeds, both patterns feel fine. As the speed rises, opposition gives way, and the limbs snap into step.

The model follows one number, the relative phase between the two limbs, phi. The rate of change of phi is minus a times the sine of phi, minus two b times the sine of two phi. Call k the ratio of b to a.

Picture phi as a marble in a landscape. With k large, there are two valleys: one for moving together, one for moving in opposition. Speed makes k smaller. When k falls below one quarter, the valley for opposition disappears, and the marble rolls into the other one.

We simulated it, with a little noise. Starting in opposition, with k sliding from 1 down to 0.05, the marble left at k equal to 0.31. Noise makes it leave a little before the quarter mark. Without noise, opposition holds at k equal to 0.3, and is gone at 0.2.

Now the key step. In 1990, Schmidt, Carello and Turvey tested the same idea between two people, in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. Two seated people watched each other's swinging lower leg, and kept a common tempo, either in step or in opposition. Opposition became less stable as the tempo rose, and switched to in-phase, like a physical bifurcation.

The only link between those two people was vision. No wire, no touch. Yet their legs followed the same kind of law as the two hands of one person.

What about the brains inside those bodies? In 2012, Dumas and colleagues built a model, in PLoS ONE. Each brain has ninety regions. Each region is an oscillator, a firefly with its own rhythm. The regions are wired together with a human connectome, measured by diffusion MRI.

Between the two brains, they added one kind of link only. The motor regions of each person drive the visual regions of the other. That channel stands for behaviour: what one does, the other sees.

In their simulations, synchrony between the two brains rose with the strength of that channel. It was weaker when the wiring inside the brains was scrambled. The authors suggest a property of the connectome that eases synchronisation between people. They also state their assumptions. The channel runs from motor to visual regions, and delays in the body and the eye are not modelled.

To see the idea at work, we built a toy version. Twelve regions per brain, in three groups of four: motor, visual, and other. The same random wiring in both brains. It is illustrative, not a real connectome. Between the brains, only eight messenger links, from each person's motor regions to the matching visual regions of the other.

First, with the messengers switched off. We compute the phase locking value of episode 6.5, for every pair of regions across the two brains. Averaged over 144 pairs and 12 simulated runs, it is 0.19. That is the floor for two similar brains that never touch.

Now switch the messengers on. The average climbs to 0.56. For the eight directly linked pairs, it reaches 0.80. Eight messengers, and synchrony has spread through the wiring of both brains. In this model, behaviour alone is enough.

The model also says where to look. Group by group, synchrony is highest between one person's motor regions and the other's visual regions, at 0.68. It is lowest between the two motor groups, at 0.40. Without messengers, every group sits near 0.2.

That is a hypothesis you can test. In a real study, ask whether the synchrony of two people sits between one person's motor areas and the partner's visual areas, rather than between their two motor areas. The model proposes the pattern. The data must confirm it or reject it. And the pattern depends on the wiring and the channel we chose.

One more model, built for tapping together. In 2019, Heggli and colleagues, in PLoS Computational Biology, gave each person two oscillators, one for perception and one for action. The action oscillator of one person drives the perception oscillator of the other. They found that different ways of tapping together differ in how within-person and between-person coupling are balanced.

Keep the limits in mind. These are models. They show what coupling through behaviour can do, in simulation. They do not show that brains work this way. In the language of the series, this speaks to route 2, rhythm and movement.

Let's recap. The HKB model describes how two rhythms hold together, or let go. The same kind of law appears between two people who only see each other. In a model with brain wiring, a handful of behavioural links is enough to synchronise two brains. And the model suggests which pairs of regions to test.

Between two brains, there is no wire, and the next question is what each one expects of the other. Next episode: Mutual prediction as a model.

PLATE 11.35:24

Mutual prediction as a model

Each firefly carries a model of its partner and acts to shrink the prediction error, the surprise. In simulation, shared models give locking whether the lights flash together or alternate, and alternation gives a correlation of minus 0.89 that plain correlation misreads. Diverging models lower synchrony, and a shared surprise makes it dip and then repair, as predictions to test.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Night on the firefly meadow. Two lights hang above the grass, one Ava's, one Otto's. In episode 11.1, each firefly was nudged by the other. It reacted. This episode asks for more. What if each firefly predicts the other?

Here is the picture. Each firefly carries a small model of its partner, drawn as a bubble. The model says when the partner will flash. Before the flash, it draws a hollow ring where the light should appear. Then the real flash arrives. The gap between ring and flash is the prediction error. It is the surprise.

A firefly has two ways to shrink that gap. It can change its model, so the next ring lands closer. Or it can change its own rhythm, so the partner's model fits better. Doing both, to keep surprise low, is the core of active inference. This is route three from chapter two, made formal: mutual prediction.

In 2015, Karl Friston and Chris Frith simulated two birds singing to each other. Each bird held a generative model of the song. When both shared the same model, their internal states fell into a generalised synchrony. When the birds could not hear each other, their expectations followed independent paths. The paper is called A duet for one.

Let's build the smallest version, and measure it. Everything from here is simulated. Ava's natural frequency is 6 radians per second, Otto's is 7, as in episode 11.1. Each firefly sees the other's phase with noise, updates its model continuously, and steers its own rhythm toward the ring. We run 100 simulated pairs, 20 seconds each.

First run. Each firefly aims its flash at the ring, to flash together. The prediction error settles at 0.12 radians. The two lights flash as one. The correlation between their flash traces is 0.89. And the phase locking value, the arrow trick of episode 6.5, is 1.00. With no steering at all, it is 0.05.

Second run. Same fireflies, same models. But now each aims its flash half a turn after the ring. It answers, instead of copying. The error is the same, 0.12. The phase locking value is the same, 1.00. But the correlation is minus 0.89. The lights take turns, perfectly. A plain correlation reads this pair as opposed, or as unrelated. The model says the opposite: a tightly bound pair.

In 2016, Uri Hasson and Chris Frith argued that social interaction goes beyond mirroring, with complementary roles, and that phase synchrony captures mirroring but misses complementary coupling. In joint tapping, Konvalinka and colleagues showed in 2010 that partners who could hear each other adapted continuously, with their intervals moving in opposite directions and no leader emerging. That study was in the Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology. It shows that mutual adaptation happens. It does not test our simulated runs.

Third run. Break the shared model. Otto's model of Ava has the wrong tempo, and he cannot learn. His error stays at 0.97 radians. The correlation falls to about zero, and the phase locking value over 20 seconds falls to 0.28. Both fireflies still watch each other. But in the birdsong simulations, the synchrony worked only because both birds had the same model. So the model predicts that synchrony falls when the models diverge.

Fourth run. A shared surprise. At 30 seconds, a gust of wind kicks both lights off their rhythm. The error jumps, the synchrony dips, and within about two seconds the models repair it. In this toy model, the error also raises how hard each firefly steers. The dip is then shallower. Averaged over the 100 pairs, the locking falls to 0.76, against 0.56 when the steering stays fixed.

That loop is borrowed. In 2019, in Trends in Cognitive Sciences, Simone Shamay-Tsoory and colleagues described social alignment as monitoring errors, aligning, and rewarding. In the same year, Leonie Koban, Anand Ramamoorthy and Ivana Konvalinka, in Social Neuroscience, proposed that we fall into sync to reduce mismatch. Our simulation is a toy. It is not fitted to any data.

So treat these four runs as predictions to test, not as results. Synchrony should hold while the models are shared, mirrored or alternating. It should fall when they diverge. It should dip and repair after a shared surprise. And a plain correlation can miss the alternating case.

Here is the habit for this route. When two brains show low or negative correlation, do not conclude that nothing is shared. Ask what each could predict about the other, and use a measure that can see a stable offset.

Let's recap. Each firefly predicts the other, and the gap is the surprise. Shared models give synchrony, copied or alternating. Diverging models lower it. A surprise makes it dip, then repair. Next episode: Real-time hyperscanning.

PLATE 11.45:53

Real-time hyperscanning

Real-time hyperscanning pairs samples by timestamp, uses causal filters that add delay, and must pick a window: short is sharp but noisy, long is smooth but late. Movement cannot be cleaned afterwards, so a motion channel and a shuffled partner run beside the display. With fNIRS delays reach seconds, so only slow changes can be trusted.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Night on the firefly meadow. In the middle of the grass stands a small camera on a tripod. Its back screen shows the synchrony of Ava and Otto at the moment it happens. This is real-time hyperscanning.

Offline, we could wait for the end, filter in both directions, throw away bad epochs, and compute at leisure. Online, we get none of that. Data arrive in small chunks, the future does not exist, and whatever reaches the screen has already been seen. Every number here comes from a simulation, unless a study is named.

Here is the pipeline. First, stream. Two amplifiers send chunks of samples with timestamps. Episode four point two showed how a shared clock keeps them aligned. Online we add one rule: pair samples by timestamp, never by order of arrival.

Second, filter, with a filter that only looks backwards. A zero-phase filter runs forward and then backward, so every output sample uses the future. Online, only a causal filter is possible, and its price is delay. In our simulation, an eight to twelve hertz band-pass delays the rhythm by about two tenths of a second. The zero-phase version starts responding more than a quarter of a second before the burst begins. That is the future leaking into the past. Widmann and colleagues, in 2015, describe how filter design distorts a signal.

Third, phase. The Hilbert transform also looks on both sides, and at the newest sample it has nothing after it. In our code, we demodulate causally instead: multiply by a complex exponential, then low-pass. Another route is to predict the signal a little ahead, as Chen and colleagues did in 2013 to track the phase of intracranial rhythms in real time.

Fourth, the window. A window of length W describes a moment W over two seconds ago. Here the camera helps. A short exposure is sharp in time, but grainy. A long exposure is smooth, but late. Compare one second with five. The dyad is coupled from twenty-five to forty-five seconds. With one second, independent dyads float around 0.27 and bounce by 0.14. Coupling lifts the display to 0.79, but the threshold from fifteen shuffled partners sits at 0.93, so only a third of the coupled windows clear it. With five seconds, the floor falls to 0.14, the threshold to 0.51, and every coupled window clears it. The price is time: half of the rise takes 2.8 seconds, instead of 0.8.

Add up the latency. For one second: 0.2 seconds of filter delay, plus half a window, 0.5. One update took about five microseconds on this laptop, and a screen refresh takes seventeen milliseconds. So the window decides: about 0.7 seconds, against about 2.7 for five.

Now the hard part. You cannot clean afterwards. Offline, you reject the epoch with a nod or a jaw clench. Online, it is on the screen before anyone sees it. In the simulation, both people nod from fifty to fifty-eight seconds, and the movement leaks into both EEG channels. The five-second display climbs to 0.87, higher than during real coupling. A viewer would see synchrony. It was movement.

So two controls run beside the display. The first is a motion channel, an accelerometer for each person. When it rises, the display is greyed out, and stays grey for one full window after the movement stops. The second is a shuffled partner: the same pipeline, on Ava and the Otto of another dyad. It stays below 0.26 during the nodding, so it misses movement that only this dyad shares. It catches something else: a stimulus or a wire that every dyad shares lifts it too. You need both. They do not say where synchrony comes from. They only say the screen is not fooling you.

Now fNIRS. The signal is already late. In our simulation, the hemodynamic response peaks five seconds after the neural event. Cui and colleagues, in PLoS ONE in 2010, noted that fNIRS systems can lag several seconds behind a change of state, and cut their decoding delay from 4.8 to 2.4 seconds. Windows must be long too. Shared activity starts at eighty seconds. A thirty-second window crosses its shuffled threshold after fifteen seconds, and clears it in two thirds of the windows. A one-minute window crosses after ten seconds, and clears it throughout. Kostorz and colleagues, in Imaging Neuroscience in 2025, found windows of about one minute feasible for wavelet coherence in fNIRS.

Real-time systems exist, but few report their latency. Mendoza-Armenta and colleagues, in Sensors in 2024, computed synchrony for pairs with wireless dry EEG headsets, in four-second windows. They note an overhead of up to one second, and that validation on a new group remains pending.

So what can you trust? In EEG, with about a second of latency, trust slow changes: synchrony rising over several seconds. Do not trust a single flash, or timing finer than the window. In fNIRS, trust changes over tens of seconds, and read the screen as the past.

Let's recap. Pair samples by timestamp. Use causal filters only. Choose the window: sharp or late. Grey out movement, and run a shuffled partner. In fNIRS, expect seconds of delay and windows of a minute. In the next episode, we hand the screen to the dyad itself.

PLATE 11.55:23

Dyadic neurofeedback

Dyadic neurofeedback shows a pair a lantern that glows with their inter-brain similarity. The lantern is both a measurement and a shared stimulus, and it also gives the pair a goal, so it is part of the system. Sham feedback from another dyad or from random numbers is the needed control, and even a win does not say which route carried the effect.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Picture a dark meadow, and two people sitting face to face. Between them stands a lantern. The lantern knows nothing about them, except one number: how much their brain signals look alike right now. When the number goes up, the lantern glows. When it goes down, it fades.

This is dyadic neurofeedback. In ordinary neurofeedback, one person watches their own brain signal on a display, and learns to change it. Here, the signal belongs to the pair. The real-time computation behind it was the topic of the last episode. Today we ask what the lantern can teach us, and what it can fool us about.

Let's start with the lantern as an artwork. In the spring of twenty sixteen, the Benaki Museum in Athens hosted As One, an exhibition by the arts organisation NEON and the Marina Abramovic Institute. It ran from the tenth of March to the twenty-fourth of April. One installation was the Mutual Wave Machine, built by Suzanne Dikker and Matthias Oostrik. Two visitors sat face to face, wearing EEG headsets, and light patterns changed as their brain activity fell in and out of sync.

Over five years, the team recorded thousands of visitors in museums and festivals. Dikker and colleagues analysed those recordings in NeuroImage, in twenty twenty-one. Offline, they used coupling measures designed to resist noise. Pairs who were more empathic, who felt closer, or who made eye contact and acted together, tended to show more synchrony, mostly in the low alpha and beta ranges.

Here is the catch. The lights came from correlations computed on the fly, and those are very noisy. In a software paper from the same group, in Frontiers in Neuroergonomics in twenty twenty-one, participants were told that what they saw could also come from synchronous noise, or synchronous movement. So the lantern is a wonderful place to meet people. As a measuring instrument, it is not clean.

Now two laboratory studies, both recent. Cheng and colleagues, in the Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, twenty twenty-five. Fifty-seven dyads of strangers wore portable EEG headsets with fourteen electrodes. Two line-drawn brains moved closer together when coherence rose. The training lasted about twelve minutes. Dyads with real feedback reached higher coupling than dyads whose feedback was random numbers, and their feeling of connectedness rose more. Offline, the difference sat in the beta band, around twenty-two hertz, and the authors warn that muscle activity cannot be ruled out.

Francis and colleagues, in iScience, twenty twenty-six, used fNIRS over the inferior frontal gyri. A swimming fish followed the coherence. Twenty-two dyads per group, three sessions over one week. The control fish was driven by another dyad's recording, matched in time, and nobody knew which group they were in. The authors report a group difference in inter-brain synchrony, but only in the third session. They also note that it may have arisen spontaneously, because the pairs had no task other than watching together.

Now look at the lantern again. Both people are watching it. It is the same light for both. A light that two people watch is a shared stimulus. That is route one. If it pulses, two visual systems can follow the pulse, and two brains can look alike because they follow the same input, not because they found each other.

And the lantern does more. It gives the pair a goal. They may look at each other, breathe together, move in step. That is route two. The lantern also shows the pair what the pair just did, so the pair adjusts. The feedback is not a window onto the system. It is part of the system.

So we need a condition where the light is not tied to this pair. In single-person neurofeedback, this is standard. The consensus checklist for neurofeedback studies, published in Brain in twenty twenty by Ros and colleagues, asks for a control group or condition, lists sham feedback as an option, and asks for blinding where it is possible.

For a dyad, that gives three lanterns. The real one. One driven by another dyad's recording, which looks like real coupling, but belongs to someone else. And one driven by random numbers, as in the Cheng study. The random lantern asks whether any light helps. The other-dyad lantern asks whether being tied to this pair matters.

Even a clean win over sham only says that the tie mattered. It does not say which route carried it. Learning to control the lantern, looking at each other, and moving together are all tied to the pair. So record the behaviour, ask people what they did, and ask whether they could tell which lantern they had. Both laboratory studies are small and short, and rely on questionnaires for their outcome.

Let's recap. A lantern that shows synchrony is a measurement and a stimulus at once. Light that two people watch is shared input, route one. Sham feedback, from another dyad or from random numbers, is the control. And even then, ask which route carried the effect.

Next, we stop watching and start stimulating, with current through both heads. Episode eleven point six, hyperstimulation.

PLATE 11.65:58

Hyperstimulation

Transcranial alternating current stimulation sent through both heads, in phase, in anti-phase or sham, tests a causal role for the phase relation between brains. Three studies gave mixed results: a gain at 20 hertz in finger tapping, a loss in drumming, and a gain in song teaching. Samples are small, brains were not recorded, and controls need sham, blinding and pseudo-pairs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the meadow, after dark. In the last episode, a light showed two people how synchronized they were. Today we stop watching, and we push. We send a weak electric rhythm through both heads at once, and we ask whether their behaviour changes.

In episode nine point five, we met three ways to move from correlation toward cause. Change the interaction, change synchrony itself, or test in animals. This episode is the second way, and it is the hardest to do well.

Here is all the technique we need. Transcranial alternating current stimulation, tACS, sends a weak current between two electrodes on the scalp. The current is not steady. It swings up and down at a chosen frequency, like a flicker. In all three studies of this episode, the swing was one milliampere, peak to peak.

With two people, there are two knobs. The first is the frequency. The second is the phase between the two people. In phase, both currents rise and fall at the same moment. In anti-phase, one rises while the other falls. And there is a third condition, sham. The current is ramped up and down briefly, so it feels like the real thing at the start, but it does not keep going.

This is why the design is a causal test. If in-phase stimulation of both brains makes a pair coordinate better than anti-phase or sham, and the phase was the only thing we changed, then the phase relation between the brains did something. That is stronger than any correlation. It does not yet prove that natural synchrony works the same way.

The first case is Novembre and colleagues, in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, in 2017. Thirty pairs. Each partner sat in a separate booth and tapped a finger twice a second. Stimulation went over the left motor cortex. They tried two, ten and twenty hertz, each in phase and in anti-phase, plus sham. At twenty hertz, in-phase stimulation made the first taps line up better than anti-phase or sham did. At two and ten hertz, nothing. And only for the first four taps, not later. The effect was also absent in pseudo-pairs, made of people who had not been stimulated together.

Now the same idea, with another design and another result. Szymanski and colleagues, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, in 2017. Thirty-eight pairs of women, drumming together. Six hertz over the right frontal and parietal scalp, for twenty-five minutes, in three sessions a week apart. Same phase, different frequencies, or sham. The prediction was that same phase would help. It did not. Both active conditions left the drummers less in time with each other than sham. The authors did not check whether the stimulation had really synchronized the two brains. No EEG was analysed, and the current may reach each head differently.

A third study. Pan and colleagues, in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, published in 2021. An instructor taught a song to a learner, face to face. Twenty-four pairs, again all women. Both received stimulation over the left inferior frontal cortex during the eight minutes of teaching, at six or ten hertz, in phase, in anti-phase or sham. At six hertz, in phase, the pairs moved more in step, and expert raters scored the learners' intonation higher. Four point one seven, against three point four six for sham. Anti-phase and ten hertz did not give this. Movement synchrony explained part of the gain, but the interval was wide, from almost zero to about point seven. And the movement analysis was exploratory.

So, three studies. A gain, a loss, and a gain on another task. They share limits. Between twenty-four and thirty-eight pairs. Two of the three with women only. One task each. And in the last two, the authors say that the brains were not recorded during stimulation, so nobody saw whether the two brains were brought in step.

Now the limits one by one. First, specificity. Novembre's gain appeared at one frequency and one phase only, which is reassuring. But another site and another frequency gave a loss in the drumming study. Second, placebo and skin sensations. Stimulation is felt. In Novembre, nine of the sixty people saw phosphenes, flashes of light. In Pan, pain, burning, itching and fatigue were reported, and did not differ across conditions. That is why sham matters, and why people must not know which condition they got. Third, both partners are stimulated, so an effect could come from each brain alone and not from the link between them. The pseudo-pairs in the first study address exactly this.

And safety. In 2017, Antal and colleagues published guidelines in Clinical Neurophysiology. For low-intensity stimulation, they cover currents below four milliamperes, for up to sixty minutes a day. In more than eighteen thousand sessions, no serious adverse event had been reported. Mild effects are common: headache, fatigue, tingling and burning. Moderate ones, like skin burns from poor electrode contact with a related method, are rare. They recommend structured interviews to record side effects. With two people, every check is done twice. These are the 2017 rules, so look for the latest version before a new study.

What do we keep? Stimulating both brains in phase is a causal test, because we choose the phase. The results so far are mixed, with small samples and fragile specificity. A good design needs sham, anti-phase, blinded participants and pseudo-pairs.

Next, we leave the models and test our own work. Episode twelve point one: Robustness one, a hyperscanning study that can fail.
