Same nodes, or not?

Before comparing two graphs, ask whether the nodes correspond. A six-node graph and its renumbered copy disagree on eight of fifteen pairs, among 720 numberings. With matched nodes a graph is a table; otherwise the search explodes. Weakening a ninety-node network's strengths by twenty percent drops its edges from 801 to 500, a Jaccard distance of 0.376 from density alone.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the model-railway room. Today the objects are graphs.

Two brain networks. Two molecules. The network of one person at two moments. How far apart are they? That is the question of this chapter.

A graph is made of dots, called nodes, joined two by two by lines, called edges. This one has six nodes and eight edges. The Graph Theory series draws such dots and lines in its episode 1.1.

We can write a graph as a table. One row and one column for each node. A one in a cell when two nodes are joined, and a zero when they are not. The Graph Theory series shows this in its episode 1.5.

Now give the six nodes new numbers. Same dots, same lines. Only the names change.

The two drawings are the same graph. But written as tables, they are not the same. Count the pairs of nodes where the two tables disagree. Eight pairs out of fifteen. Only four edges are shared.

A distance that counts disagreements would call these two graphs far apart. And they are one graph.

So try every way to number six nodes. There are seven hundred and twenty. For each one, count the pairs that differ. The count goes from zero to fourteen, with a mean of seven point four seven. Zero happens only twice: the graph's own symmetries.

Before any distance between two graphs, there is one question. Do the nodes correspond?

First case: yes. The ninety regions of one atlas, in two people. The same electrodes, in two conditions. Then a graph is a table, one cell for each pair of nodes, and the distances of the first chapters apply.

Counting the edges that differ is the Hamming distance of episode 3.2. Each pair of nodes is one cell, and the cells are compared one by one. Comparing the two sets of edges is the Jaccard distance of episode 3.1. If the edges carry weights, we can use a straight line between the two lists of weights, or a correlation, or chapter 8 when the weights are correlations.

Second case: no. Then we must take the minimum over all the numberings. That is the recipe of episode 10.5.

For six nodes, seven hundred and twenty tries. For sixty-four nodes, a number with ninety digits. For ninety nodes, a number with one hundred and thirty-nine digits.

Deciding whether two graphs are the same, up to a renumbering, is a famous problem. No fast method is known for every case, and nobody has proved it hard either. The best general result is Babai's, from 2016, corrected in 2017.

Finding the best numbering when the graphs differ is harder still. It is done by approximation, for example by Umeyama, in 1988.

Another road gives up matching each node to a single other node. The Gromov-Wasserstein distance of episode 12.7 spreads the mass of each node over several. Xu, Luo, Zha and Carin used it for graphs in 2019. In their version, each graph brings a table of how its own nodes relate to one another, derived from its links.

Here is a test. The chemical network of the worm C elegans. It has three hundred and two neurons, and two hundred and seventy-nine of them are connected. Vogelstein and colleagues shuffled the numbering at random, a thousand times. Their method found the original numbering every single time. That is recovering a copy. It is not matching two brains.

Back to the first case, which has its trap. Two networks of ninety nodes. A brain network starts with a connection strength for each pair of regions. The second network is the first with every strength weakened by twenty percent. Same organization, quieter signal.

To turn connection strengths into a graph, we keep the pairs above a threshold. Here, the same threshold for both.

Among four thousand and five possible pairs, the first graph keeps eight hundred and one. The second keeps five hundred. The share of possible edges that are present is the density. Twenty percent for the first, twelve and a half percent for the second.

The network indices move too. The clustering coefficient is the share of a node's neighbours that are linked to each other, averaged over the nodes. It goes from zero point six seven one to zero point six three eight. Graph Theory 7.1 defines it.

Efficiency is built from paths. For each pair of nodes, count the edges on the shortest path between them, take the inverse, and average over all pairs. It is large when you go fast from anywhere to anywhere. It goes from zero point five zero nine to zero point four zero zero. Graph Theory 7.2 defines it.

For scale, a random graph with as many edges as the second network has a clustering coefficient of zero point one two, and an efficiency of zero point five two six.

Three hundred and one edges differ. The Jaccard distance is zero point three seven six.

Now keep, in each network, its eight hundred and one strongest connections. No edge differs. The whole difference came from one global factor. Not from the organization.

Network indices depend on the number of nodes and on the density, and no method of comparison is fully protected from that. This is the conclusion of van Wijk, Stam and Daffertshofer, in 2010. On a hundred people, the indices are more stable at a fixed density than at a fixed threshold. That is Garrison and colleagues, in 2015. Graph Theory treats the threshold in 14.2, the density in 7.5, and the comparison of networks of different densities in 14.4.

Now the card of this distance. The object: two graphs on matched nodes. The distance: the number of pairs whose edge differs. What it ignores: where the differing edges fall. An edge between two halves of the graph counts like any other. What it costs: one pass over the pairs of nodes. Its trap: the density, and believing that nodes correspond when they only roughly do.

So, before any distance between two graphs, one question. Do the nodes correspond? If yes, a graph is a table. If not, there are seven hundred and twenty numberings for six nodes, and a number with one hundred and thirty-nine digits for ninety.

How many edits apart?

Graph edit distance counts the fewest repairs, adding or removing edges and nodes, that turn one graph into another: chain to square is one, chain to star two, star to square three. It ignores node names and where a repair falls, and it is expensive: the exact program took about seven seconds for ten nodes and did not finish in two minutes for twelve.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the model-railway room. Last time we met the hard part of comparing two graphs: nobody tells us which node of one is which node of the other. Today, the first of three answers.

Remember episode three point three. Between two words, we counted the fewest repairs: insert a letter, delete one, replace one. Each repair cost one. We can play the same game with graphs.

A graph is made of dots, called nodes, joined by lines, called edges. The Graph Theory series introduces them in episode one point one. Between two graphs, the repairs are these: add an edge, remove an edge, add a node, remove a node. When nodes carry labels, change a label. Each repair costs one. The fewest repairs that turn one graph into the other is the graph edit distance.

It was formalised in nineteen eighty-three, by two groups: Sanfeliu and Fu, and Bunke and Allermann.

Let us try it on four nodes and three drawings. A chain. A star with three branches. A square. Our inchworm will hop from one drawing to the next, one hop for each repair.

Chain to square: one repair. Add the edge that closes the loop. Distance one.

Chain to star: two repairs. Remove one edge, add another. Distance two.

Star to square: three repairs. At best they share two edges. One edge of the star goes, and two edges of the square arrive. Distance three.

Two more pairs. A triangle, and four nodes all linked to each other: four repairs. One new node, three new edges. And a hexagon, against two separate triangles: also four. Remove two edges, add two edges.

Now notice what we did not do. We never numbered the nodes. The distance tries every way of matching the nodes of one graph with the nodes of the other, and keeps the cheapest. That is why renaming changes nothing. The graph of last time and its renumbered copy are at distance zero. And if someone hands us the matching, there is nothing to search. We only count the edges that differ, which is the count of last episode.

As in episode three point three, the prices are a choice. Suppose adding a node costs ten, and adding an edge costs one. The triangle and the four linked nodes are now thirteen apart, instead of four. Before, that pair was tied with the hexagon pair as the farthest. Now it stands far above every other.

Is it a true distance? Yes, when the price of every single repair is itself a distance. Neuhaus and Bunke are usually credited with showing this, in a book from two thousand seven. With every price equal to one, that is our case. And two graphs at distance zero are the same graph, up to the names of their nodes.

There is a second way to say the same thing. Look for the largest part that the two graphs share, and compare its size with the larger graph. Bunke and Shearer proposed this in nineteen ninety-eight. With one particular choice of prices, the two ways come to the same thing, as Bunke showed in nineteen ninety-seven.

Now, what does it ignore? First, the names of the nodes, by design. Second, where a repair falls. Take two groups of ten nodes, all linked inside, and joined by a single edge. Cut that edge: the graph falls in two pieces. Remove an edge inside a group: nothing breaks. Both cost one. The distance cannot tell a bridge from a back road.

And what does it cost? This is the point of the episode. The most natural distance between two graphs is also the most expensive.

The exact method tries the matchings. For two graphs of ten nodes there are already three million six hundred twenty-eight thousand eight hundred ways to pair the nodes, and that is before allowing deleted nodes. For twelve nodes: four hundred seventy-nine million.

So we timed an exact program, on one laptop. Two random graphs, each edge present with probability point four, three pairs per size, and we report the median. Six nodes: a hundredth of a second. Eight nodes: a third of a second. Ten nodes: about seven seconds. Twelve nodes: none of the three pairs finished in two minutes.

A warning. When this program is stopped at its time limit, it returns the best value found so far. That number is not the distance, and we never show it as one. These times depend on the program and on the graphs. The trend is what matters.

The problem is hard in the computer-science sense: it is NP-hard in general, as Zeng and colleagues recall in their paper of two thousand nine. In benchmarks of small labelled graphs, the best exact programs compute the distance up to about sixteen nodes, within a thousand seconds. That is the work of Blumenthal and Gamper. A brain network of ninety regions is out of reach.

So people approximate. The most common method describes each node by its immediate neighbourhood, then looks for the cheapest matching of nodes, which is an assignment problem and runs fast. Riesen and Bunke introduced it in two thousand nine. It always gives a valid list of repairs, so its number is at least the true distance. Often it is larger, and nothing tells us by how much.

Let us fill the card. The object: two graphs, nodes unmatched. The distance: the fewest repairs, over all matchings. It ignores the names of the nodes, and where a repair falls. It costs a time that explodes with the number of nodes. And the trap: the prices, which decide the ranking, and the approximation, which overestimates without saying by how much.

The most natural distance between graphs is exact up to ten nodes, and an approximation beyond.

Hearing a graph

Renumbering a graph shuffles its table but leaves its eigenvalues unchanged, so comparing sorted eigenvalues ignores node names and is cheap. A hexagon and two triangles are 2.449 apart. The trap is twins: a four-branch star and a square plus an isolated node share eigenvalues, and one graph in five at ten or eleven nodes has a twin.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two graphs, and no way to tell which node goes with which. Episode 13.2 faced that by trying matchings, and paid for it in seconds, then in minutes.

This episode takes another road. Forget the numbering altogether, and listen to the graph.

Here is why that can work. In episode 12.8, the eigenvalues of a matrix did not move when we changed the sensors. A change of coordinates leaves them alone.

Now write a graph as a table. One row and one column per node, a 1 in a cell when two nodes are linked, a 0 otherwise. Renumber the nodes, and the rows and the columns are shuffled in the same way. That is a change of coordinates. So the eigenvalues of the table cannot move.

Take our six-node graph from episode 13.1, and its renumbered copy. Their tables differ on 8 of the 15 pairs of nodes. Their eigenvalues are the same, all six of them.

So here is a way to compare two graphs. Sort each list of eigenvalues, and measure the straight line between the two lists, as in chapter two. For these two graphs, the distance is 0.

People usually take a close cousin of the table, called the Laplacian. On the diagonal, the number of neighbours of each node. Everywhere else, minus 1 for each link.

Think of its eigenvalues as the bars of a wind chime. Tap the graph, and one bar rings for each eigenvalue. A long bar is a high note. And a chime can be read.

First, count the zeros. The number of zeros is the number of pieces the graph is cut into. One piece, one zero. Two pieces, two zeros.

Second, look at the second smallest eigenvalue. Fiedler showed in 1973 that it is zero exactly when the graph is in pieces. A small value means a bottleneck.

A ring of twenty nodes is a long thin loop: 0.098. Twenty nodes, all linked to each other: 20. Two groups of ten, every node linked inside its group, joined by a single edge: 0.169. Now cut that edge. The second note drops to 0, and the chime has two zeros. Two pieces.

Now the distance itself. A hexagon rings 0, 1, 1, 3, 3, 4. Two triangles, apart from each other, ring 0, 0, 3, 3, 3, 3. Subtract the two sorted lists, square, add, and take the root: the root of 6, which is 2.449. That distance does not care how the nodes are named.

What does it cost? One decomposition per graph, then a subtraction. Here, on one laptop, with random graphs where one pair in ten is linked: 500 nodes take a hundredth of a second, 1,000 nodes three hundredths, 2,000 nodes about two tenths of a second. Compare episode 13.2: about 7 seconds for ten nodes. These times depend on the program and on the graphs.

Now the trap, and it is an old question. In 1966, Mark Kac asked: can one hear the shape of a drum? Could you recover a drum's shape from its notes? In 1992, Gordon, Webb and Wolpert answered no. They built two different flat shapes with exactly the same notes.

Drums are not graphs, but graphs behave the same way. Collatz and Sinogowitz knew in 1957 that different graphs can share their eigenvalues. Here we will call such graphs twins.

The smallest twins have five nodes. One is a star with four branches: a centre linked to four leaves. The other is a square, plus a node linked to nothing. They are not the same graph. One is in one piece, the other is in two. Yet the tables of both have the same five eigenvalues: minus 2, 0, 0, 0, 2.

The Laplacian does tell this pair apart: 0, 1, 1, 1, 5 against 0, 0, 2, 2, 4. Count the zeros. But the Laplacian has its own twins.

How common are twins? Take all the graphs with a given number of nodes, and count those that share their eigenvalues with at least one other graph. We counted up to seven nodes.

With four nodes, none of the 11 graphs. With five, 2 of 34: the pair you just met. With six, 10 of 156 for the ordinary table, and 4 for the Laplacian. With seven, 110 of 1,044, about one in ten, and 130 for the Laplacian, one in eight.

For the ordinary table, the enumeration published by Haemers and Spence goes up to eleven nodes. At ten nodes, 21 percent of the graphs have a twin. At eleven nodes, 215 million of 1.02 billion: 21 percent again. One graph in five. These counts are for the ordinary table, not for the Laplacian.

The share peaks around ten and eleven nodes, then falls: 19 percent at twelve. Haemers conjectured that it goes to 0 for large graphs. But for trees, Schwenk showed in 1973 that almost all of them have a twin.

So a distance of 0 does not prove that two graphs are the same. It says only that they sound the same.

There is a second trap: importance. Go back to the two groups of ten, joined by one edge. Cut the bridge between the groups: one edit. The eigenvalues move by 1.839. Instead, remove one edge inside a group: also one edit. The eigenvalues move by 2.0. The first change cuts the graph in two pieces. The second leaves it in one. And the distance finds the second change bigger.

Time for the card. The object is a sorted list of eigenvalues. The distance is the straight line between two lists. It ignores the names of the nodes, and everything that separates twins. It costs one decomposition per graph, about two tenths of a second at 2,000 nodes. Its trap: a distance between two lists of eigenvalues is not a distance between two graphs.

Of the four rules, the second one falls: twins are at distance 0 without being the same graph.

Hearing a graph is cheap, and it is deaf in places.

Counting small pieces

Counting small pieces, or colour counts from Weisfeiler and Leman refinement, turns graphs into lists. By edits the chain and square are closest; by colours the chain and star are. The colour kernel cannot tell a hexagon from two triangles, which eigenvalues separate, so each family has its own twins. Density also fools triangle counts: 3,173 against 1,173 after weakening connections by twenty percent.

Welcome back to the model railway room. We have two ways of comparing graphs so far. In episode thirteen point two, we counted edits. A chain, a star and a square, four nodes each. Chain to star, two edits. Chain to square, one. Star to square, three. In episode thirteen point three, we listened to eigenvalues. They told the hexagon from two separate triangles. But a star with four branches, and a square with one lone point, share the same five eigenvalues of the adjacency table: minus two, zero, zero, zero and two. Today, a third family. Describe each graph by a count, then compare the counts. A count is a list of numbers. The dot product of two such lists is a kernel, like the ones of chapter seven. So the only question is: what do we count?

Start with the simplest pieces. A triangle is three nodes all linked to each other. An open triple is three nodes joined by exactly two links. Take the hexagon. No triangle. But every node sits in the middle of one open triple: six in all. Zero triangles, six open triples. Take two separate triangles. Two triangles, and no open triple. Two and zero. Each graph is now a list of two numbers. The hexagon is zero, six. The two triangles are two, zero. Their dot product is zero: they share nothing. These counts tell the two graphs apart.

We can count more pieces. Every pattern of three, four or five nodes. Looking for small patterns in networks goes back to Milo and colleagues, in two thousand and two. They kept the patterns that appear more often than in random networks. In two thousand and four, Pržulj, Corneil and Jurisica compared networks through the frequencies of small connected pieces, which they called graphlets. And in two thousand and nine, Shervashidze and colleagues turned graphlet counts into a kernel. Here is the six-node graph of episode thirteen point one. It holds fifteen groups of four nodes. Each group, with its links, is one of eleven possible shapes. Drop each group in its bin. Seven of the fifteen land in the same bin. Now a realistic size. Ninety nodes. That makes two million, five hundred fifty-five thousand, one hundred ninety groups of four. And forty-three million, nine hundred forty-nine thousand, two hundred sixty-eight groups of five. So we sample. On the ninety-node network of episode thirteen point one, with eight hundred and one links, we drew ten thousand groups of four nodes. This is a simulation, seed one thirty-four. Every share in the sample lands within half a point of the exact share, counted over all two and a half million groups.

A different idea counts neighbourhoods instead. Nodes get colours, and the colours are refined. Start with the chain. Every node has the same colour. That is the start. In each round, a node takes a new colour that sums up its own colour and the colours of its neighbours. In the first round, everyone started equal, so the new colour is simply the number of neighbours. The two ends have one, the two middle nodes have two. In every later round, the new colour sums up the old colour and the list of the neighbours' colours. After a few rounds, a colour describes a whole neighbourhood. We stop after three rounds, counted from the common start. We keep the colour counts at the start and after each round. Those are the lists. This procedure carries the names of Weisfeiler and Leman, after a paper of nineteen sixty-eight. The kernel on colour counts is by Shervashidze and colleagues, in two thousand and eleven. Chain against star. At the start, four nodes against four: sixteen. After the first round, the two ends of the chain share a colour with the three leaves of the star: six. Later, nothing in common. Total: twenty-two. Divide by the square root of the two graphs' own totals, forty and forty-six. The kernel is zero point five one three. As a distance, the square root of two minus two k: zero point nine nine. Chain against square: kernel zero point four seven four, distance one point oh three. Star against square: kernel zero point two nine five, distance one point one nine. Put them next to the edits: two, one, three. The scales differ, so only the order can be compared. By edits, the closest pair is the chain and the square. By colours, it is the chain and the star. Two distances, two rankings.

Now the trap. In the hexagon, every node has two neighbours, and each of them has two neighbours. In two triangles, every node has two neighbours, and each of them has two neighbours. Everyone starts equal, and everyone stays equal. At every round, six nodes of one colour. The kernel finds the two graphs identical. Distance zero. Yet the eigenvalues of episode thirteen point three told them apart: zero, one, one, three, three, four, against zero, zero, three, three, three, three. The reverse holds too. The star with four branches and the square with a lone point have the same eigenvalues. For colours, they differ from the first round: one node with four neighbours, against four with two and one with none. The same blindness hits any two graphs of the same size where every node has the same number of neighbours. Over all graphs of six nodes, there are one hundred fifty-six. Colours give one hundred fifty-two classes: eight graphs have a colour twin. At seven nodes, one thousand forty-four graphs, one thousand twenty-two classes, forty-four with a twin.

Can this be repaired? Not completely. A kernel that never gives the same list to two different graphs is at least as hard to compute as deciding whether two graphs are the same. That is the result of Gärtner, Flach and Wrobel, in two thousand and three. For graphs drawn at random, each link present with chance one half, a method of this type is almost always enough, as Babai, Erdős and Selkow showed in nineteen eighty. That model is far from brain networks. And graph neural networks that pass messages between neighbours, with no added node features, tell apart no more graphs than this procedure. Xu and colleagues, and Morris and colleagues, both showed it in two thousand and nineteen. So each family has its own twins, and they are not the same. Eigenvalues separate the hexagon from two triangles, which colours confuse. Colours separate the star from the square with a point, which eigenvalues confuse. No fast distance tells every graph apart. We pick the one whose twins do not matter for our question.

Here is the card of the colour kernel. The object: a graph. The distance: a straight line between lists of colour counts, after three rounds. It ignores node names, and everything beyond the neighbourhoods counted. It costs a pass over the edges for each round. For small patterns, it costs from a few passes to millions of groups. Its trap: size and density. Take the ninety-node network of episode thirteen point one, with eight hundred and one links. Weaken every connection by twenty percent, and keep the same threshold: five hundred links. Count the triangles. Three thousand, one hundred seventy-three, against one thousand, one hundred seventy-three. The counts differ, before any difference of organisation. Now keep twenty percent of the pairs in both. The two graphs are identical, with three thousand, one hundred seventy-three triangles each. Counts, not names. Cheap, flexible, with twins of their own, and fooled by density. Which graphs are you comparing?

Whether the nodes correspond comes before any distance between graphs. When they do, simple measures go far: Finn and colleagues matched 117 of 126 people between two sessions by correlating lists of connections. Two graphs from the same random model of ninety nodes still have Jaccard distance 0.889, so zero is never the right reference.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Which graphs are you comparing? This is the last episode of the chapter on graphs, so the screen has two columns. On the left, what is established. On the right, what is only worth trying. First, the one question that came before every distance in this chapter.

Do the nodes of your two graphs correspond? Suppose they are the same regions of one atlas, in two people. Or the same electrodes, in two conditions. Then a graph is a table, one cell per pair of nodes, and the distances of the early chapters apply. If the nodes do not correspond, remember the graph with six nodes. Its renumbered copy differs from it on 8 of the 15 pairs. Matched by name, the two differ on none. Three families of answers followed: edits, eigenvalues, and counting small pieces. Each one confuses some graphs, its twins, and the twins are not the same from one family to the next.

A word on how this episode was prepared. Some papers we read. Some we read only in part. Others we know only through their abstract or their catalogue record. Every card on the board carries a tag that says which.

Established, first. When the nodes correspond, simple measures go a long way. In 2015, Finn and colleagues took 126 people from the Human Connectome Project, each scanned in two resting sessions. They used an atlas of 268 regions, so 35,778 connections per person. They simply correlated the two lists of connections. Matching the first session against the second, 117 of the 126 people were found: 92.9 percent. Matching the second against the first, 119: 94.4 percent. With the identities shuffled a thousand times, the best chance result was 6 out of 126. This is not a distance between graphs in the sense of this chapter. No structure is used, only a list of numbers.

Second, testing where two groups of networks differ. Testing every connection alone means thousands of tests. Zalesky, Fornito and Bullmore proposed, in 2010, to control the error over sets of connections that are linked to each other. We know this method from its abstract.

Third, the density trap. In episode 13.1, the same threshold gave densities of 0.200 and 0.125, with 801 and 500 edges, and 301 edges differed. At an equal density of 20 percent, none did. This is documented. Van Wijk, Stam and Daffertshofer found, in 2010, that no method they examined was entirely free of bias, though fixing the density removes much of it. Garrison and colleagues, in 2015, found measures more stable with proportional thresholds, and a group difference could even flip its sign with an absolute threshold. Fornito, Zalesky and Breakspear reviewed the pitfalls in 2013. The Graph Theory series covers density in its episode 14.4.

Fourth, a network index is read against reference graphs that keep each node's number of neighbours. Maslov and Sneppen did it in 2002, by swapping the ends of pairs of edges. Váša and Mišić reviewed null models in 2022. Here is what it looks like. The 500-edge graph of episode 13.1 has an average clustering coefficient of 0.638. A random graph with as many edges has 0.12. Graphs with the same neighbour counts, rewired by swaps, give 0.146 on average, over 100 draws. Which reference you choose is part of the answer.

Fifth, no distance wins everywhere. In 2019, Tantardini and colleagues compared methods on a test bed of simulated and real networks. Counting small pieces was the most reliable, with their own caveat: this holds for their test bed. Methods that need matched nodes depended strongly on density and size. Wills and Meyer, in 2020, compared eigenvalue distances with table distances, and in the part we read, they do not see the same structures. We did not read their final recommendations. The twins also change from family to family. The hexagon and the two triangles get identical colours from colour counting, as episode 13.4 showed. Their eigenvalues differ, at a distance of 2.449, as episode 13.3 showed. The four-branch star and the square with an isolated node share their adjacency eigenvalues, yet their neighbour counts differ.

Sixth, a floor. In 2020, Hartle and colleagues proposed a reference: the distance between two graphs drawn from the same model. Here is one, simulated. 90 nodes, each pair linked with probability 0.2, and 200 pairs of draws. Two graphs from the same model differ on about 1,285 of the 4,005 pairs. Their Jaccard distance is 0.889. Zero is never the right reference. Mheich, Wendling and Hassan took stock for brain networks in 2020.

That was the left column. Now the right column, worth trying.

In hyperscanning, network indices have been computed on one graph that joins two brains. In 2010, De Vico Fallani and colleagues had 26 pairs play a cooperation game, the iterated prisoner's dilemma. In the pairs where both players defected, the joint graph had fewer links between the two brains, and a higher modularity. Müller, Sänger and Lindenberger studied guitarists who improvise, in 2013. The structure of this graph depended on the frequency. Toppi and colleagues, in 2015, simulated which indices react to communication in one direction only.

Now an absence. Our search found no graph distance applied to networks of two brains. It was a limited search. That is a limit of our search, not a proof that none exists. One preprint from 2025 follows the curvature of such networks, and its abstract mentions no graph distance.

Three tracks, not results. One: measure the distance between the graph of a pair in two conditions, at a fixed density, rather than compare an index. Two: compare the graph of a real pair with the graphs of pseudo-pairs, which episode 7.5 of the Hyperscanning series explains. Three: compare the networks of the two partners, whose nodes correspond if they wear the same cap.

With one reservation. A graph inherits the defects of its edges. Avendano-Diaz and colleagues simulated inter-brain connectivity in 2026, and phase-based measures were highly sensitive to differences in peak frequency between people. We read part of that paper. It does not study network indices, but any graph built on such edges inherits the problem.

Two columns. Established: when the nodes correspond, simple measures often suffice, and the density, the reference graph and the floor come before the distance. Worth trying: a distance between two-brain graphs. One question separates them. Do the nodes correspond, and what is the distance between two graphs that should not differ?

Between two brain networks, the nodes almost always correspond, and the distance is simple. What is hard comes before it: the threshold, the density, and the reference graph.
