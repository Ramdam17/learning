Why change space at all?

One recording can be viewed as voltage over time, spectrum, time-frequency map, amplitude and phase, or sensors, components and sources. Each space is a map that keeps some things and hides others, and transformations are the roads between maps. Some are invertible, like Fourier, while others throw information away, so the right space is the one that makes the question easy.

TRANSCRIPTEN

One EEG recording, many ways to look at it. As a voltage over time, as a spectrum, as a time frequency map, as amplitude and phase, as electrodes on a head as components or as sources in the brain. This chapter is about these spaces of representation. Before diving in, let's ask the obvious question, why change space at all?

Think of a city and its maps, a street map to walk, a metro map to travel fast, an elevation map to find the hills. None of them is the city. Each one keeps some things and hides others, so that one kind of question becomes easy to answer.

Spaces of representation are the maps of your data. In the time domain, the question when is easy. In the frequency domain, the question which rhythms is easy. In the time frequency plane, you can ask when a rhythm grows or fades. Amplitude and phase separate how strong a rhythm is from where it is in its cycle. And in space, sensors, components, and sources tell you where and which process.

Transformations are the roads between maps. The Fourier transform goes from time to frequency. Short-time Fourier and wavelets go from time to time frequency. The Hilbert transform gives amplitude and phase. Re-referencing moves between sensor views. Spatial filters, like independent component analysis or beamformers, go from electrodes to components or sources.

Here is the rule to keep in mind on every road. Some transformations are invertible, you can always travel back. The Fourier transform is one of them. It creates no information and destroys none. It only repackages what was there.

Other steps are one way. Keeping only the power spectrum throws away the phase. Averaging over trials throws away what varies from trial to trial. Filtering to a band throws away everything outside it. These steps are often exactly what you want. Just know what you leave behind because you cannot get it back later.

So the right space is the one that makes your question easy. When did it happen? Stay in time. Which rhythm? Go to frequency. Did the rhythm change after the stimulus? Use time frequency. Where in the brain? Go to source space. And when you change space, ask two questions. What does this view make visible and what does it hide?

The rest of this chapter walks these roads one by one. Fourier, Windows and Leakage, Welch and Multitaper, Spectrograms, Wavelets, Hilbert references the Laplacian and Spatial Projections.

Let's recap. A space of representation is a map of the same data. No map is the true one. Transformations are roads between maps. Invertible ones lose nothing. One-way steps discard something on purpose. Choose the space that makes your question easy, and know what it hides.

Next time, the first road and the most famous. Fourier by winding a signal around a circle.

PLATE 3.24:36

Fourier I · Winding a signal around a circle

The Fourier transform finds hidden rhythms by winding a signal around a circle at a chosen speed. When the speed matches a rhythm, the ups and downs pile up on one side and the center of mass leaves the middle, its distance giving amplitude and its angle phase. Sweeping the speed gives the spectrum, and the operation is reversible.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is one second of EEG-like signal. It wiggles, it bumps, it looks messy. Yet it hides a simple recipe, a few steady rhythms added together. Today we learn how to find those rhythms. We wind the signal around a circle.

The idea goes back to Joseph Fourier. In 1807, while studying how heat spreads through solid bodies, he argued that a temperature profile could be written as a sum of sinusoids. His full theory of heat appeared as a book in 1822. Two centuries later, his trick sits inside every EEG spectrum you will ever compute.

Let's build the recipe. Our example signal is illustrative. A 10 Hz rhythm with amplitude 1 plus a 20 Hz rhythm with amplitude 1.5. Now picture a turntable. As time goes by, the turntable spins and we draw the signal onto it. Each sample becomes a little arrow from the center. Its length is the signal value. Its direction is set by how far the turntable has turned. When the signal is negative, the arrow points the other way.

The speed of the turntable is our choice. We call it the winding frequency. Let's start at 7 turns per second. The drawing spreads out evenly in every direction like a flower. Now let a pearl roll to the center of mass of the drawing, the average of all those arrows. With 7 turns per second, the pearl stays at the center. Nothing at 7 hertz.

Now spin at exactly 10 turns per second, the speed of our rhythm. Something special happens. Every positive bump lands on the same side of the turntable. And every negative dip lands on the opposite side but pointing back, so it adds to the same side too. The whole drawing leans one way, and the pearl rolls away from the center.

That is the entire trick. When the winding speed matches a rhythm in the signal, its ups and downs pile up on one side. When it does not match, they cancel out.

Now sweep the winding frequency slowly from 0 to 30 Hz and plot how far the pearl sits from the center. Flat, flat, flat, then a peak at 10 Hz. Flat again, then a smaller peak at 20 Hz. This plot is the amplitude spectrum. We just computed a Fourier transform by hand.

Look at the heights. The peak at 10 Hz is one half, not one. Where did the other half go? Remember episode 2.2? A real cosine is two arrows spinning in opposite directions. Half the amplitude lives at plus 10 Hz, the other half at minus 10 Hz. Software usually folds the two halves back together for you.

The pearl gives us one more thing, its direction. The angle of the pearl measured from the horizontal axis is the phase of that rhythm. Shift the 10 Hz rhythm in time, and the pearl slides around the circle while staying the same distance from the center. Distance gives the amplitude. Angle gives the phase. One complex number per frequency.

Written as a formula, it looks like this. For each frequency f, multiply the signal by an arrow spinning backwards at f turns per second, an average over time. In symbols x of t times e to the minus i2 pi f t. The minus sign is just the direction of winding. In episode 2.3, we will meet this again as a dot product, the signal compared with a spinning template.

And nothing is lost. If you keep every complex number, amplitude, and phase at every frequency, you can rebuild the original signal exactly by adding the spinning arrows back together. That is the inverse Fourier transform. We have not created information. We have moved it into another space.

But this space has a blind spot. The winding averages over the whole recording. It tells you which rhythms were present, not when. If alpha appears only in the last second, the spectrum shows a peak and stays silent about the timing. That is the reason for the time frequency methods later in this chapter.

Let's recap. Wind the signal around a circle at a chosen speed. When the speed matches a rhythm, the drawing leans, and the center of mass moves. Its distance gives the amplitude, its angle gives the phase. Sweeping the speed gives the spectrum. And the whole thing is reversible.

PLATE 3.33:54

Fourier II · The DFT in practice

The discrete Fourier transform sorts a signal's energy into evenly spaced frequency bins, like drawers in a chest. The spacing is one over the recording length, whatever the sampling rate, so only more real data sharpens resolution. Zero padding merely interpolates between drawers, a rhythm falling between bins leaks into its neighbors, and software libraries scale their outputs differently.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we wound a smooth signal around a circle. A computer never sees a smooth signal. It sees a finite list of samples. Today, the Fourier transform the way your software actually computes it. The discrete Fourier transform, its frequency bins, and the one number that sets its resolution.

Picture a chest of drawers. The discrete Fourier transform does not give you a continuous spectrum. It sorts the signal's energy into a fixed set of drawers, one per frequency, evenly spaced. These drawers are called frequency bins.

How many drawers and how far apart? Take n samples at a sampling rate fs. The recording lasts t seconds, with t equal to n divided by fs. The transform returns n complex numbers, and the bins are spaced exactly 1 over t hertz apart. For a real signal, the useful half runs from 0 hertz up to the Nyquist frequency. The rest is the mirror image, the negative frequencies.

An example, 2 seconds of EEG at 250 hertz is 500 samples. The bins sit every half a hertz, 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and so on, up to 125 hertz.

Notice what is missing from that formula, the sampling rate. The spacing of the bins depends only on how long you record. Sampling faster lets you see higher frequencies. Recording longer lets you see finer frequency detail.

A word on speed. Computed naively, the discrete Fourier transform needs about n-squared operations. The fast Fourier transform, published by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, gets the exact same result in about n times log n. For a million samples, that is the difference between a trillion operations and about 20 million. Every spectrum in MNE, SciPy or NumPy goes through it.

Now resolution. Imagine two rhythms at 10 and at 10.5 Hz. With one second of data, the bins are 1 Hz apart and the two rhythms fall into the same neighborhood. One blurry bump. With 4 seconds, the bins are a quarter of a Hz apart and two separate peaks appear. To separate two rhythms, you need a recording long enough that 1 over T is clearly smaller than their distance.

Here comes the classic trap, zero padding. Add zeros at the end of your one second segment, until it is four seconds long. The transform now returns four times more bins, and the curve looks smoother. But look closely, the two rhythms are still one single bump. Zero padding interpolates between the drawers. It does not add information, so it cannot add resolution. Only more real data can.

One more subtlety. What if a rhythm falls between two drawers, say at 10.25 Hz, with bins every half Hz? Its energy spills into the neighboring drawers, and into drawers further away. That spill is called spectral leakage, and it is the subject of the next episode.

A practical note on units. Libraries scale their output differently. Some divide by n, some do not, and a power spectral density is also divided by the bin width, giving microvolts squared per hertz. Before comparing numbers across tools or papers, check which convention was used.

Let's recap. The discrete Fourier transform sorts energy into evenly spaced bins. The spacing is 1 over the recording length, whatever the sampling rate. The fast Fourier transform computes the same thing much faster. Resolution needs real data. Zero padding makes the curve smoother not sharper. And always check the units.

PLATE 3.44:00

Real, imaginary, amplitude, phase, power

A Fourier coefficient is an arrow that can be read as a mixing desk: real and imaginary parts are two shadows, the length is amplitude, the fader, and the angle is phase, the knob. Power is amplitude squared. Phase lives on a circle and only means something relative to something else, yet it holds the timing: scrambling phases while keeping power destroys sharp events.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, winding gave us one arrow per frequency. Today we take that arrow apart. Real part, imaginary part, amplitude, phase, and power. Five words you will meet in every EEG paper and in every line of analysis code. They all describe the same arrow.

Think of the spectrum as a mixing desk. Each frequency gets one channel. Each channel has two controls, a fader which sets how loud that frequency is and a rotary knob which sets where in its cycle that frequency starts. Turn every channel to the right setting and the desk plays back your signal.

First the two shadows. A Fourier coefficient is a complex number, A real part plus i times an imaginary part. The real part tells you how much the signal resembles a cosine at that frequency. The imaginary part tells you how much it resembles a sine. With the usual winding direction, it carries a minus sine. Two numbers, two shadows, one arrow.

Next, the length of the arrow. That is the amplitude, the square root of the real part squared plus the imaginary part squared. It is the fader. It says how strong that rhythm is, whatever its timing.

Square the amplitude and you get the power. Power is what most EEG spectra show. One reason is energy. By Parseval's theorem, the total power of the signal in time equals the total power summed over all frequencies. Nothing is lost when you move from one space to the other. Because power spans many orders of magnitude, it is often plotted on a log scale or in decibels. 10 times the log of a power ratio.

Then the angle of the arrow. That is the phase. It is the rotary knob. Software reports it in radians from minus pi to plus pi. And those two ends are neighbors, not opposites. An angle just below pi and an angle just above minus pi point almost the same way. Phase lives on a circle, so you cannot average phases like ordinary numbers. That is why phase measures use their own circular tools, as we will see with intertrial coherence and the phase locking value.

There is a catch with phase. Shift the start of your analysis window by a few milliseconds, and every phase changes, while every amplitude stays the same. On its own, the phase of one frequency in one window depends on where you started the clock. What carries meaning is how phase relates to something else. An event, another trial, another electrode.

So which matters more, the faders or the knobs? Here is a famous experiment. Take a signal with a sharp event in it. Keep every fader exactly where it is, the same amplitude spectrum, the same power. Then spin every knob to a random position. Play it back.

The sharp event is gone. The signal now looks like noise. Same power at every frequency, completely different signal. The timing, the shape, the sharp edges, all of it was written in the phases. The same lesson is a classic in image processing described by Oppenheim and Lim in 1981.

This trick is also a tool. Scrambling phases while keeping the power spectrum creates surrogate data, signals with the same spectrum but no timing structure. In Chapter 10, we will use such surrogates to ask whether a connectivity value is larger than chance.

Let's recap. A Fourier coefficient is an arrow. Real and imaginary parts are its two shadows. Its length is the amplitude, and amplitude squared is power. Its angle is the phase, which lives on a circle and only makes sense relative to something. And the phases hold the timing, scramble them, and the signal falls apart, even with the same power spectrum.

PLATE 3.53:20

Windowing and spectral leakage

The discrete Fourier transform treats a segment as if it repeated forever, so a jump at the join creates spectral leakage. Cutting a segment is a hard-edged spotlight, a rectangle with high side lobes. A Hann taper removes the jump and lowers the side lobes, so a weak rhythm emerges beside a strong alpha peak, at the price of a wider main lobe.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the end of the last episode, a rhythm that fell between two frequency bins spilled its energy into its neighbors. That spill has a name, spectral leakage, and a cause you can see with your own eyes. Today we find the cause and the simple fix, a window.

Picture a theater stage. A spotlight with a hard edge cuts the scene abruptly. Inside is fully lit, outside is pitch dark. A spotlight with a soft edge fades gently into the shadows. Analyzing a segment of EEG is like lighting it with a spotlight. Its edges matter more than you would think.

Here is why. The discrete Fourier transform quietly assumes that your segment repeats forever. The end is glued back onto the start. If the segment holds a whole number of cycles, the glue is invisible. If it does not, there is a jump at the join. A jump is a sharp event, and sharp events contain many frequencies, so energy appears in bins far from the true rhythm. That is leakage.

Cutting a segment out of a longer recording is the hard-edged spotlight. Mathematically, it multiplies the signal by a rectangle. And by the convolution theorem, multiplying in time means convolving in frequency. Every peak in the spectrum gets smeared by the spectrum of the rectangle, a narrow main lobe and a long row of side lobes on both sides. The first side lobe is only about 13 decibels below the peak.

The fix is a taper. Multiply the segment by a smooth bump that fades to zero at both ends, like the soft-edged spotlight. The most common one is the Hann window, a raised cosine. Now the start and the end are both near zero, and the glue has no jump.

The spectrum of the Hann window has much lower side lobes. The first one is about 31 decibels below the peak and they fall away quickly. The Hamming window, a close cousin, pushes the first side lobe down to about 43 decibels, but its far side lobes decay more slowly.

Here is where it matters. Take a strong alpha peak at 10 hertz and a small rhythm at 13 hertz, 20 times weaker. With the rectangular cut, the side lobes of alpha spill over the small rhythm and hide it. With a Hann window, the side lobes drop and the small peak reappears.

Nothing is free. The taper widens the main lobe, roughly doubling it for Hann, so very close peaks blur a little more. And the taper down weights the data near the edges, so part of your segment counts less. The next episode shows how overlapping segments win that data back.

So in practice, never take a spectrum with a hard cut when you can taper. Hann or Hamming windows are common defaults in analysis tools. And when you compare spectra, compare them with the same window.

Let's recap. The Fourier transform treats each segment as if it repeated forever. A jump at the join creates leakage. Cutting is multiplying by a rectangle whose spectrum has high side lobes. A taper, like Hann, removes the jump and lowers the side lobes at the price of a wider main lobe and less weight on the edges.

Next time, Welch's method, or how to trade a little resolution for a lot of stability.

PLATE 3.63:04

Welch's method · Trading resolution for stability

A raw periodogram is jagged at every frequency, and recording longer does not fix it. Welch's method cuts the recording into overlapping tapered segments and averages their periodograms, like polling many people, which lowers the variance. Resolution is then set by the segment length, so short segments give stable but blurry spectra, and taking the median instead of the mean resists artifacts.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Compute one Fourier transform over a whole recording, square it, and you get a spectrum called a periodogram. Look at it closely and it is shockingly jagged. Today we see why and we meet the method behind most EEG spectra you will ever see — Welch's method.

Think of an opinion poll. Ask one person and their answer can be anything. Ask many people and average and a stable picture emerges. Welch's method polls many short pieces of your recording, instead of trusting one long one.

First the problem. The raw periodogram is noisy at every frequency. Each bin bounces up and down around the true value, with a spread as large as the value itself. And here is the surprise, recording longer does not fix it. A longer recording gives you more bins, closer together, but each bin is just as jumpy as before. Statisticians say the periodogram is not a consistent estimator.

The fix was published by Peter Welch in 1967. Cut the recording into shorter segments. Taper each segment with a window such as Hann. Compute a periodogram for each one. Then average them bin by bin. The jagged bumps of individual segments cancel out and a smooth spectrum appears.

Segments usually overlap, often by half. Remember the taper from the last episode? It gives little weight to the edges of each segment. Overlapping lets the edge of one segment sit in the middle of the next, so no stretch of data is wasted.

How much smoother? Roughly, the variance shrinks in proportion to the number of segments you average. Overlapping segments are not fully independent, so the gain is a bit smaller than the raw count suggests, but the improvement is dramatic.

And the price? Resolution now depends on the segment length, not on the total recording. Two-second segments give bins every half a hertz, whether you recorded one minute or ten.

So there is a dial. Short segments, many of them, a very stable spectrum but blurry peaks. Long segments, sharp peaks but fewer to average and a noisier curve. For resting state EEG, segments of a few seconds are a common compromise. Choose it before looking at the results and report it.

One more option worth knowing. Instead of averaging the segment periodograms, you can take their median. A few segments hit by an artifact will barely move the median, while they can drag the mean. Both SciPy and MNE offer this choice.

Let's recap. A single periodogram is jagged, and recording longer does not help. Welch's method cuts the signal into overlapping, tapered segments and averages their periodograms. The variance drops with the number of segments. Resolution is set by the segment length. And the median is a robust alternative to the mean.

Next time, another way to tame the noise without cutting the signal at all. Multi-taper.

PLATE 3.73:22

Multitaper · Several windows, one spectrum

Multitaper keeps the whole segment and multiplies it by several orthogonal Slepian tapers, like a panel of judges watching from different seats. Each taper gives a nearly independent spectrum, and averaging them reduces variance. The half-bandwidth W sets both the smoothing and the number of tapers, about two times the time-bandwidth product minus one, so it suits short segments and broad rhythms.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welch tames a jagged spectrum by cutting the recording into short segments and averaging. But short segments cost resolution and some recordings are short to begin with. A two-second window after a stimulus, for example. Today, a different strategy that keeps the whole segment, Multitaper.

Think of a panel of judges. One judge alone gives a quirky score. A panel of judges, each watching the same performance from a different seat, gives a fair average. In multi-taper analysis, the performance is your whole segment, and each judge is a different window shape, called a taper.

The method was introduced by David Thomson in 1982. Instead of one Hann window, you multiply the same segment by several tapers, compute one spectrum per taper, and average the spectra. No cutting. Every taper sees all of the data.

Why does that help? Because the tapers are special, they are called Slepian sequences or discrete prolate spheroidal sequences. The first looks like a smooth bump, the second has one swing, the third two, and so on. They are orthogonal to each other, so each taper weighs the data differently and the spectra they give are nearly independent. Averaging nearly independent estimates reduces the variance, just like averaging Welch segments.

They are also optimal in a precise sense. Each Slepian taper concentrates as much of its spectral energy as possible inside a chosen frequency band and leaks as little as possible outside it. That chosen band is the key parameter, the half-bandwidth W in hertz.

Here is the bookkeeping. Multiply the segment length T by the half-bandwidth W and you get the time half bandwidth product. The number of useful tapers is about two times that product minus one. For a two-second segment and a half bandwidth of two hertz, the product is four, so about seven tapers. More smoothing buys more tapers, and more tapers buy a smoother spectrum.

The price is now explicit. The spectrum is smoothed over a band two W wide. A narrow peak becomes a plateau about 2W wide. With W at 2 Hz, an alpha peak at 10 Hz spreads from about 8 to 12. That is a lot for alpha. It is perfectly fine for gamma activity spread over many Hz.

So multi-taper shines when segments are short and when the rhythms of interest are broad, like high-frequency activity. Welch with long segments is often enough for narrow peaks in long resting recordings. In MNE, multi-taper is available both for spectra and for time frequency maps. Whatever you choose, report T, W, and the number of tapers.

Let's recap. Multi-taper keeps the whole segment and applies several orthogonal Slepian tapers. Each gives a nearly independent spectrum, and averaging them reduces the variance. The half-bandwidth W sets both the smoothing and the number of tapers, about 2T W minus 1. Use it for short segments and broad rhythms and choose W to match your question.

PLATE 3.83:17

The STFT and the spectrogram

The short-time Fourier transform slides a tapered window along the recording and computes one spectrum per position, like pages of a flip book. Stacked together, the columns form the spectrogram: time, frequency and power as color. Window length sets the time and frequency trade-off while the step only sets smoothness, and one fixed window cannot suit slow and fast rhythms at once.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A spectrum is a single photograph of a whole recording. But EEG changes. Alpha appears when the eyes close, a burst of beta comes and goes. To follow those changes, we need a movie, not a photograph. Today, the short-time Fourier transform and the picture it draws, the spectrogram.

Think of a flip book. Each page is a small drawing. Flip through the pages, and the drawing comes alive. The short-time Fourier transform makes one page per moment. A little spectrum of a short stretch of signal. Stack the pages side by side and you can watch the spectrum move.

Here is the recipe. Take a short window, say one second, with a taper such as Hann. Place it at the start of the recording, compute its spectrum. Slide it forward by a small step, compute again. Repeat until the end. Each spectrum becomes one column. Time runs from left to right, frequency from bottom to top, and color shows power, usually in decibels. That image is the spectrogram.

Watch it on a simulated recording. For the first four seconds the eyes are open and the 10 Hz band is quiet. Then the eyes close and a bright alpha stripe appears. Around nine seconds, a short burst of beta lights up near 20 Hz. The single spectrum of the whole recording would have shown both peaks and said nothing about when.

Three choices shape the picture. First, the window length. It sets the frequency resolution one over its length and the time blur its length. Second, the step between windows. Smaller steps give a smoother image, but they do not add resolution. Overlapping columns share most of their data. Third, the taper for the leakage reasons we saw in episode 3.5.

And here is the limitation that drives the next episode. The window is the same for every frequency. One second holds 10 cycles of alpha, which is comfortable. But it holds only one cycle of a 1 Hz delta wave, too few to estimate it well. And for a 40 Hz burst lasting 100 ms, one second is far too long. Its timing is smeared across the whole window. One window size cannot suit slow and fast rhythms at once.

A few practical notes. Every column of a spectrogram is just a windowed spectrum, so leakage and the time-frequency trade-off still apply. Raw power is dominated by the slow frequencies, which is why spectrograms are often shown in decibels or relative to a baseline, as we will see in Chapter 9. In Python, SciPy provides STFT and spectrogram functions. MNE has an STFT function too, though its time-frequency tools mostly rely on wavelets and multitapers.

Let's recap. The short time Fourier transform slides a tapered window along the signal and computes one spectrum per position. Stacked together they form the spectrogram, time, frequency, and power as color. The window length sets the trade-off between time and frequency, and the step only sets smoothness. And one fixed window cannot suit every frequency.

PLATE 3.94:11

Wavelets · Morlet and the number of cycles

A Morlet wavelet is a complex sinusoid inside a Gaussian bell, stretched or squeezed like an accordion to listen to each frequency. Convolving it with the signal gives power and phase over time. Its number of cycles is the one dial, since more cycles sharpen frequency and blur time, and cycles often grow with frequency.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The spectrogram uses one window size for every frequency. Slow rhythms get too few cycles, fast bursts get smeared. What if the window could stretch for slow rhythms and shrink for fast ones? That is exactly what wavelets do. Today, the Morlet wavelet, the most used wavelet in EEG, and the one parameter you must understand, its number of cycles.

Think of an accordion. Pull it open and it plays long, low notes. Squeeze it and it plays short high ones. A wavelet behaves the same way, the same shape stretched or squeezed to listen to each frequency.

A Morlet wavelet is a short wave packet, a sinusoid wrapped in a Gaussian bell. It is complex, a cosine as its real part and a sine as its imaginary part under the same bell. So just like the analytic signal of episode 3.10, it gives both an amplitude and a phase.

How do we use it? Pick a frequency, say 10 hertz, and build the Morlet wavelet at 10 hertz. Slide it along the signal and take the dot product at every position. That is a convolution from episode 2.4. The result is one complex number per time point. Its squared length is the power at 10 hertz, moment by moment. Its angle is the phase. Repeat for every frequency, stack the rows and you get a time frequency map.

Now the key parameter, the number of cycles inside the bell. The width of the bell in time is the number of cycles divided by 2 pi times the frequency. Its spread in frequency is the frequency divided by the number of cycles. More cycles a longer bell, sharper in frequency, blurrier in time. Fewer cycles a shorter bell, sharper in time, blurrier in frequency. The trade-off from episode 2.7, now with one dial.

Watch the same simulated recording analyzed twice. With three cycles, the short gamma burst at 6 seconds is beautifully localized in time, but every rhythm is smeared vertically and alpha looks like a fat band. With 10 cycles, the alpha band becomes a thin, clean stripe, but the gamma burst is stretched sideways and the beta burst blurs in time.

Notice something else. With a fixed number of cycles, the bell automatically gets shorter at higher frequencies. 10 cycles at 40 Hz last a quarter of a second. 10 cycles at 4 Hz last 2.5 seconds. That is the changing tile shape from episode 2.7, Narrow Tiles Up High, Wide Tiles Down Low.

So a common strategy is to let the number of cycles grow with frequency. For example, from about 3 cycles at the lowest frequencies to about 10 at the highest. MNE tutorials often set the number of cycles to the frequency divided by 2. This keeps reasonable timing for slow rhythms and reasonable frequency precision for fast ones. Mike X Cohen suggested an even clearer description, report the full width at half maximum of the bell in milliseconds and in hertz. A width in milliseconds is easy to compare across papers. Whatever you choose, report it.

A word on edges. A wavelet at 2 Hz with 5 cycles lasts several seconds. Near the start and the end of an epoch, it hangs over empty space. Those edge regions grow wider at low frequencies, a shape sometimes called the cone of influence. The cure is the same as before, cut longer epochs than you need and discard the edges after the transform.

Let's recap. A Morlet wavelet is a complex sinusoid inside a Gaussian bell. Convolving it with the signal gives power and phase over time at one frequency. Its number of cycles sets the trade off, more cycles, sharper frequency, blurrier time. Fixed cycles make the bell shorter at high frequencies, and many analyses let the cycles grow with frequency. Report the cycles or better, the width in milliseconds and trim the edges.

Next time we meet the other road to amplitude and phase, the Hilbert transform and the analytic signal.

PLATE 3.104:47

Hilbert and the analytic signal

The Hilbert transform shifts every frequency by a quarter cycle and supplies the missing vertical shadow of a real signal. Together they form the analytic signal, an arrow like a lighthouse beam whose length is the amplitude envelope and whose angle is the instantaneous phase. The phase is meaningful only for a narrowband signal, so filtering first is essential, and the edges are distorted.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is an alpha rhythm, filtered between 8 and 12 Hz. It swells, it fades, it swells again. At this exact moment, how strong is it? And where is it in its cycle? Fourier cannot tell us, it averages over the whole window. Today we build a tool that answers both questions at every single moment. The Hilbert transform and the analytic signal.

Picture a lighthouse at night. Its lamp turns steadily and its beam reaches further when the lamp is bright, shorter when it dims. The angle of the beam is the phase. The length of the beam is the envelope. Our goal, turn an EEG signal into that lighthouse.

Remember episode 2.2. A real oscillation is the shadow of a spinning arrow. But a real signal only gives us one shadow, the horizontal one. A real cosine is actually two arrows spinning in opposite directions, and their vertical shadows cancel. To get a single arrow spinning one way, we need the missing vertical shadow.

That missing shadow is the Hilbert transform. It takes the signal and shifts every frequency component by a quarter of a cycle. A cosine becomes a sine. A sine becomes minus a cosine. The timing of each rhythm is delayed by exactly 90 degrees, while its amplitude stays the same.

Now put the two together. The original signal becomes the real part. Its Hilbert transform becomes the imaginary part. The result is the analytic signal, a complex number at every moment in time. The arrow no longer flickers back and forth. It spins steadily in one direction like the lighthouse.

From that arrow everything follows. Its length is the amplitude envelope, how strong the rhythm is right now. Its angle is the instantaneous phase, where the rhythm is in its cycle right now. And how fast the angle turns gives the instantaneous frequency.

There is a second way to see the same thing in the frequency domain. Take the spectrum, delete every negative frequency, double the positive ones, and transform back. What comes out is the analytic signal. One arrow per rhythm, all spinning the same way. This is exactly how SciPy computes it, with a fast Fourier transform.

A practical warning. In SciPy, the function called Hilbert does not return the Hilbert transform. It returns the analytic signal. Take its absolute value for the envelope and its angle for the phase. The Hilbert transform itself is only the imaginary part.

Now the most important rule of this episode. The Hilbert transform only gives a meaningful phase if the signal is narrowband. One rhythm with an envelope that changes slowly compared with the oscillation itself.

Watch what happens with raw broadband EEG. Alpha, beta, slow drifts and muscle noise all mixed together. The arrow jumps around. The envelope is ragged. The phase hops from one value to another with no clear cycle. The mathematics still runs and still returns numbers. They just do not describe any single rhythm.

So the recipe is always the same. First, band pass filter around the rhythm you care about, for example 8 to 12 Hz for alpha. Then apply the Hilbert transform. The choice of filter, its width and its shape now matters a lot. That is the topic of the next episode.

One more trap, the edges. Because the computation goes through the Fourier transform, the start and the end of the recording are treated as if they were joined together. The first and last moments get distorted. The usual remedy is to compute on a longer stretch of data and throw away the edges.

Where will you meet this arrow again? The envelope gives you the amplitude dynamics of a rhythm, like alpha waxing and waning, which we will use for long-range temporal correlations in Chapter 12. The phase is the raw material for phase synchrony measures, like the phase locking value and the weighted phase lag index, in Chapter 10. And combining the phase of a slow rhythm with the envelope of a fast one gives phase amplitude coupling, in Chapter 9.

Let's recap. A real signal has only one shadow. The Hilbert transform builds the missing one, shifted by a quarter cycle. Together they form the analytic signal, a single arrow spinning one way. Its length is the envelope, its angle is the instantaneous phase. In frequency terms, drop the negative frequencies double the positive ones. Filter to a narrow band first and watch the edges.

Next time we compare two cousins, filtering plus Hilbert and complex wavelets. Different recipes very similar results.

PLATE 3.113:15

Filter–Hilbert vs wavelets · Cousins, not rivals

Bandpass filtering followed by the Hilbert transform and convolution with a complex Morlet wavelet both amount to applying a complex bandpass filter that keeps only positive frequencies, like two recipes for the same cake. With matched parameters their results are nearly identical. Filters give explicit control of band edges, wavelets cover many frequencies smoothly, and the parameters matter more than the method.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We now have two roads to the amplitude and phase of a rhythm. Road 1, bandpass filter the signal then apply the Hilbert transform. Road 2, convolve the signal with a complex Morlet wavelet. People sometimes argue about which one is better. Today we see that they are close cousins and when each one is the more convenient choice.

Think of two recipes for the same cake. One says sift the flour then add the sugar. The other says mix them together first. Different steps, the same cake on the table provided the quantities match.

Let's look at both roads in the frequency domain. A Morlet wavelet is a band pass filter. Its spectrum is a smooth Gaussian bump centered on its frequency with a width set by its number of cycles. And because it is complex with its sine and cosine a quarter cycle apart, it only responds to positive frequencies. Its output is already an analytic signal.

Now Road 1. The bandpass filter multiplies the spectrum by a bump around your band. The Hilbert step then deletes the negative frequencies and doubles the positive ones. The result is the same kind of object, the spectrum multiplied by a bump that lives only on the positive side.

So both roads do the same thing, they apply a complex bandpass filter. The only real difference is the shape of the bump. In a careful comparison, Andreas Bruns showed in 2004 that Fourier, Hilbert, and wavelet approaches are formally equivalent, and give nearly identical results when their parameters are matched.

Watch them on the same simulated alpha rhythm. A filter between 8 and 12 Hz followed by Hilbert and a 10 Hz Morlet wavelet with a matching width. The two envelopes almost lie on top of each other. So do the phases.

So when does each road shine? Filter plus Hilbert gives you explicit control of the band edges. If your hypothesis is about 8 to 12 Hz exactly or you want a flat passband with steep edges, design the filter you want. It is also the usual route for a single band, for example before computing phase synchrony.

Wavelets shine when you need many frequencies at once, often spaced logarithmically to draw a full-time frequency map. Their Gaussian shape is smooth and well-behaved, and their width scales naturally with frequency.

Either way, the parameters matter more than the name of the method. For a filter, its type, its band edges, its order or transition width. For a wavelet, its frequencies and its number of cycles or its width in milliseconds. Report them and two analyses become comparable.

Let's recap. Filter plus Hilbert and complex wavelets both apply a complex band pass filter that keeps only positive frequencies. With matched parameters, their results are nearly identical. Filters offer control of the band edges. Wavelets offer convenient, smooth coverage of many frequencies. Choose by convenience and report the parameters.

Next time we leave time and frequency for space, starting with a choice every EEG analysis makes, the reference.

PLATE 3.124:00

The reference is a space

Every channel is a difference, so the reference is a point of view, like moving the zero mark on a tide gauge. Re-referencing shifts all channels together, preserving electrode differences but changing waveforms and polarities. Cz, linked mastoids and the common average each assume something different, and the N170 shrinks as the reference nears the occiput, so the choice must be justified and reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every EEG channel is a difference. The amplifier compares one electrode with another, and there is no absolute zero anywhere on the head. So the choice of that other point, the reference, is not a technical detail. It is a choice of space, a point of view on the data.

Think of measuring altitude. A mountain's height is always measured from some zero, usually sea level. Move the zero mark on the tide gauge, and every altitude you read changes. The landscape itself does not move. The biomedical engineer David Geselowitz used almost exactly this image, a lake whose water level rises and falls over a mountain range.

Let's write it down. A recorded channel is the potential at the electrode minus the potential at the reference. Re-referencing means subtracting something new, the same something from every channel, at every instant. At each moment, the whole map shifts up or down together. The differences between electrodes stay exactly the same. What changes is where zero sits, and therefore which places look positive, which look negative, and how big each waveform appears.

Let's try three common choices on the same data, an illustrative evoked response at the back of the head.

First, a vertex reference, at Cz. Cz is set to zero by definition. Anything happening at the vertex disappears there, and shows up, inverted, in every other channel.

Second, linked mastoids, the average of the two electrodes behind the ears. A classic for evoked potentials, and it keeps you comparable with decades of studies. But the mastoids are not silent. They sit close to temporal and posterior sources, so effects there are partly subtracted away.

This is not a hypothetical worry. Carrie Joyce and Bruno Rossion re-referenced the same face responses to several references in 2005. The face sensitive N170, measured at occipito temporal sites, shrank as the reference moved closer to the back of the head, while its frontal mirror image, the VPP, grew. Same brains, same data, a different story at each electrode.

Third, the common average. Subtract the mean of all electrodes. The idea comes from physics: over a closed surface surrounding all the sources, the potential sums to zero. So with many electrodes covering the whole head, the average approaches a neutral reference.

But we only cover the top of the head. With sparse or uneven coverage, the average is biased, and the bias is largest at the edges of the montage. Markus Junghöfer and colleagues called this the polar average reference effect. And if one channel is noisy, averaging spreads its noise into all the others. Clean your bad channels first.

The reference also travels into every later measure. Whatever the reference picks up, alpha, muscle, a heartbeat, is now shared by every channel. Spectra inherit it, and connectivity can rise between all pairs for no other reason than a common reference.

So which one is right? None of them is the true zero. Each makes an assumption: a silent point, a silent pair, or a well-sampled sphere. Pick the one that fits your montage and your question, keep it consistent across conditions and participants, and report it. In MNE-Python, it takes a single line of code.

Let's recap. EEG has no absolute zero. Re-referencing shifts every channel by the same amount at each instant, so differences between electrodes are preserved, but waveforms, polarities and apparent effects change. Cz, linked mastoids and the average each assume something different. The reference is a point of view. Choose it, justify it, and report it.

Next time, we meet two tools that try to escape the reference altogether: the surface Laplacian, and REST, the reference at infinity.

PLATE 3.135:03

Surface Laplacian and REST

The surface Laplacian is the local curvature of the scalp potential, like asking how much the sea bulges against its neighbours. It is reference free, estimates radial current, and acts as a spatial high pass filter that cuts volume conduction but attenuates broad sources. REST instead uses a head model to rebuild a reference at infinity, and is only as good as that model.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that every reference is a point of view, and that none of them is a true zero. Today, two tools that try to step out of that trap. The surface Laplacian, which gets rid of the reference altogether. And REST, which tries to rebuild a reference at infinity.

Picture the sea. A long, slow swell lifts the whole surface, and on top of it ride small, sharp ripples. Now imagine you only care about the ripples. You do not ask how high the water is. You ask, at each point, how much the surface bulges compared with its immediate neighbours. That question does not care about the water level, and it barely notices the swell.

That is the surface Laplacian. Mathematically, it is the second spatial derivative of the potential along the scalp: its local curvature. The simplest version, proposed by Bo Hjorth in 1975, takes each electrode and subtracts the mean of its nearest neighbours. Modern versions fit a smooth surface through all electrodes, usually with spherical splines, following François Perrin and colleagues in 1989, and compute the curvature of that surface.

Why is it reference free? Re-referencing adds the same value to every electrode at a given instant. A constant has no curvature. So whatever the original reference was, the Laplacian comes out the same.

And what does curvature mean physically? It estimates the current flowing radially through the skull and into the scalp, at each location. That is why it is also called current source density, or C S D. Where current leaves the head, we see a source. Where it enters, a sink.

Watch what it does to a topography. Here is an illustrative map of two nearby sources under a broad smear. The potential map shows one wide blob. The Laplacian map shows two sharp, separate spots. It acts as a spatial high pass filter: it keeps local detail and removes broad patterns.

That is a strength and a limit. Volume conduction spreads activity widely across the scalp, so a spatial high pass filter removes much of it. Neighbouring electrodes become more independent, which makes the Laplacian a good friend of connectivity analyses. But truly broad activity, from deep or very widespread generators, is attenuated too. The Laplacian favours superficial, focal sources.

A few practical rules. It needs good electrode density, and estimates at the edge of the montage are less reliable. It is sensitive to noisy channels, because a bad electrode corrupts the curvature of all its neighbours. And its units change, from microvolts to microvolts per square metre. In MNE-Python, it is computed by compute current source density.

Now the second tool: REST, the reference electrode standardization technique, published by Dezhong Yao in 2001. The idea is to measure every electrode against a point infinitely far away, a point where the brain's potential is truly zero.

Back at sea, it is like measuring wave heights not from a buoy that bobs on the swell, but from a fixed point far away, out at the horizon.

How can we reach infinity? With a head model. We assume the scalp data were produced by sources inside the head, through a known lead field. We estimate equivalent sources from the data as recorded, then project them back onto the electrodes as they would appear against infinity. Note that we never need to trust those sources individually. They are only a bridge.

The catch is that REST is only as good as its head model and its coverage. Errors in conductivities, positions or electrode density all leak into the result. In noise free simulations with a realistic head model, it can beat the common average. But in a 2015 comparison by Liu and colleagues, that advantage vanished when electrode positions were imprecise, and with a dense montage the average did better. In MNE-Python, it is a reference option that takes a forward model.

So which one, when? Use the Laplacian when you want sharp, local, reference free maps, or connectivity with less volume conduction. Use REST, or the average, when you want potentials you can compare with the rest of the literature, and broad patterns matter. Many labs report both potentials and their Laplacian.

Let's recap. The surface Laplacian is the local curvature of the scalp potential. It is reference free, it estimates radial current, and it acts as a spatial high pass filter that reduces volume conduction but also attenuates broad sources. REST rebuilds a reference at infinity using a head model, and it is only as good as that model.

Next time, we zoom out: sensors, sources and components are all just different projections of the same data.

PLATE 3.144:36

Sensor space, source space, component space

Sensors, sources and components are different projections of one channels by time matrix. A spatial filter is a recipe of weights over electrodes, like mixing juices, and covers references, Laplacians, ICA, beamformers and CSP. A pattern, how a source shows on electrodes, differs from a filter and is what to interpret, because no projection adds information beyond the data's rank.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have met many spaces in this chapter. Today we close it with a simple idea that ties a whole family of them together. Sensors, sources and components are not different data. They are different projections of the same data.

Start with what we record: a matrix. One row per electrode, one column per time sample. Sixty four rows, maybe a few hundred thousand columns. Every method in this episode starts from that matrix, and multiplies it by another one.

Picture a juice bar. Each electrode is a jar of juice. A new channel is a recipe: so much from this jar, so much from that one, and sometimes a negative amount, which means taking some away. That recipe is a spatial filter. A list of weights, one per electrode. Multiply, add up, and you get one new time series.

You have already used spatial filters without calling them that. Re-referencing to the average is a recipe: with sixty four electrodes, each channel minus one sixty-fourth of every channel. A bipolar montage is a recipe with two ingredients, plus one and minus one. And the Hjorth Laplacian from last episode: the centre electrode, minus a quarter of each of its four neighbours. Stack several recipes as rows, and you have a matrix that turns sensor space into another space.

Now the reverse direction. Suppose there is a source, somewhere, with its own time course. Through volume conduction, it reaches every electrode, with a different strength at each. That set of strengths is the source's pattern, its fingerprint on the scalp. The recorded data are, to a good approximation, a weighted sum of patterns, each multiplied by its source's time course, plus noise.

So there are two kinds of maps, and they are easy to confuse. A pattern tells you how a source shows up on the electrodes. A filter tells you how to combine the electrodes to recover the source. They are not the same. A good filter often gives large weights to electrodes far from the source, simply to cancel noise. Stefan Haufe and colleagues made this point in 2014: to interpret a linear model, look at its patterns, not at its filter weights. Patterns can be computed from the filters and the data covariance.

Three famous families are just three ways of choosing the recipes.

Independent component analysis, in chapter seven, chooses filters so that the outputs are as statistically independent as possible. Its unmixing matrix holds the filters. The columns of its inverse, the mixing matrix, hold the patterns, the topographies you inspect to spot a blink.

Source imaging, in chapter thirteen, uses a head model. A beamformer, for instance, builds one filter per brain location, designed to pass that location and suppress everything else. Minimum norm methods build a whole matrix at once that maps sixty four electrodes to thousands of points on the cortex.

Common spatial patterns, in chapter fourteen, chooses filters whose output variance is high in one condition and low in the other, like left hand versus right hand imagery. Despite its name, what it optimises are filters. The patterns come afterwards.

One rule holds for all of them. A projection cannot create information. If you have sixty four electrodes, there are at most sixty four independent directions in your data, and fewer after an average reference or an interpolated channel. Thousands of source points are an estimate drawn from those few dimensions. They do not hold more information than the sixty four channels they came from. That is why spatial resolution in source space has limits, and why data rank matters so much for ICA.

So when you meet a new method, ask three questions. What are its weights chosen to do? Where do you read its patterns? And how many dimensions really go in?

Let's recap. The data are a channels by time matrix. A spatial filter is a weighted sum of electrodes, and a matrix of filters moves you into another space: a reference, a Laplacian, components, sources. A pattern is how a source projects onto the electrodes, and it is what you interpret. ICA, beamformers and CSP are different recipes for choosing the weights. And no projection adds information.
