Moving earth

A spike at ten hertz is as far from eleven as from thirty bin by bin, so Monge's 1781 question asks what it costs to carry mass between distributions, weight times distance. Three heaps and three holes give six plans, four costing nine and two fifteen. Now ten to eleven hertz costs one and ten to thirty costs twenty, but a heap cannot be cut.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last chapter ended on a trap. A spike at ten hertz is exactly as far from a spike at eleven hertz as from a spike at thirty. Bin by bin, they are simply in different bins. The distance knows nothing about where the bins are.

This chapter fixes that with a different idea. Do not compare the shelves. Ask a question about work. How much does it cost to turn one distribution into the other, by carrying mass from place to place?

The question is old. In seventeen eighty-one, the French geometer Gaspard Monge wrote a memoir on cut and fill. In French, déblais and remblais. The job was practical: earth for fortifications. Dig it here, and use it to build up there. What is the cheapest way to carry it?

Here is his problem in its smallest form. A heap of earth. A hole of exactly the same volume. Carrying a shovelful costs its weight times the distance it travels. Find the way of sending every shovelful that costs the least in total.

Notice the unit. Weight times distance. If the ground is measured in metres, the answer is in metres of earth. This distance will always speak the language of its ground.

Now a line of ground, with numbers on it. Three heaps, one unit each, at positions zero, one and five. Three holes, one unit each, at positions two, six and seven. In Monge's version, each heap goes whole into one hole.

How many ways are there? Three choices for the first heap, two for the second, one for the last. Six ways. Let us price them.

A plan is just a list. This heap goes to that hole, the next heap goes to the next hole. To judge one plan, you add up its price tags. To find the best plan, you have to compare plans. That is a search, and a search has a cost of its own.

Send the heaps to two, six and seven, in that order. Two, plus five, plus two. Nine. Send zero to seven, one to six, and five to two. Seven, plus five, plus three. Fifteen.

Go through all six. Four of them cost nine. Two of them cost fifteen. The cheapest plan costs nine, and four plans tie for it.

Six ways is easy. But the count grows like a factorial. With ten heaps and ten holes, there are three million, six hundred twenty-eight thousand, eight hundred ways. With twenty, there are more than two billion billion. Nobody tries them all. We will come back to how to avoid that, in the next three episodes.

Now put distributions on the ground. A histogram is a heap. Its bins are places, and its heights are earth. Two histograms that each sum to one are a heap and a hole of the same volume.

That is why the totals must match. A heap and a hole of different volumes cannot be paired off, and the bookkeeping would be meaningless.

Remember the spikes of episodes four point five, five point one and five point seven. A spike at ten hertz, one at eleven, one at thirty. The ground is the frequency axis, in hertz.

To turn the ten-hertz spike into the eleven-hertz spike, carry one unit of mass by one hertz. The cost is one. To turn it into the thirty-hertz spike, carry it by twenty hertz. The cost is twenty.

Bin by bin said one, and one. Moving mass says one, and twenty. For the first time, a distance between distributions says that eleven is nearer than thirty.

Real peaks are not one bin wide. Take bells, three tenths of a hertz wide, as in episode five point one. Total variation says point nine when the bells are one hertz apart. It says one when they are twenty hertz apart. It has saturated. Moving mass says one, and then twenty. It keeps growing with the gap.

Picture the two curves as the gap grows. Total variation climbs to one, and then it lies flat. Moving mass is a straight line, rising for as long as the bells move apart.

What changed? Not the masses. We needed a distance between the bins themselves. We call it the ground distance. Bin by bin never had one. Its bins were names. Now they are places. That is the whole difference between this chapter and the last one.

Back to the row of changes from chapter five. Same simulated signal, same histogram of amplitudes, twelve bins from minus three to three. The ground distance is the amplitude, in the signal's own units.

Shifted by half a unit: every bin moves by exactly one bin, so the distance is point five. Total variation said point two four, a number with no unit. Amplified by one point six: point three four. Delayed: zero. Shuffled: zero. Noisy: point zero four.

The two zeros are still there. They are not the distance's fault. The histogram threw the order of time away before any distance was applied. But the size of the change now shows, and it comes in the unit of the signal.

Now the limit. In Monge's version, every heap goes whole into a single hole. Take one heap of one unit, at zero. Take two holes of half a unit each, at one and at three.

Send the heap to the first hole. It overflows, and the second hole stays empty. Send it to the second hole. The same thing happens. There is no way to send it. The problem has no solution at all.

The best plan would cut the heap in two. Monge's version cannot. Nineteen forty-two, Leonid Kantorovich: let the heaps be cut. That is the next episode.

One more thing, and it is the point of this one. This distance between distributions stands on another distance, the one between places. It knows that two bins are neighbours only because we told it. Everything it answers depends on that ground distance, and the ground distance is a choice.

Take two pairs of spikes. Ten and eleven hertz. Thirty and thirty-two hertz. In hertz, the first pair costs one and the second costs two, so ten and eleven are nearer.

Now measure the ground in octaves, where a doubling of frequency is one step. Ten to eleven is point one four. Thirty to thirty-two is point zero nine. Now thirty and thirty-two are the nearer pair.

Same spikes. Same masses. Another ground, another answer. On a scalp, the ground could be centimetres through the air, or centimetres along the cortex. The distance cannot choose for you. Moving earth, then, is a distance built on top of another distance. The first one is only as sensible as the second.

In one line: heaps and holes on a ground; the cheapest total of weight times distance, with every heap whole; it ignores what the ground distance ignores; it costs a search through a factorial number of plans; and its trap is that a heap cannot be cut. The four rules wait for next time.

The best plan

Kantorovich let heaps be cut in 1942, so a plan becomes a table whose rows add up to the first histogram and columns to the second. On three bins, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 against 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, the cheapest plan costs 0.6, where total variation says 0.3. Stretch the axis tenfold and it costs six: the answer carries the ground unit.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, Monge gave us a rule. Every heap of earth goes whole into a single hole. That rule has a limit. Take one heap, and two holes that are each half its size. Then there is no solution at all.

Today the rule changes. In nineteen forty-two, the Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich allowed the earth to be cut. A heap may now send a share here, and another share there.

Let us rebuild our running example. Three bins, at positions one, two and three. The first histogram holds point five, point three and point two. That is our earth. The second histogram holds point two, point three and point five. Those are our holes. This data is illustrative.

A solution is no longer a list of shipments. It is a table. One row for each bin we leave, and one column for each bin we arrive at. In each cell, the mass that makes that trip.

Only two things are asked of the table. The rows must add up to the first histogram. The columns must add up to the second. Every grain leaves, and every hole is filled. Think of the rows as what each bin has to give away, and the columns as what each bin has to receive. The table is the bookkeeping of the whole job.

Many tables pass that test. Here is a lazy one. Every row spreads its mass over the columns in the same proportions. It is a valid plan. Here is another one. Every heap goes to the far side. It is valid too. So which table is the best?

To decide, we need a price. A trip costs the mass that travels, multiplied by the distance on the ground. This second table holds those ground distances. Between bin one and bin three, the ground distance is two.

We multiply each cell of the plan by its ground distance, and we add everything up. The lazy plan costs one point zero. The plan that crosses over costs one point four.

Now look for the cheapest. Mass that stays put travels a distance of zero, so it is free. Keep what can stay. Point two stays in bin one, point three stays in bin two, and point two stays in bin three. Only point three has to travel, from bin one to bin three. Point three, times a distance of two. Total cost: point six.

That is the best we can do. A solver agrees, and so does a second solver. Another table ties with it: send point three from bin one to bin two, then point three from bin two to bin three. Also point six. So the best plan is not always unique. The best cost always is.

Compare this with total variation, from chapter five. It said point three. It counted how much mass has to move, and never asked how far. The transport distance says point six: the mass that moves, times the distance it travels.

Now exchange the positions of bins two and three. Total variation does not care about positions. It still says point three. But the transport changes. The same table now has a trip from bin one to bin three that is only one step long. The cost drops to point three.

Stretch the axis ten times. Positions ten, twenty and thirty. Total variation still says point three. The transport says six point zero. The plan is the same, and only the ground distances were multiplied by ten, so the bill is ten times larger. The distance knows where the bins are, because we told it.

Do the four rules still hold? First, a cost is a mass times a distance, so it can never be negative. Second, it is zero only when nothing has to move, so only for a histogram and itself. Third, reverse every trip, and the plan reads the same way backwards: the same both ways. Fourth, a detour through a third histogram is never cheaper than the direct plan. Chain the two plans, and you get a plan for the whole way.

All four hold, with one condition. The ground distance must itself be a distance. If two different bins sat at ground distance zero, then moving all the mass from one to the other would cost nothing. Two different histograms would be at distance zero. We computed exactly that. On random histograms, with a proper ground distance, two thousand triples showed no broken triangle.

You can also charge the square of the distance. At the end, you take the root of the total. The price changes the plan. A long jump of two now costs four. Sending point three that far costs one point two. Two short hops of one cost only point six. So the best plan now relays through the middle bin. The distance is the root of point six: point seven seven.

This distance has many names. In probability, Wasserstein, or Kantorovich and Rubinstein, or Mallows. In computer vision, the earth mover's distance, from Rubner, Tomasi and Guibas, in two thousand. It was found more than once. The name Wasserstein is the most common. It is said to come from a nineteen seventy paper by Dobrushin, about the work of Vaserstein. Specialists find the name questionable, and it has stuck. So whenever you read one of these names, ask the same two questions. What is the ground distance, and what is the cost of a trip?

What does it ignore? Only what the ground distance ignores. Here is our row of changes, on the amplitudes of the simulated signal from chapter five. The ground distance is the amplitude itself, in signal units.

Shifted by half a unit: point five. Exactly the shift. Amplified by one point six: point three four. Delayed: zero. Shuffled: zero. Noisy: point zero four. Both zeros are deliberate. The histogram forgot the order of time before any distance looked at it.

Now the trap. The answer comes in the unit of the ground distance. Remember the peaks at ten, eleven and thirty hertz. Total variation gave the same answer for ten against eleven, and for ten against thirty. The transport tells them apart. Ten and eleven hertz are one hertz apart. Ten and thirty are twenty hertz apart. Measured in octaves, those become point one four, and one point five eight. Same peaks, two sets of numbers, and both are correct. A transport distance without its unit means nothing.

There is a second trap. The two totals must be equal. A spectrum divided by its sum has thrown away its total power. A peak of power four and the same peak with power one become identical, and the distance cannot tell them apart. Episode eight returns to this.

What does it cost? That is the subject of episode four.

So here is the card. The object is a histogram on a ground. The distance is the cheapest plan. It ignores only what the ground ignores. Its cost comes later. Its trap is the unit, and the totals.

And here is the best part. The plan is worth as much as the number. It says which mass went where. This is a distance that arrives with its explanation.

On a line, just sort

On a line the cheapest plan needs no search: swapping crossing cords never hurts, so sorting both samples and pairing by rank is optimal. Of the 720 pairings of six against six, the sorted one cost 5.8, an average gap of 0.97. Two bells at means zero and three, spreads one and two, are 3.16 apart. It only works on a line.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we found the best plan: a table that says how much mass goes from each place to each place. Finding it is a search. But on a line, there is nothing to search. You sort.

Picture six piles of sand on a line, and six holes. Every pile holds the same amount, and so does every hole. Each pile is tied to a hole by a cord. Positions run from zero to ten.

Here is a first pairing, chosen badly. The cords cross each other, and the total length of the six cords is nineteen point eight.

Now take two cords that cross. Untie them, and swap their ends. There are two ways to join the same four points. The crossed way is never shorter than the other one. So a swap never hurts.

Swap again, and again. The total goes from nineteen point eight to thirteen point four, then ten point four, seven point six, six point six, and five point eight. When no two cords cross, you stop.

Look at what is left. The leftmost pile goes to the leftmost hole. The second pile goes to the second hole. And so on. That is the sorted pairing: sort both lists, then match them by rank.

Is it really the best one? With six against six, we can check. There are seven hundred and twenty ways to pair them, and we measured every one. The worst has length twenty-nine point two. The middle one, nineteen point six. The cheapest is five point eight, and it is the sorted one. Only one of the seven hundred and twenty has no crossing, and it is that one.

So the distance is simply the average gap between values of the same rank. Five point eight, divided by six: zero point nine seven. No bins. No parameter. And the answer is in the unit of your data. This is for two samples of the same size. With different sizes you compare quantiles instead, and the idea does not change.

You have met this number before. In the episode on Kolmogorov and Smirnov, we drew two staircases of cumulative sums. Kolmogorov and Smirnov measured the tallest gap between the two staircases. That is a height.

Transport measures the area between them. Cut that area into vertical strips, and you add up gaps between the cumulative curves. Cut it into horizontal strips, and each strip is the gap between two values of the same rank. Same area, same number: zero point nine seven.

The peaks of the last episodes show why this matters. A peak at ten hertz, and a peak at eleven. The tallest gap between their staircases is one. The area is one. Now a peak at thirty. The tallest gap is still one. The area is twenty.

Height says: both pairs are as different as can be. Area says: eleven is close, thirty is far. The area knows how far the mass has to travel. But notice what it relies on: the gap between ten and eleven is a choice. Measured in hertz it is one. Measured in octaves it would be different, and so would every answer. The line is yours to choose.

Now two bell curves. This time, let each pile pay the square of the distance it travels, and take the square root of the average at the end. For two bells there is a shortcut. The square of the distance is the square of the gap between the means, plus the square of the gap between the spreads.

Take a bell with mean zero and spread one, and another with mean three and spread two. Nine plus one is ten. The square root is three point one six. We checked by sorting two thousand points from each: three point one three.

A shape moved by three is at distance three, exactly, because every sorted gap equals three. And two bells with the same centre, and spreads one and two, are at distance one. Kolmogorov and Smirnov said zero point one six, because their tallest gap is small even though one bell is twice as wide.

In several dimensions, there is still a formula for two bells. It involves their covariance matrices, and it goes back to Dowson and Landau, and to Olkin and Pukelsheim, in nineteen eighty-two. It is a door to chapter eight.

Now the row of changes, on the signal of episode five point one, through its twelve bins. The distance between two bins is the gap between their centres, in signal units.

Shifted: zero point five. That is the shift itself, in the unit of the signal. Total variation said zero point two four, in no unit at all. Amplified: zero point three four. Delayed: zero. Shuffled: zero. Noisy: zero point zero four.

Look at the two zeros. They come from the histogram, not from transport. A histogram forgets the order in time before any distance sees it. Sort the four thousand raw samples, with no bins at all, and you read zero point five, zero point three six, zero, zero, and zero point zero five.

What does it cost? A sort. Two samples of a thousand points: about thirty-five microseconds on this machine. A million against a million: about sixty-five milliseconds. No plan to search for, and no table to store. A sort is what a laptop does all day.

Now the trap. This works on a line, and only on a line. In two dimensions, nothing is sorted. Take eight points against eight in a square. Pair them by horizontal position, and the average cord is zero point four. Pair them by vertical position, and it is zero point two eight. The best pairing, found by searching, gives zero point two two. Two orders, two answers, and neither one is right.

There is a second trap, with the squared cost. Take a hundred values, and push the largest one thirty units higher. With the straight cost, the distance is zero point three. With the squared cost, it is three. Ten times more, from a single point.

Let us fill in the card, line by line. The object: two samples on a line. The distance: the average gap between values of the same rank, which is the area between the two staircases. What it ignores: only what the ground distance ignores. Its answer is no more than what you told it about the line. What it costs: a sort. Its trap: it only works on a line, and with the squared cost, one far point weighs heavily.

The four rules hold: never negative, zero only for the same sample, the same in both directions, and no shortcut. They hold because, on a line, the ground distance is itself a distance.

On a line, optimal transport is a sort. No bins, no parameter, and the answer in the unit of the data. For latencies, for intervals between beats, for amplitudes, it is the cheapest serious distance between distributions.

What it costs

Exact transport has two bills. With an exact solver, one thousand points take 0.04 seconds and sixteen thousand take twenty, and the table needs n times n cells. Two samples of one distribution also sit at a floor: 0.1 at one hundred points in a square, 0.73 in ten dimensions, where a real shift of 0.3 measures 0.62.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, moving mass on a line turned out to be a sort. Put both samples in order, pair the first with the first, the second with the second, and you are done.

Off the line, there is no sorting. Two clouds of points in a plane have no left and no right. To find the cheapest plan, you have to search.

So what does the exact answer cost? It comes with two bills. One is paid in computing. The other is paid in data.

Let us open the first bill.

Take n points in one cloud and n points in the other. A plan sends each point to exactly one partner. Ten points already give three million six hundred twenty-eight thousand eight hundred ways to pair them. Twenty points give more than two billion billion.

Nobody tries them all. In nineteen fifty-five, Harold Kuhn published the Hungarian method, which finds the best pairing without trying them all. Since then, exact methods have kept improving, but the best guarantees known for the general problem still grow like the cube of the number of points, with a logarithm on top.

The cube means this: ten times more points, a thousand times more time.

So we measured it. The solver is the exact one from the POT library, a network simplex. The ground distance is the straight line. All timings are measured here, on this machine, best of several runs.

One thousand points against one thousand points: four hundredths of a second. Two thousand: two tenths of a second. Four thousand: one second. Eight thousand: four point four seconds. Sixteen thousand: twenty seconds.

Look at the slope. Each time the number of points doubles, the time is multiplied by about four and a half. That is a power of about two point two. On these sizes, it is steeper than a square, and gentler than the cube.

The cube is a guarantee for the worst case. It is not a promise for every cloud. But the trend is clear: the cost grows much faster than the data.

And there is a second wall, which is memory.

The solver reads a table. One row per point in the first cloud, one column per point in the second, and in each cell, the distance between the two. That table has n times n cells.

At sixteen thousand points, it is two thousand megabytes. At one hundred thousand points, it is ten billion cells. In eight-byte numbers, that is eighty gigabytes. This machine has sixty-four.

We did not run it. If the measured trend simply continued, one hundred thousand points would take about twenty minutes. At the pace of the cube, about an hour and twenty. These are extrapolations, not measurements. The table does not fit anyway.

Compare with the sort. One million values against one million values, on a line: six hundredths of a second, measured here.

That is the first bill. Now the second one.

Draw two samples from the very same distribution. Points scattered evenly in a square, a hundred pink ones and a hundred blue ones. They come from the same source, so the true distance is zero.

But the samples are not the same. Pink points land here, blue points land there. To turn one sample into the other, mass still has to move.

Connect each pink point to its blue partner by the best plan. The yarn is never zero. Its average length is the floor.

With one hundred points, the floor is zero point one. With one thousand points, it is zero point zero three seven. Ten times more data, a floor about three times lower.

Now go to ten columns. The same experiment, in a ten-dimensional cube. With one hundred points, the floor is zero point seven three. With one thousand points, zero point five four six.

Ten times more data, and the floor drops by a quarter.

How big is that floor? In this cube, two points picked at random are about one point two seven apart. The floor of zero point five five is forty-three percent of that. Two samples of the same distribution look nearly half as far apart as two random points.

It is a law, not an accident. Once the dimension passes two, the floor falls like the number of points raised to the power minus one over the dimension. The law goes back to Dudley, in nineteen sixty-nine. Fournier and Guillin gave a full proof in twenty fifteen.

In the square, the dimension is two, and the floor falls like one over the square root of n. In ten dimensions, it falls like n to the minus one tenth.

On our measurements, the slope in the square is minus zero point four five, and in the cube minus zero point one two five. To halve the floor in the square, you need about five times more points. In the cube, the measured slope says about two hundred fifty times more. The law, in the long run, says a thousand times.

Now a real difference. In the ten-dimensional cube, shift one cloud by zero point three along a single column. The true distance is exactly zero point three.

With one thousand points per cloud, the measured distance is zero point six two. The floor alone was zero point five five. The real shift hides almost entirely beneath the floor.

What saves the situation, often, is that the number of columns is not what counts. What counts is the dimension of the cloud itself. Weed and Bach showed in twenty nineteen that the floor follows this hidden dimension.

We tested it. Take a flat sheet, and lay it inside ten columns, with a little noise in every one. With one hundred points the floor is zero point one one. With one thousand points, zero point zero five.

That is close to the flat square, and nowhere near the cube. Ten columns, but only about two dimensions of real structure.

Now the card.

The object: two clouds of points, or two histograms on a ground. The distance: the cheapest plan. What it ignores: only what the ground distance ignores. What it costs: a table with n times n cells, and a time that grew here like n to the power two point two. Its trap: in many dimensions, it mostly measures that the two samples are sparse.

All four rules hold, as long as the ground distance is itself a distance.

Remember the floor from episode five point six? A distance between two samples of the same thing is never zero. Here the floor is far higher, and it falls very slowly.

So this is the most faithful distance to the geometry, and also the most expensive. It is expensive in computing, and expensive in data.

Blur the plan

Blurring the transport plan with an entropy bonus, scaled by epsilon, lets a loop of row and column corrections find it: thirteen turns at epsilon one, 54 at 0.03. The plan costs 0.695 instead of 0.6. The trap: a histogram against itself costs 0.398. Subtracting half of each self-score repairs the zero, but the triangle broke in 731 of 3,000 triples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we priced the best plan. Moving mass exactly is the most faithful way to compare two histograms, and it is also the most expensive.

The next three episodes are three ways to pay less. Today's way is to blur the plan.

Here is our small problem again. Two histograms on three bins, at positions 1, 2 and 3. The first is 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. The second is the same numbers in the other order. The ground distance is the gap between positions.

The best plan is a table. One row for each place the mass starts, one column for each place it ends, and in each cell the mass that makes that trip. The rows must add up to the first histogram. The columns must add up to the second.

The cheapest table leaves 0.2, 0.3 and 0.2 where they are, and sends 0.3 from bin 1 to bin 3. The cost is 0.6.

Now look closer. Sending that 0.3 in two hops, from 1 to 2 and then from 2 to 3, costs exactly the same. There is a whole family of equally cheap tables, and a solver hands you one of them, usually a corner of the family.

In 2013, Marco Cuturi proposed to stop insisting on a corner. Add a bonus for plans that are spread out.

How spread out is a table? Measure its entropy. The more cells share the mass, the higher it is. The corner table has 1.97 bits.

The bonus is multiplied by one number, epsilon. That number is the blur. Epsilon is in the unit of the cost, and it multiplies the entropy taken with the natural logarithm, so that the formulas stay simple. The bits on the screen are only there to read the spread.

When epsilon is small, the blurred plan is almost an exact plan. Among all the equally cheap tables, it picks the most spread one: 0.15 in each of four cells, 2.57 bits.

With epsilon equal to 1, the plan puts a little mass everywhere. Its entropy is 2.78 bits, and it costs 0.695 instead of 0.6.

How do you find that plan? With a loop so simple that you can run it by hand.

Start from a table of kernel values. Each cell is e to the minus its trip cost, divided by epsilon. Cheap trips start large.

First correct the rows. Scale each row so that it adds up to the first histogram. Now the columns are wrong.

Then correct the columns. Scale each column so that it adds up to the second histogram. Now the rows are a little wrong again, but less than before.

Repeat. One row step and one column step make a turn.

Watch the row error. It falls by a factor of about five at every turn. After 13 turns, it is below one billionth, and we stop.

The loop is old. Statisticians have adjusted tables to known row and column totals this way since the 1940s, from Deming and Stephan. That it always lands was proved by Sinkhorn in 1964, and by Sinkhorn and Knopp in 1967. That is why the loop carries his name.

Now turn the knob. With epsilon equal to 1, 13 turns and a cost of 0.695. With epsilon equal to 0.1, 29 turns, and the cost is 0.6. With epsilon equal to 0.03, 54 turns.

A smaller blur gives a sharper plan, and a longer loop.

Why would anyone want this plan? Because every step is a table times a vector, then a division. No search, nothing to branch on. Those are the operations that graphics cards do best, and they can be differentiated, so a model can learn against this distance. That is the argument of Genevay, Peyré and Cuturi.

We measured it here, on a busy computer, with plain numpy on the processor, no graphics card. For 1,000 points against 1,000, the exact solver takes 0.08 second and the loop 0.06. For 5,000 against 5,000, the exact solver takes 3.2 seconds and the loop 1.4. The loop's plan costs about 4 percent more, 0.444 against 0.428. That is the price of the blur.

Now the trap. Keep epsilon equal to 1, and ask for the cost between the first histogram and itself.

It should be zero. It is 0.398.

The blurred plan spreads the mass even when nothing needs to move, and it pays for that spread. The zero rule has fallen.

The repair comes from Genevay, Peyré and Cuturi, in 2018. Compute the blurred score between the two histograms. Then subtract half of the score of each histogram against itself. The score is the cost of the plan, minus epsilon times its entropy.

Between a histogram and itself, the result is exactly zero again. For our pair, at epsilon equal to 1, it is 0.226.

The repair costs two more loops. And it does not restore everything. On 3,000 random triples of three-bin histograms, the triangle rule broke in 731 of them. The square root of the repaired value did not break in that test, but we have no proof of it.

Epsilon is a slider between two chapters. Turn it down, and the repaired value climbs toward exact transport: 0.592 at epsilon 0.01. Turn it up, and it settles at 0.18.

That number is half the squared energy distance of the two histograms. It is built from average distances between points, how far apart the two histograms are on average, minus how spread each one is on its own. That is a kernel distance, the kind of chapter 7. Feydy and colleagues showed this link in 2019.

So at one end of the slider, you move mass. At the other, you compare averages of similarities.

The same holds for spectra. A peak at ten hertz and a peak at thirty hertz average, by transport, to a peak at twenty. Is a peak at twenty hertz a real thing? Only if thirty is the ten, moved. That is the ground distance of episode six point one. It is a choice, and the average inherits it.

So every distance has its own average. The bin by bin average answers one question: which mixture of the two? The transport average answers another: what lies halfway? Before you average histograms, choose the distance, and say it.

Exact transport is faithful, and it is expensive. Episode 6.4 showed the bill: a plan to search for, and a floor that falls slowly when the dimension grows.

Today we pay less, with one idea. Never move mass in many dimensions. Look at shadows instead.

Picture a turntable with two clumps of clay beads on it. The blue clump is one cloud of points. The pink clump is another cloud, shifted three units to the right. They are drawn here in a plane, but they could live in ten dimensions, or a hundred.

Now switch on a lamp, and let the beads cast their shadows on a wall. On the wall, everything is on a line. And on a line, you already know the answer from episode 6.3: transport is a sort.

So sort the blue shadows from left to right. Sort the pink shadows the same way. Pair the first with the first, the second with the second, and so on. Pay the squared gap of each pair, and take the average. That is one slice.

One lamp sees only one shadow. So turn the lamp. A new angle gives a new wall, a new sort, and a new number. The values here are simulated, with seed sixty seven. At zero degrees the two clumps look far apart. At ninety degrees, they almost overlap.

Two different clouds can share one shadow. But they cannot share every shadow, from every side. So we look from many sides.

How do we pick a direction at random? Draw one random number per axis, and rescale the arrow to length one. That is the recipe of episode 2.6.

Now pick many angles at random, and average the squared values. Take the root, to get back to the unit of the data. This is the sliced distance.

Casting shadows of a cloud has an old name in mathematics: the Radon transform, from 1917. It is the mathematics behind the CT scanner, which also turns around its object. Rabin, Peyré, Delon and Bernot used this idea to average distributions in 2011. Bonneel and colleagues developed it in 2015.

Now the distance card. The object: two clouds of points, in any number of dimensions. The distance: the root of the average, over random directions, of the squared sorted gap. What it ignores: what the ground distance ignores, and in each slice, everything the lamp does not see. What it costs: one sort per slice. Its trap: the number of slices, and its scale. We come back to both. In the ignores line, one slice alone is blind to every direction across its lamp. That is why the eye closes.

And the four rules? Bonnotte proved them in his thesis in 2013. Each slice is a distance on a line. Their average is zero only if every shadow matches, and then the two clouds are the same cloud. So all four lights stay on.

Let us measure it on the clouds you saw. Shifted by three in the plane. Exact transport says three. A lamp pointing along the shift sees three. A lamp pointing across it sees nothing. In between, the shadow of the shift is three times the cosine of the angle.

The average of the squared cosine over all angles is one half. So the sliced distance is three divided by the root of two: 2.12. With five hundred lamps on two thousand points, we measured 2.13.

Careful here. 2.12 is not three, and that is not a failed approximation. It is another distance, on another scale. Compare sliced values with sliced values, never with transport values.

Now raise the dimension. Same shift of three, two thousand points per cloud. In ten dimensions, the sliced distance is 0.99. In a hundred, 0.31. The scale shrinks like three divided by the root of the dimension, because a random lamp is almost perpendicular to the shift. Episode 2.6 gave the size of that effect: in a hundred dimensions, a random direction makes an angle of about ninety degrees with the shift, give or take six.

So the shadow is short. Is it lost in the noise? Draw two samples of the same cloud, and measure their distance. That is the floor. When the totals differ

When totals differ, mass may be created or destroyed at a fixed price, here five per unit, besides being moved. Back at the earthworks. Every plan we have drawn so far hid one condition. The pile of earth and the hole must have exactly the same volume. Today we drop that condition.

Why would you want to? Two reasons. The first is about spectra. To compare two spectra by transport, we scaled each one to add up to one. That throws away how much power there was. A spectrum with one and a half times the power, scaled back, looks exactly like the original. Transport says: distance zero. The change is gone.

The second reason is about strays. A little mass very far away can pull the whole distance. Here is one pile at ten. In the first variant, a tenth of the pile appears at forty. So we change the rules of the site. You may destroy earth here, and create earth there, at a fixed price. Say five for each unit destroyed, and five for each unit created. Moving earth still costs its weight times the distance. And we always take the cheapest plan.

Take the first variant again. Carrying the speck costs three. But we can destroy a tenth at ten, and create a tenth at forty. That is five times a tenth, twice. It costs one. One is cheaper than three, so the distance is one.

Now the second variant. Moving the whole pile costs three. Destroying it and creating it again would cost ten. So we move it. The distance stays three. The two changes finally get different answers: one for the stray speck, three for the real shift.

Now watch what the price does to the road. One unit of earth, moved farther and farther. At first the cost simply grows with the distance. Five apart costs five. Nine apart costs nine. But at ten it stops growing, and it never grows again. Eleven apart costs ten. Twenty apart costs ten. Thirty apart costs ten.

Why ten? Because destroying a pile and creating it elsewhere costs five plus five. Nobody carries earth farther than twice the price. The price is a leash, and the leash has a length.

Remember the spectra from the start of the chapter. A peak at ten hertz and a peak at eleven are still one apart. But the peak at thirty, which plain transport put at twenty, is now at ten. The distance saturates, and that is on purpose.

Piccoli and Rossi studied this version in twenty fourteen, and called it the generalized Wasserstein distance. There are newer versions. They do not charge a flat price. They charge the created or destroyed mass with a gap from chapter five, such as Kullback and Leibler, in bits. Established, first entry. The name itself. In the year two thousand, Rubner, Tomasi and Guibas used the earth mover's distance to compare the colours of images, for image retrieval. The mass is the share of each colour. The ground is the distance between two colours, in the Lab colour space.

Second entry. Domain adaptation. Courty and colleagues, in twenty seventeen, move one cloud of points onto another, so that a classifier trained on the first one works on the second. The mass is the points. The ground is the squared straight-line distance between them.

Third entry. Cells. Schiebinger and colleagues followed cells measured every half day, and linked each day to the next by transport. The mass is cells. Fourth entry. Brain maps. Gramfort, Peyré and Cuturi, in twenty fifteen, averaged activity maps across people, in functional MRI and in magnetoencephalography. On a grid of voxels, the ground is a distance in millimetres. On the folded cortex, it is the distance along the surface.

Fifth entry. Janati and colleagues, in twenty twenty, used transport to keep the estimated sources of several people close to each other. The ground is again the distance along the cortex. They locate sources. They do not average them.

Sixth entry. Music. Flamary and colleagues compared spectra of piano notes. Their ground cost is unusual: energy may move to a harmonically related frequency for free, so that a note and its overtones count as one. It is a small-scale test, and it shows how free the ground is.
