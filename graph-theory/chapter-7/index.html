PLATE 7.16:32

Friends of friends

Granovetter found that of fifty-four people who found a job through a contact, only nine saw that contact often. The clustering coefficient, the share of a node's pairs of friends who are friends, explains why. The worm's wiring scores six times higher than a random network. Links that close no triangle act as doors between circles, bringing in new information.

TRANSCRIPTEN

How do people find a job? It sounds like a question for economists. It is also a question about shapes.

The sociologist Mark Granovetter put it to professional, technical and managerial workers who had recently changed jobs, in a suburb of Boston. Some had found the job through a contact. He asked how often they saw that person. Often meant at least twice a week. Rarely meant once a year or less.

Fifty-four people had found their job through a contact. Nine of them, sixteen point seven percent, saw the contact often. Thirty, fifty-five point six percent, saw the contact occasionally. And fifteen, twenty-seven point eight percent, saw the contact rarely.

Only one in six saw the contact often. For most, the help came from people they saw only occasionally or rarely. Why? The answer is in the shape of the network. Here is a street meal. Neighbours sit along one long table, and everybody talks to a few others.

Put a point on every guest. Draw a line between two guests who know each other. Twelve guests, eighteen links. The street meal was a graph all along. This one is invented, to keep things clear.

Look at Ana. She knows Bea and Cal. Do Bea and Cal know each other? Here they do, so the three of them make a triangle. Friends of friends are often friends.

Let us turn that into a number. Take all the pairs of one person's friends, and ask how many of those pairs are linked.

Ana has three friends. That makes three pairs, and all three are linked. She scores one.

Now take Dee. She has four friends, so six pairs. Only three pairs are linked, because Eli knows none of the others. Her score is one half.

This score is the clustering coefficient of a node. It is the share of a node's pairs of friends that are friends too. Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz used it this way in nineteen ninety-eight, in their paper on small-world networks. A node with fewer than two friends has no pairs, and by convention its score is zero.

For a whole network, there are two usual recipes. The first is to average the scores of all the nodes. Here that gives zero point six eight.

The second recipe counts triangles. A connected triple is one person with two friends, a V shape. A triangle is a V whose two ends are linked, so every triangle hides three of them. Here there are thirty-seven V shapes and eight triangles. Eight times three is twenty-four closed ones, out of thirty-seven. That share is zero point six five, and it is called the transitivity.

The two recipes differ. The first gives every person the same weight. The second gives more weight to people with many friends. Whenever you meet a clustering number, ask which recipe made it.

Now a real network. The worm Caenorhabditis elegans has a wiring diagram, published by Varshney and colleagues in two thousand eleven. Link two neurons when a chemical synapse joins them, in either direction. That gives two hundred seventy-nine neurons and one thousand nine hundred sixty-one links.

The average clustering is zero point three two. The transitivity is zero point two zero. Is that high? Compared with what?

Build a random network with the same number of neurons and the same number of links. Do it two hundred times, with a fixed seed. Over the two hundred, the average is zero point zero five. In a random graph, the score is about the density of the graph, here five percent.

The worm is six times higher. Its neurons are wired in tight little groups, far more than chance would give. A number like this needs a reference.

Now the trap from episode one point four. Remember the actors and the films. We squeezed the films away, and kept a link between two actors who shared one.

Take one film with four actors. Every pair of them shares it, so the four make a clique. Each actor has three friends, and all three are linked. The score is one, for every actor of the film. The projection builds it.

On the real attendance table from Natchez, the women's projection scores zero point nine four. That looks like a very tight community. But shuffle who went where. Keep each woman's number of events, and keep the size of each event. Project again. It scores zero point nine four.

So the number tells almost nothing about friendship here. It is what the projection produces, whoever attends. Before you read a clustering number, ask whether the network was built by projection.

Back to the job. Close friends tend to know each other. Granovetter said so: if A is close to B, and A is close to C, then B and C are probably linked too. Close ties close triangles.

Look at Ana's circle. Four close friends, all linked. News that one of them hears, the others hear too. Tell it again, and it comes back. In a circle, news goes round in circles.

Now look at the thin link between Dee and Eli. They are only acquaintances. They have no friend in common, so this link closes no triangle. Granovetter called a link a bridge when it is the only path between its two ends. When the best detour is longer than two steps, it is a local bridge.

Through this thin link, an offer for a job at the bakery crosses from Eli's circle into Ana's. Nobody in Ana's circle had heard of it. The weak tie brings the news in. The strong ties then spread it around the circle.

Cut that one link, and Ana's circle hears nothing from the other eight guests. This is also why Dee scores one half instead of one. She has a door in her circle.

Notice what is not said. A weak tie is not good because it is weak. It is useful because of where it stands, between two circles. Granovetter's numbers rest on fifty-four people. In two thousand twenty-two, experiments on LinkedIn with over twenty million people found that moderately weak ties worked best.

To sum up, the clustering coefficient of a node is the share of its pairs of friends that are friends. For a network, average the scores, or count the triangles, and say which. Compare with a random network, and beware of projections. And the links that close no triangle are the doors between circles, where new information comes in.

Next time, we leave the circles and ask a question about the whole network. How far does a piece of news have to travel, on average?

PLATE 7.25:26

How far, on average

The characteristic path length averages shortest distances over all pairs, and small means news travels fast. Cut the one link between two circles of five guests and it becomes infinite. The global efficiency averages inverse distances instead, so a missing path counts as zero and a network in pieces still has a score. A local version asks the same of one neighbourhood.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At a street meal, a rumour starts with one guest. How many whispers does it take to reach the far end of the table? Last time we stayed inside the circles. Now the question is about the whole network. How far apart are two people, on average?

We already know how to measure one distance. It is the length of a shortest path, counted in links, and the breadth-first search of episode two point two finds it.

Here are ten guests, invented to keep things clear. Two circles of five, joined by one link.

Start from guest A. Three guests are one link away. Two are two links away. Three are three links away. And one is four links away.

Now do it from everybody. Ten guests make forty-five pairs. Give each pair a marble, and let it roll into the bin of its distance.

Fifteen pairs are one link apart. Eleven are two apart, nine are three apart, eight are four apart, and two are five apart.

Add up all forty-five distances and you get one hundred six. Divide by forty-five, and you get two point three six. The marbles balance on a beam at two point three six.

That number is the characteristic path length. Watts and Strogatz used it in nineteen ninety-eight. When it is small, news, or a signal, or a disease, needs few steps to get from anywhere to anywhere.

Now a real network. The worm Caenorhabditis elegans has two hundred seventy-nine neurons, so thirty-eight thousand seven hundred eighty-one pairs. Link two neurons when a chemical synapse joins them, in either direction, as in the last episode.

Most pairs are two or three links apart. Only one pair is six links apart. The average is two point five seven. From any neuron, a signal reaches any other in under three links, on average.

Now cut the link between our two circles. We get two islands of five guests. A guest on the left has no path at all to a guest on the right. Their distance is infinite.

Twenty-five pairs are infinite. One infinite marble is enough to make the average infinite. The beam has no balance point.

One fix is to ignore the infinite pairs. The twenty pairs that are left average one point three. But we cut the network in two, and the score got better. That cannot be right.

In two thousand one, Vito Latora and Massimo Marchiori proposed another average. Do not average the distances. Average their inverses.

Two guests one link apart score one. Two links apart, one half. Three links apart, one third. And no path at all scores zero, which is exactly what it should be. Their average is called the global efficiency. A network where everyone is linked to everyone scores one. A network with no links scores zero. Chapter six has a version of this trick for a single node.

Back to the beam, with inverse distances. Fifteen marbles sit at one, eleven at one half, nine at one third, eight at one quarter, and two at one fifth. They add up to twenty-five point nine. Divided by forty-five, that is zero point five eight.

Cut the link again. Fourteen pairs are still at one. Six are at one half. And the twenty-five pairs on opposite islands sit at zero. The beam still balances, at zero point three eight.

That is the moment to remember. A network cut in two keeps an efficiency. It is lower, about two thirds of what it was, but it exists. Its path length became infinite, and the efficiency did not.

This tolerance of islands is why efficiency is popular for brain networks built with a threshold. A threshold can leave a network in pieces. Chapter fourteen comes back to that.

The worm shows the same thing. Keep only the pairs joined by at least five synapses, as in episode two point four. The network falls into fifty-seven pieces. Its characteristic path length is infinite. Its efficiency is zero point one five, against zero point four three for the whole wiring.

Raise the threshold step by step, and the efficiency slides down smoothly. The path length cannot follow. From two synapses on, it is infinite.

Efficiency also has a local version. Take guest B, and look only at B's neighbours: A, C and D. Can they still reach each other, without going through anyone else? A and C are linked, and so are C and D. A reaches D in two steps. Their efficiency is zero point eight three.

Now take guest E, at the end of the bridge. Its neighbours are A, D and F, and none of them is linked to another. That neighbourhood scores zero. If E leaves, its neighbours are cut off from each other.

So local efficiency measures how well a neighbourhood copes without one of its members. Averaged over all ten guests, it gives zero point four five.

To sum up, the characteristic path length is the average distance over all pairs, and small means fast. One island makes it infinite. The efficiency averages the inverse distances, so an infinite distance counts as zero, and a network in pieces still has a score. The local version asks the same of one neighbourhood.

Next time, a different question about the whole network. When a guest with many friends makes a new friend, does it pick another guest with many friends? Do hubs link to hubs?

PLATE 7.36:09

Do hubs link to hubs?

Assortativity measures whether hubs link to hubs, as the correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link. The same twelve guests with the same degrees score plus 0.47 or minus 0.86, depending only on the wiring. After removing the ten top hubs, one network keeps a piece of eighty-eight nodes and the other forty-four.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended on a question. At a street meal, when a guest with many friends makes a new friend, who does it pick? Another guest with many friends, or a quiet one? Do hubs link to hubs? Today we measure it.

Look at one link. It has two ends, and each end is a guest with a number of friends, its degree, as in episode six point one. So every link comes with two numbers.

Here are twelve guests, invented to keep things clear, and twenty links. Guest A has six friends, the most. B and C have five, D and E have four, F and G have three, and the five others have two.

Now give every link two coins, one for each way round. A coin lands on a board. One side of the board is the degree at one end of the link. The other side is the degree at the other end.

A link between a guest with six friends and a guest with five lands in the cell six, five, and again in five, six. Twenty links make forty coins.

Look at where they land. Guest A is linked to all six other guests who have three friends or more. The coins gather along the diagonal, where both ends have a similar degree.

The tilt of that cloud is a number. In two thousand two, Mark Newman proposed to measure it as the correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link. It runs from minus one to plus one. Here it is plus zero point four seven. That is called assortative mixing: hubs link to hubs.

Now keep the same twelve guests, and keep every guest's number of friends. Only change who is linked to whom.

Guest A still has six friends, but five of them are guests who have only two. The two most connected guests, A and B, are no longer linked. The coins leave the diagonal for the corners. The number is now minus zero point eight six. That is disassortative mixing: hubs link to quiet guests.

The degrees did not change. The wiring did. So the degrees alone cannot tell you which one you have.

What do real networks do? Newman measured ten, and printed them in a table. Social networks first. The coauthorship of physicists scores zero point three six three. Company directors score zero point two seven six. Both are assortative.

The technological and biological networks he measured were all on the other side. The Internet scores minus zero point one eight nine. A food web scores minus zero point two seven six. Newman's reading for the Internet is that the high degree nodes are connectivity providers, with many clients who each have only one connection.

Let us measure some ourselves. Here are three real networks, on the same board, with degrees grouped in powers of two. Darker means more links.

Take the coauthorship of fifteen hundred eighty-nine network scientists, compiled by Newman in two thousand six. It scores plus zero point four six. The American air network of nineteen ninety-seven, three hundred thirty-two airports, scores minus zero point two one. The wiring of the worm scores minus zero point zero nine.

It is a tendency, not a law. Newman wrote that social networks are often assortative. Zachary's karate club is a social network too, and its score is minus zero point four eight.

Now the consequence. Take two networks with exactly the same degrees, every node keeping its number of friends. Both have a hundred nodes and a hundred ninety-six links, and both are in one piece. One scores plus zero point two four, the other minus zero point five four. Now remove the most connected nodes, one at a time.

After one hub, the biggest piece still holds ninety-nine nodes in both. After three, ninety-seven against seventy-four. After five, ninety-five against fifty-six. After ten hubs, the same ten in both networks, the assortative network keeps a piece of eighty-eight nodes, and the disassortative one keeps forty-four.

Why? In the disassortative network, not one pair of the ten hubs is linked. Each hub held its own group of quiet nodes, and its removal sets them free. Forty-six nodes are left completely alone. In the assortative network, the hubs are linked to each other, the quiet nodes hold together without them, and only two nodes are left alone.

This is not a lucky draw. We repeated it with fifty other networks of a hundred nodes. The assortative one kept the larger piece every time, eighty-five nodes against thirty-eight on average. With a thousand nodes, after a hundred hubs, it is eight hundred ninety-eight against three hundred twenty-four.

Newman found the same in his own models: assortative networks are more robust to the removal of their highest degree vertices. His conclusion was grim. The networks we want to protect look fragile, and the ones that spread disease look sturdy.

But be careful. Remove ten nodes at random instead of ten hubs, and the result flips. The assortative network keeps sixty nodes on average, the disassortative one eighty-nine. Same degrees, opposite fragility, and which one breaks depends on the kind of damage. Chapter nine comes back to this.

One more use. Degree is not the only thing to compare at the two ends of a link. Give each guest a label, such as a language, or an age. Newman extended the idea to labels in two thousand three.

Here are nine invented guests who speak three languages. Nine of the twelve links stay inside a language. The same coefficient, computed on the labels, is zero point six two five. It is one when every link stays inside a group, and zero when the groups make no difference.

To sum up, assortativity compares the degrees at the two ends of a link. Positive means hubs link to hubs. Negative means hubs link to quiet guests. The same degrees can come with opposite signs, and the sign changes what an attack on the hubs does.

PLATE 7.46:19

The rich club

The rich-club coefficient is the share of possible links that exist among nodes above a given degree. It rises with degree even in shuffled networks, so the raw value proves nothing. Dividing by the value in shuffles that keep every degree gives rho. For the eleven busiest US airports rho is 1.08, a mild club; other networks tell different stories.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended on a question. Take only the most connected guests at the street meal. How tightly are they linked to one another? Today we measure it, and we meet a trap.

Here are the twelve guests of last time, with the friends each one has. Stretch a velvet rope across the room, at a number of friends. Only the guests above the rope stay in the club.

Put the rope at three friends. Five guests stay: A, B, C, D and E. Five guests can form ten pairs. Count the pairs that are really linked. All ten. This club is a clique.

The rich-club coefficient is the share of the possible pairs, inside the club, that exist. Here it is one, the largest it can be. Zhou and Mondragón defined it in two thousand four, to study the Internet.

Now take the other wiring from last time. Every guest keeps the same number of friends. The rope stays at three. The same five guests stay in the club, but only four of their ten pairs are linked. The coefficient is zero point four. Same degrees, a different coefficient.

Now a real network, the airports of the United States in nineteen ninety-seven. There are three hundred and thirty-two airports and two thousand one hundred and twenty-six routes. Across the whole network, four percent of the possible pairs of airports have a route.

Raise the rope, one route at a time. The club shrinks, and the share of linked pairs climbs. Above sixty-four routes, eleven airports remain. All fifty-five pairs among them have a direct route. So the coefficient reaches one.

It looks like a very rich club. Do not believe it yet.

Here is why. The busiest airport has routes to one hundred and thirty-nine of the three hundred and thirty-one others. That is forty-two percent of the network. Two airports that big are likely to share a route, even if routes were drawn blindly. So the hubs would end up linked to one another with no club at all.

Colizza's team made this point in two thousand six. Even a completely random network shows a coefficient that rises with the degree. The rise alone proves nothing.

The fix is to compare. Take the airport network and shuffle it. Pick two routes, one from A to B and one from C to D. Swap their ends: A to D, and C to B. Every airport keeps its number of routes. Repeat this tens of thousands of times, and the network forgets who was linked to whom, but not who had many routes.

We built one hundred shuffled networks, with a fixed seed. Here is one of them. Its coefficient climbs too, all the way up to ninety-six percent among the ten busiest airports. A network with no club, by design.

So divide the real coefficient by the shuffled one. Call the result rho. Rho of one means no more than chance. Rho above one means a real club.

For the eleven busiest airports, the shuffled networks average ninety-two percent. The real network has a hundred percent. Rho is one point zero eight. At its best, rho is one point two, for a club of thirty-nine airports. That is a mild club. Colizza's team also found a mild one in a worldwide air network.

Now three real networks side by side. The raw coefficient rises in all three. In the airports, from four percent to a hundred. In the worm's neurons, from five percent to eighty-four. Among network scientists, from well under one percent to fifty-five.

Now rho. For the worm, rho stays between zero point nine one and one point oh seven, for every club of fifteen neurons or more. Its rise comes almost entirely from the degrees. Among the last ten neurons, rho reaches one point four, and no shuffled network does as well. Towlson and colleagues reported in two thousand thirteen a rich club of eleven neurons in the worm, ten of them command interneurons. Their network has more links than ours, so the two clubs are not the same measurement.

For the scientists, rho climbs to seven and a half. Among the twenty-seven most connected, a hundred and ninety-three of three hundred and fifty-one pairs have written together. In shuffled networks, it is seven percent. Coauthorship is a projection, as in episode one point four, so read this club with care.

Next, Colizza and colleagues applied this to four real networks. Collaboration between scientists: a strong rich club. A protein interaction network: none. The Internet, at the level of autonomous systems: none, contrary to earlier claims. Worldwide air transport: a mild one. Their reading of the Internet is that its biggest hubs serve local regions, and are not tightly linked to each other.

A rich club is not the same as the assortativity of last time. Colizza and colleagues sketch a disassortative network that still has a rich club.

Now the brain. In two thousand eleven, van den Heuvel and Sporns built the structural networks of twenty-one healthy people, from diffusion MRI. They found twelve hub regions, strongly linked to one another, across both hemispheres. The precuneus, the superior frontal and superior parietal cortex, and deeper down, the hippocampus, the putamen and the thalamus.

They compared with one thousand random networks that kept the degrees. The normalised coefficient was above one for every threshold from eleven to seventeen.

Then they took the paths between regions that are outside the club. Eighty-nine percent of the shortest paths went through at least one club region. For random groups of regions of the same size, thirty-two percent.

Notice what made this work. The coefficient alone meant nothing. It meant something only against a reference. Chapter ten builds those references, and asks how to shuffle without cheating.

To sum up, the rich-club coefficient is the share of possible links inside the club of nodes above a degree. It rises with the degree even when no club exists. So divide it by the same number in shuffled networks that keep every degree. A rho above one is a club. A rho near one is hubs being hubs.

PLATE 7.54:36

Density changes everything

On the Les Misérables network, adding meaningless random links raises efficiency from 0.44 to 0.75 and shortens path length from 2.64 to 1.5, while clustering first dips, then climbs. Two simulated groups with identical organisation but weaker correlations in one differ on every measure after thresholding. Raw measures of networks with different densities cannot be compared.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended with a question. Take a network and add links, one at a time, at random. What happens to the measures of this chapter?

Today we find out. The answer decides whether two networks can be compared at all.

First, a word for how full a network is. The density is the number of links divided by the number of possible links.

Our network is the cast of Les Misérables. Two characters are linked if they appear in the same chapter of the novel. The data were put together by Donald Knuth, in nineteen ninety-three.

There are seventy-seven characters, and so two thousand nine hundred twenty-six possible pairs. Two hundred fifty-four of them are linked. That makes a density of eight point seven percent.

We read three dials. Clustering is the average share of a character's pairs of friends that are friends themselves. It reads zero point five seven. Efficiency is the average of one over the distance, and reads zero point four four. And the average path length reads two point six four steps. Path length is always taken on the largest connected piece. In every network built from these characters, that piece holds at least seventy-six of the seventy-seven nodes.

Now add links. Every original link stays. A spool draws new ones between pairs chosen at random, one at a time, until half of all pairs are linked.

The organisation of the network never changes. Nothing is removed. We only add links that mean nothing.

Watch the dials. Efficiency climbs, from zero point four four to zero point seven five. The average path length falls, from two point six four to one point five.

Clustering does something stranger. At ten percent density, it has dropped to zero point four five. At twenty percent, zero point two nine. Then it climbs again, and reaches zero point five one at half density.

The blue needle is a random network with the same number of nodes and the same number of links. Its clustering is just the density. Zero point two at twenty percent, and a straight line up.

At half density, the real network and the random one agree to within one hundredth on every dial. Even at the start, the random network is close on the other two. For efficiency, zero point four five against zero point four four. Path length, two point four eight against two point six four.

This is the warning of van Wijk, Stam and Daffertshofer, in two thousand ten. If the number of nodes or the average degree changes, graph measures differ, even when the topology stays identical.

They saw it in real data. An MEG experiment had twenty participants, in six conditions. Clustering and path length differed between the conditions. But those differences moved together with the average degree.

Now the case that matters in brain research. Two groups of twenty people, simulated. Each person has a matrix of correlations between thirty regions.

The patients have exactly the same matrices as the controls, multiplied by zero point eight. Their correlations are weaker everywhere, and nothing else differs. The average correlation is zero point two eight for the controls, and zero point two three for the patients.

We apply one rule to both groups. Keep a link when the correlation is above zero point three.

The controls keep one hundred eighty-seven links, on average. The patients keep one hundred nine. Every patient has fewer links than every control.

Now measure. Clustering, zero point four four against zero point three two. Efficiency, zero point seven one against zero point six zero. Path length, one point five seven against one point nine zero. Every measure separates the two groups, with no overlap.

A study would report a less clustered, less efficient network in the patients, with longer paths. But the organisation is the same by construction. Only the density differs.

Comparing two networks of different densities is comparing different objects. Chapter fourteen deals with what to do about it.

To sum up. Density is the share of possible links that exist. Clustering, efficiency and path length all change with it, even when the organisation does not. So the raw measures of two networks of different densities cannot be compared.
