Which covariates, and why

A covariate is a variable the norm is allowed to depend on, so adding one means comparing a person only with others sharing its value. Age, sex and site are the usual ones, while head size, image quality and head motion are choices to justify, since adjusting for a variable linked to the disorder erases part of the signal. Diagnosis never enters.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we chose a model. We hung the seven centile charts side by side, and saw that good models agree near the middle and differ in the tails. But every one of those charts had the same horizontal axis, and we never asked why. Today we ask which variables belong in the model, and what each one quietly does to the question.

Welcome to the covered market, where every trader has a scale. Here is the model label, the small card that travels with every model in this series. Most lines are filled in. Today we fill in the line called covariates.

A covariate is a variable that the norm is allowed to depend on. Until now, there was one: age. Look at the simulated chart again. A young person and an old person do not share a norm. So age goes in.

The second one is sex. Many brain measures differ on average between females and males, and the demonstration of the braincharts pre-trained lifespan model uses two covariates, age and sex, plus one column per site. In that code, sex is a column of zeros and ones.

The third one is site. Each scanner, each hospital, each trader has a scale of their own. Sites do not behave like age. There are only a few of them, and they are labels, not numbers. The next three episodes are about them, so today we only write the word on the card.

Now come the candidates. These are the variables people argue about. Intracranial volume, image quality, head motion.

Start with intracranial volume, the size of the skull's inside. Bigger heads tend to hold bigger regions. For regional volumes, a study of healthy ageing and Alzheimer's disease, published in twenty fourteen, showed that raw regional volumes rise with head size, but not in strict proportion. The way you correct for head size matters. Dividing by it is not the same as regressing it out, and the two give different answers.

Now image quality. A blurry scan gives a different brain. One quality measure from the FreeSurfer pipeline is the Euler number, which counts the defects in the reconstructed surface. Rosen and colleagues, in twenty eighteen, compared it with manual ratings of more than eighteen hundred scans, and it followed the raters well. They also found that data quality can both inflate and hide age associations in adolescence. Quality is not noise that averages out. It can look like biology.

Head motion is the same story. Reuter and colleagues, in twenty fifteen, showed that the more a person moves in the scanner, the smaller the grey matter volume and cortical thickness appear. And removing the scans that fail a visual check does not remove the whole bias.

So these three variables clearly change the measurement. Should they go into the model? Here is the key moment.

A covariate defines the comparison.

When you put a variable into the model, you say one sentence. I compare this person only with people who have the same value of this variable. Same age, same sex, same site. The norm is no longer the whole population. It is a slice of it.

That is exactly what we want for age. An eight-year-old should be compared with eight-year-olds. But now imagine a variable that is itself linked to the disorder you study. Suppose, and this is purely illustrative, that people with a certain condition tend to move more in the scanner. If motion goes into the norm, each person is compared with people who move as much as they do. Part of the difference you were looking for is now in the norm, and the deviation is smaller. You have erased a piece of what you were searching for.

This is an old trap in statistics. Miller and Chapman wrote about it in two thousand one, in a paper called Misunderstanding analysis of covariance. Adjusting for a variable that is related to the group does not simply remove a nuisance. It changes the question.

So here is the rule. Ask, for each candidate, two questions. Does it change the measurement for reasons that have nothing to do with the brain you want to describe? And is it linked to the trouble you will study? If the answer to the first is yes and the second is no, it is a good covariate. If the answer to the second is yes, stop and think.

And two things never go into the norm. The diagnosis, and a symptom score. They are the outcome. A norm that knows the diagnosis can no longer tell you who deviates from it.

Image quality and motion can instead be handled before the model. You can set a quality threshold, and report it, so that every person in the reference passed the same bar. That is a choice about who is in the reference. It is not a choice about what the norm depends on. Both choices are written on the label.

So, to sum up. A covariate defines who you compare with. Age, sex and site are the usual ones. Head size, quality and motion are choices you justify. And the diagnosis is never one of them.

Same brain, different scanner

The same brain gives different numbers on different scanners, like one pumpkin on three market scales. Travelling subjects show the effect is both a shift and a stretch, and in the simulation a site offset equals about a decade of ageing. A value of 110 sits at the top centile on one site and well inside the range on another.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we chose what goes into the model. Age, sex, and the rule that a covariate defines the comparison. Today we meet a variable that nobody chooses, and that is always there. The scanner.

Welcome to the covered market of scales. Every merchant here has a scale, and every scale has its own habit. One reads a little high. One reads a little low.

Meet KILO. KILO is a market scale with a face, and its needle is set slightly off. Each site of our study gets its own KILO. Site A, site B, site C. These three sites belong to our simulated dataset, so everything you see here is labelled simulated.

Now a single person walks in. Same brain, same morning. We place her on scale A, then on scale B, then on scale C. Three scales, three readings. Nothing about her changed. Only the instrument.

There is a guest at the market today. SPIN comes from the fMRI series, and SPIN knows scanners very well. SPIN shows us the three machines behind the scales. Different manufacturers, different magnets, different software for turning the image into a number.

How do we know how big this effect is? We cannot ask a person to be in two places at once. But we can ask them to travel. A traveling subject is someone scanned on several machines, within a short time. Their brain does not change in a few weeks. So any difference between their scans belongs to the machines. The traveler is the gauge that we use to measure the scales.

Here is what travelers have shown. In a study from 2006, Han and colleagues scanned the same healthy people on several scanners. Repeat scans on the same scanner agreed closely, within under three hundredths of a millimetre for global cortical thickness. Across scanners from two manufacturers, the average difference was about fifteen hundredths of a millimetre. Across two field strengths, one and a half tesla and three tesla, it was about seventeen hundredths, with thicker readings at three tesla.

Now look at the effect in a real multi-site study. In 2018, Fortin and colleagues looked at cortical thickness in two studies, eleven scanners in all. In one study of depression, four sites each had a three tesla scanner. Even so, fifty-three of ninety-eight regions were linked to the site. That is more than half of the map.

And in the fMRI world, nine healthy men traveled to twelve sites. Yamashita and colleagues, in 2019, found that the measurement bias in resting-state connectivity was mostly larger than the effects of the psychiatric disorders they studied.

Two kinds of shift appear, and the market shows both.

First, the additive effect. A scale that reads three units too high shifts everybody by three units. In our simulated data, site B adds three units, and site C subtracts four. At the age of sixty, the true average on site A is about ninety-five and a half. On site C it is about ninety-one and a half.

Second, the multiplicative effect. A scale can also stretch the readings. The needle moves further for the same change. Then the spread of the people changes, not only their position. In our simulation, at sixty, the standard deviation is about five point nine on site A, six point eight on site B, and five point three on site C.

Now the key moment. Take a value of one hundred and ten, at age sixty. On a norm built from site A, that sits right at the ninety-seventh and a half centile. Place the same value on site C, and it lies beyond that site's own ninety-seventh and a half centile, which is at about one hundred and four and a half. On site B, it is well inside the range, because the top line is at about one hundred and fifteen. The same number says three different things.

Now measure the shift against the age effect. In this simulation, between sixty and seventy, the true average falls by about three and a half units. A shift of four units is about a decade of ageing. A person on scale C looks ten years older, without having aged at all.

This is the point. A site effect can be as big as the effect we are looking for, and sometimes bigger. If the model does not know about it, a person looks abnormal because she was scanned somewhere else. And a site can be confounded with the group we study, because one clinic may scan mostly patients. Then the scale and the disease are tangled together.

The multi-site transfer set that we use in later chapters comes from six different OpenNeuro datasets. Six sites, six scales.

Travelers also tell us what a good correction looks like. In 2021, Maikusa and colleagues scanned twenty healthy volunteers with several acquisition setups. Using the travelers to correct, they removed up to eighty-five percent of the measurement bias. A correction that learns from the data alone, called ComBat, removed about fifty-nine percent.

So, to sum up. The same brain gives different numbers on different scanners, by a shift and by a stretch, and that can be as large as the clinical effect. Next time, we meet the repair man of the market. ComBat, and its limits.

PLATE 5.36:36

ComBat and its limits

ComBat repairs site effects before modelling by estimating each site's shift and scale and sharing strength across regions with empirical Bayes. It fails quietly when site is mixed up with age or diagnosis, when it must be refitted for each new site, when it sees test data, when age cannot curve, and because it cannot change distribution shape.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the same brain was scanned on three different machines, and it came out three different ways. A site effect can be bigger than the effect we are looking for. Today we meet the most famous repair for it. Its name is ComBat. And we will see exactly where it stops working.

Back at the covered market of scales. Each scale has its own habit. One reads a little high. One reads a little low. One is jumpy, and one is calm. At the end of the aisle, a repairman has a small screwdriver.

His job is simple to say. Take each scale, and fix two things. First, the position of the needle, so that zero is zero. Second, the size of the swing, so that a unit means the same thing on every scale.

In statistics, those two fixes have names. A shift, and a scale. Here is the model, one term at a time.

The measurement is y. It equals a typical value, plus the effect of the covariates we want to keep, such as age. Then comes the site. The site adds a shift. And the site multiplies the leftover noise by its own scale.

ComBat estimates the shift and the scale for each site, and then undoes them. What remains is the typical value, the covariates, and the noise, all on a common ruler.

Now the clever part. Each brain region has its own shift and its own scale, for each site. A small site gives a shaky estimate for a single region. So ComBat borrows strength. It uses an empirical Bayes estimate. It looks at the shifts of all the regions together, and gently pulls each shaky estimate toward the shared pattern. It is the prior from episode three point seven, learned from the data itself.

This idea came from genomics. In a paper from two thousand seven, Johnson, Li and Rabinovic built it for gene expression microarrays, where each batch can be small. Ten years later, Fortin and colleagues carried it into brain imaging. First for diffusion measures, in two thousand seventeen. Then for cortical thickness, in two thousand eighteen.

Let us try it on our simulated data. Every dot is simulated, and the measure is a fictional cortical index. Three sites. Site A has two hundred and forty people, site B a hundred and fifty, and site C sixty. In this simulation we know the truth. Site B reads three units higher than A. Site C reads four units lower. And site B is a little more spread out.

So far, so good. That is what the repairman fixes. But a repairman can only fix what he can tell apart. Here is the key moment. The limits, one by one.

Limit one. When the site is mixed up with something real.

Site C is the small new site. It only scanned people older than about forty-five. The average age is sixty at site C, and forty-four at site A. Now compare the raw averages. Site C reads about eight units lower than site A. Eight, not four.

Where do the other four come from? Ageing. Older people have a lower index. If we ask ComBat to harmonise without telling it about age, it believes all eight units belong to the scanner. It lifts site C by eight. It has just erased four units of real, biological ageing.

Tell it about age, and the problem shrinks. Compare site C only with site A people of the same ages, and the gap is about four, which is the truth. But look at what we did. We could separate scanner from ageing only because site A also scanned older people. If a site had scanned only patients, and no other site had scanned patients, nothing could tell the scanner from the disease. The repairman would call the disease a bad scale.

Limit two. ComBat needs every site at the same time.

The shift and the scale of each site are estimated together, with all the data on the table. Add a new site next year, and the estimates change. So do the harmonised values of everyone already analysed. Some software lets you save the fitted corrections, and apply them later to new people. Then the new people must look exactly like the old ones, with the same covariates, in the same order.

Limit three. Harmonising before you split.

Imagine you harmonise everybody first, and then cut the data into a training set and a test set. The test people already helped to set the shift and the scale. Information has leaked. In a study of autism classification, using the public ABIDE data, Serra and colleagues compared both orders. Harmonising everybody first gave better scores. Harmonising on the training set alone gave scores close to no harmonisation at all. The extra performance was leakage. The rule is to fit the correction on the training people, and only apply it to the rest. We come back to this in chapter eleven.

Limit four. ComBat bends only what you tell it to bend.

The covariate part of the model is linear in age, unless you add curved terms. But across a lifetime, the cortex does not change along a straight line. On our simulated data, it rises fast in childhood, then declines in a curve. If the model forces a straight line, the curve leaks into the shift, and gets distorted. Pomponio and colleagues fixed this in two thousand twenty. Their variant, called ComBat G A M, lets age follow a smooth curve.

Limit five. ComBat fixes the average and the spread. Nothing else.

Our simulated people are skewed, with a long tail on the older side. A shift and a scale do not change the shape. Tails stay as the site made them. And that is where a z-score is read. Other variants go further. Longitudinal ComBat, from Beer and colleagues in two thousand twenty, handles people scanned several times. And CovBat, from Chen and colleagues, also corrects how regions vary together.

So the repairman is useful, but he works before the model, on the data. He changes the numbers, and he needs the whole market.

To sum up. ComBat recalibrates each site in position and in scale, and it shares information between regions. It fails quietly when the site is mixed up with age or disease, when it must be refitted for a new site, when it sees the test data, when age is not allowed to curve, and when the shape matters.

Next time, we do the opposite. We stop correcting the data, and we put the site inside the model, as a level of the hierarchy.

PLATE 5.46:51

Sites as a level in the hierarchy

Instead of correcting the data, the site becomes a level in the hierarchy, with an offset and spread learned by the model alongside the age curve. A new site is calibrated with a small set of matching healthy controls, and the doubt from that small sample stays visible in every z-score. Tens of controls appear to be a working order of magnitude.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we met ComBat. It corrects the data first, and only then fits the model. And we saw where that goes wrong. If the site is tangled up with age or with diagnosis, the correction eats real signal. It needs every site at fitting time. And done before the split between training and test, it leaks. Today, a different answer. We stop correcting the data. We put the site inside the model.

Welcome back to the covered market of scales. Remember the pumpkin that weighed three different weights on three different scales? Tonight the scales are not scattered over the market any more. They stand on one long shelf.

Each scale has a face, and a needle that sits a little off. Each one is wired to the same master template, the one we drew on the shipyard floor in episode four point six. Every site bends the template a little. None of them is thrown away, and none of them is corrected.

Our simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units, comes from three sites. Site A has two hundred and forty people. Site B has one hundred and fifty. Site C has sixty, all of them older than about forty-five. In the simulation, B reads about three units above A, and C reads about four units below. Their spreads differ too.

So what does it mean to make the site a level in the hierarchy?

Look at how the data are nested. People sit inside sites. Sites sit inside a population of sites. At the bottom, a person's value is the curve of their site, plus their own noise. One level up, each site's offset is drawn from a shared distribution, and so is each site's noise level.

That shared distribution is called a random effect. The spread of the offsets, tau, says how much sites are allowed to differ. And tau is learned from the sites themselves.

Now compare this with ComBat. ComBat changes the numbers, then forgets the sites. The hierarchy keeps the raw numbers, and keeps the sites. The site's offset and its spread are parameters that the model estimates, with their uncertainty, at the same time as the age curve. Nothing is subtracted beforehand, so there is nothing to leak, and nothing real to erase by accident.

Now the scene that matters. A new merchant walks into the market. She has a scale nobody has seen. And she brings ten reference pumpkins. The number ten is illustrative.

Her ten pumpkins are all of the same kind as the reference. For a brain measure, that means healthy volunteers, scanned with the same protocol that she will use for her patients. Never the patients themselves.

We do not rebuild the whole model. Often we cannot. The original data may live elsewhere, and only the model travels. That is what the federated version of this approach, from Kia and colleagues, was built for.

Instead, we keep what the model has learned about the shelf. We know how far from the middle a typical scale sits, and how spread out the needles usually are. That is the prior for the newcomer. Her ten pumpkins update it. The question is just two numbers. How far is her needle off, and how wide is her scatter?

Here is the key moment. Ten pumpkins do not give a sharp answer. So the newcomer's offset comes out as a range, not as a point. And that range does not vanish. It travels into every score. The z-score of a person at her site carries the doubt of her small sample, as a band around it, the blue dotted band carried by MIST.

Let us put a number on that doubt. This is illustrative arithmetic, and it assumes a Gaussian bell. If you estimate a site's offset from n controls, the error is about the standard deviation divided by the square root of n. For ten people, that is about a third of a standard deviation. Every z-score at that site can slide by that much. For thirty people, it is about a fifth. For fifty, about a seventh.

The spread is harder still. For ten people, its estimate is off by roughly a quarter of its own size. For thirty, by about a seventh. For fifty, by about a tenth.

It is the same square root law as averaging epochs, in the EEG series. Quadruple the people, halve the doubt.

So, how many controls do we need? Honest answer. Nobody has a single number, and the published advice is dated. Here is what we found, as of November twenty twenty-six.

First, the braincharts demonstration, the real data set we use in later chapters. Its adaptation set holds one hundred and ten people, spread over six sites. That is between sixteen and twenty-three per site.

Second, a practical guide posted in twenty twenty-five. The authors say they cannot recommend a minimum. In their experience, results were reliable with at least thirty controls, and more is better. They suggest bootstrapping the controls, to see whether the scores stay stable.

Third, a study published in eLife in February twenty twenty-six, on a cohort called OASIS-3. When a model was pre-trained on large data and then adapted, performance levelled off at about fifty people. A model trained from scratch inside one cohort needed about two hundred. And the age of the controls had to match the age of the people being scored.

So the order of magnitude is tens, not thousands. Thirty looks like a floor, fifty like a comfortable place to start, and there is no guarantee. These numbers depend on the measure, the model and the question. Say which you used, and test its stability.

Now the pitfalls.

The first is who the controls are. Match their age and sex to the people you will score. Our site C shows why. All sixty of its people are older than about forty-five. A site that scanned only older controls teaches the model nothing about younger ones. Below that age, the model can only carry over what it learned from older people, and the blue band should open wide to say so.

The second is that the offset and the spread are not everything. If the new scanner distorts the shape of the curve, a model with an offset and a spread cannot absorb it. We check on held-out controls from the new site, as we will in chapter seven.

The third is the prior again. With ten people, the prior does much of the work. And when the shelf has only a few scales, the spread between sites is itself poorly known. The choice of its prior then matters a great deal. State it.

So, to sum up. Instead of correcting the data, we make the site a level in the model, and a new site is calibrated with a small set of matching controls. The doubt from that small set stays visible in every z-score.

PLATE 5.55:39

Sex, gender and social covariates

Sex can enter a norm as a shift of one shared curve or as separate models for each sex, and the choice costs data and should be checked on unseen controls. The files used record sex but not gender. Social covariates such as income can correct a bias or hide a real effect, so the choice is both scientific and ethical.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we put the site inside the model. A new merchant arrived with a scale nobody knew and ten reference pumpkins, and the doubt from those ten stayed visible in every score. Today the scales stay on their shelf. We look at the people who stand in the queue, and at the labels they carry. Sex, gender, and the conditions of a life.

Welcome back to the covered market. First, the label that looks simplest. Sex.

Open the real data set we use in later chapters, the braincharts transfer data. Its test file has a column called sex, with zero or one in each row. In the demonstration script, zero means female and one means male. There are two hundred and forty-two zeros, and one hundred and ninety-four ones. That column records sex as registered. It says nothing about gender. We will come back to that.

So what do we do with it? There are two common ways.

The first is to keep one model for everybody, and let sex enter as a covariate, like age. In its simplest form, sex shifts the curve up or down. The pre-trained lifespan models of Rutherford and colleagues, from twenty twenty-two, work with age, sex, and site. They report that sex effects are adequately modelled using a global offset.

That assumes the same curve shape and the same spread for both. Only the level differs.

The second way is to fit separate models, one for each sex. This is what Bethlehem and colleagues did for the brain charts of twenty twenty-two. They followed the logic of the sex-stratified growth charts. Each model then has its own curve, its own spread, and its own shape.

The price is data. Remember our new merchant, with ten reference pumpkins. Split by sex, that is about five and five. By the square root rule from last time, the offset of her scale is then uncertain by about forty-five percent of a standard deviation, instead of thirty-two. This is illustrative arithmetic, and it assumes a Gaussian bell.

Does it matter? The literature does not speak with one voice.

Ritchie and colleagues studied five thousand two hundred and sixteen people from the UK Biobank, aged forty-four to seventy-seven. Males had larger raw volumes, females had thicker cortex, and the two groups overlapped a great deal. The raw measures generally varied more among males.

A shift in the mean cannot capture a difference in spread, and the spread is what turns a deviation into a z-score.

Eliot and colleagues reviewed three decades of studies. They conclude that few differences are reliable beyond brain size, and that independent of size, sex explains only about one percent of the variance. A commentary in the same journal argued against the strongest reading of that review.

So, which way? Not by habit. Fit your choice, then check the centiles in each sex on controls the model has never seen, as we will in chapter seven. And say which you used.

Now, gender. The files we use do not hold it. Bethlehem and colleagues write that future work would benefit from more detailed and dimensional self-report variables relating to sex and gender. Until then, say which column you used, and what it records.

Now the key moment. Social covariates.

In twenty fifteen, Noble and colleagues measured the brains of one thousand and ninety-nine children and young people, aged three to twenty. Family income was associated with the surface area of the cortex, on a logarithmic scale. Among families with lower income, a small difference in income went with a large difference in surface area. Among families with higher income, the same difference went with much less. This held independently of genetic ancestry. It is an association. It does not say what causes it.

Now you build a norm. Two choices, each with a cost.

If you leave income out, the norm is the average of your reference sample. Rutherford and colleagues note that race, education, and socioeconomic variables were not fully available for all their data sets, and that their samples are likely drawn predominantly from Western, educated, industrialised, rich, and democratic societies. Bethlehem and colleagues add that their brain charts lean toward European and North American cohorts. A person from a poorer neighbourhood may then look low, when the model only says that they are unlike the people it learned from.

If you put income in, you compare each person with people of the same income. That can correct a real bias. But if living conditions shape the brain, you have just removed part of what you might want to see.

Neither is neutral. And a covariate must be measured the same way at every site.

Country and ancestry are harder still. These labels stand in for many things at once. Schooling, food, neighbourhood, access to care, even the scanner. Put one in the model, and you cannot tell which of those you removed.

So the choice is scientific, and it is also ethical. Make it for a stated question, fix it before looking at the patients, justify it, and write it on the model label, in the covariates line.

To sum up. Sex can shift the curve or split the model, gender is missing from our files, and social covariates can correct a bias or hide a real effect. Say what you chose, and why.
