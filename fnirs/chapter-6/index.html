From channels to images · DOT and HD-DOT

A classic fNIRS montage gives a topographic map of measurements, each channel a banana of tissue mixing scalp and brain. High-density diffuse optical tomography overlaps many distances, then combines a forward model of light in the head with a regularized inverse problem to reconstruct an image. Resolution and localization improve, but the depth limit and the modeling choices remain and must be reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 6, Going Further and Staying Honest. We start with a question every fNIRS map invites. Is it an image of the brain? Usually not quite. Today, how to turn channels into real images with diffuse optical tomography.

First, what a classic montage gives you. Sources and detectors about 3 cm apart, one distance per channel. Each channel is drawn as a dot, halfway between its source and detector, and the map between the dots is interpolated. It looks like an image, but each channel is a whole banana of tissue, scalp, and brain mixed together, with no idea of depth. It's a topographic map of measurements, not a picture of the cortex.

Now pack the optodes tightly. In high-density diffuse optical tomography, every source is seen by many detectors at several distances. In the system Zeff and colleagues built in 2007, 24 sources and 28 detectors gave measurements at 13, 30, 40, and 48 millimeters. Short pairs see shallow tissue, long pairs see deeper. And the bananas overlap, crisscrossing the same piece of cortex from many directions.

To turn that into an image, you need two things. The first is a forward model. Take a model of the head, scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid gray matter. Simulate how light travels through it with finite element or Monte Carlo methods, and compute for every measurement how sensitive it is to a change in every voxel. That's the sensitivity matrix, the bridge between what you measure and what you want to see. Each row is one banana.

The second is the inverse problem. You know the measurements and the matrix and you want the voxels. But there are many more voxels than measurements and the light is diffuse, so many different images could explain the same data. The problem is ill-posed. The solution is regularization. Among all possible images, prefer smooth, small ones. Turn the regularization up, and the image gets smoother and quieter but blurrier. Turn it down, and it gets sharper but noisier. Every reconstructed image hides that trade-off.

The head model matters too. Ideally, each participant's own MRI with optode positions digitized, as we saw in episode 3.4. Otherwise, an atlas head warped to the participant. Either way, the reconstruction is only as good as the geometry behind it.

So does it work. In 2007, Zeff's system mapped the visual cortex in detail. It imaged responses about 1.7 centimeters across and detected shifts of less than 1 centimeter, reproducing the classic retinotopic maps known from fMRI. In 2012, Eggebrecht and colleagues compared high-density optical images with fMRI in the same five people. The average localization error was 4.4 millimeters, less than the distance between neighboring folds of the cortex. And in 2014, they scaled up 96 sources and 92 detectors, more than 1,200 usable measurements, covering about two-thirds of the head. They mapped language and resting state networks and even imaged patients with implanted deep brain stimulators, people who cannot easily go into an MRI scanner.

The field keeps moving. Wearable, fiber-free high-density systems now exist commercially, and ultra-high-density grids with optodes about 6.5 mm apart have been built and tested, improving resolution further, with diminishing returns below that spacing.

But here is the honest part. Tomography does not remove the depth limit. Sensitivity still falls off quickly with depth, so you still see the outer centimeter or so of cortex and nothing deep. And an image is a reconstruction, not a photograph. It depends on the head model, the light model, and the regularization you chose. So report those choices, show the sensitivity of your array, and be modest about anything near the edge of what light can reach.

So, a classic montage gives you a map of measurements. High-density, multi-distance, overlapping measurements, plus a forward model and a regularized inverse, give you an image. Better resolution, real localization, but the same depth limit, and choices you must report. Next time, fNIRS in the wild.

PLATE 6.24:21

fNIRS in the wild

Four field scenes show what fNIRS gains outside the lab: infants on a parent's lap, a walk down a street, walking studies, and classrooms or bedsides. Each brings a strength and a trap, since movement, sweat, light and uncontrolled events multiply noise. Recording context video and body signals, keeping some structure, planning for data loss and handling consent early keeps naturalistic work credible.

TRANSCRIPTEN

fNIRS has a superpower that most brain imaging methods don't. You can leave the lab with it. Today, we take it out into the wild. Four scenes, each with its strength and its trap.

Scene 1, a baby on a parent's lap. No scanner, no sedation, no need to lie still. That's why fNIRS has become a favorite tool of developmental science. And because it's portable and relatively cheap, it can travel. In the Bright Project, researchers followed infants in rural Gambia and in the United Kingdom from birth to two years. In one task, with 115 Gambian and 62 British infants at five and eight months, the Gambian infants showed weaker habituation responses and no recovery to novelty at the group level. The strength, reaching children that brain imaging had rarely reached. The trap, infants move, fall asleep, and pull at the cap, their heads are smaller, their hair and skin vary, and the head models built for adults don't fit them. Everything we said about quality and motion matters twice.

Scene 2 A Street in London In 2015, Pinti and colleagues sent a participant, wearing a wireless, fiber-free headset over the prefrontal cortex, walking through a real street, performing a prospective memory task, remembering to respond to cues like a person or a parking meter, while doing something else. It was a single case, a feasibility test. But it shows the promise, real-life tasks which can reveal problems that lab tests miss. The trap, no stimulus computer controls what happens. So the team recorded everything with cameras to know afterwards when each event occurred.

Scene 3, Walking Prefrontal activity during walking, especially while doing a second task, is a popular question in aging, Parkinson's disease, or stroke. But a systematic review by Vitório and colleagues in 2017 asked a pointed question in its title, Artifact or Cortical Activity? Of 73 studies, 31 met their criteria and their protocols, and their methods for removing artifacts and noise varied widely. Walking moves the head, raises heart rate and blood pressure, and changes breathing. All of that lands in the signal. Without short channels, motion sensors, and careful controls, a walking response can be mostly body.

Scene 4, The Classroom and the Bedside Several people, real interactions, real learning, or a patient who cannot go into a scanner at all. The strength is obvious. The trap is that you control almost nothing, and events happen when they happen.

Now the common thread. Leaving the lab doesn't change the physics. It multiplies the noise. ambient light leaks in, people sweat, talk, laugh, turn their heads, temperature changes, and the body reacts to the real world much more than to a screen in a quiet room.

So the design becomes even more important than in the lab. First, record context, video, audio, eye tracking, or a phone app so that you can rebuild an event timeline afterwards. Second, record the body, accelerometers, heart rate, breathing, and short channels every time you can. Third, keep some structure. Even in the wild, you can build in repeated segments a baseline and a control condition. Naturalistic doesn't mean uncontrolled. Fourth, expect to lose data and plan for more participants and more trials than in the lab.

And finally, think about people. Recording video in public, in a classroom, or in a hospital means recording people who never signed up for your study. Plan consent, anonymization, and storage before you start, not after.

Done well, fNIRS in the wild can answer questions no scanner ever could. How the brain develops where children actually live, how it works while we move, learn, and talk. Done carelessly, it produces beautiful curves made of motion and blood pressure.

So fNIRS travels. Babies, streets walking, classrooms, hospitals. Each setting adds strength and noise. Record context and the body. Keep structure, expect losses, and protect the people around you. Next time, robustness part one. Designing a study that can fail.

PLATE 6.34:47

Robustness I · Designing a study that can fail

A study worth believing could have proved the researcher wrong. That requires a precise hypothesis with one primary test, and a sample sized for a plausible effect, since low power both misses effects and inflates those it finds. Pilots serve feasibility, not effect-size estimates, while counterbalancing, blinding, pre-registration and registered reports protect the design and make null results informative.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Robustness Part 1. A good study is not one that confirms your hypothesis. It's one that could have proved you wrong and didn't. Today, we design a study that can fail.

Start with the question, does the prefrontal cortex do something during this task? Can't really fail, something always happens somewhere. A testable hypothesis names the region, the chromophore, the contrast, the time window, and the direction. For example, oxygenated hemoglobin in left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is higher during the hard condition than the easy one, between 5 and 15 seconds after onset. Now there's one primary test. Everything else is exploratory and labeled as such.

Next, how many participants? This is where most studies fail before they start. Statistical power is the probability of detecting an effect if it really exists. In 2013, Button and colleagues analyzed hundreds of neuroscience studies and estimated a median power of just 21%. A later reanalysis showed that power varies a lot between fields, but the average picture remains. Many studies are too small.

fNIRS is no exception. In a systematic review of fNIRS neurofeedback studies, the median sample was about 20 participants. A reanalysis found that the median study had only about 22% power to detect a medium-sized behavioral effect, and 80% power only for very large ones.

Let's build a power curve for a simple within-subject comparison with a medium effect, a Cohen's D of 0.5. With 10 participants, power is around 30%. With 20 under 60, you need about 34 participants to reach 80%. For a small effect D of 0.3, you need about 90. Compare two separate groups instead, and the medium effect needs about 64 per group. And interactions like a group by condition effect need far more again.

Why does this matter beyond missing effects? Because small studies don't just miss effects. When they do find one, they exaggerate it. Here's a simulation. The true effect is D equals 0.3 with 20 participants per study. Only about one study in four reaches significance. And those significant studies report, on average, an effect of about 0.6, twice the truth. Publish only those, and the literature is full of inflated effects. Then the next study plans its sample size on that inflated number, and it's underpowered too.

So which effect size should you plan for? Not the one you hope for. Not the one from a single small pilot either. Pilot estimates are far too noisy for that. Use a meta-analysis if one exists, shrink published effects to be cautious, or define the smallest effect that would actually matter for your question and power for that.

What are pilots good for then? Feasibility. Does the cap fit? Is the signal quality acceptable? How many trials will you lose? Do participants understand the task? How long does a session really take? That's precious information and it should shape the design. Just don't use the pilot effect size to justify your sample.

Then protect the design from bias. Counterbalance the order of conditions so that fatigue or learning doesn't masquerade as an effect. Randomize trial order and jitter the intervals. Keep the person who scores or processes the data blind to condition when you can. And plan your exclusion criteria for channels, trials, and participants before the data arrive.

Finally, write it all down before you collect a single sample. A pre-registration records your hypothesis, your primary outcome, your sample size and its justification, your pre-processing pipeline, and your statistical model. A registered report goes further. The journal reviews your plan before the data exists and commits to publishing the result, whatever it is. That's the clearest sign of a study that was allowed to fail.

And if your result is null. With a well-powered, pre-registered design, a null result is informative. You can even test whether the effect is smaller than your smallest effect of interest, with equivalence testing.

So, a precise hypothesis, one primary test, a sample size built on a plausible effect, pilots for feasibility, counterbalancing, and blinding, and a plan written before the data. A study that can fail, and is worth believing when it doesn't. Next time, Robustness Part 2. Statistics that hold up.

PLATE 6.44:44

Robustness II · Statistics that hold up

Analyses can fool even honest researchers. Flexible choices in a garden of forking paths inflate false positives, double dipping on noise yields fake significance, and comparing significance levels between groups is not a test of their difference. Fixes include pre-registration, multiverse analysis, independent region selection, direct interaction tests, and permutation tests that shuffle only exchangeable units, plus intervals, equivalence tests and Bayes factors.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we designed a study that can fail. Today, robustness part two — Statistics that hold up. Because even with good data, the analysis itself can fool you, and it usually fools you in the direction you were hoping for.

Start with the garden of forking paths. Every analysis is a walk through a garden full of choices. Which channels? Which chromophore? Which time window? Which filter cutoffs? Which motion correction? Which exclusions? Which covariates? Each choice is a fork, and if you make those choices after seeing the data, even honestly, even once, your p-values no longer mean what they say.

How bad can it get? In 2011, Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn simulated four ordinary flexibilities, choosing between two outcome measures, adding a few more participants, adding a covariate, or dropping a condition. Each one alone nudged the false positive rate a little above 5%. All four together pushed it to about 61%. No fraud required. Just the freedom to choose.

The cure starts with last episode, a pre-registered plan. And when you do explore, say so, and label those results as exploratory. There's also a more ambitious option, a multiverse analysis. Instead of picking one path, run all the reasonable ones and show the whole distribution of results. If your conclusion survives most of the paths, it's robust. If it lives on only one, you know it's fragile.

The second trap, double dipping. In 2009, Kriegeskorte and colleagues looked at 134 fMRI papers in top journals and found that 42% contained at least one circular analysis, the same data used to select and then to test. Let's do it ourselves on pure noise. 40 channels, 20 participants, no real effect anywhere. Pick the three channels with the highest T values, average them, and test that region. Significant P below 1%. From nothing, the fix is independence. Select your region with data that the test won't use. Use an anatomical or a priori region. Or a separate localizer run. Or split your data, select on one half test on the other. On our noise, the split half test finds exactly what's there, nothing. The third trap is sneakier, comparing significance levels. The effect is significant in patients but not in controls so the groups differ. That's wrong. The difference between significant and not significant is not itself necessarily significant. You must test the difference directly with an interaction. Nieuwenhuis and colleagues reviewed 513 neuroscience papers in top journals. In the 157 where this comparison came up, half made the error.

Now a tool that holds up well, the permutation test. Let's build one. 16 participants, each with a difference between condition A and condition B. The observed mean difference is our statistic. If there were really no effect, the labels A and B would be interchangeable for each person. So flip them, randomly change the sign of each participant's difference and recompute the mean. Do it again and again, 5,000 times. That builds the null distribution, what you'd see if nothing were going on. Now place the real value. The p-value is simply the proportion of shuffled results at least as extreme. Here fewer than one in a thousand.

Permutation tests make few assumptions about the shape of your data. And they scale, take the maximum statistic across all channels in each shuffle, and you get a threshold that controls false positives across the whole montage at once. One warning, only shuffle what is truly exchangeable. Never shuffle individual time samples of an autocorrelated fNIRS signal. Shuffle participants' conditions or whole blocks.

A few more habits that hold up. Report confidence intervals and bootstrap them when the data are odd. Use robust methods when outliers lurk. And if you want to say there is no effect, don't rely on a non-significant p-value. Use an equivalence test or a Bayes factor which can quantify evidence for the null.

So, every analysis choice is a fork, so fix them in advance or test them all. Never select and test on the same data. Test differences directly. Build honest null distributions with permutations, shuffling only what's exchangeable. And report intervals not just stars. Next time, report it so others can redo it.

PLATE 6.54:15

Report it so others can redo it

Reproducible means same data and code give the same numbers, replicable means new data give the same conclusion, robust means the conclusion survives analysis changes. The community checklist asks strangers' questions about participants, task, device, optodes, quality control, preprocessing and analysis. Methods should read like a recipe, with data in SNIRF and BIDS, versioned code and honest reporting.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here's the final test of any study. Somewhere in another lab, a PhD student reads your paper. Could they redo it? Today, how to report and share so that the answer is yes.

First three words people often mix up. Reproducible, same data, same code, same numbers. Replicable, new data, same methods, same conclusion. And robust, the conclusion survives reasonable changes in the analysis. Reproducibility is the floor. If even you can't get the same numbers twice, nothing else matters.

In 2021, a large group of fNIRS researchers led by Yücel, von Lühmann, and Scholkmann published best practices for fNIRS publications with a checklist for authors and reviewers. Let's walk through it as questions a stranger would ask.

Who took part? Demographics, inclusion, and exclusion criteria and ethics. How many were recruited? How many were excluded? And why?

What was the task? Timings, instructions, incentives, and anything that might have influenced performance.

What device? The system, the wavelength, the sampling rate, the number of sources, detectors, channels, and short channels.

Where were the optodes? The montage, the cap, the target regions, and how positions were measured or estimated. A figure of the montage, ideally with its sensitivity on the cortex.

How was quality checked? Which metrics, which thresholds, and how many channels were rejected in each participant?

How was the signal cleaned? How motion was detected and corrected? Which filters with which cutoffs? How systemic physiology was handled and in which order?

How was it analyzed? The model, the regressors, the contrasts, the correction for multiple comparisons, and the effect sizes for both chromophores.

That's a lot. That's why the best method sections read like a recipe, not a summary. Data were processed with standard methods is not a recipe.

Next, share the data. The format is settled. SNIRF for the files, which we met in episode 3.4, and NIRS-BIDS for organizing a whole dataset, published in Scientific Data in 2025. BIDS defines where each participant's files go and which metadata travel with them. Events, channels, optode positions. The OpenfNIRS group even built a cloud tool that takes your SNIRF files and generates much of the BIDS metadata for you. Then deposit the dataset in a public repository, like OpenNeuro, which accepts fNIRS data in BIDS format.

But plan this early. Participants must consent to data sharing so it belongs in your ethics application and consent form before the first recording. And think about anonymization. Remove names and dates and be careful with videos and faces.

Then share the code. The whole pipeline as scripts from raw files to every figure and table in the paper. Record the software versions or better share an environment file or a container so the code still runs in five years. Give it a permanent identifier, for example by archiving a release on Zenodo and cite it in the paper.

And finally, be honest in the text. Report everything you tested, not just what worked. Separate confirmatory from exploratory results. Report null results. And discuss the limitations, the depth limit, the systemic confounds, the sample size.

Now imagine the payoff. Another lab downloads your dataset. They run your code. The same figure appears on their screen. Then they collect new data, follow your recipe, and find the same effect. That's science doing what it promises. And the stamp at the bottom of the page reads, Reproduced.

So reproducible, replicable, robust. Follow the community checklist. Write methods like a recipe. Share data as SNIRF in BIDS, share code with versions and an identifier, plan consent early, and report everything honestly. That closes chapter 6. Next, Chapter 7, Two Brains at Once.
