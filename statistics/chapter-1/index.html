PLATE 1.16:50

Population and sample

A cook tastes one ladleful to judge a whole pot of soup: the pot is the population, the ladle a sample. A number describing the pot is a parameter, one computed from the ladle a statistic. A stirred ladle speaks for the pot, but a ladle taken at the surface leans the same way every time, even when bigger.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A canteen, a big pot of soup, and a cook who wonders whether it needs salt. She does not drink the whole pot. She stirs it, and she tastes one ladleful. That small gesture is most of statistics: say something about a pot you cannot swallow, using a ladle you can taste.

Let us turn the pot into numbers. Ten thousand simulated people, each with a reaction time in milliseconds. This is the population: everyone we want to say something about. Where the pot ends is decided by the question. Ask about the students of one university, and the pot is those students. Ask about all adults, and the pot is much bigger. So write down the pot before you taste it.

The average of the whole pot is 423 milliseconds, and its standard deviation is 153. These two numbers describe the entire population. They are fixed, and in real life, nobody knows them. A number that describes a population is called a parameter.

Now the ladle. Take twenty people from the pot, and that is a sample. Here are our twenty, lifted out of the soup. Their average is 443 milliseconds. A number computed from a sample is called a statistic. The parameter is fixed and unknown. The statistic is computed, and known. It is our guess for the parameter, and here it is 19 milliseconds too high.

Statisticians keep two alphabets for this. The parameter gets a Greek letter: mu, for the mean of the pot. The statistic gets a Latin letter with a bar: x-bar, for the mean of the ladle. Greek for what we cannot see, Latin for what we hold.

Now a common misreading. Do not write that people take 443 milliseconds to react. Write that our twenty took 443 on average. The first sentence speaks about the pot. The second speaks about the ladle. Only the second is something we know.

The same four words fit every pot. Take the heights of children in a country, simulated. Ten thousand children, and their average height is 147 centimeters. That is the parameter. Measure thirty of them, and their average is the statistic.

Take an election. Fifty thousand voters, and 52 percent will vote yes. That is the parameter, and this time it is a share. Ask two hundred voters, and 57.5 percent say yes. That is the statistic. Not 52, but a guess for 52.

Take a clinic that scores its patients' pain. The population is every patient of the clinic. The parameter is their average score. The sample is the forty who filled in the form, and the statistic is their average. The words never change. Only the pot does.

Now the pot can change under your feet. Here is a school inside that country, six hundred pupils, a little taller than the country. We measure thirty of its pupils, and their average is 153 centimeters. Is that a good guess? It depends on the question. If the question is about the school, the pot is the school, whose true average is 153.9. The guess is under by 0.9. If the question is about the country, the pot is the country, whose average is 147. Now the same thirty pupils are 6 centimeters too high. Same data, two questions, two pots, two verdicts.

Back to the soup. Why should our first ladle say anything about the pot? Because the pot was stirred. Every person had the same chance of ending up in the ladle: one in five hundred. A sample chosen this way is called a random sample. The mixing is the whole trick.

Now let the soup settle. In real life, people do not stay mixed. Those who are easiest to reach, who answer at once, float to the top. In this illustration, they are also a little faster than the rest. Before we dip, make a guess: will a ladle taken at the surface average above 423, or below? Take a moment, and commit to an answer. Now dip. Twenty people light up, and their average is 262 milliseconds, 162 too low. Look at the surface: 84 percent of it is faster than 350 milliseconds, against 35 percent of the whole pot.

A sample taken where it is easiest is called a convenience sample. Stir the pot again, and dip. The average is 395 milliseconds, 28 too low. Any ladle misses a little. We will look at that next time. But the surface ladle misses in a different way: it leans, and it leans the same way every time. We checked, with a thousand ladles of twenty at the surface. Their averages never went above 341, and they average 273. A thousand stirred ladles of twenty average 424.

Is it always so bad? Turn a knob: how strongly being easy to reach goes with being fast. At zero, the surface is just as good as the stirred pot. The thousand surface ladles average 428. At 0.2, they average 370. At 0.4, 318. At 0.6, our setting, 273. At 0.8, 233. The stronger the link, the harder the lean. A convenience sample is harmless when the reason you reached people has nothing to do with what you measure. In real life, you rarely know where the knob stands, and that is why we stir.

Can a bigger ladle fix it? Take two hundred people from the surface. Their average is 275. A bigger ladle at the surface only tastes the surface better.

This is not a toy problem. Between 2003 and 2007, in six leading psychology journals, 96 percent of participants came from Western industrialized countries, which hold 12 percent of humanity. And in one of those journals, two thirds of the American samples were psychology undergraduates. They were the easiest to reach. Whether they speak for everyone is the question the ladle cannot answer.

The same trap hides in our clinic. Ask only the patients who come to their appointments, and the ones who stay home are in the pot, but never in the ladle. Whatever made them hard to reach may be tied to their pain.

Here is the catch. In real life, the pot has a lid. You never see the population. You see one ladle. All of statistics is the craft of saying something about the pot with a ladle, and saying by how much you could be wrong.

Remember: the population is the pot, a sample is the ladle, and the ladle speaks for the pot only if the pot was stirred. Next time, in episode 1.2: a sample is not a small population.

PLATE 1.26:50

A sample is not a small population

Twenty ladles from the same pot give twenty different means, because each handful differs. That is sampling variability, unlike measurement error from a jittery scale. Small ladles wander most: the most extreme means all come from ladles of five. Coin flips, sixty equally good hospitals and small schools crowd both ends of rankings by chance alone.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the canteen. Last time we said that any ladle misses a little. Today we find out how much, and why. Here is the question. If two ladles come from the same pot, do they give the same answer?

Take the pot again. Ten thousand simulated people, each with a reaction time in milliseconds. The average of the whole pot is 423, and the spread is 153. Now dip a ladle of twenty people and compute its mean. Here it is: 437, which is 14 too high. Empty the ladle and dip again. 434. A third ladle: 371, which is 52 too low. A fourth: 462. A fifth: 411.

Same pot. Same size of ladle. Same way of dipping. Five different answers. Keep going, until there are twenty ladles, and put each mean on a ruler of milliseconds. They spread out on both sides of the pot's average: ten above it, ten below. The lowest is 340, and the highest is 503. All twenty are different.

Did somebody make a mistake? No. Each mean was computed exactly. A ladle is only a handful of people, and every handful is different. A sample is not a small population. It is one handful, and it does not copy the pot.

Here is a common misreading. Two studies take a ladle from the same pot, and they disagree. The first says 437. The third says 371. So one of the two studies must be wrong. No. Both are honest handfuls. They disagree because they are different handfuls, not because somebody slipped.

Compare this with another kind of wobble. Take the first ladleful, and weigh it on a scale that jitters a little. This is an illustration: a jitter of five milliseconds. Weigh the same twenty people twenty times, and the readings run from 428 to 444. That is measurement error: the same ladleful, read badly. The twenty ladles ran from 340 to 503. That is sampling variability: different ladlefuls, each read perfectly. Buy a perfect scale, and the first wobble disappears. The second one stays. It comes from the ladle, not from the instrument.

So does the size of the ladle matter? Before we look, make a guess. We will take ladles of 5 people, of 20, and of 100. Which size will scatter its means the most? Decide now. Try ladles of 5 people, of 20, and of 100: twenty ladles of each, from the same pot. Ladles of five run from 320 to 485. Ladles of twenty run from 340 to 503. In these twenty draws, those two stretches are almost the same. Twenty draws is not many. Ladles of a hundred run from 394 to 451, a much narrower stretch. Now count the ladles that land more than 50 milliseconds from the pot's average. Eight out of twenty for ladles of five. Three for ladles of twenty. None for ladles of a hundred.

To be sure it is not luck, ladle a thousand times for each size. Ladles of five give means from 264 to 722. Ladles of twenty give means from 327 to 529. Ladles of a hundred give means from 377 to 483. How many land more than 100 milliseconds from the pot's average? For ladles of five, 121 out of a thousand. For ladles of twenty, one. For ladles of a hundred, none.

Now take the thirty most extreme means out of all three thousand, whether too low or too high. All thirty come from ladles of five. The most extreme results, in both directions, come from the smallest samples.

Is this only about reaction times? No. Take a fair coin: half heads, half tails. Flip it 5 times, and note the share of heads. Do it twenty times. The share runs from 0 percent to 100. Now flip it 20 times, twenty times over. The share runs from 20 to 70. Flip it 100 times: from 39 to 61. Repeat each ten thousand times, and count the runs where one face takes at least 80 percent of the flips. With 5 flips, 3,658 runs out of ten thousand. With 20 flips, 127. With 100 flips, none. So four heads in five flips does not prove a loaded coin. A small ladle of flips wanders.

Now a league table. Sixty hospitals, all fictional, and all exactly equally good: every patient recovers with a chance of 90 percent. Each hospital treats a different number of patients, from 21 to 980. At the end of the year, we rank them by recovery rate. The best hospital treated 29 patients, and all 29 recovered. The worst treated 22, and 17 recovered. Look at the ten best and the ten worst. Five of the best are among the twenty smallest hospitals. Eight of the worst are too. Nobody is better. The small ones just wander more, in both directions.

This is not only a story. Howard Wainer looked at 1,662 Pennsylvania schools. Among the 50 best-scoring schools, six were among the smallest three percent, four times what chance alone would give. Among the 50 lowest-scoring, nine were among the 50 smallest. Small schools crowd both ends. He found the same in American counties: the lowest and the highest kidney cancer rates both go to small counties. A county of a hundred people with no cancer death would rank among the lowest. With one death, among the highest.

One last consequence. A researcher tests a pretend treatment that does nothing. Two groups of 5 people, from our pot, and no difference between them. Compare the two means. Run a thousand such studies. In 296 of them, the two means differ by more than 100 milliseconds. One group looks much faster, from nothing at all. With 100 people per group, none of the thousand does.

Here is the catch. In real life, you get one ladle. Whatever its size, its mean wobbles around the pot's average, and the smaller the ladle, the more it wobbles. So when a small study gives a striking number, remember the ladle of five.

To remember: a sample is not a small population. Every ladle gives a different mean, even with a perfect instrument, and small ladles wander the most. Next time, in episode 1.3: the sampling distribution.

PLATE 1.36:46

The sampling distribution

Ladling a thousand times and stacking the means builds the sampling distribution, the most important picture of the series. It centres on the pot's average, 424 against 423 milliseconds, and is far narrower than the people themselves. In real life only one tile of this pile is seen, yet standard errors, intervals and p-values all describe it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the canteen. Last time, twenty ladles from the same pot gave twenty different averages. Today we stop arguing with each ladle. We keep ladling, a thousand times, and we stack the answers. The pile we get has a name. It is the sampling distribution, and it is the most important picture in this series.

Here is our pot, turned into numbers. Ten thousand simulated people, each with a reaction time in milliseconds. Most of them answer between 300 and 600 milliseconds. A few are slow, and they stretch a long tail to the right. The average of the whole pot is 423 milliseconds, and the spread, the standard deviation, is 153. These are the parameters of the population. They are fixed. In real life, nobody gets to see them.

Now the machine. On the left, the population, in grey dots. In the middle, the ladle. It takes twenty people at random, and they light up in pink. We compute one number from them: their mean. Here it is 469 milliseconds. That number falls as a tile into the histogram on the right. One draw.

Empty the ladle and dip again. Twenty new people. This time the mean is 428. A second tile. Again: 457. Again: 405. Again: 539, a ladle that happened to catch several slow people. Each tile is one possible study, with the same pot, the same ladle, and a different stroke of luck.

Before the machine runs, make a guess. The pot leans to the right, and its average is 423. Will the pile of means centre below 423, on 423, or above it? Decide now.

Let the machine run. Ten draws. A hundred. A thousand. The tiles stop looking like noise. They build a shape. That shape is the sampling distribution of the mean. It lists all the answers a study of twenty people could have given, and how often each one comes up.

Read it like a map. First, where it sits. The thousand means average 424 milliseconds, right on top of the population mean. If you guessed below or above, the ladle says no. It is not biased: it misses to the left as often as to the right. Second, how wide it is. The means run from 339 to 549, with a standard deviation of about 34 milliseconds. People spread by 153. Their means spread four and a half times less. Third, its shape. The population leans to the right. The means lean much less. Episode 1.6 will explain why.

Now line up three histograms on the same axis, because they are easy to confuse. At the top, the population: ten thousand people. It says what people are like. In the middle, one sample: twenty people. It is the only thing a real study ever sees. At the bottom, the sampling distribution: a thousand means. It says how far our number would move if we ran the study again. Same unit, milliseconds. Three different questions.

Now a misreading that catches many readers. Someone looks at the bottom histogram and says: nine people in ten answer between 375 and 475 milliseconds. No. Only about one person in four does. It is nine means in ten that land in that window. The mean of twenty people is far more predictable than any one person. That is the whole reason we average.

Is this only true for reaction times? Turn one knob: the shape of the pot. The machine stays the same, and so does the ladle of twenty. Here is a new simulated pot, of ten thousand hospital stays, counted in days. Most stays are short. A few last for weeks, and the longest lasts 91 days. This pot is far more lopsided than the reaction times. Its average is 4.5 days, but its middle, the median, is only 3.

Before we dip, guess again. Will the pile of means for ladles of twenty centre on the median, or on the average? Decide now. Here is the pile, from a thousand ladles. It centres on 4.6 days. On the average of the pot, not on its median. The means run from 2.1 to 10 days, and this pile still leans to the right, more than the reaction times did. How a pile gets its shape is a story for episode 1.6. What the knob did not move is where the pile sits: on the average of the pot.

Now use that pile. Say a hospital claims that its stays average 4.5 days. We take twenty stays, and they average 7 days. Is that an ordinary ladle? Count in the pile. Only 33 means in a thousand reach 7 days. A single stay of 7 days or more is common: about one in five. But a ladle of twenty that averages so much is rare. A pile tells you which ladles to expect from a pot, and so which ones should surprise you.

Here is the catch. In real life, there is no machine. You run one study. You get one ladle, one mean, one tile. The other nine hundred and ninety-nine tiles belong to studies nobody ran. The third histogram is invisible.

Suppose three labs run this study. They report 405, 539 and 358 milliseconds. Nobody made a mistake: each lab holds one tile of the pile. But no lab can tell, from its own tile alone, how wide the pile is, or where its middle sits.

And yet almost every number in a results section is a statement about it. The standard error is its width. A confidence interval is a net whose size comes from that width. A p-value is an area in its tail, drawn in a world where nothing is going on. When a paper reports one of these, it describes a histogram that nobody has ever seen.

So how can anyone know its shape from a single sample? There are two roads. The first is mathematics: under some assumptions, a formula gives the width and the shape without running anything. The second is simulation: we build the machine ourselves, from the one sample we have, by resampling or by shuffling. This series takes both roads, and chapter 14 is built on the second.

To remember: the population describes people, a sample is what we see, and the sampling distribution describes our statistic, study after imaginary study. Its pile centres on the pot's average, whatever the shape of the pot. Next time, we measure the width of that invisible histogram. It is called the standard error.

PLATE 1.47:05

Standard error

Ladles of four, sixteen and sixty-four people show the spread of means halving each time the ladle holds four times more people. The standard error is the standard deviation divided by the square root of n. The standard deviation describes people and does not shrink; the standard error describes our ignorance of the mean and does.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the canteen. Last time we stacked a thousand ladle means and got the sampling distribution. Its width was about 34 milliseconds, while the people in the pot spread by 153. Today we ask what sets that width. Can we make it narrower? And by how much?

Start with small ladles, of four people. Dip two thousand times and stack the means. The pile is wide. The spread of those means is 79 milliseconds.

Now, before we look, make a guess. Take ladles of sixteen, four times as many people. Will the spread be a quarter of 79? Half of it? Or about the same? Decide now. Here is the pile. It squeezes in, and the spread is 37. About half, not a quarter. Now ladles of sixty-four. The spread is 19.

Look at the sizes: four, sixteen, sixty-four. Each ladle holds four times as many people as the one before. And each time, the width is cut roughly in half. From 79 to 37, and from 37 to 19.

Now let the ladle grow slowly, from four people up to two hundred and fifty-six, and watch the pile. It keeps narrowing, fast at first, then more and more slowly. At two hundred and fifty-six people, the spread is 9.6 milliseconds. Four times more people again, and again only half of the width is gone.

Why four times? Here is a picture. Put the people of a ladle in a square. Four people make a square of two by two. Sixteen people make four by four. Sixty-four make eight by eight. The side of the square is the square root of the number of people. The width of the pile goes down with the side, not with the number of people. To double the side, you need four times the people.

That is the rule. The standard error of the mean is the standard deviation of the people, divided by the square root of n. Let us check it. The spread of the pot is 153. For ladles of sixty-four, divide by the square root of sixty-four, which is eight. That gives 19.1. The machine gave 18.9. For ladles of sixteen, divide by four: 38.3. The machine gave 36.9. For ladles of four, divide by two: 76.6, against 79.2 from the machine. The formula and the simulation agree. The rule holds when the people in a ladle are drawn independently of each other. Later in the series, we will see what happens when they are not.

This rule has a price. Ten more people do not buy much. To cut the standard error in half, you need four times the data. To cut it by ten, you need a hundred times the data. A study that wants to be twice as precise must be four times as large.

Does the rule belong to reaction times? Let us try another world: adult heights. This is a new simulated pot of ten thousand people. Their average is 170 centimeters, and their spread is 9. Run two thousand studies of 36 people. The rule says nine divided by six: about 1.5 centimeters. The machine gives 1.47. Different pot, same rule.

Now use the rule backwards, the way someone planning a study would. You want to know the average height with a standard error of half a centimeter. The spread of people is 9, and nobody can change that. So divide nine by one half. That is 18. Now square it: 324 people. Check it: two thousand studies of 324 people give a spread of 0.49. And if one centimeter is enough? Nine squared is 81 people. Twice the precision costs four times the people: from 81 to 324.

Now, the second half of the story. The standard error is easy to confuse with the standard deviation, and many figures show both as little bars. So take one real study of twenty people from the reaction-time pot. Its mean is 432 milliseconds, and its standard deviation is 153. Divide by the square root of twenty, and the standard error is 34.

Draw the same data twice. First with a bar of plus or minus one standard deviation. It reaches 153 on each side of the mean, and it covers most of the people. In the whole pot, about 73 people in 100 lie within one standard deviation of the mean. This bar tells you where the people are. Now draw the same data with a bar of plus or minus one standard error. It reaches only 34 on each side. It says nothing about where people are. It says how far the mean might move if we ran the study again. Same data, two bars, two different stories. So whatever bar a figure shows, it must say which one it is.

Now collect more data. Eighty people: the mean is 420, the standard deviation 157, the standard error 18. Three hundred and twenty people: the mean is 423, the standard deviation 156, the standard error 9. The standard deviation bar stays about the same length. More data do not make people more alike. The standard error bar shrinks. The standard deviation describes the people. The standard error describes our ignorance of the mean, and more data reduce that ignorance.

Here is a mistake you will meet in real papers. A figure shows the mean height of 36 adults: 170.7 centimeters, with a short whisker of plus or minus 1.9. A reader thinks: so most adults here are between 169 and 173. Let us check. Here are the 36 people. Only 3 of them fall inside the whisker. That whisker is a standard error. It describes the mean, not the people. In this study the people spread by 11, and 28 of the 36 lie within one standard deviation. The fix is simple. Read the label, and when the question is about people, show the people.

One more catch. In real life, there is no machine and you have one study. But you do not need the machine. You compute the standard deviation of your own sample, divide by the square root of n, and you have an estimate of the width of the invisible histogram. That estimate wanders too. In a thousand studies of twenty people, the estimates ran from 17 to 72. Most of them fell between 24 and 44. On average they gave 34, close to the true width of about 35. A single estimate is a good guide, not a measurement.

To remember: the standard deviation describes the people, and it does not shrink when you collect more. The standard error describes our ignorance of the mean, and it shrinks with the square root of n. To halve it, you need four times the data. Next time, in episode 1.5: the law of large numbers.

PLATE 1.57:17

The law of large numbers

A fair coin tossed ten thousand times shows the share of heads settling on one half while the gap between heads and tails keeps wandering, typically about a hundred. Kerrich's real coin and Monte Carlo's twenty-six blacks illustrate the point. The coin has no memory, so streaks are diluted, never repaid; the law fails when the average does not exist.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the canteen, someone has left a coin on the counter. Everybody knows that if you toss it many times, heads and tails even out. Is that true? Half of it is. Today we separate the true half from the false half. The coin is fair, and everything today is simulated.

The law behind this is old. In seventeen thirteen, eight years after the death of Jacob Bernoulli, his book was published. It holds the first version of the law of large numbers, which he called his golden theorem.

Here is one run of ten thousand tosses. The pink line is the share of heads so far. After ten tosses it is 0.4: four heads. After a hundred tosses, 0.41. After a thousand, 0.458. After ten thousand, 0.498. The line settles on one half.

Now count differently: heads minus tails. After ten tosses, tails are ahead by 2. After a hundred, by 18. After a thousand, by 84. At its worst, tails were ahead by 139. After ten thousand, by 38. This gap does not settle. It wanders, and nothing pulls it back to zero.

Look at the same run through a lens that divides the gap by the number of tosses, raised to a power. At power zero, we see the gap itself, wandering. At power one half, the trail keeps about the same size: the square root rule of the last video. At power one, we divide by n itself, and the trail is pressed flat against zero. That flat trail is the share of heads. The gap grows, but more slowly than the tosses.

One run could be luck. So toss the coin ten thousand times, a thousand times over. Before we look, make a guess. At ten thousand tosses, how far apart are heads and tails, typically? Close to zero, about a hundred, or about a thousand? Decide now.

On the left, the gaps. The cloud opens like a funnel. Measured over the thousand runs, its spread is 3.1 at ten tosses, 9.8 at a hundred, 31 at a thousand, and 100 at ten thousand. About a hundred. On the right, the share of heads: the same runs close like a funnel. The spread is 15.5 points at ten tosses, 4.9 at a hundred, 1.5 at a thousand, and 0.5 at ten thousand.

Multiply the tosses by a hundred. The spread of the gap is multiplied by ten. The spread of the share is divided by ten. And in 93 runs out of 100, the gap at ten thousand tosses is bigger than the gap at a hundred.

The law promises nothing about how fast, or about a short run. At ten tosses, one run in ten has at most 20 percent heads, or at least 80 percent.

Is this only a computer game? During the Second World War, a mathematician named John Kerrich was interned in Denmark. He tossed a real coin ten thousand times and counted 5,067 heads. That is 50.67 percent: close to one half. It is also 134 more heads than tails, a bit more than the typical gap of a hundred. Both statements are true at once. The share is nearly one half. The gap is not nearly zero.

Now the misreading. Some think the law is a debt collector: after many tails, heads must come to even things out. This belief is the gambler's fallacy. In 1913, at the Monte Carlo casino, the ball of a roulette wheel fell on black twenty six times in a row. Gamblers kept betting on red, sure that red was due. It was not.

The coin has no memory. Guess: after ten tails in a row, is heads more likely, less likely, or the same? Decide now. We tossed twenty million simulated coins and looked for every moment when a streak of ten tails had just been completed. There are 9,826 of them. On the next toss, heads came up 5,054 times: 51.4 percent. Chance alone wobbles by half a point, so this is almost three wobbles above one half. That is rare, so we repeat with fresh tosses: 9,962 streaks, 4,944 heads, 49.6 percent. After five tails in a row, 50.0 percent. After three, 50.0 percent. One chance in two, every time.

So what does the law do with a streak of ten tails? It dilutes it. We followed the next thousand tosses after each streak. On average, heads minus tails moved by minus half a toss, which is zero within the wobble. The hole of ten tails is not refilled. It just becomes small. Ten tails ahead among a thousand and ten tosses means 49.5 percent heads.

Now other quantities than coins. Roll a die ten thousand times. The running average heads for 3.5, and it reads 3.49. The running total, compared to 3.5 times the rolls, was off by 161 at its worst. Take the heights of people through the canteen door, illustrative. The average reads 170.03 centimeters, for a true 170. The total height was off by more than thirteen meters at one point.

Weigh a hundred gram mass ten thousand times on a noisy scale, illustrative. The average reads 100.00 grams. But single readings still scatter by about half a gram. The next reading is no better than the first.

In a poll where 52 percent favor an idea, illustrative, the share reads 57 percent after a hundred answers and 51.1 after ten thousand. The count of supporters was off by 104 at its worst.

Two dice making a twelve: a rare event, one in thirty six. The share reads 2.85 percent, close to 2.78. A free throw shooter who hits 75 percent of shots, illustrative: 75.0 percent after ten thousand shots, and a streak of 7 misses along the way. After three misses in a row, the next shot still goes in 75.1 percent of the time. In every case, a share or an average settles. A total's distance from its expected value, or the next outcome, does not.

Finally, where the law needs care. It needs the average to exist. Divide one random number by another. This ratio has a monstrous tail: now and then one draw is enormous. Here is a running average of this ratio. After ten draws it is minus 10.6. After a hundred, minus 1.1. After a thousand, 2.6. After ten thousand, minus 44. It never settles. Over two thousand runs, the typical distance from zero of a well behaved average falls from 0.214 to 0.007. For this ratio it stays about 1. More data does not help.

Remember: the share of heads settles, the gap between the counts does not, and the coin has no memory. Next, the central limit theorem: why the pile of ladle means is a bell.

PLATE 1.66:47

The central limit theorem

Adding dice copies, slides and stacks the flat shape until it becomes a bell: the bell is the shape of sums, not of data. Ladle means from the skewed reaction-time pot fit it near thirty people, but hospital stays need about a thousand, and the Cauchy pot never does. Draws must also be independent, as the ten tables show.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a die. Roll it ten thousand times and count each face.

The bars come out flat. Every face is as likely as any other.

Now roll two dice and add them. Ten thousand times again. The bars make a triangle. Seven is the most common sum. Two and twelve are rare.

Why a triangle? Count the ways. Only one way makes two. Six ways make seven. Adding a second die copies the flat shape six times, slides each copy along by one step, and stacks them. The stack is a triangle.

Add a third die, a fourth, a fifth. Each die copies the shape, slides it, and stacks it. After five dice the corners are rounded off. The shape is a bell. Ten thousand rolls agree with the count. Lay a bell-shaped stencil over them, and it fits closely.

Out of seven thousand seven hundred and seventy six ways to roll five dice, one way makes five. And seven hundred and eighty ways make seventeen. Extreme sums need every die to go the same way. Middle sums have thousands of ways to happen.

People saw this long ago. In seventeen thirty three, Abraham de Moivre approximated the number of heads in many coin tosses with a bell curve. The name central limit theorem came later, from George Pólya, in nineteen twenty.

Does it need fair dice? Load the die, so that face one comes up sixty percent of the time. One loaded die is a tall bar on the left, and a low plateau. Guess: do five loaded dice make a bell? Five loaded dice still lean to the left. Twenty loaded dice are close to a bell. A lopsided die only needs more dice. Notice that the dice never changed. Only their number did.

Back to the canteen. The pot holds ten thousand reaction times. The mean is four hundred twenty three milliseconds, the spread one hundred fifty three. And the pot is not a bell. It leans to the right, with a long tail of slow people.

But a ladle mean is a sum of draws, divided by n. Just like the dice. So will the pile of ladle means turn into a bell?

We will lay a bell-shaped stencil over each pile. To compare shapes fairly, every pile is rescaled to the same width. The squeeze from the last episode is gone. Only the shape remains. Each panel shows one ladle size, with thousands of ladles behind it. A number measures the gap to the stencil. Zero means identical.

Guess before you look. How many people must a ladle hold before its pile fits the stencil? Five? Thirty? A hundred?

A ladle of one person is the pot itself. The gap is eight hundredths. Two people: the lean softens. Five. Ten, and a small lean is still visible on the right. Thirty people: the gap is two hundredths, as small as this simulation can tell. A hundred looks the same. The lean itself, its skewness, falls from one point three for one person to two tenths for thirty.

So for this pot, about thirty is enough. Keep the words, for this pot.

Now a common misreading. With enough data, my data become normal. Here is one study of three hundred people. The data are still leaning right, with a long tail. More people draw the pot more faithfully, and the pot leans. The bell belongs to the means, not to the people.

Is thirty always enough? Take another pot, illustrative: one hundred thousand hospital stays, in days. The median is four days. The mean is eight. Most stays last a few days, and a few last for months. This pot leans far harder than the reaction times. Guess again: will thirty be enough here?

Ladles of five: the gap is fifteen hundredths. Thirty people: still nine hundredths, and the pile still leans. A hundred: just over five hundredths. Three hundred: four. A thousand: two hundredths, at last the stencil.

So there is no magic n. Thirty is a habit, not a theorem. The speed depends on how skewed the pot is, and how heavy its tail. A team that trusts the bell for the mean stay of thirty patients leans on the stencil too early.

One more pot, a strange one. Its values follow the Cauchy law. Think of a ratio of two noisy measurements, when the bottom one can fall close to zero. Most values are small. Now and then one is enormous.

Guess again. If I average more and more draws, will the average settle? Watch the running mean, draw after draw. For the canteen pot it settles near the pot mean. After three thousand draws it reads four hundred twenty six. For the Cauchy stream it keeps wandering. At draw two thousand four hundred forty seven, one single value of three thousand four hundred twenty six shoves the whole average.

Now the spread of ladle means. For the canteen, a ladle of a thousand people has three percent of the spread of one person. For the Cauchy pot, a ladle of a thousand is as spread out as a single draw. Averaging a thousand values is no better than taking one.

In a thousand Cauchy draws, the largest value is typically seventeen percent of the total. In the canteen pot, it is a third of one percent. The theorem needs a finite spread. Cauchy has none. Its mean does not even exist.

One last condition. The draws must be independent. Take a hundred people seated at ten tables of ten. Everyone at a table shares the same noisy shift, a queue or a bad day. Guess: would the spread of the mean be the same as for a hundred independent people?

It is not. The spread comes out at thirty five milliseconds. A hundred independent people would give eighteen. Tables are one example. Classmates, twins and patients of the same ward also resemble each other. The ladle is half as informative as its size claims. The bell is still there, but twice as wide as the formula from the last episode promised.

Say it with the canteen. The ladle mean is not a bell because the soup is a bell. It is a bell because it is a sum.

Three pots, three answers. The canteen needed about thirty people. The hospital stays needed about a thousand. The Cauchy pot needed infinity, and never got there.

Why does the bell matter? Because it tells us how far a ladle mean can stray from the pot mean. Every interval and every p-value that follows leans on that shape. Now you know when to trust it: with many independent draws, and a pot without a monstrous tail.

Remember: the bell is the shape of sums, not of data. No n is magic, and the draws must be independent.
