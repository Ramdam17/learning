Embeddings: turning anything into a necklace

An embedding is a necklace of numbers an encoder loom produces for a word, picture or signal. The cosine between necklaces finds nearest neighbours: suns match suns in a toy set of twelve pictures. Rotating the space changes every bead but leaves cosines intact, so meaning lives in distances, not single beads, and flat maps such as t-SNE can mislead about those distances.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the bead workshop. Here, anything can be threaded on a string: a word, a picture, even a slice of EEG. The string holds a list of numbers, which we draw as a necklace. By the end of this video, you will know what an embedding is, how to find the picture most like another one, and why the beads themselves are not the point.

Meet ENKO, our encoder. ENKO is a little loom. A picture slides in at the top, and a necklace hangs out at the bottom. That necklace is an embedding: a list of numbers that stands for the thing you gave to the loom.

Our loom is a toy, and so are the pictures: tiny, made up, twelve pixels on a side. Its eight beads simply measure things like brightness on each side, and how much the picture changes along rows and columns. A real encoder learns its own measurements, as we will see later.

Now we feed it twelve pictures: three suns over a hill, three sets of stripes, three sets of bars, and three targets. Each one becomes a necklace, and we hang them all on a rail. Necklaces from the same kind of picture show a similar pattern of pink and blue.

So how do we find the picture most like a given one? We use the compass from episode two point one. Take the cosine between the necklace of the query, here the first sun, and every other necklace on the rail. Then sort, from the highest to the lowest.

The two nearest necklaces are the other two suns, with cosines of zero point nine eight and zero point eight six. Then the numbers fall: zero point three eight for a target, and nearly zero for a set of stripes. Further down, the cosine turns negative. Run the same search from each of the twelve pictures, and the nearest neighbour is always a picture of the same kind.

In NumPy, the whole search is a matrix product and a sort. This is nearest-neighbour search, and much of what we do with embeddings starts there.

The same trick works on words. In twenty thirteen, Tomas Mikolov and his colleagues at Google trained word vectors, known as word2vec, by teaching a small network to predict words from the words around them. Words used in similar contexts received similar necklaces. Differences between words carry meaning too: researchers showed that the vector for king, minus the vector for man, plus the vector for woman, lands closest to the vector for queen.

Now for the important point. Look at bead number three. Does it mean something on its own? Not really. Watch. We mix the eight beads together by rotating the whole space. Every necklace changes. Every bead is different. But the cosines between necklaces stay the same, up to computer rounding, and so do the nearest neighbours.

For similarity by cosine, the choice of axes is arbitrary. An embedding is useful for its distances, not for its beads taken one by one.

One last trap, about maps. To look at thousands of necklaces, people flatten them onto a page with tools such as t-SNE or UMAP. A flat map always loses something. Here is a plain flattening of our rail, using the two directions with the most variation. One set of bars and one target, with a cosine of only zero point one one, land almost side by side.

The t-SNE method is cleverer than that, but it has its own traps. A well-known guide in Distill, from twenty sixteen, shows that in a t-SNE plot, cluster sizes can mean nothing, the distances between clusters may mean nothing, and random noise can look like clusters. The caution applies to any flat map. Use a map to get ideas, then check them with cosines, in the full space.

One last link, with the Quantum series. A function that scores how similar two things are is called a kernel, and the dot product of two embeddings is the simplest one. In episode fourteen point two of the Quantum series, a kernel is the inner product of two quantum states that encode two data points.

To sum up. An embedding is a necklace of numbers that stands for a word, a picture or a signal. The cosine between two necklaces finds the neighbours that look alike. The meaning lives in the distances, not in the beads, and flat maps can mislead about them. Next time: latent space, what happens when a picture must squeeze through just a few beads, and come back.

PLATE 4.26:15

Latent space

A latent space is what fits through a narrow bottleneck: a 784-pixel picture squeezed into eight beads and rebuilt on the other side. Principal component analysis serves as a linear stand-in, and fewer beads mean blurrier copies. Walking in the space changes the picture smoothly, but latent means hidden, not meaningful: a bead may match a knob, mix several, or match none.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the bead workshop. Today we squeeze a picture through the eye of a needle. A picture is hundreds of numbers, and a necklace is only a few. By the end of this video, you will know what a latent space is, what you can do inside it, and what the word latent does not promise.

Here is our picture: a small image, twenty-eight pixels on a side. That is seven hundred and eighty-four numbers. It shows one bar, drawn with NumPy. The bar has three knobs, the ones we turned to draw it: its length, its angle, and its thickness. Here are a dozen of them. These pictures are made up, so they are illustrative. We drew three thousand to learn from, and five hundred more, kept aside for testing.

Now the rules of the game. The picture must go through the eye of a needle, and come out on the other side. Only eight beads fit through the eye. On the far side, a second machine must rebuild the whole picture from those eight beads alone. Seven hundred and eighty-four numbers go in, eight numbers pass through the middle, and seven hundred and eighty-four come out. That narrow middle is called a bottleneck.

What fits in the eight beads is the latent space. Every picture becomes one necklace of eight beads, which is one point in a space with eight dimensions. Latent comes from the Latin word latere, to lie hidden. The beads are hidden in the sense that nobody draws them: the machine computes them.

We need a machine for the bottleneck. A real one is learned, and we build it properly in chapter six. Today we use a stand-in, and we say so clearly: it is linear, and it is only illustrative. It is called principal component analysis, or PCA. Karl Pearson described the idea in nineteen oh one, as the line or plane that fits a cloud of points best. Harold Hotelling worked it out, under the name principal components, in nineteen thirty-three. PCA looks at the three thousand pictures and finds the eight directions along which they vary the most. The code of a picture is how far it sits along each of them. In NumPy, one singular value decomposition gives those directions.

And you already know the tools. From episode two point one, a code is a vector, so distances and cosines work on it. From episode two point two, the decoder is a matrix: multiply the necklace by it, and out comes a picture.

Does it work? Here are four test pictures that the machine never saw, and what comes out of their eight beads alone. The bars come back, a little blurred, with faint halos. We measure the damage as the share of the pictures' variation that is lost. On the five hundred test pictures, ten point eight percent is lost.

Make the eye wider, and the copy gets sharper. With two beads, thirty-six percent is lost. With four, twenty-one. With eight, eleven. With sixteen, four. With thirty-two, one point two. And with seven hundred and eighty-four beads, nothing is lost, but nothing was learned: the necklace is just the picture written again. A bottleneck is useful because it is narrow.

Now let's walk in this space. Take the necklace of one picture, and nudge a single bead. The picture changes. Nudge it further, and it changes further, without jumping. Bead one, pushed one way, turns the bar one way. Pushed the other way, it turns the bar back.

We can also walk between two pictures. Take the necklaces of two test pictures, and move from one to the other in seven even steps. At each step, decode the necklace. The bar turns from one angle to the other, almost like a film. One honest warning. Our decoder is linear, so each in-between picture is exactly a cross-fade of the two rebuilt pictures. We checked: the difference is zero, up to computer rounding. It looks smooth here only because the two bars are close. A learned decoder is free to do better, and that is part of why chapter six exists.

Now the important point. We said latent means hidden. It does not mean meaningful. Nobody told PCA about length, angle, or thickness. It found its eight directions alone. So we can ask each bead: does it match one of our knobs? We computed the correlation of every bead with every knob, over the three thousand training pictures.

Bead one correlates zero point nine five with the angle. For practical purposes, it is an angle dial. That is the readable case.

Bead four is different. It correlates zero point seven one with thickness, and zero point five eight with length. It mixes two knobs. Push it, and the bar gets fatter and longer at once. It does follow the amount of ink, with zero point nine two, so it has a name, but it is not one of our three.

Bead three is weaker still. Its correlation with each of our knobs is at most zero point two four. If it means something, our three knobs do not say what.

And bead two is a trap. Its correlation with the angle is zero. Yet with the size of the tilt, whichever way it leans, it is minus zero point nine six. Correlation only sees straight-line links, so it can miss a meaning that is really there.

There is a second reason not to read beads one by one. Remember the rotation of episode four point one. Rotate the eight axes, and decode with the matching rotation. The pictures come back the same, up to rounding, yet every bead is different, and the angle is now smeared across several of them. The space is the same. Only the axes changed.

One pointer to where this goes. In two thousand and six, Geoffrey Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov reported in Science that deep autoencoders, trained to rebuild their input through a small central layer, gave better low-dimensional codes than PCA. That is the machine we build in chapter six.

To sum up. A bottleneck forces a picture through a few beads, and what fits is the latent space. Walking in it changes the picture smoothly, though our linear stand-in can only cross-fade. Latent means hidden and learned, not meaningful: a direction may match a knob, mix several, or match none of our names. Next time: what makes a good representation?

PLATE 4.34:17

What makes a good representation?

A good representation makes needed information easy to read, which a linear probe tests with one taut thread. Two rings of points defeat any thread in raw coordinates, yet a single bead giving distance from the center separates them perfectly. A failed probe does not mean the information is absent; a good necklace also drops junk beads, but discarding something like color is irreversible.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the bead workshop. The loom hangs necklaces on the racks, and every necklace is a list of numbers. But some racks are much easier to use than others. By the end of this video, you will know a simple test for a good representation, and what a good one keeps and throws away.

Meet LENS, our inspector. LENS carries just one tool: a single taut thread. The test goes like this. Lay the thread across the rack, so that everything on one side gets one answer, and everything on the other side gets the other. If one straight thread is enough, the information is easy to read. This test is called a linear probe.

Here is a toy rack, made up for the occasion. Forty points, two kinds, twenty of each. The first necklace has two beads: the two coordinates of a point. The first kind sits on an inner ring, the second kind on an outer ring. Nothing is hidden: the answer is all there. But lay the thread anywhere, and it cuts both kinds. Trained on four hundred points, and tested on two thousand new ones, the best thread gets forty-nine percent right. That is chance.

Now change the necklace. A new loom keeps a single bead: how far the point is from the centre. The two kinds fall onto a line, the inner ones on the left, the outer ones on the right. One thread, set near one and a half, and every point lands on the right side. One hundred percent, on new points too.

Nothing was added. The rings already held the answer, and the new necklace made it easy to read. That is the first rule. A good representation makes the information you will need easy to read, so that a simple tool can use it. Yoshua Bengio and two colleagues put it this way in their review of representation learning, in twenty thirteen: different representations can hide or reveal the factors that explain the data.

The probe is deliberately weak. It computes a dot product, as in episode two point one, adds an offset, and looks at the sign. In a network, people freeze the encoder and train only this probe on its output. Alain and Bengio placed probes at many layers of two image networks, and reported that linear separability grew steadily with depth.

If you decode EEG, you already know this tool. A linear decoder, tested on trials it has never seen, is a linear probe. That is episode fourteen point one of the EEG series. Here, the features are the necklaces of an encoder.

One trap. When a probe fails, it does not say the information is missing. Our rings failed at forty-nine percent, and the answer was there all along. It says only that the information is not easy to read with a straight thread.

The second rule is about what to throw away. Take a necklace of two beads, where only one bead follows the class, and give it twenty labelled examples. On average, a probe gets eighty-one percent on new points. Now add junk beads: numbers that carry nothing about the class, like noise, lighting or background. With twenty junk beads, the average falls to seventy-three percent. With eighty, to sixty-six. Each figure is an average over a hundred random draws. With few labels, every junk bead is one more chance to be fooled.

Throwing away is risky too. In another toy, a necklace keeps the shape of an object and drops its colour. For shape, the probe scores one hundred percent. For colour, about fifty percent: chance. A necklace with both beads scores one hundred on both. The colour is gone, and no probe will bring it back.

So a good representation keeps what serves the tasks to come, and drops what does not. The Information series has a name for this: the information bottleneck. Tishby, Pereira and Bialek asked for a short code that keeps as much information as possible about what you want to predict. We will keep meeting that question: what to keep, and what to drop.

To sum up. A good representation makes what you need easy to read: one thread should be enough. It keeps what serves the tasks to come, and drops the junk. A failed probe does not mean the information is gone. Next time: invariance and equivariance, necklaces that stay put when a picture is turned, and necklaces that move with it.

PLATE 4.45:41

Invariance and equivariance

Invariance means a necklace ignores a change such as a rotation or crop, while equivariance means it follows the change predictably, like a scale versus a pointer on a turntable. Convolution is shift-equivariant, and pooling turns that into fragile, approximate invariance. Every invariance is a choice: self-supervised augmentations impose them, and ignoring colour discards exactly what ripe-fruit sorting needs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the bead workshop. Every necklace so far has described a picture. But a picture can change without changing what it shows: you can turn it, crop it, tint it. By the end of this video, you will know two ways a necklace can respond to such changes, and why choosing between them is a design decision.

Take a photo of a cat wearing a collar. Rotate it a little. Crop in closer. Shift its colours toward orange. It is still the same cat. If the necklace stays exactly the same through all of this, we say the representation is invariant to these changes. Call a change g, and the weaving machine f. Then f of g of x equals f of x. An invariant necklace ignores the change.

Now slide the cat from the left of the picture to the right. We may want the opposite of ignoring. We may want a necklace that records where the cat went. Then, when the picture changes, the necklace changes too, in a predictable way: the output transforms along with the input. This is equivariance. Now f of g of x equals g prime of f of x, where g prime is the matching change on the necklace side. Invariance is the special case where g prime does nothing.

Picture a turntable on the workshop bench, with the cat sitting on it. The turntable is the change. Next to the cat stand two instruments. A kitchen scale reads the cat's weight, and turning the table does not move its needle: invariant. A pointer is fixed to the turntable and aims at the cat's nose. It turns exactly as the table turns: equivariant. Both are useful. They answer different questions.

Where do we meet these in practice? A convolution slides the same small filter along the input. Here is a toy signal of ten samples: zero, one, three, one, then zeros. Convolve it with the filter one, two, one. The output holds a bump: one, five, eight, five, one. Now shift the signal three samples to the right and convolve again. The same bump comes out, three samples later. Shift the input, and the output shifts by the same amount. That is translation equivariance. These numbers are illustrative, and computed with NumPy.

Now keep a single number from each output: the largest value. Eight, in both cases. Or the total: twenty, in both cases. The position is gone, and what is left does not depend on the shift. This is what pooling does at the end of a convolutional network: it turns an equivariant map into an invariant number.

But this invariance is less solid than it sounds. Back to our output. Keep the larger value of each pair of neighbours. Shift the signal by two samples, and the pooled output simply shifts by one. Shift it by one sample, and the pooled output reads zero, five, eight, one, instead of one, eight, five, zero. That is a different pattern, not a shifted one. Azulay and Weiss, in the Journal of Machine Learning Research in twenty nineteen, tested image networks on shifts of a single pixel. The predicted class changed more often than one would expect, in some of their tests for as many as thirty percent of the images. They traced it to the strides, which ignore the sampling theorem.

A convolution only knows about shifts. Cohen and Welling, at the International Conference on Machine Learning in twenty sixteen, built networks whose layers share their weights across rotations and reflections as well, and reported the best results of the time on rotated MNIST and CIFAR ten. So an architecture can be built to be equivariant to the changes you choose.

Which brings us to the big idea: every invariance is a choice. In self-supervised learning, we usually do not build invariance into the architecture. We ask the network for the same necklace from two altered views of one picture. The alterations we pick are the augmentations, and we will meet them in chapter seven. SimCLR, from episode seven point two, is the example. Chen and colleagues found that no single augmentation was enough, and that composing random crops with colour distortion stood out. Their explanation: two crops of one picture often share a similar colour histogram, so the network can match them by colour alone. Distorting the colours removes that shortcut.

But the wrong choice erases information. Here is an illustrative case. Suppose we want to sort ripe fruit from unripe fruit. Ripeness lives in the colour. A necklace trained to ignore colour has thrown away exactly what we needed. Xiao, Wang, Efros and Darrell made this point at the International Conference on Learning Representations in twenty twenty one. Contrastive methods assume a fixed set of invariances, such as colour, and can perform poorly when a task breaks that assumption. Their example: telling red cars from yellow ones. No invariance is right in itself. It is right for a task.

One more reason this matters, in a series about world models. A world model must predict what happens next. If the ball moves right, the prediction must move right too. A necklace that keeps track of where and how things moved can feed such a prediction. A fully invariant necklace has thrown that information away.

To sum up. Invariant means ignoring a change. Equivariant means following it in a predictable way. Convolution follows shifts, and pooling turns that into approximate invariance. Every invariance is a choice, and the augmentations are where we make it. Next time: supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised: who gives the answers?
