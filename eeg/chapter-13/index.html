The forward problem · Building a head

The forward problem predicts what each electrode records for each possible cortical source, like a model of how light bends through the atmosphere for an observatory. It needs the participant's anatomy, a source space on the cortex, a head model such as a three-layer boundary element model, conductivities and aligned electrode positions, and yields the lead field matrix. Skull conductivity is poorly known.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter thirteen: source space. First, the forward problem: building a head.

Until now, we worked with electrodes on the scalp. But what we really care about are the sources inside the brain. Moving from one to the other has two halves. The forward problem asks: if a source is active here, what would each electrode record? The inverse problem asks the reverse: given what the electrodes record, where are the sources? The forward problem comes first, because the inverse needs it.

Picture an observatory. To find where a faint star really is, astronomers first need a model of how light bends through the atmosphere and the lenses. The forward model is that model of bending, for electric fields in the head.

Step one: the anatomy. Ideally, we start from the participant's own M-R-I. Software like FreeSurfer segments it into the brain, the skull and the scalp, and reconstructs the cortical surface.

Step two: the source space. We place thousands of possible source locations on the cortical surface, often around four thousand per hemisphere. Because the electric sources of the E-E-G are mostly pyramidal neurons, as we saw in chapter one, their orientation is often constrained to be perpendicular to the cortical surface.

Step three: the head model. The simplest realistic option is the boundary element method, or B-E-M. It treats the head as nested compartments, typically three: brain, skull and scalp, each with a uniform conductivity. More detailed models, using the finite element method, can include the cerebrospinal fluid and different skull layers, at a higher computational cost.

Step four: the conductivities. The skull conducts electricity much less than the brain or the scalp. M-N-E's default values are zero point three siemens per metre for the brain and scalp, and zero point zero zero six for the skull, a ratio of fifty. But as we saw in episode one point two, the true ratio is poorly known, and estimates range from about fifteen to eighty. This uncertainty is one of the main limits on E-E-G source accuracy.

Step five: the electrode positions. They must be aligned with the M-R-I, using landmarks like the nasion and the ears, and ideally digitized positions. A misalignment of a centimetre shifts every source estimate.

Finally, we compute the lead field, also called the gain matrix. It has one row per electrode and one column per source. Each column tells you what the electrodes would record if that one source were active with unit strength. This matrix is the forward model.

What if there is no individual M-R-I? A common option is a template, such as fsaverage, the average brain provided with FreeSurfer. It is far better than nothing, but individual anatomy, especially the folding of the cortex, differs from the template. Results in template space should be interpreted with lower spatial precision.

In Python, M-N-E handles every step: setting up the source space, building the B-E-M, aligning the electrodes, and computing the forward solution. The fsaverage template ships with M-N-E's datasets.

To recap. The forward problem predicts what each electrode records for each possible source. It needs anatomy, a source space on the cortex, a head model such as a three-layer B-E-M, conductivities, and aligned electrode positions. The result is the lead field matrix. Skull conductivity is poorly known, and template heads reduce precision.

PLATE 13.23:53

The inverse problem · Too many answers

Sixty-four electrodes cannot determine eight thousand cortical sources: infinitely many configurations give the same scalp map, and some are completely silent. Every inverse method therefore adds assumptions: a few dipoles, minimum energy, uncorrelated sources or sparsity, plus regularization trading fit against stability. Like a single point of light in the sky, a source estimate is a model-based solution, not a photograph.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The inverse problem: too many answers.

Last time, we built the lead field: for every possible source, what the electrodes would record. Now we want to go backwards. We measure the electrodes, and want to know which sources produced the data.

Here is the difficulty. We have, say, sixty-four electrodes. And we have thousands of possible sources, around eight thousand on the cortex. That is sixty-four equations with eight thousand unknowns. There are infinitely many source configurations that produce exactly the same scalp map.

This is not a limitation of our computers. It is a property of physics. Hermann von Helmholtz showed in 1853 that the distribution of currents inside a conductor cannot be uniquely determined from measurements on its surface. Some source configurations are even completely silent: they produce no field at all outside the head.

Picture the observatory again. You see a single point of light in the sky. Is it one bright star far away, or a dimmer star closer? Or two stars side by side? From one image alone, you cannot tell. You need extra assumptions.

Every inverse method adds such assumptions, called priors or constraints. Choosing a method means choosing assumptions.

Here are the main families.

Dipole fitting assumes there are only a few active sources, say one or two dipoles. It searches for their positions and orientations that best explain the data. It works well when that assumption is true, for example for early sensory responses, or epileptic spikes.

Minimum-norm methods assume that, among all solutions that explain the data, the best is the one with the least total energy. This gives a distributed solution, spread over the whole cortex. We meet this family next episode.

Beamformers assume that different sources are not correlated with each other over time. They build a spatial filter for each location, which passes activity from that point and suppresses the rest. That is episode thirteen point four.

Sparse methods assume that only a small number of locations are active, but let the data choose which ones.

There is also the question of noise. Data are never perfect, so we do not want a solution that explains every wiggle exactly, including the noise. All methods include some form of regularization: a trade-off between fitting the data and keeping the solution plausible. More regularization gives smoother, more stable solutions, but less detail.

The consequence for interpretation is important. A source estimate is not a photograph of brain activity. It is the solution that best fits the data under a given set of assumptions. Different methods, applied to the same data, can give different maps. When they agree, you can be more confident. When they disagree, the assumptions matter.

And spatial resolution is limited. Even with the best forward model, E-E-G sources are blurred over centimetres, not millimetres. Deep sources are especially hard to recover, because their fields are weak at the scalp.

In Python, M-N-E implements dipole fitting, minimum-norm methods, beamformers and sparse solvers, all with the same forward model.

To recap. The inverse problem has infinitely many solutions, because there are far more sources than electrodes, and because physics allows silent sources. Every method adds assumptions: few dipoles, minimum energy, uncorrelated sources, or sparsity. Regularization balances fit and stability. A source estimate is a model-based solution, not a photograph, with limited spatial resolution.

The minimum-norm family · MNE, dSPM, sLORETA, eLORETA

The minimum-norm estimate picks, among all sources explaining the scalp data, the one with the smallest total energy. It is a single linear inverse operator, needing a noise covariance and a regularization parameter. Plain minimum norm favours superficial sources; depth weighting, dSPM, sLORETA and eLORETA correct this bias differently, and zero localization error for one source does not mean high resolution.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The minimum-norm family: M-N-E, d-S-P-M, s-LORETA and e-LORETA.

Among all the source distributions that explain the scalp data, which one should we pick? The minimum-norm estimate gives a simple answer: the one with the smallest total energy, meaning the smallest sum of squared source strengths.

Matti Hamalainen and Risto Ilmoniemi developed this approach for M-E-G in the nineteen-eighties. It has a closed-form solution: a single matrix, called the inverse operator, that you multiply with the data at each time point. That makes it fast, and linear.

Picture the observatory. You have a blurry image of the sky, and many possible arrangements of stars could explain it. The minimum-norm choice is the arrangement that uses the least total light.

Two ingredients are essential. The first is the noise covariance: how noisy each electrode is, and how the noise is shared between electrodes. It is usually estimated from a baseline period or an empty recording. It lets the method trust clean electrodes more than noisy ones.

The second is regularization, controlled by a parameter often called lambda squared. In M-N-E-Python, it is set from an assumed signal-to-noise ratio. The default is a signal-to-noise ratio of three, which gives lambda squared equal to one ninth. More regularization gives a smoother, more stable solution.

But the plain minimum-norm estimate has a known bias. Superficial sources are close to the electrodes, so a small current there produces a large signal. Deep sources need a large current to produce the same signal. Because the method prefers small currents, it tends to explain everything with superficial sources. Deep activity gets pulled toward the surface.

Several variants correct this bias in different ways.

Depth weighting is the simplest: it penalizes superficial sources a little, to compensate. M-N-E uses a depth weighting exponent of zero point eight by default.

Dynamic statistical parametric mapping, or d-S-P-M, from Anders Dale and colleagues in 2000, divides each source estimate by its estimated noise level. The result is a kind of signal-to-noise map, in which deep and superficial sources are on a more equal footing.

Standardized low resolution tomography, or s-LORETA, from Roberto Pascual-Marqui in 2002, normalizes by the estimated variance of the source estimate, including the signal. For a single source and no noise, it localizes that source with zero error.

Exact low resolution tomography, or e-LORETA, from Pascual-Marqui in 2007, uses a weighting chosen so that, under similar ideal conditions, localization is exact without the need for normalization afterwards.

An important caveat: zero localization error for a single source does not mean high resolution. Several simultaneous sources still blur together. And "exact" holds only under the method's idealized assumptions.

Another choice is orientation. You can force each source to point perpendicular to the cortex, called a fixed orientation; let it point in any direction, called free orientation; or something in between, a loose orientation. M-N-E's default is loose, with a value of zero point two.

So, which one should you use? There is no universal winner. The choice depends on your question. Many analyses use d-S-P-M or e-LORETA for mapping, and report the parameters: noise covariance, regularization, depth weighting and orientation.

In Python, M-N-E provides all of them through make inverse operator and apply inverse, with the method parameter set to M-N-E, d-S-P-M, s-LORETA or e-LORETA.

To recap. The minimum-norm estimate picks the solution with the least total energy. It is linear and fast, and needs a noise covariance and a regularization parameter. Plain minimum norm is biased toward superficial sources. Depth weighting, d-S-P-M, s-LORETA and e-LORETA correct this bias in different ways. Zero localization error for one source is not the same as high resolution. Report your parameters.

Beamformers · LCMV and DICS

A beamformer builds a virtual sensor for each brain location: electrode weights that pass the target with unit gain while minimizing total output power, like radio dishes steered at one patch of sky. LCMV works in time and DICS in frequency, with weights adapted to the data covariance. Strongly correlated sources partly cancel each other, so regularization, data rank and baseline contrasts matter.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Beamformers: L-C-M-V and D-I-C-S. A spatial filter that listens to one point at a time.

Minimum-norm methods solve for all sources at once. Beamformers take a different approach. They ask, for each location in the brain separately: what combination of electrodes would let me hear this location, and silence everything else?

The name comes from antenna arrays and microphone arrays. Picture the observatory's radio dishes: by combining the signals of many antennas with the right weights, you can point the array at one region of the sky, without physically moving anything.

Here is the idea for E-E-G. For a target location, we look for a set of weights, one per electrode. Applied to the data, these weights form a virtual sensor. Two conditions define the weights. First, activity coming from the target location must pass through unchanged: this is called the unit-gain constraint. Second, among all weights that satisfy this, we pick the ones that make the total output power as small as possible.

Why does minimizing output power help? Because anything else that leaks into the virtual sensor, from other sources or noise, adds power. Minimizing power therefore suppresses everything except the target, as much as possible.

The weights depend on the data covariance matrix: how the electrodes vary together during the recording. That is why beamformers are called adaptive: the filter is shaped by the data itself.

The time-domain version is the linearly constrained minimum variance beamformer, or L-C-M-V, described by Barry Van Veen and colleagues in 1997. The frequency-domain version is the dynamic imaging of coherent sources, or D-I-C-S, by Joachim Gross and colleagues in 2001. D-I-C-S uses the cross-spectral density at a given frequency, which makes it popular for localizing oscillations.

Now the key assumption, and its consequence. Beamformers assume that different sources are not correlated over time. If two sources are strongly correlated, for example two auditory cortices responding together to the same sound, the beamformer can partly cancel them. Each one looks like interference for the other, and minimizing power suppresses both. Their estimated activity drops, sometimes dramatically.

This matters for connectivity too: a method that suppresses correlated sources is a delicate tool for studying correlated sources. Variants exist that model both hemispheres together, or use dual-source beamformers, when this is a concern.

Practical points. The data covariance must be well estimated, which needs enough data relative to the number of electrodes. Regularization is added to stabilize it: M-N-E's default for L-C-M-V is five percent. After average referencing, interpolation or I-C-A, the data have a reduced rank, as we saw in episode seven point ten, and the beamformer must account for it. And beamformer outputs are usually expressed as a contrast, for example task against baseline, or as a normalized index, rather than as raw power, because raw output depends on depth.

Beamformers often give focal, well-localized maps for strong, isolated sources, and they reject noise from outside the target well. But like every inverse method, their output reflects their assumptions.

In Python, M-N-E provides make L-C-M-V and apply L-C-M-V, and make D-I-C-S for frequency-domain beamforming.

To recap. A beamformer builds, for each location, a spatial filter that passes the target with unit gain and minimizes total output power. L-C-M-V works in time, D-I-C-S in frequency. The weights adapt to the data covariance. Correlated sources can cancel each other. Regularization, data rank and contrasts against a baseline are essential.

PLATE 13.54:05

Spatial leakage and resolution

Source reconstruction blurs a single active point into a patch of several centimetres, the point-spread function, while each estimate also mixes in neighbouring sources, the cross-talk function. Both come from the resolution matrix. Leakage produces zero-lag correlations between nearby regions and ghost connections near truly interacting pairs, so leakage-robust measures and attention to resolution remain necessary.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Spatial leakage and resolution. Why volume conduction does not disappear in source space.

In chapter ten, we spent a lot of time on volume conduction: each electrode sees a mixture of sources. A natural hope is that source reconstruction fixes this. It helps. But it does not fix it completely.

Here is a simple experiment. Simulate a single active point on the cortex. Compute what the electrodes would record, using the forward model. Then apply an inverse method, and look at the reconstruction. Instead of a single point, you get a blurred patch, spread over several centimetres. This blur is called the point-spread function.

Picture the observatory. A single distant star, seen through a telescope, does not appear as a perfect point. It appears as a small disc with a halo, because of the optics. Two stars close together can merge into one blob.

Now flip the question. Take one location in the reconstruction. Which true sources contribute to its estimate? Usually, not just the source at that location, but also its neighbours, and sometimes distant regions. This is called the cross-talk function.

Both views come from one object: the resolution matrix. For linear methods like minimum norm, it is simply the inverse operator multiplied by the lead field. In a perfect world, it would be the identity matrix: each source reconstructed only by itself. In practice, it has broad off-diagonal values. Columns describe point spread, rows describe cross-talk. Olaf Hauk and colleagues have written extensively about using these functions to evaluate and compare inverse methods.

The resolution depends on many things. More electrodes help. A better head model helps. Superficial sources are better resolved than deep ones. Regularization trades noise against blur. And methods differ: some have smaller localization errors, others smaller spatial spread.

Now, why does this matter so much? Because leakage is volume conduction in a new form. If one source is active, its activity leaks into neighbouring source estimates, instantly and linearly. Two reconstructed regions next to each other will look correlated, with zero delay, even if only one of them is active.

And there is a subtler effect, described by Satu Palva, Matias Palva and colleagues in 2018. If two distant sources truly interact, leakage spreads that interaction to their neighbours. You get ghost connections, between regions that are near the true pair, but do not interact themselves. And because the true interaction may have a delay, these ghosts can survive measures that ignore zero lag.

So everything in chapter ten still applies in source space. Prefer leakage-robust measures, such as imaginary coherence, weighted phase lag index, or orthogonalized envelope correlations. Be cautious with nearby regions. And look at the resolution of your method for the regions you report.

There are also tools designed against leakage. Multivariate orthogonalization, proposed by Giles Colclough and colleagues in 2015, removes zero-lag leakage between all regions at once. Some parcellations are designed to minimize leakage between regions.

In Python, M-N-E can compute the resolution matrix, and extract point-spread and cross-talk functions, for any linear inverse operator.

To recap. Source reconstruction blurs a single point into a patch: the point-spread function. Each estimate receives contributions from other sources: the cross-talk function. Both come from the resolution matrix. Leakage creates zero-lag correlations between nearby regions, and ghost connections near true interactions. Use leakage-robust measures, check resolution, and treat nearby regions with care.

PLATE 13.64:19

Connectivity in source space

Source-space connectivity starts by grouping thousands of cortical locations into regions with a parcellation, like constellations drawn on a star chart. Each region is summarized by mean-flip or first principal component, then connected with leakage-robust measures. Parcellation size should match EEG's centimetre resolution, and every pipeline choice changes the result, so choices must be reported and robustness checked.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Connectivity in source space. From thousands of sources to a network of regions.

A source estimate gives thousands of time series, one per location on the cortex. That is too many for a connectivity matrix, and neighbouring locations are not independent anyway, because of leakage. So the usual approach is to group locations into regions, extract one time series per region, and compute connectivity between regions.

Picture the observatory's star charts. Instead of tracking every individual star, astronomers group them into constellations. The choice of constellations is a convention. Change the borders, and the description of the sky changes too.

Step one: choose a parcellation. Many atlases exist. The Desikan-Killiany atlas divides the cortex into sixty-eight regions, based on the folding pattern. The Destrieux atlas has around one hundred and fifty. The H-C-P multimodal parcellation, from Matthew Glasser and colleagues in 2016, has three hundred and sixty. The Schaefer parcellations, from 2018, come in versions from one hundred to one thousand regions.

But E-E-G has a resolution of a few centimetres. A parcellation with hundreds of small regions is finer than what the data can resolve: many neighbouring regions will carry nearly the same signal. Coarser parcellations, or ones designed to minimize leakage, are often a better match for E-E-G.

Step two: summarize each region. A region contains many source locations, with orientations that differ across the folds of the cortex. Simply averaging them can make activity cancel, because sources on opposite walls of a sulcus point in opposite directions. Common options are the mean after flipping signs so that orientations agree, often called mean-flip, or the first principal component of the region. Each choice gives a slightly different time series.

Step three: compute connectivity between regional time series, with the measures from chapter ten. Because leakage remains, leakage-robust measures are recommended: imaginary coherence, weighted phase lag index, or orthogonalized amplitude correlations.

Step four: interpret, carefully. Each choice in this pipeline changes the result: the head model, the inverse method and its regularization, the parcellation, the way of summarizing regions, and the connectivity measure. Studies comparing pipelines on the same data, such as the work of Mahjoory and colleagues in 2017, found that source and connectivity estimates can differ substantially across reasonable choices.

So these choices should be reported as parameters of the analysis, chosen in advance when possible. And a robustness check with a second, reasonable pipeline makes conclusions stronger.

Template brains add another layer. With fsaverage, regions are defined on an average anatomy, and individual differences in folding blur them further.

The reward for all this work is real. Region-level networks can be compared across participants, linked to anatomy, and compared with networks from f-M-R-I. Source-level connectivity also reduces, though it does not remove, the mixing that dominates sensor-space analyses.

In Python, M-N-E reads parcellations as labels, and extracts one time series per label with extract label time course, with modes such as mean, mean-flip or P-C-A-flip. mne-connectivity then computes the connectivity matrix between labels.

To recap. Source-space connectivity groups locations into regions with a parcellation, summarizes each region with a choice such as mean-flip or the first principal component, and computes connectivity between regions with leakage-robust measures. The parcellation, the summary method and every other pipeline choice change the results. Report them as parameters, match the parcellation to E-E-G resolution, and check robustness.
