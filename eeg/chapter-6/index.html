PLATE 6.14:17

Montages · 10-20, 10-10, 10-5

Electrode placement follows a rule, like hikers planting flags at fixed fractions of the way between landmarks. The ten-twenty system spaces electrodes at ten and twenty percent steps between nasion, inion and the preauricular points; letters name the region, odd numbers the left, even the right, z the midline. Ten-ten and ten-five densify it, and source analysis calls for digitising the real positions.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter six: recording. We leave the design desk and go to the lab. The first question is the simplest one: where exactly do the electrodes go? Today, we learn the international montages: ten twenty, ten ten, and ten five.

Picture a group of hikers placing flags on a hill. If everyone says "put a flag somewhere near the top", the flags end up everywhere. But if the rule is "start at the north marker, walk ten percent of the way to the south marker, plant a flag, then every twenty percent", any group, on any hill, will plant the flags in comparable places. The montage is that rule, and the head is the hill.

The rule starts from four landmarks on the skull. The nasion, the dip between the forehead and the nose. The inion, the bump at the back of the skull. And the two preauricular points, just in front of each ear.

Measure the distance from nasion to inion over the top of the head. The first electrode sits ten percent of the way from the nasion. Then every twenty percent, another electrode, and the last ten percent brings you to the inion. Do the same from ear to ear. That is the origin of the name ten twenty: steps of ten and twenty percent. The system was published by Herbert Jasper in 1958 and became the international standard.

Each position has a name. The letters say the region: F P for frontal pole, F for frontal, C for central, T for temporal, P for parietal, and O for occipital. Note that there is no central lobe: C is just a label for the strip over the sensorimotor cortex. Numbers say the side: odd numbers on the left, even numbers on the right, and the letter z, for zero, on the midline. So C three sits over the left central region, C four on the right, and Cz at the very top.

The classic ten twenty system has about twenty one positions. That is enough for clinical reading, but not for topographies, source analysis or a good average reference. So in 1985 the system was densified to ten percent steps, the ten ten system, with intermediate names like F C, between frontal and central, and C P, between central and parietal. The ten ten nomenclature also renamed four positions: T three and T four became T seven and T eight, and T five and T six became P seven and P eight. You will still meet both names in older papers and clinical files.

In 2001, Robert Oostenveld and Peter Praamstra extended the scheme to five percent steps: the ten five system, with up to three hundred and forty five named positions, designed for high density EEG and source analysis.

Some high density caps, though, do not follow these names at all. Geodesic nets, for example, use their own equidistant layouts and numbered sensors. That is fine, as long as you know the actual coordinates.

Which brings us to the most important practical point. A montage gives nominal positions, the places where electrodes should be. Real caps shift, heads are not spheres, and a cap that fits well over Cz can still be rotated by a centimetre. For topographies, this rarely matters much. For source analysis, which we meet in chapter thirteen, it matters a lot: the forward model needs to know where each electrode really was relative to the head.

So when source analysis is planned, digitise the real positions. Electromagnetic digitisers, photogrammetry with several cameras, or 3 D scanners can record the electrodes and the landmarks. In Python, M N E stores these as a montage, and uses the landmarks to align the electrodes with a head model.

Let's recap. The ten twenty system places electrodes at ten and twenty percent steps between the nasion, the inion and the preauricular points. Letters give the region, odd numbers the left, even numbers the right, z the midline. Ten ten and ten five densify the system, with some renamed positions. And when source analysis is planned, digitise the real electrode positions.

Next time: capping a real head.

PLATE 6.24:26

Capping a real head

Capping a head well takes care, like planting seedlings: measure the head, place Cz at the vertex, make gentle contact under each electrode, then check impedances and balance and the signal itself with blinks, jaw clenches and closed eyes. Hair type affects contact and has led to excluded participants, so braiding, suitable electrodes, extra time and documentation of poor contacts are advised.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Putting an EEG cap on a head looks simple in photographs. In practice, the quality of everything that follows depends on the twenty or forty minutes you spend doing it. Today, we cap a real head.

Think of planting seedlings in a garden. You can scatter them on the surface and hope. Or you can make room in the soil for each one, press it in, water it, and check that it took. Each electrode is a seedling, and the scalp is the soil.

Step one: measure the head. Measure the circumference around the head, passing over the eyebrows and the inion, and choose the cap size that matches. Then find the vertex: the midpoint between nasion and inion, and between the two preauricular points. Mark it. Cz on the cap should sit exactly there.

Step two: put the cap on and align it. Pull it down from front to back, check that the midline runs from the nose to the inion, and that Cz sits on the mark. Check symmetry: the distances from the ears to C three and C four should match. A cap shifted by a centimetre or rotated a little is the most common placement error.

Step three: make contact. With gel based systems, part the hair under each electrode with a blunt tip, gently move the hair aside, and fill the electrode with gel until it touches the skin. Light abrasion of the outer skin layer, with the blunt tip or a little abrasive gel, lowers impedance. It should never hurt or break the skin.

Step four: check impedance, electrode by electrode. Most acquisition software shows a colour map of impedances. Typical targets for passive electrodes are a few kilo ohms. Active electrodes, which amplify the signal right at the electrode, tolerate higher values, as episode one point five explained. Just as important as the absolute value is balance: electrodes with very different impedances pick up line noise unequally.

Step five: check the signal itself. Ask the participant to blink, clench the jaw, close the eyes, and relax. You should see blinks at the front, muscle bursts at the sides, and alpha at the back when the eyes close. If a channel looks flat, very noisy or drifting, fix it now. It takes one minute now, and costs hours later.

Now the part many manuals skip: hair. Hair type changes everything about contact. Thick, coarse, tightly curled hair, braids, twists and locs can keep electrodes far from the scalp, and many standard caps were designed without them in mind. The consequence is not only practical. In 2021, Tricia Choy, Elizabeth Baker and Katherine Stavropoulos described how this has led EEG research to exclude Black participants, through exclusion criteria, rejected data, and discouraging experiences.

Solutions exist. Ask participants in advance about their hair, and tell them clearly what to expect. Allow extra time. In 2020, Arnelle Etienne, Pulkit Grover and colleagues showed that braiding hair in cornrows aligned with ten twenty positions exposes the scalp where the electrodes go, and designed small clip electrodes, called Sevo, that slide under the braids. In their tests, braiding and these electrodes lowered impedance dramatically compared with standard electrodes on unbraided hair. Other approaches include different electrode shapes, more gel and time, and trained experimenters.

And whatever you do, document it. Record which electrodes had poor contact, and why, rather than silently discarding participants. Report the demographics of who was tested and who was excluded.

Finally, the comfort of the participant is part of the protocol. Explain each step, warn about the cold gel, check that the chin strap is not too tight, and offer a mirror and a towel at the end. A comfortable participant moves less, and comes back.

Let's recap. Measure the head, find the vertex, align the cap. Make contact under each electrode, gently. Check impedances and their balance, then check the signal with blinks, jaw clenches and eyes closed. Plan for every hair type, with time, techniques like braiding, and suitable electrodes. Document poor contacts instead of silently excluding. And look after the participant.

Triggers and clocks

An ERP is only as sharp as the knowledge of stimulus timing, like a relay race filmed by cameras with unsynchronised clocks. A trigger marks when a code was sent, not when the stimulus appeared, and screens and sound cards add delays that may vary. A photodiode or recorded audio measures them, jitter smears ERPs, and Lab Streaming Layer aligns several devices.

TRANSCRIPTEN

An E R P is only as sharp as your knowledge of when the stimulus happened. If your timestamps are off, or worse, vary from trial to trial, the averages blur. Today, we look at triggers and clocks.

Imagine a relay race filmed by several cameras, each with its own clock. One camera runs a few frames behind, another drifts during the race. If you try to judge the handover of the baton by comparing videos, you will see a fumble that never happened. Synchronising the clocks is not a detail; it is the measurement.

The classic setup has two computers. The stimulus computer shows the pictures or plays the sounds. The acquisition computer records the EEG. When the stimulus computer presents something, it sends a short code, the trigger, through a cable, often a parallel port, a USB trigger box or a network message. The amplifier writes that code into a trigger channel, sample by sample, next to the EEG.

The trouble is that the moment the code is sent is not the moment the stimulus reaches the participant. A monitor draws the image line by line, at its refresh rate. At sixty hertz, one frame lasts about sixteen point seven milliseconds. Screens also add processing delays, which can be tens of milliseconds on some displays. Sounds go through buffers in the sound card and the operating system. So there is a delay between trigger and stimulus, and, more dangerous, that delay can vary.

How do you measure it? With a photodiode for vision: a small light sensor taped to a corner of the screen, where your program draws a white patch at the same time as the stimulus. Record the photodiode as an extra channel, and compare its onset with the trigger. For sound, record the audio output itself, through a splitter, into an auxiliary channel.

A constant delay is easy to fix: shift all your events by that amount. A variable delay is worse. Jitter from trial to trial smears the E R P in time: sharp peaks become lower and wider, and latency measures become unreliable. Fast components and high frequency activity suffer most. The best fix is to use the measured onsets, from the photodiode or audio channel, as your events.

Now the clocks. Many experiments add other devices: an eye tracker, a motion capture system, a second EEG for hyperscanning, physiological sensors. Each has its own clock, and clocks drift: two devices that start aligned can be off by many milliseconds after an hour.

The Lab Streaming Layer, or L S L, is a widely used software solution. Each device publishes a stream with time stamps, and L S L estimates the offsets between the clocks of the computers on a local network, so all streams can be put on a common timeline. In a 2025 paper, Christian Kothe and colleagues describe its design and performance, and also an important limit: delays inside a device, before data reach the computer, cannot be seen by L S L itself. You still need to measure them, for example with a photodiode or a shared pulse recorded by all devices.

A few habits make timing robust. Test the whole chain with a photodiode before the study, and again whenever any hardware or software changes. Keep a shared trigger in all recordings when you have several devices. Use clear, documented trigger codes, and log them in your stimulus program too, so you can cross check. And always look at the trigger channel at the start of analysis: count the events, check the codes and the intervals.

Let's recap. A trigger marks when a code was sent, not when the stimulus arrived. Screens and sound cards add delays, often variable. Measure them with a photodiode or the recorded audio. Constant delays can be corrected; jitter smears E R Ps, so prefer measured onsets. With several devices, synchronise clocks, for example with L S L, and still measure device delays. And check the events before analysing.

PLATE 6.43:54

Live quality control

During a recording the experimenter acts as a pilot scanning instruments and keeping a log. The usual suspects on screen are flat channels, line noise, drifts, muscle, cable movement, eye activity and drowsiness, with intervention rules decided in advance. A timed logbook from a template, a short calibration block of eye and jaw artifacts, and a log saved with the data make later cleaning easier.

TRANSCRIPTEN

During a recording, it is tempting to press start, sit back and wait. But the minutes of the session are the only time you can still fix problems. Today, we learn to watch the data live, and to keep a logbook.

Think of a pilot during a flight. Most of the time, the autopilot flies. But the pilot keeps scanning the instruments, and writes down anything unusual in the log, so that the next crew knows what happened. In the EEG lab, you are the pilot, the acquisition screen is the instrument panel, and the logbook is your best gift to whoever analyses the data, including your future self.

What should you scan for? Let's go through the usual suspects on the screen.

A flat channel: a line with almost no activity. Often an electrode with no contact, a broken wire, or a channel bridged to its neighbour, which then looks identical to it.

Line noise: a thick, regular fuzz at fifty or sixty hertz, depending on the country, usually on electrodes with poor contact, or near a power cable or a screen. A quick look at a live spectrum shows a sharp peak at that frequency.

Slow drifts: channels that wander up and down over seconds, from sweat, drying gel, or a loose electrode. A warm room makes this worse.

Muscle: fast, irregular, high amplitude activity, especially at temporal and frontal electrodes, when the participant clenches the jaw, frowns or tenses the neck.

Movement and cable sway: large, slow swings on many channels at once, when the head or the cables move.

Blinks and eye movements: large frontal deflections. These are normal, but if the task requires fixation, a stream of saccades tells you the participant is not following instructions.

And the participant's state: if alpha keeps appearing and disappearing in an eyes open task, or slow waves creep in, drowsiness may be setting in.

For each problem, decide: fix now, or note and continue? A bad electrode can often be fixed in a minute during a break, by adding gel or adjusting it. A participant who looks sleepy can be given a break, a glass of water, or a brief chat. Decide your intervention rules in advance, so that you react the same way for every participant.

Now the logbook. Note everything that can explain a strange piece of data later, with the time: "the participant coughed at twelve oh three", "fire alarm test at twelve twenty", "channel T eight re gelled during the second break", "participant reported feeling sleepy in block four", "the room was hot". It takes seconds, and it can save an hour of puzzling during cleaning.

Use a template so that nothing is forgotten: date and time, experimenter, participant code, cap size, impedance summary at the start and end, room temperature, deviations from the protocol, technical problems, and the participant's own comments. A paper sheet works; a digital form works too. What matters is that it travels with the data, which brings us to the next episode.

Two more habits. First, record a short calibration block at the start: eyes open, eyes closed, blinks, saccades left and right, jaw clench. These samples help you recognise each artifact in this participant's data, and can train artifact removal methods. Second, at the end, check impedances again and save the log with the data files immediately.

Let's recap. During the recording, keep scanning for flat channels, line noise, drifts, muscle, movement, eye activity and drowsiness. Decide in advance when to intervene. Keep a timed logbook with a template. Record a short calibration block of artifacts. And save the log with the data.

PLATE 6.54:14

Saving it · BIDS-EEG

BIDS is a shared kitchen layout for brain data, with the same shelves and labelled jars for everyone. A dataset holds a description, a participants table and one folder per participant, with files named by subject, task, session and run. Each recording has a sidecar, channels table and events table; converting early makes data reusable by tools and other labs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the end of a study, you have a folder full of files: recordings, logs, events, notes. A year later, can you still understand them? Could another lab? Today, we save EEG data the way the community has agreed on: B I D S.

Imagine a shared kitchen where every cook stores ingredients in their own way, in unlabelled jars. Every evening, someone wastes an hour finding the salt. Now imagine a kitchen where every shelf has the same layout, every jar has a standard label, and the recipe card is pinned to it. Anyone can walk in and cook. B I D S is that kitchen layout for brain data.

B I D S stands for Brain Imaging Data Structure. It started with M R I, and was extended to EEG in 2019, in a paper led by Cyril Pernet with many co authors. It is a set of rules for naming folders and files, and for describing the data with simple text files.

Let's open a B I D S dataset. At the top, a file describes the dataset: its name, authors and licence. Another lists the participants, with columns such as age and group. Then there is one folder per participant, sub dash zero one, sub dash zero two, and inside, optionally, one folder per session, and then a folder called e e g.

Inside, each recording has a name built from key value pairs: the subject, the task, and, if needed, the session and run. For example: sub dash zero one, underscore task dash oddball, underscore e e g. The raw data stay in an accepted EEG format, such as European Data Format, BrainVision or BioSemi files.

Next to each recording sit small companion files. A J S O N sidecar describes the recording: sampling frequency, reference, power line frequency, amplifier, task description. A channels table lists every channel, its type, its units, and whether it is good or bad. An events table lists every event: its onset in seconds, its duration, its type, and any extra columns you need, such as the response or the reaction time. And, when you have them, an electrodes table with the measured positions, and a file describing their coordinate system.

Why bother? First, for yourself. Six months later, everything you need to analyse a recording is in the files, not in your memory or in an email. Second, for tools: many analysis tools can read B I D S directly, and pipelines can loop over participants without custom code. Third, for sharing: repositories such as OpenNeuro expect B I D S, and a well organised dataset can be reused by others, which is the foundation of reproducibility, as chapter fifteen will discuss.

In Python, the M N E B I D S package converts raw recordings into a B I D S dataset, and reads them back into M N E. And a validator program checks that a dataset follows the rules, and tells you what is missing.

A few practical tips. Convert to B I D S right after each session, while the details are fresh, rather than at the end of the study. Put your logbook information into the right place: bad channels in the channels table, notes in the sidecar or in a separate text file. Never rename or edit the original raw files by hand; keep a copy of the source data. And before sharing, check consent and anonymisation: remove names and dates of birth, and make sure participants agreed to data sharing.

Let's recap. B I D S is a shared layout for brain data. A dataset has a description, a participants table, and one folder per participant, with files named by subject, task, session and run. Each recording comes with a sidecar, a channels table, an events table, and optionally electrode positions. Use M N E B I D S to convert, a validator to check, and convert early.
