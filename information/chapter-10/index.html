Regularity is conditional entropy — Approximate Entropy and Sample Entropy.

Welcome to Chapter 10, Complexity and Integration. Many measures of brain complexity were invented outside information theory, with their own names and recipes. In this chapter, we show what each of them actually estimates, in the language of the first nine chapters. We start with the two most used regularity measures, Approximate Entropy and Sample Entropy.

Here is the intuition. Picture a bee that keeps a diary of its dance, one step at a time. Take any short phrase of the dance, say two steps. Search the diary for every other place where the bee danced nearly the same two steps. Then ask in those places did the third step also match. If the dance is regular, a matching start almost always continues the same way. If the dance is irregular, a matching start tells you little about what comes next. Regularity is the predictability of the next step, given the last few. In chapter 7, that had a name, the entropy rate.

Approximate entropy and sample entropy turn that question into a counting recipe. Once we see the recipe in information terms, we will know what their numbers mean, and what can change them without any change in the brain.

Here is how sample entropy works. Choose a template length m, often 2, and a tolerance r, often 0.2 times the standard deviation of the signal. Two templates match if every pair of corresponding points is within r. Count the pairs of templates of length m that match, call it B. Count the pairs of length m plus 1 that match, call it A. Sample entropy is minus the logarithm of a over b.

A over B is the probability that two segments which are similar for m points stay similar for one more point. So sample entropy is minus the log of a conditional probability, the surprise of the next point, given that the previous m points matched. That is a conditional entropy, and as m grows, the entropy rate of chapter 7.

A small worked example with illustrative counts. Suppose that among all pairs of two-point templates, 1,000 pairs match. Among those, 110 pairs still match when we add the third point. A over B is 0.11, and sample entropy is minus the natural log of 0.11, 2.21 nats. If instead 900 of the 1000 had continued to match, the ratio would be 0.9, and the entropy only 0.11 nats, a very regular signal.

Notice what the tolerance does in this recipe. It decides what counts as the same step. With a generous tolerance, many templates match and many continue to match. With a strict tolerance, few match at all. The count of matches is really a count of samples falling in a box of width 2r around each template, like counting samples in a histogram bin.

Approximate entropy came first. Steven Pincus introduced it in 1991. Its recipe is similar, but each template is counted as matching itself. Joshua Richman and Randall Moorman introduced sample entropy in 2000, removing those self-matches, because they bias approximate entropy toward regularity, especially in short recordings.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 2,000 points of three autoregressive signals, standardised to unit variance. White noise, a signal with memory 0.5 and one with memory 0.9. All values are in nats with m equal to 2.

First, sample entropy orders them as expected, with r equal to 0.2, 2.21 for white noise, 2.04 for memory 0.5, 1.38 for memory 0.9. More memory, more regularity, lower entropy.

Second, the tolerance. For white noise, sample entropy is 2.98 with r equal to 0.1, 2.21 with 0.2, 1.81 with 0.3, and 1.30 with 0.5. The same signal, four very different values. That is the strong point of today. The tolerance r plays the role of a bin width. Like the bins of episode 2.6, a finer tolerance gives a larger number, and the value has no meaning without the tolerance attached.

In fact, for a continuous signal, the dependence follows the rule of episode 2.6. Add the logarithm of 2r to sample entropy, the log of the width of the tolerance window, and the four white noise values become 1.37, 1.30, 1.30, and 1.30, nearly constant. The true entropy rate of this standardised white noise is 1.43 nats. For memory 0.9, the corrected values sit around 0.46 to 0.61, against a true entropy rate of 0.56. So sample entropy is a coarse estimator of the entropy rate. Read through a window of width 2r.

Third, approximate entropy and the length of the recording. On white noise with r equal to 0.2, approximate entropy is 0.55 with 100 points, 1.09 with 300, 1.67 with 1000, and 2.02 with 3000. The signal is the same, only its length changes. Sample entropy stays near 2.2 from 300 points on. With 100 points it is noisier, 2.81. Approximate entropy's self-matches make short recordings look regular.

What does a regularity value depend on?

First, the tolerance r as we just saw. And r is usually set relative to the standard deviation. So anything that changes the variance like an artefact or a filter changes the effective tolerance.

Second, the template length m. Longer templates condition on more of the past but leave fewer matches, so the estimate becomes noisier or even undefined when it is zero.

Third, the length of the recording, dramatically so for approximate entropy.

Fourth is spectrum. Filtering a signal changes its regularity because it changes how predictable the next sample is. A low-pass filter makes any signal look more regular.

And fifth, the sampling rate. Oversampling makes consecutive points more similar, which raises the matching probability and lowers the entropy.

Sixth, slow drifts and non-stationarity. A drift inflates the standard deviation, which inflates r, which makes every template match more easily. The signal looks more regular because the tolerance grew, not because the dynamics changed.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study compares approximate entropy between patients and controls and finds lower values in patients, more regular brain activity. But the patients' recordings were shorter after artefact rejection removed more segments. As our simulation showed, shorter recordings alone lower approximate entropy.

The fix, use sample entropy, report m, r, and the number of points, match the recording lengths between groups, and check that the groups do not differ in variance or spectrum before reading a difference as regularity.

Sample entropy is minus the log of the probability that two segments similar for m points stay similar for one more, a conditional entropy, and a coarse estimator of the entropy rate.

The tolerance r acts as a bin width. White noise goes from 2.98 to 1.30 nats as r grows, and adding the log of 2r makes it nearly constant.

Permutation entropy · Shannon on ordinal patterns

Permutation entropy replaces values by their ranks and computes the Shannon entropy of the resulting ordinal patterns, like counting how three flying bees line up. It needs no tolerance and is unchanged by any strictly increasing transformation, but it is blind to amplitude, variance and outliers. Order, lag and ties shape its value: quantising white noise to five levels lowered it from 1.79 to 1.58.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Permutation entropy — Shannon on ordinal patterns.

In episode 10.1, regularity measures counted matches within a tolerance, and the tolerance turned out to be a bin width. Today we meet a measure with no tolerance at all. It throws away the values, keeps only their order, and computes a plain Shannon entropy.

Here is the intuition. Three bees fly past, one after another. Forget how high each one flies. Only note who is highest, who is in the middle, and who is lowest. That is a lineup, and three bees can line up in six different orders. Watch many groups of three consecutive bees, count how often each of the six lineups appears, and compute the entropy of those counts. That is permutation entropy, introduced by Christoph Bandt and Bernd Pompe in 2002.

Here is the recipe for a signal. Choose an order d, often 3 to 7 and a lag tau. Take d samples spaced by tau. Replace their values by their ranks, which one is smallest, which is next, and so on. That pattern is one of d factorial possible orders. 6 for d equal to 3, 120 for 5. Slide along the signal, build the histogram of patterns, and compute its Shannon entropy. Dividing by the log of d factorial gives a normalised value between 0 and 1.

A tiny example. Take the five values 4, 7, 9, 2, and 5 with order 3 and lag 1. The first triplet 4, 7, 9 rises all the way. The second 7, 9, 2 goes up then falls below its start. The third 9, 2, 5 falls then climbs back halfway. Three triplets, three different lineups. On a long signal, we simply count how often each of the six lineups appears.

Compare this with sample entropy. Sample entropy asks whether nearby values stay nearby. Permutation entropy asks whether the ups and downs repeat. The first depends on a tolerance and on the amplitude distribution. The second depends on neither. They answer related but different questions about predictability.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 10,000 samples of three signals and used order 3 and lag 1. Values are in nats, the maximum is the log of 6, 1.79.

White Gaussian noise, 1.79 the maximum. Every lineup is equally likely. A signal with memory 0.9, 1.74 slightly less because rising and falling runs become a little more common. A sine wave with a period of 50 samples, 0.88. Most triplets are simply rising or falling, and the histogram is dominated by two lineups.

Now the strong point of today. Take the signal with memory 0.9 and transform it, exponentiate it, or raise it to the third power. These transformations distort the values enormously. Permutation entropy does not move 1.74, 1.74, 1.74. Any strictly increasing transformation keeps every rank and therefore every pattern. That is the same reason mutual information is invariant to such transformations. As we saw in episode 4.3, what does not depend on the labels cannot be changed by relabelling them.

The other side of that coin is just as important. Permutation entropy is blind to everything the ranks do not record. Independent samples from a Gaussian and from a heavy-tailed Cauchy distribution look completely different on a plot. Their permutation entropies are identical, 1.79. Amplitude, variance, outliers, all invisible.

What does permutation entropy depend on?

First, the order d. Larger orders capture longer patterns but need many more samples. You want many observations per pattern, and the number of patterns grows as d factorial. With order 7, there are 5,040 patterns.

Second, the lag tau together with the sampling rate. With a high sampling rate and lag 1, consecutive samples are nearly always rising or falling together, and the measure mostly sees the smoothness of the signal. The lag decides which time scale the lineups describe.

Third, ties. When two values in a pattern are equal, their order is undefined. Software usually breaks ties by position, which favours some patterns over others. We quantised white noise to five levels. Its permutation entropy dropped from 1.79 to 1.58, with no change in the underlying process. Low-resolution recordings, clipped signals, and flat segments all create ties.

And fourth, noise. Small noise reshuffles nearly equal values. On a smooth signal, it pushes permutation entropy up toward the maximum.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study compares permutation entropy between two groups recorded on different systems, or with different filters, and finds lower values in one group. But one system had coarser amplitude resolution, so more ties, or a stronger low-pass filter, so smoother signals at lag 1. Either alone lowers permutation entropy.

The fix, report the order, the lag, the sampling rate, and the tie handling, check the fraction of patterns containing ties, and match filtering and resolution across groups, or adapt the lag to the time scale of interest.

Let's recap.

Permutation entropy is the Shannon entropy of ordinal patterns, d-samples replaced by their ranks with d-factorial possible patterns.

It needs no tolerance and no bins and is unchanged by any increasing transformation. The exponential and the cube both gave 1.74 like the original.

It is blind to amplitude, Gaussian and Cauchy noise both reach 1.79.

Order, lag, and ties are part of the measure. Five quantisation levels dropped white noise from 1.79 to 1.58.

It is a tolerance-free cousin of sample entropy. It asks whether the ups and downs repeat, not whether values stay close.

Multiscale entropy is sample entropy computed after coarse graining, a low-pass filter followed by downsampling. A tolerance fixed across scales turns shrinking variance into falling entropy for white noise, and extra fast noise shifts entropy at fine and coarse scales although the slow dynamics are identical. A scale is a filter, not a time scale of the dynamics, so the spectrum should be reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Multiscale entropy — and what scale really means.

In episode 10.1, sample entropy measured the regularity of a signal at its own sampling rate. But brain signals have structure at many timescales. Multiscale entropy promises a profile of complexity across scales. Today we look at what its scales really are, and what can move its curve without any change in complexity.

Here is the intuition. Picture a beekeeper who looks at a hive from different distances. Up close every bee is visible and the scene looks chaotic. From a few metres away, individual bees blur into moving patches. From across the field, only the slow swelling of the swarm remains. Each distance is a filter. What you call complex depends on how much of the fine detail your distance lets through.

Here is the recipe, introduced by Madalena Costa, Ary Goldberger and Chung-Kang Peng in 2002. Take the signal. For scale 2, average consecutive pairs of samples without overlap. For scale 3, average consecutive triplets and so on. This is called coarse graining. Then compute sample entropy on each coarse grained signal and plot it against the scale.

Now look at what coarse graining does. Averaging tau consecutive samples is a moving average filter, followed by keeping one sample out of tau. That is a low-pass filter, then downsampling. Scale tau keeps roughly the frequencies below the sampling rate divided by 2 tau and removes the rest. So multiscale entropy is the sample entropy of a series of increasingly low-pass filtered signals.

There is a second detail. In the original recipe, the tolerance r is set once, as a fraction of the standard deviation of the original signal and kept fixed for every scale. But filtering removes variance. At coarse scales the signal is smaller, so a fixed tolerance becomes relatively larger and matches become easier.

A quick check of the size of the effect. Average tau independent samples, and the standard deviation shrinks by the square root of tau. At scale 4, white noise keeps half of its standard deviation, at scale 16 a quarter. A tolerance set at 0.15 original standard deviations is then 0.3, and then 0.6 of the signal's own standard deviation. In episode 10.1, we saw how strongly sample entropy depends on that ratio.

For signals with slow structure, like pink noise, averaging removes less variance because neighbouring samples are already similar. So the same fixed tolerance hurts white noise much more than pink noise. Part of the classic contrast between the two is a contrast in how fast their variance falls when you filter them, which is another way of describing their spectra.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 6,000 samples of white noise and of pink noise, whose power falls as one over frequency, both standardised. We used m equal to 2 and r equal to 0.15 times the standard deviation of the original signal, fixed across scales. Values are in nats.

First, the classic result. White noise starts at 2.47 at scale 1 and falls steadily to 1.21 at scale 15. Pink noise stays nearly flat, around 1.8 to 1.9. This was the original picture of Costa and colleagues. White noise is irregular only at the finest scale, and pink noise, like healthy physiological signals, keeps its complexity across scales.

Second, the same computation with the tolerance recomputed at each scale from the standard deviation of the coarse-grained signal. White noise now stays flat, around 2.4 to 2.6 at every scale. That makes sense. Averaged white noise is still white noise, with no structure at any scale. The fall in the classic curve came from the shrinking variance meeting a fixed tolerance.

Third, two signals with identical slow dynamics. Signal A is our pink noise. Signal B is the same pink noise, plus independent fast noise, extra high frequency power, and nothing else. At scale 1, B looks more complex, 2.20 against 1.89. At scale 15, B looks less complex, 1.67 against 1.77 because its tolerance was set from a larger standard deviation that included the fast noise.

That is the strong point of today, a complexity at fine scales that is really a difference in spectral power. Adding fast power raised fine scale entropy, and through the fixed tolerance lowered coarse-scale entropy. Neither change says anything about the slow dynamics which were identical.

This is exactly what Julian Kosciessa, Niels Kloosterman and Douglas Garrett showed in 2020 in brain data and simulations. Standard multiscale entropy reflects neural dynamics at mismatched timescales. Entropy at coarse scales thought to reflect slow dynamics was biased by fast dynamics through the fixed tolerance. And entropy at fine scales was highly sensitive to broadband power, which is dominated by slow frequencies. A scale label is not the time scale of the dynamics it describes.

What does a multiscale entropy curve depend on?

First the filter. Coarse graining is a crude low-pass filter with poor frequency separation. Each scale mixes many frequencies. Variants replace it with proper filters or keep only band-specific content, so that a scale corresponds to a known frequency range.

Second, the tolerance. Fixed across scales, it turns variance changes into entropy changes. Recomputed per scale, it removes that effect, but then the values reflect pattern structure only. Both are legitimate and the choice must be reported.

Third, the spectrum of the signal. Any difference in power between groups at any frequency can appear as a difference in entropy at some scale.

Fourth, the length of the recording. Each coarse graining divides the number of samples by the scale. At scale 15, 6,000 samples become 400, and sample entropy becomes noisy or undefined.

And fifth, the parameters inherited from sample entropy, m, r, and the sampling rate of the original signal.

Six, the choice of reference spectrum for comparison. Pink noise is a common benchmark, but brain signals have peaks like alpha that a smooth power law does not. A peak at 10 hertz affects every scale whose filter still lets 10 hertz through, and no scale whose filter removes it.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study reports that older adults show higher entropy at fine scales and lower entropy at coarse scales than young adults, and concludes that brain dynamics shift from slow to fast complexity with age. But the groups also differ in spectral power. More high-frequency power and less low-frequency power in older adults would produce the same pattern, with no change in dynamics at all.

The fix, always report the power spectrum alongside the entropy curve. Use a scale definition with a known frequency content, recompute the tolerance per scale or show both versions, and test whether entropy differences remain after accounting for spectral power.

The notebook for this episode implements coarse graining and multiscale entropy, reproduces white and pink noise with fixed and recomputed tolerance, and builds the pair of signals with identical slow dynamics and different fast power.

Let's recap.

Coarse graining is a low-pass filter followed by downsampling. Multiscale entropy is sample entropy along a ladder of filters.

With a fixed tolerance, white noise falls from 2.47 to 1.21 nats across scales. With a recomputed tolerance, it stays flat, as white noise should.

Adding fast power raised fine scale entropy from 1.89 to 2.20 and lowered coarse scale entropy from 1.77 to 1.67 with identical slow dynamics.

Kosciessa and colleagues showed the same mismatch in brain data. Scale is a filter, not a time scale of the dynamics. Report the spectrum next to the entropy curve.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Lempel–Ziv — Complexity. Complexity as compressibility.

In Episode 2.3, Entropy was the limit of compression. No code can describe a source in fewer bits per symbol than its entropy. Today we run that idea backwards. Take a signal, see how well it compresses, and read the result as an estimate of its entropy rate. That is Lempel–Ziv complexity, one of the most used complexity measures in EEG.

Here is the intuition. A bee leaves a trail of dance steps, and a scribe copies the trail into a notebook. To save ink, the scribe never writes a phrase that has already appeared. Each time the trail produces something new, the scribe writes one new word, the shortest piece that could not be copied from earlier in the trail. A repetitive dance needs very few words. A random dance needs a new word almost at every step. Counting the words measures how much is genuinely new.

Here is the recipe. First, the signal is turned into a string of symbols, usually zeros and ones, one when the signal is above a threshold, zero when it is below. Then the string is parsed from left to right into words, each word being the shortest piece not already seen in the string so far. This parsing was defined by Abraham Lempel and Jacob Ziv in 1976. The number of words c is the Lempel-Ziv complexity.

A classic example from Kaspar and Schuster in 1987, who gave the standard algorithm. The 16 symbols 0001101001000101 split into six words. 0, 001, 10, 100, 1000, and 101. Each word is a copy of something seen before, plus one new symbol at its end. A raw count grows with the length of the string so it is normalised. c times the base 2 logarithm of n divided by n. For long strings from a stationary source, this normalised value approaches the entropy rate of the symbol sequence, in bits per symbol.

Now the calculation in one picture.

First, a check with sources whose entropy rate we know. Independent random bits with a probability of one of 0.5, 0.3, or 0.1. With 10,000 symbols, the normalised complexity is 1.03, 0.89, and 0.44 bits per symbol. The true entropy rates are 1, 0.88, and 0.47. Close with errors of a few hundredths at this length, with 1,000 symbols, the errors are larger. Lempel-Ziv complexity is an entropy rate estimator and a fairly slow one to converge.

Second, the strong point of today. Take one signal, an autoregressive process with memory 0.5, 10,000 samples. Binarise it at its median, complexity 0.93. Binarise the same signal at its 70th percentile, 0.83. At its 90th percentile, 0.44. Same signal, same dynamics, three answers.

Much of that change has nothing to do with dynamics. A threshold at the 90th percentile produces ones only 10% of the time, and a binary sequence with 10% ones cannot have an entropy rate above 0.47 bits, whatever its dynamics. The threshold sets a ceiling through the proportion of 1s. The threshold is part of the measure, and it must be reported.

The same holds for amplitude when the threshold is fixed in absolute units. Double the amplitude of the signal and keep the threshold at 0.5. Complexity goes from 0.87 to 0.94, with no change in dynamics. That is why many studies binarise at the median of each signal, which keeps half 1s and half 0s.

What does Lempel-Ziv complexity depend on?

First, the binarisation, the threshold, the use of the raw signal or its envelope, and whether the signal was filtered first. Each choice defines a different symbol sequence, with a different entropy rate.

Second, the length of the signal. The normalisation is exact only in the limit. Short segments give biased values, so comparisons need equal lengths.

Third, the sampling rate. Oversampling creates long runs of identical symbols, which compress well and lower the complexity per sample.

And fourth, everything binarisation throws away. Two signals with different amplitudes and shapes can give the same binary string.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study compares Lempel-Ziv complexity between two states, binarising each recording around its mean. But one state has a skewed amplitude distribution, so the mean is not the median and the proportion of ones differs between states. The complexity difference may come from the proportion of ones, not from the dynamics.

The fix, report the binarisation precisely, prefer the median, check the proportion of ones, use equal lengths and sampling rates, and compare with surrogates that keep the spectrum, such as phase randomised signals, to see whether the result goes beyond what the spectrum alone predicts.

Integration and segregation · Neural complexity (TSE)

Neural complexity scores the balance between segregation and integration by summing, over subset sizes, how far average subset integration falls below a straight line from parts to whole. It is zero for independent regions, intermediate for total synchrony, and highest for teams that are integrated inside but independent of each other. With Gaussian signals it keeps growing as correlations rise, so comparisons need matched integration.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Integration and segregation — Neural complexity So far in this chapter, complexity meant the irregularity of one signal over time. Today the question changes. A brain is many regions at once, and its organisation seems to combine two opposite properties. Regions do different things, which is segregation, and they work together, which is integration. In 1994, Giulio Tononi, Olaf Sporns, and Gerald Edelman proposed a single number for that balance. It is called neural complexity or TSE complexity after its authors.

Here is the intuition. Picture a hive. If every bee did its own thing, ignoring the others, the hive would be a crowd, not a colony. Maximal diversity, no coordination. If every bee did exactly the same thing at the same time, the hive would be a single giant bee: perfect coordination, no diversity. A real colony is in between, teams with different jobs coordinated with each other. Neural complexity is designed to be low at both extremes and high in between.

Here is the idea behind the formula, built from chapter 9. Take the total correlation of episode 9.2, which Tononi and colleagues called integration, the sum of the individual entropies minus the joint entropy. Now look at subsets of the system. For every size k from one region to all n regions, take all subsets of that size and average their integration. If the system had no special structure, the integration of a subset of size k would simply be k over n times the integration of the whole. Neural complexity adds up over all subset sizes how far the actual average falls below that straight line. Equivalently, in entropies, it adds up how much the average entropy of subsets of size k exceeds k over n times the entropy of the whole.

In words, a complex system has small subsets that look nearly independent and larger subsets that are increasingly integrated, both at once.

Notice what the formula does not need. No target, no direction of time, no redundancy measure. Only entropies of subsets. That makes it simple to state and, as we will see, expensive to compute.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We use a toy system that can be computed exactly. Six binary regions each carrying one fair bit of entropy with different patterns of dependence. Values are in bits.

First six independent regions. Every subset's entropy is just its size. The entropy curve is a straight line, and the complexity is exactly zero. Maximal segregation, no integration.

Second, six regions that are all identical copies of one bit. Total synchrony. The integration is five bits, the highest possible here. But every subset of any size has an entropy of one bit. Small subsets are already as integrated as the whole. The complexity is 2.5 bits.

Picture the curve. On the horizontal axis, the subset size from 1 to 6. On the vertical axis, the average entropy of subsets of that size. The reference is a straight line from 0 up to the entropy of the whole system. For independent regions, the curve lies exactly on the line. For total synchrony, the whole system has 1 bit, so the line climbs slowly from 0 to 1, and the curve sits flat at 1 bit for every size: above the line but only by a little and less and less as the size grows.

Third, three teams of two. Within each team, the two regions are identical. The teams are independent of each other. The integration is only three bits, less than for total synchrony, but the complexity is 3.5 bits, higher than for total synchrony. Small subsets, which usually pick regions from different teams look nearly independent. Larger subsets become more and more integrated as they include both members of teams. Two teams of three give the same complexity, 3.5 bits with four bits of integration.

Here are the numbers behind the teams of two. The average entropy of single regions is one bit, of pairs 1.8, of triplets 2.4, then 2.8, 3, and 3 bits for the whole. The straight line climbs from 0 to 3 bits. The curve rises steeply at first, then flattens far above the line in the middle. That bulge is the complexity.

That is the strong point of today. Neural complexity is 0 for independent regions, intermediate for fully synchronous regions, and highest for modular structures in between: segregated teams that are each internally integrated. More integration is not more complexity. It is the mixture that scores high.

Now a warning about continuous signals. For Gaussian signals, entropies have no upper bound, and for a group of regions with equal correlations, neural complexity keeps growing as the correlation approaches one. We computed it for eight Gaussian regions: about 1 at a correlation of 0.3, 6.6 at 0.9, 12.5 at 0.99. The low value at total synchrony depends on bounded entropies like our binary regions or on measurement noise. With continuous data, comparisons are fair only at fixed integration. At 4 bits of integration for 8 Gaussian regions, a homogeneous system gave 3.14 bits of complexity, two clusters gave 4.33 and four independent pairs gave 6.

What does neural complexity depend on?

First, the estimator of the entropy is usually a Gaussian model built from covariances, with all the properties of episode 2.8, blind to nonlinear dependence and unbounded as correlations approach 1.

Second, the number of regions. The number of subsets grows exponentially, 2 to the power n. For more than a couple of dozen regions, people sample subsets at random or use approximations, and the value depends on that choice.

Third, the noise. Measurement noise caps correlations and shapes the curve. Adding or removing noise changes the complexity.

Fourth, the parcellation. Which signals count as regions decides which subsets exist.

And fifth, the relation to chapter 9. Neural complexity is closely related to the O information and S information of episode 9.4. All of them describe how dependence is distributed across scales of a system, and they can be written in the same entropy terms. A high value says the dependence is spread across scales. It does not say whether that dependence is redundant or synergistic.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study finds higher neural complexity in one condition and concludes that the brain is better balanced between segregation and integration. But with a Gaussian estimator, neural complexity also grows whenever overall correlations grow, for example because of a stronger global signal or more shared noise. Higher complexity can simply mean more correlation everywhere.

The reverse also happens. Removing a global signal lowers correlations everywhere and lowers complexity with them, without any change in how the regions are organised.

The fix, report integration alongside complexity, compare conditions at matched integration or with normalised values, control global signals and shared noise, and show the full subset curve not just its area.

Part 1

Measuring integrated information — A family of Φ.

In episode 10.5, neural complexity looked at how dependence spreads across subsets at one moment. Integrated information asks a dynamical question. How much more does the whole system know about its own future than its parts do, taken separately? Many measures try to answer it, and they are all called Φ. Today we line some of them up and test them on the same small systems.

Here is the intuition. A beekeeper splits a hive into two boxes, with no bees allowed between them. Each box carries on with its own life. Then she asks how well can each box predict what it will do next, compared with the undivided hive. If the two boxes predict their own futures just as well as the whole hive did, the hive was really two colonies sharing a roof. If something is lost by the cut, the whole was doing something its parts cannot. Integrated information measures that loss.

For brain signals, the boxes are groups of regions or channels, and prediction means the information that the recent past of a group carries about its next state. A system of two regions that never influence each other should lose nothing when cut. A system whose regions constantly inform each other should lose a lot. Every member of the Phi family agrees on those two extremes in principle. As we will see, they disagree about almost everything in between.

And which cut? A hive can be split in many ways. The idea from the integrated information literature is to look for the cut that loses the least, the minimum information partition, the weakest seam of the system. The integrated information is what is lost across that weakest seam. If even the weakest cut loses a lot, the system is integrated through and through.

Now the members of the family. In 2011, Adam Barrett and Anil Seth proposed practical measures for time series, computed from the information between the past and the present of a system. In 2019, Pedro Mediano, Anil Seth, and Adam Barrett compared six candidate measures in a single study, on networks of eight nodes with Gaussian autoregressive dynamics. We take four of them on two-node systems where everything can be computed exactly from covariances.

First, whole minus sum. The time-delayed mutual information of the whole minus the sum of each part's time-delayed mutual information with itself. We met it in episode 9.12.

Second, integrated stochastic interaction: how much more uncertain each part's future is, given only its own past, compared with the whole future given the whole past. It compares conditional entropies instead of mutual informations.

Third, causal density, the average transfer entropy between the parts in both directions. It counts the information each part sends to the other.

Fourth, phi R, the revised whole minus sum from episode 9.12, which adds back the redundancy-to-redundancy atom, here with the minimum mutual information definition.

The 2019 comparison also included two measures by Masafumi Oizumi and colleagues, from 2016, a decoder-based phi and a geometric phi. They need more machinery, and we leave them to the notebook's reading list. Four members are enough to see the family resemblance and the family quarrels.

Part 2

Now the calculation in one picture.

Two regions, each with memory 0.4, coupled to each other with a strength that we increase from 0 to 0.4. In a first family, the two noise sources are independent. In a second family, they are correlated, with a correlation of 0.5. Values are in bits.

With independent noise, the four measures agree on the direction. All are zero without coupling and all grow with coupling, but not on the size. At a coupling of 0.4, whole minus sum 0.12, causal density 0.15, stochastic interaction 0.3, phi R 0.43, a factor of more than three between them on the same system.

With correlated noise, the agreement breaks down. Without any coupling at all, stochastic interaction is already 0.21 bits, two disconnected regions scored as integrated because their noise is shared. As coupling grows, whole minus sum goes the other way. It falls below zero, down to minus 0.29 bits at a coupling of 0.4. Causal density rises more slowly than before. And phi R first dips from 0.03 to 0.02 before rising to 0.22.

Look at the arithmetic behind whole minus sum at a coupling of 0.4. With independent noise, the whole system carries 0.74 bits from past to present, and each part carries 0.31 bits about its own future. 0.74 minus twice 0.31 leaves 0.12. With correlated noise, the whole carries exactly the same 0.74 bits. But each part now predicts its own future better, 0.515 bits, because the shared noise makes each part's past informative about both. 0.74 minus twice 0.515 is minus 0.29. Nothing about the coupling changed, only the shared noise did.

So on the same family of systems, one measure is positive without any interaction, one becomes negative as interaction grows, one grows steadily, and one is not even monotonic.

That is the strong point of today. Applied to the same systems, the candidate measures of integrated information do not agree with each other. The comparison by Mediano, Seth, and Barrett reached the same conclusion on larger networks. No two measures showed consistent agreement across all their analyses.

Why do they disagree? Because each one formalises a different intuition. Whole minus sum subtracts the parts' self-prediction, and so subtracts shared, redundant information twice, which can make it negative. Stochastic interaction counts any dependence between the parts' futures, including shared noise. Causal density counts directed transfer only. Phi R repairs whole minus sum with one particular redundancy definition. The word integration covers all of these, but they are not the same quantity.

A useful way to remember them is by what moves them. Coupling moves all four. Shared noise moves stochastic interaction up and whole minus sum down. Redundancy between the parts pulls whole minus sum down and phi R corrects for it. Causal density ignores anything that is not directed transfer between the parts.

What does a phi value depend on?

First, the measure itself as we just saw.

Second, the partition. With two regions, there is only one way to cut. With eight, there are 127 ways to cut the system in two, and many more ways to cut it into several parts. Finding the weakest seam requires a search, and comparing cuts of unequal size requires a normalisation, which itself changes which cut is chosen.

Third, the time lag between past and present and the sampling rate as for transfer entropy.

Fourth, the estimator. Most practical versions assume Gaussian linear dynamics which makes them computable and blind to nonlinear structure.

And fifth, shared noise and common inputs, which several measures read as integration.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study reports that integrated information drops under anaesthesia and concludes that the brain integrates less information. But only one measure was computed. With another candidate, the same data might show no change or the opposite change, especially if anaesthesia also changes shared noise or global signals.

If anaesthesia also changed slow, shared fluctuations, or if recording conditions changed with it, a measure that reads shared noise as integration and a measure that reads it as negative integration could tell opposite stories about the same recordings.

The fix, name the measure, give its definition and partition rule, and report at least two measures that formalise different intuitions. Check the measures on simulated systems with known coupling and known shared noise, so that readers know what each measure responds to.

Integrated Information Theory — A critical look.

In Episode 10.6, Phi was a family of statistical measures, ways to quantify how much a system's whole knows about its future beyond its parts. The same letter Phi also names the central quantity of a theory of consciousness, Integrated Information Theory, or IIT. Today we look at that theory, its versions, its computational cost, and the public controversy of 2023. We will not decide whether the theory is right. We will separate two things that are often confused.

Here is the intuition. A beekeeper owns a ruler. With it she measures how tightly the cells of a comb are packed. That is a measurement and anyone can use the ruler, whatever they believe about bees. Now imagine someone claims that the packing measured by the ruler is what makes a hive alive. That is a theory. It may be right or wrong, but it is a different kind of claim, and using the ruler does not commit you to it. Today's episode is about telling the ruler from the theory.

Now the theory. Giulio Tononi proposed Integrated Information Theory in 2004, and it has been revised several times since. The current version, IIT 4.0, was published by Larissa Albantakis, Tononi and colleagues in 2023. Unlike most theories, it does not start from brain data. It starts from axioms about experience itself presented as self-evident. Experience exists. It is intrinsic, it exists for itself. It is specific, it is this experience and not another. It is unified, it cannot be split into independent parts. It is definite with particular borders, and it is structured, composed of distinctions and relations. From each axiom, the theory derives a postulate about the physical substrate that could support such an experience. The quantity at the centre is phi, how irreducible the cause and effect structure of a system is across its weakest partition. The theory then identifies consciousness with the maximally irreducible structure.

Notice how this phi differs from the phi of episode 10.6. The measures of 10.6 are computed from observed time series, the statistics of a system as it actually behaves. The phi of IIT is defined from the causal structure of a system, how every possible state of its elements constrains every other, as revealed by perturbing it, not by watching it. In its full form, it applies to discrete systems with Markovian dynamics, whose complete transition probabilities are known.

Now the cost in one picture.

Computing the phi of IIT requires examining every subset of elements, every possible mechanism, every possible set of elements it could affect, and every way of partitioning them. The developers of the reference software, PyPhi, describe its algorithm as exponential in the number of nodes, of order n times 53 to the power n. In practice, they report that this limits analysis to roughly 10 to 12 nodes. Their own timing example. A ring of three simple logic elements takes about one second, five elements about 16 seconds, and seven elements about two and three-quarter hours on 32 processor cores.

To feel the explosion, count only the ways of partitioning a system into groups. Four elements 15 ways. Eight, 4,140. Twelve more than four million. A human brain has tens of billions of neurons. The full phi of a brain is not something anyone can compute. Every empirical study that cites the theory computes a proxy instead.

One well-known proxy shows how the ruler and the theory can travel separately. The Perturbational Complexity Index, introduced by Adenauer Casali, Marcello Massimini and colleagues in 2013, was inspired by the theory's idea that a conscious brain is both integrated and differentiated. It stimulates the cortex with transcranial magnetic stimulation, records the EEG response, and compresses the pattern of significant activity with the Lempel-Ziv method of episode 10.4. A response that spreads widely and in a complex pattern compresses poorly. The index is not the phi of the theory. It is an empirical measure, inspired by it, whose value as a marker of consciousness is judged by data, independently of whether the theory is true.

And even on small systems, phi is not always uniquely defined. Adam Barrett and Pedro Mediano argued in 2019 that the measure is not well defined for general physical systems. Jake Hanson and Sara Walker showed that ties in the intermediate optimisations can leave many possible phi values for the same system. For one small logic gate system, they found 83.

Now the controversy. A large adversarial collaboration, called the Cogitate Consortium, tested predictions of integrated information theory against those of global neuronal workspace theory, with proponents of both agreeing on the predictions in advance. Preliminary results were presented in June 2023 at a conference in New York. The full results were published in Nature in April 2025. Neither theory came out unscathed. For integrated information theory, for example, the team did not find the sustained synchronisation within posterior cortex that the theory had been taken to predict.

In September 2023, an open letter signed by 124 scientists and philosophers argued that integrated information theory should be labelled pseudoscience. Among the reasons given were the difficulty of testing its core assumptions and some of its implications, for example about which systems might be conscious. The letter caused an uproar. Many researchers objected to the label, including some who are not proponents of the theory. Anil Seth, for instance, said he disagreed with calling it pseudoscience. The letter's authors later published a commentary explaining their objections in more detail in Nature Neuroscience in 2025, and the debate continues. We present it here without deciding it.

That is the strong point of today, separate phi as a statistical measure of a system from phi as a theory of consciousness. You can use the one without subscribing to the other.

Concretely, when a study computes an empirical integrated information measure on EEG or functional MRI, it is measuring a statistical property of the signals, with all the sensitivities of episodes 10.5 and 10.6. Whether that property tracks consciousness is an empirical question, to be tested like any other. And whether integrated information theory is a true theory of consciousness does not decide whether the measure is useful for describing brain dynamics.

What should you check when you read a study that mentions phi?

First, which phi? An empirical measure from time series or the phi of IIT computed on a small model? If it is empirical, which member of the family and with which partition rule?

Second, the system. Brain signals are not discrete Markovian systems with known transition probabilities, any phi computed from them is an approximation built on extra assumptions. The PyPhi developers themselves warn that their assumptions should be checked before applying the software to transition probabilities estimated from recordings such as EEG.

Third, the claim. Does the paper report a statistical difference in a measure, or does it infer something about consciousness from it. The second step needs its own justification.

Now the classic pitfall.

A paper finds that an empirical integration measure is higher in one condition, and concludes that the participants were more conscious, citing integrated information theory. But the measure was not the phi of the theory, the system did not meet the theory's assumptions, and the step from measure to consciousness was taken from the theory, not tested.

The fix, name the measure precisely, keep the statistical result and the theoretical interpretation in separate sentences, and state which assumptions of the theory, if any, the data could actually test.

There is no notebook for this episode. It is about concepts, not a new estimator. The phi measures we can compute were in the notebooks of episodes 10.5 and 10.6.

Let's recap. Integrated information theory starts from axioms about experience derives postulates about physical substrates and identifies consciousness with maximally irreducible cause and effect structure its current version is 4.0 its phi is defined from causal structure under perturbation for discrete Markovian systems and its computation explodes about 10 to 12 elements in practice A large adversarial collaboration challenged predictions of both theories it tested, and a 2023 open letter calling the theory pseudoscience sparked a debate that is not settled.

Proxies such as the Perturbational Complexity Index are judged by data, not by the theory that inspired them.

Use phi as a measure without adopting phi as a theory, and keep the two claims apart.
