The Laplacian

Put a number on every node, and the Laplacian, D minus A, turns it into a score from the gaps with each node's neighbours. Multiplying the numbers by L and again by the numbers gives the roughness, the sum of squared gaps over links, which is zero when every linked pair agrees. Hall's circuit placement means making this roughness small under a fixed rule.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: what if we put a number on every node?

Today we answer, in a violin maker's workshop.

On the wall hangs a sketch. It is a circuit, drawn by hand. Seven components, nine wires. We made it up for this episode.

In nineteen seventy, an engineer named Kenneth Hall faced a puzzle like this one. Where should the components go, so that the wires are as short as possible?

There is a twist. Each wire counts as the square of its length. A wire twice as long costs four times as much.

That sounds like electronics. But look again. Forget the board. Keep the dots and the lines.

There it is. A graph in disguise.

Now put a number on every node. Think of a temperature.

Pick one node, E. It has four neighbours. Compare E with each of them. Take the gap: the number on E, minus the number on the neighbour. Add the four gaps. That sum is the score of E.

Now do the same for every node.

Let every node hold a five. Each gap is zero, so each score is zero.

Next, let the left half hold a one, and the right half a five. Inside each half, nothing changes, so the score stays zero. Only two links cross between the halves.

B scores minus four. C scores minus four. D scores plus four. E scores plus four.

So the score is zero where the numbers agree. And it is large where the number jumps.

This recipe is a matrix. First, the degrees, written on the diagonal. Then subtract the adjacency table from episode one point five. What is left is the Laplacian, called L.

Read row E. A four on the diagonal, because E has four neighbours. A minus one for each neighbour. Zero everywhere else.

Look at the rows. Each one adds to zero.

Now multiply L by the column of numbers. Row by row, out come exactly the scores.

You have met this table before. In episode five point six, it described a circuit with eight junctions: degree on the diagonal, minus one for a wire. Its rows added to zero too. Delete one row and one column, and the determinant counted nine spanning trees. Same matrix, seen from another side.

Now the question that matters. How rough is a signal on a graph?

Take the gap on every link. Square it. Add them all up, counting each link once. Call the total the roughness.

For our step, two links cross. Each has a gap of four. Four squared is sixteen. Two times sixteen is thirty-two.

Here is the surprise. Multiply the column of numbers by L, and then by the same column again. You get thirty-two as well.

That is no accident. For any numbers, on any graph, this product is the sum over the links of the squared gaps.

Try other signals. A constant one gives zero. A smooth ramp, where neighbours differ by at most one, gives six. A zigzag gives one hundred and fifty-one. The rougher the signal, the bigger the number. And zero means that every linked pair agrees.

Now back to the wall. Read the sideways position of each component as a number, from zero to ten.

In this messy placement, the squared sideways gaps add up to three hundred and fifty-eight. In a tidier placement, they add up to forty-two.

A wire's squared length is its sideways gap squared, plus its up-and-down gap squared. So the best placement makes both sets of numbers smooth.

But there is a catch. The smoothest numbers of all are the same everywhere. Roughness zero. Every component in one spot.

Hall rules the pile out. His positions must obey a fixed constraint, so the components cannot gather. Under that rule, the best placement comes from this very matrix. How? That is for episode eleven point four.

To sum up. Put a number on every node. The Laplacian, D minus A, turns each number into a score. The roughness is the number times L times the number, and Hall's placement makes it as small as the rule allows.

We will meet this matrix again in chapters twelve and thirteen.

PLATE 11.24:56

Eigenvalues as a fingerprint

An eigenvalue is how much a matrix stretches a special pattern, and the spectrum lists them from zero. In a seven-component circuit, two islands give two zeros and three islands give three, so the zeros count the islands. Renumbering the nodes changes nothing. Yet two different six-node graphs share the same spectrum, so one cannot always hear the shape of a graph.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: what numbers hide inside this matrix?

Today we find out, back in the violin maker's workshop.

The violin maker taps the wood. It rings with a few clear notes. The shape and the wood decide those notes, so they are a kind of fingerprint of the violin.

A matrix rings too. Its notes are called eigenvalues.

Take the smallest example: three nodes in a row. Here is its Laplacian, called L.

Put a number on every node and apply the table. Out come new numbers.

Try a one on the first node, and zeros elsewhere. We get a one, a minus one, and a zero. The pattern has changed shape.

But some patterns keep their shape.

Put a one on every node. L turns it into zeros. That is the same pattern, stretched by zero.

Try a one, a zero, and a minus one. Multiply by L, and you get a one, a zero, and a minus one again. Stretched by one.

Now try a one, a minus two, and a one. L gives a three, a minus six, and a three. The same pattern, stretched by three.

Such a pattern is special. The matrix does not bend it. It only stretches it. The stretch is an eigenvalue, and the pattern is its eigenvector.

This little graph has three eigenvalues: zero, one, and three.

And you have met them before. Shrink each pattern until its squares add up to one. Then its roughness, from last time, is exactly its eigenvalue. The flat pattern has roughness zero. The zigzag has the largest, three.

So an eigenvalue tells you how rough its pattern is.

Now return to our circuit. Seven components, nine wires. Its Laplacian has seven eigenvalues. Line them up from smallest to largest.

Zero. Zero point six three. One point seven four. Three. Three point three two. Three point eight seven. Five point four four.

This list is the spectrum of the graph. These are the notes it rings with.

The first note is always zero. Every graph has the flat pattern, and the flat pattern gets stretched by zero.

Now break the circuit. Cut two wires, B to D and C to E. It falls into two islands. Look at the spectrum. Two zeros.

Cut two more, E to F and E to G. Three islands. Three zeros.

So count the zeros, and you count the islands. Those are the connected components of episode two point four, read straight from the numbers.

Next, renumber the nodes. Same circuit, new labels.

The table changes. Twenty-nine of its forty-nine cells are different. Yet the spectrum is exactly the same.

The notes do not depend on how we number the nodes. They belong to the graph itself.

That makes the spectrum a fingerprint. Any two drawings of the same graph give the same one.

So here is the question. If two graphs ring with the same notes, are they the same graph?

In nineteen sixty-six, the mathematician Mark Kac asked the same thing about drums. Can one hear the shape of a drum?

A drum rings with notes too. They come from the eigenvalues of its own Laplacian, the cousin of our matrix for a flat sheet.

In nineteen ninety-two, Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert gave the answer. No. They drew two different flat drums, with straight edges, that ring with exactly the same notes.

Graphs do the same. Here are two graphs with six nodes and seven wires.

The first has a dangling node, held by a single wire. The second has no dangling node, but one node holding four wires. Only the second has a triangle.

No renumbering turns one into the other. They are different graphs.

Now compute the spectrum of each. Zero. Zero point seven six. Two. Three. Three. Five point two four. The same six notes, for both.

Graphs that share a spectrum have been known since nineteen fifty-seven, from the work of Collatz and Sinogowitz.

So we cannot always hear the shape of a graph. Different spectra prove that two graphs differ. The same spectrum does not prove they are alike.

The spectrum tells us a lot. It knows the islands, and it ignores the numbering. But it does not know everything.

Another series of ours, Geometry of Data, compares graphs by their spectra. It meets this limit again.

To sum up. An eigenvalue is how much the matrix stretches a special pattern, and how rough that pattern is. The spectrum lists them, starting from zero. The zeros count the islands. Renumbering changes nothing. And two different graphs can share one spectrum.

PLATE 11.34:29

How hard to cut: the Fiedler value

The Fiedler value, the first note above zero, is zero exactly when a graph is in pieces. On a dumbbell of two bunches of five nodes, it falls from two to about 0.3 as the cords between them are snipped from five to one. It needs no source or sink, and Cheeger's inequality brackets the narrowest cut between two numbers made from it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: what does the first note above zero know about a graph?

Today we find out, back in the violin maker's workshop.

On the bench lie two bunches of five nodes. Inside each bunch, every node is linked to every other. Then a few cords join the two bunches. We made this graph up for the episode.

It is called a dumbbell.

Begin with five cords. Each one joins a node on the left to its partner on the right.

Now read the first note above zero. It is two.

Snip one cord. Four are left, and the note drops to one point four four.

Snip another. Three cords, and the note is one.

A third snip leaves two cords. The note is zero point six three.

Then a fourth leaves one cord. The note is about zero point three.

Last, snip the final cord. The dumbbell falls into two pieces. And the note is zero.

This note has a name. In nineteen seventy-three, Miroslav Fiedler, of Prague, called it the algebraic connectivity of a graph. We call it the Fiedler value.

It is zero exactly when the graph is in pieces. And the more cords we leave, the larger it grows.

But it is not simply the number of cords. With one cord it is about zero point three, not one.

Fiedler proved a ceiling. When a graph is not complete, the value never exceeds the fewest nodes you must remove to disconnect it. In our dumbbell, that number is the number of cords.

Now try other shapes, each with ten nodes.

A path of ten nodes scores about zero point one. It can be cut at any of its nine links.

A ring of ten scores zero point three eight.

The dumbbell with one cord scores about zero point three.

And the complete graph, with every pair linked, scores ten. Fiedler's paper shows that it equals the number of nodes.

So the value ranks shapes by how hard they are to cut.

Remember chapter three. There we found bottlenecks with flows. We chose two nodes, and asked how many links must be cut to separate them.

Take two nodes in the same bunch, and the answer is four. Take one node on each side, and the answer is one.

Across all forty-five pairs, the smallest answer is one. But we had to choose well, or try every pair.

The Fiedler value asks for no choice. No source, no sink. One number for the whole graph.

It does not equal the cut. But it rises and falls with it.

How well does it measure the narrowest cut? A theorem gives a sandwich. It is called Cheeger's inequality.

It began with smooth surfaces. In the nineteen eighties, mathematicians carried it over to graphs. We state it without proof.

Two changes first. We use the normalized Laplacian, where each row is scaled by the degrees. And we judge a cut by its conductance.

Conductance is the number of links cut, divided by the volume of the smaller side. The volume of a side is the sum of the degrees of its nodes.

This is a cousin of the normalized cut from episode three point four. That one divides by both sides.

Take the dumbbell with one cord. Cut the cord, and one link is cut. Each side has volume twenty-one.

So the conductance is one over twenty-one, or zero point zero four eight. We tried all five hundred and eleven ways to split the ten nodes in two. None does better.

Now the normalized first note above zero. It is zero point zero seven three.

Half of it, zero point zero three six, is the floor. The square root of twice the note, zero point three eight one, is the ceiling.

The conductance sits between them.

Add cords, and the sandwich holds each time. With five cords the floor is touched. Both numbers are zero point two.

So a small note means a narrow cut exists. A large note means no narrow cut exists.

To sum up. The Fiedler value is the first note above zero. It is zero when the graph is in pieces. It grows with the cords between the bunches, and never passes the number of nodes you must remove to split the graph. It needs no source and no sink. And Cheeger's inequality brackets the narrowest cut between two numbers made from it.

Drawing and splitting with eigenvectors

The Fiedler vector, the list of numbers belonging to the first note above zero, places nodes on a line, and its sign splits the graph in two. Two such lists draw Hall's circuit. This works because the hard cut problem is relaxed into an eigenvector, then rounded. Rounding can fail on a row of three bunches, so try several thresholds.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: the first note says how hard a graph is to cut. Can it also say where?

Today we answer, back in the violin maker's workshop.

On the wall hangs the circuit from episode eleven point one. Seven components, nine wires. That episode ended on a question. Where should each component go?

First, a fact about notes. Every note of the Laplacian comes with a shape. Pluck a string, and it does more than ring at a pitch. It bends into a pattern.

For a graph, that pattern is a list of numbers, one per node. It is called an eigenvector. Multiply the list by L, and the same list comes back, only stretched by the note.

Take the first note above zero. Its list is the Fiedler vector.

Now return to Hall's rule. His positions must be centred. And their spread must be fixed. The spread is the sum of the squared distances from the middle. We fix it at one hundred.

Remember the messy placement of episode eleven point one. Its wires cost three hundred and fifty-eight.

The tidy one had a spread of only fifty-five. Stretch it to one hundred, and its wires cost seventy-six.

Now use the Fiedler vector as the sideways positions. The wires cost sixty-three.

That is no accident. The cost is exactly one hundred times the note, zero point six three.

Hall found that the best placement is built from these lists. We checked it the hard way. We tried one hundred thousand random placements, all with a spread of one hundred. The best of them cost sixty-five.

For the up-and-down positions, take the next list. Its note is one point seven four. The total squared length of all nine wires is two hundred and thirty-seven.

F and G are twins. They have the same neighbours, so they land on one spot.

That is Hall's placement. Two eigenvectors draw the graph.

Now back to the dumbbell, with one cord.

Place each node on a line, at the number the Fiedler vector gives it.

The two bunches pull apart. Four nodes of each bunch sit together at the far ends. The two nodes that hold the cord stand nearer the middle.

Nobody told the vector where the bunches are. Cut by sign. Negative numbers on one side, positive on the other.

That cuts the cord, and nothing else. In episode three point four, we judged a cut by dividing by the volume of each side. Here that normalized cut is zero point zero nine five. It is the best of all five hundred and eleven splits.

In nineteen ninety, Alex Pothen, Horst Simon and Kang-Pu Liou used this idea on a big problem. They split the graph of a sparse matrix in two, to order it for solving on parallel computers.

But why does the sign work? The answer comes from Shi and Malik, in the year two thousand.

Finding the best normalized cut means giving every node one of two labels. For our ten nodes, that is one thousand and twenty-four ways. For the eight hundred and thirty-two pixels of the espresso cup, it is two to the power eight hundred and thirty-two. That is about ten to the two hundred and fiftieth.

Shi and Malik proved that this exact problem is NP-complete, even on a grid of pixels.

So they relaxed the rule. Let the labels be any numbers at all. Now the question has an exact answer, and it is an eigenvector.

The relaxed best is zero point zero seven three. The true best is zero point zero nine five. The relaxed value can never be higher, because it allows more lists.

We traded a problem we cannot solve for a neighbour we can. Then we round. Positive goes to one side, negative to the other.

Rounding can fail. Look at a row of three bunches, with five nodes, then three, then six.

The numbers of the first and last bunch are far from zero. But the middle bunch, a triangle, sits right on the line. Its three numbers are minus zero point two eight, plus zero point one four, and minus zero point zero eight.

Now slice by sign, and the cut runs through the triangle. It slices two links, and the normalized cut is zero point one three six.

The best cut is a single cord, between the triangle and the last bunch. Its normalized cut is zero point zero six seven. We tried all eight thousand, one hundred and ninety-one splits.

So the sign cut is twice as bad as the best. Scaling by the degrees, as Shi and Malik do, does not rescue this graph.

What did Shi and Malik do instead? They did not round at zero. They tried every threshold along the sorted list, and kept the best normalized cut. Here, that finds the single cord.

To sum up. Every note has a shape, a list of numbers. The first note above zero places the nodes on a line, and its sign splits them in two. Two lists draw the graph, as Hall did. This works because the hard problem is relaxed into an eigenvector, then rounded. And rounding can miss, so try several thresholds.

PLATE 11.56:33

Counting walks

Powers of the adjacency table count walks, and for long walks the biggest eigenvalue decides how fast the count grows, with its list giving eigenvector centrality. On the Florentine marriages that biggest note is 3.26. An infection dies out when beta over delta is below one over it, tested on US air routes where that threshold is 0.024.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: count every walk of a given length. Which note decides the count?

Today we answer, back in the violin maker's workshop.

But first, a change of matrix. For four episodes, our notes came from the Laplacian: the degrees on the diagonal, minus the table of episode one point five. Today we use the table itself, the adjacency matrix, with nothing subtracted. Same graph, different matrix, different notes.

Take the graph of episode one point five. Six nodes, two triangles and a bridge. It is illustrative, and the positions mean nothing.

A walk is a trip along links, one step at a time. Nodes may repeat, as in episode two point one.

Square the table, and each cell counts the walks of length two. From A to C there is one, through B. From A back to A there are two: out to B and back, or out to C and back. That is the number of links at A.

Why does this work? A walk of length three is a walk of length two, plus one more step. And one more step is one more multiplication by the table. So the cube counts the walks of length three. To go from A to C in three steps, there are four walks. In general, the power k counts the walks of length k.

Now count all the walks, wherever they start and end. Fourteen of length one, which is twice the seven links. Thirty-four of length two. Then eighty-two, one hundred ninety-eight, four hundred seventy-eight.

Divide each total by the one before. The answers are two point four three, two point four one, two point four one. And from there on, the same again and again. Each extra step multiplies the count by the same number.

That number is the biggest note of the table, its largest eigenvalue. The reason is simple. A count of walks mixes all the notes, each raised to the power k. As k grows, the biggest one wins.

Now a real network: the Florentine marriages of episodes six point one and six point four. Fifteen families, twenty marriages.

Forty walks of length one. One hundred thirty-four of length two. Four hundred twenty, then one thousand three hundred ninety-six. At length ten, about one point six million. At length twenty, about two hundred twenty billion.

The ratios zigzag: three point three five, three point one three, three point three two. Then they close in on the biggest note, three point two six.

Now ask where the walks start. For each family, count the walks of length k that begin there, as a share of all of them.

The Medici hold fifteen percent of the walks of length one, then ten, fourteen, eleven. The share bounces, then settles near twelve percent. The Pazzi, with one marriage, settle near one percent. From length ten on, every family's share is within half a percentage point of its final value. The shape has settled.

Remember episode six point four. Each family took the sum of its neighbours' scores, then we divided by the largest. That is one multiplication by the table. After k rounds, a family's score is the number of walks of length k that start there, divided by the largest count.

We checked it against that episode, and the scores agree to within a millionth.

The list that stops moving is the eigenvector of the biggest note. That is eigenvector centrality. So, which note decides the count? The biggest one. Its size says how fast the count grows. Its list says where the walks come from.

That same number rules something else. In two thousand and three, Yang Wang, Deepayan Chakrabarti, Chenxi Wang and Christos Faloutsos asked when an infection can survive on a network. They presented the answer in Florence, at a symposium on reliable distributed systems.

Their model is simple. At each step, every infected node tries each healthy neighbour, and succeeds with probability beta. And every infected node recovers with probability delta. A recovered node is healthy again, and can catch it again. The model is called S I S.

This is not the model of episode twelve point three. There, each link is tried once, and the quantity is T. Here people catch it again and again, and the quantity is a ratio of two rates, beta over delta.

Their result: the infection dies out when beta over delta is below one over the biggest note.

Now the idea, not the proof. An infection travels along walks. The more walks a network has, the faster the infection can spread. And the biggest note says how fast the walks multiply.

Let us test it on a real network: the American air routes of nineteen ninety-seven. Three hundred thirty-two airports, two thousand one hundred twenty-six routes. Its biggest note is forty-one point two. One over that is zero point zero two four. The epidemic is simulated, with seed one fifteen. Every airport starts infected, and delta is zero point four.

First run: beta over delta at half the threshold. After twenty-five steps, nothing is left.

Second run: twice the threshold. About forty airports stay infected, step after step. After five hundred steps, it is still going.

Now sweep the ratio, with two hundred runs for each value. Below the threshold, no run survives to step five hundred. The median run is gone after sixteen steps at half the threshold, and thirty-two at the threshold itself.

Just above, the infection lingers, then fades. At one and a half times the threshold, about half of the runs survive. At twice, all two hundred do.

One number from the spectrum tells us whether an infection can take hold.

But read it with care. The line is a guarantee on one side only. Below it, the infection dies. Above it, the infection can survive, and in a finite network it still dies out sooner or later. Their analysis rests on an approximation. And this is a model, not a forecast of any real disease.

To sum up. The power k of the table counts the walks of length k. For long walks, the biggest note decides: it sets how fast the count grows, and its list is eigenvector centrality. The same number gives an epidemic threshold, one over it. That closes the chapter on the spectrum.
