In which space do your data live?

Roll up a sheet of paper and the ruler says two points are close, while an inchworm crawling on the paper says far. Same points, two spaces, two answers, and the choice decides neighbours, groups and outliers. Data never come with their distance; someone chose what close means. The series catalogues those choices, asking what each distance ignores, costs and where it traps you.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a sheet of paper, with twelve points on it. Each point could be anything: a recording, a person, a cell, a word.

Take the two points at opposite ends of the sheet. How far apart are they? Lay a ruler between them. Far.

Now ask someone who walks on the paper. This little walker is an inchworm. Its family, the geometer moths, is named after the Greek for "earth measurer", because it seems to measure the ground with every loop. It crawls from one point to the other. Far, again. The two agree.

Now roll the sheet up. The paper curls around, and the two ends come close. Nothing has been drawn, erased or moved. Every point sits exactly where it was on the paper.

Lay the ruler again. It cuts straight through the air, and it says: close. Ask the inchworm. It cannot cut through the air. It has to walk on the paper, all the way round the roll. It says: far.

So who is right? Both. The ruler measures in the space around the paper. The inchworm measures in the paper itself. Same points, two spaces, two answers. And the answer decides what comes after: which points are neighbours, which ones form a group, which one looks out of place.

Your data are in the same situation. They do not come with their distance. Before any analysis, someone has chosen what "close" means, often without noticing. That choice is a space.

This series is a catalogue of those choices, ordered by the kind of object you want to compare. Distances, between lists of numbers, signals, histograms, matrices, graphs and shapes. Spaces, flat ones, curved ones, and spaces with no common frame, like two brains. Algebras, other ways of computing, each one brought in by the question it makes simple. For every distance, we ask what it ignores, what it costs, and where it can trap you.

To follow the main path, you need vectors, and the main path of our Statistics series.

So here is the one question to carry through every episode. In which space do your data live?
