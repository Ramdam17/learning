PLATE 1.13:34

A model is a function with knobs

A model is a function with knobs: a line from stimulus contrast to reaction time has a slope and an offset, its parameters. Learning means turning the knobs to make one number, the typical miss, smaller. More knobs allow curves and billions of parameters, but ten knobs can hit ten dots exactly, which raises the question of overfitting.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the greenhouse. A gardener has a climbing plant and a trellis. She cannot tell the plant where to grow, but she can nudge it, a little here, a little there, until it follows the wires. Hold on to that picture. Today we meet the idea behind this whole series: a model is a function with knobs.

Let's start with some data. A volunteer watches a screen and presses a button as soon as a patch appears. The patch has a contrast, and we record the reaction time. Each dot is one contrast level. Low contrast, slow reaction. High contrast, fast reaction. These ten dots are illustrative numbers, but the trend is real: reaction times shrink as stimulus intensity grows, a regularity known as Pieron's law.

We want a rule that goes from contrast to reaction time. A rule that takes an input and gives back an output is called a function. The simplest candidate is a straight line. The reaction time equals a slope times the contrast, plus an offset. Two numbers: the slope, and the offset.

Think of those two numbers as knobs on a machine. A contrast goes in at the top. A predicted reaction time comes out at the bottom. Turn the slope knob, and the line tilts. Turn the offset knob, and the whole line slides up or down. These knobs have a proper name: parameters. A model is the shape of the machine, plus the setting of its knobs.

But which setting is a good one? We need a number to tell us. For every dot, measure how far the line misses it, then take the typical miss, in milliseconds. Start with a flat line at three hundred. The typical miss is about one hundred sixty milliseconds. Slide the offset up to four hundred twenty, and it drops to fifty-nine. Tilt the slope to negative two, with an offset of five hundred, and we are down to twenty-seven. Keep nudging. A slope of negative two point six, an offset of five hundred twenty-eight: the typical miss is twenty-two point five milliseconds.

That is all that learning means. Turn the knobs, watch one number, and keep the settings that make it smaller. This number has a name, the loss, and next time we will look at it closely.

Now give the machine a third knob, one that lets the line bend. A curve has three knobs: how much it bends, its slope, and its offset. It can follow the dots more closely, and the typical miss falls to seventeen milliseconds. More knobs, more freedom.

Keep going. A model with a hundred knobs can draw wiggles. A model with a billion knobs can describe pictures, sentences and sounds. For scale, GPT-3, a language model described in twenty twenty, has one hundred seventy-five billion of them. Nobody turns that many knobs by hand. The machine has to turn them itself, guided by that single number.

Freedom has a price, though. Ten knobs are enough for a curve to pass exactly through our ten dots, with a typical miss of zero. Is that a good model? Hold that thought, because episode one point four is all about it.

Everything in this series is a variation on three questions. Which function should the machine compute? Which knobs does it have? And which number tells us how well it is doing? Fix those three, and you have a learning problem. Change them, and you get the next method.

To sum up. A model is a function with knobs. Learning means turning the knobs to make one number smaller. Next time: that number, the loss, and the landscape it draws.

PLATE 1.24:38

The loss, one number to blame

The loss turns every miss into one number. Squared gaps drawn as tiles average to the mean squared error, while absolute gaps drawn as planks give the mean absolute error. Each knob setting has a loss, forming a valley that learning descends, and a single blink trial drags the squared fit but barely moves the absolute one, so choosing the loss is choosing what matters.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the greenhouse. Last time, we gave a line two knobs and a number that told us how well it was doing. Today we open that number up. It is called the loss, and it is the only thing the machine ever hears.

Here are our ten dots again: reaction time against contrast, with illustrative numbers. Take the line with a slope of negative two and an offset of five hundred. For every dot, there is a gap between the dot and the line. That gap is the miss, in milliseconds. At five percent contrast, the line misses by sixty-four. At forty percent, it misses by only three.

We cannot simply add the misses up, because some dots sit above the line and some below, and they would cancel. So we square each one. Draw a square on every gap, with the gap as its side. Each square is a tile, and its area is the miss squared. Sixty-four becomes four thousand ninety-six. Three becomes nine.

Now take the mean of the ten tiles. That is the mean squared error. For this line, it is about seven hundred twelve. Its square root, twenty-six point seven, is the typical miss from last time. In NumPy, it is one line: the mean of the residuals, squared. The loss is just the average tile. Check it by hand on four dots that miss by three, minus one, two, and minus two. The tiles are nine, one, four, and four. They add up to eighteen, and the mean is four point five. The typical miss is its square root, about two point one.

Notice what squaring does. A big miss makes an enormous tile, and the loss listens to it. That one miss of sixty-four outweighs the other nine put together. Keep that in mind.

Now let's flip the question. Every setting of the two knobs gives a line, and every line gives a loss. So every point on a map of slope against offset has an altitude, and that altitude is its loss. Draw the contour lines, like a hiking map. The map has a valley, and the best line sits at its bottom: a slope of negative two point six four, an offset of five hundred twenty-eight, and a mean squared error of about five hundred five. Nobody has to try every line. Later in the series, in episode two point four, we will let a marble roll down this valley by itself.

But the loss is a choice. Suppose the volunteer blinked on one trial and pressed late: six hundred forty milliseconds, at fifty-five percent contrast. That is one lapse among eleven dots. With squared tiles, that single dot owns about two thirds of all the tile area. The best line tilts to reach for it. The slope drops from negative two point six four to negative zero point eight six.

Change the loss, and the story changes. Replace the squares with planks. Each gap becomes a plank as long as the miss, and the loss is the mean length: the mean absolute error. A plank grows only in proportion, so the lapse barely counts. The best line now has a slope of negative two point five and an offset of five hundred twenty-two. It almost ignores the blink. For this loss there is no tidy formula, so we found it by checking every line through two dots. Both fits were computed with NumPy, on illustrative data.

Neither loss is right. Choosing the loss is choosing what matters. Squares say that big errors are disasters. Planks say that a few strange trials are not worth bending the whole line. There are reasons to like squares. A tile has a smooth bottom, so a marble can roll down it, while a plank has a sharp corner at zero. And in eighteen oh nine, Gauss showed that if the noise follows a bell curve, the most likely line is the one with the smallest squared error. But the argument is old. Legendre published the squared version in eighteen oh five, in a book on comet orbits. About fifty years earlier, Boscovich, working on the shape of the Earth, had already tried to minimize absolute misses.

Other tasks need other losses. When the answer is a category and not a number, the loss is the cross-entropy, and it arrives in episode two point five. And in chapter six, when a model must predict every pixel of an image, the squared loss comes back, and we will see why that choice proves costly.

To sum up. The loss turns every miss into one number: squares give the mean squared error, planks give the mean absolute error. Every setting of the knobs has a loss, and learning means going downhill. And choosing the loss is choosing what matters. PLATE 1.33:47

Train, validation, test

A model is graded on data it never learned from. Trials go into a training box that turns the knobs, a validation box that chooses between models, and a test box opened once. When trials from one volunteer land on both sides of the split, a nearest-neighbor rule reads personal signature and scores 89 percent; splitting by person gives chance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the greenhouse. A student has a book of exercises with the answers printed at the back. She reads it until she knows every answer by heart. Then comes the exam, with new questions, and she fails. Did she learn anything? Today's question is how to tell a model that learned from a model that only memorized.

Here is a toy dataset. We recorded twenty volunteers, fifteen trials each, three hundred trials in all. Each trial is a short snippet of brain activity, and the goal is to guess which of two groups its owner belongs to. These numbers are simulated, but the trap they hide is real.

The first rule: never grade a model on the data it learned from. So we deal the trials into three boxes. The first is the exercise book, the training set. It is used to turn the knobs, and for nothing else. The second is the mock exam, the validation set. It is used to choose between models, and to pick settings that are not knobs, such as how much a curve may bend. The third is the final exam, the test set. Here that makes one hundred eighty trials, sixty, and sixty.

What can the exercise book tell us? Almost nothing. The loss on it always goes down, because the knobs were turned to make it go down. A good score there says the machine remembers the book, not that it can answer new questions.

The mock exam can be used again and again, but not for free. Try a hundred random settings on sixty validation trials, and keep the best one. Here the labels are pure noise, so every setting is a coin flip. Even so, the best of the hundred scores sixty-six percent on average, while fresh trials give fifty. Each time we look at a box and then change something, a little of that box leaks into the model.

That is why the test box has a padlock. We open it once, at the very end, after every choice is made, and we report what we find. If the number is bad, we do not go back and tweak. Open it twice, and the final exam becomes a second mock exam.

Now the trap that catches real studies. Each volunteer's cards carry their own color. Suppose we shuffle all three hundred trials and deal them at random. Trials from the same person land in the training box and in the test box. Brain activity has a personal signature, like handwriting, and it is much stronger than the difference between the groups. The model does not need to learn anything about the groups. It only needs to recognize the handwriting.

With the random shuffle, a simple nearest neighbor rule reaches eighty-nine percent on the test box. Now deal by volunteer: twelve in training, four in validation, four in test, and nobody in two boxes. The same rule, on the same data, scores forty-nine percent. That is chance. Both numbers are averages over two hundred simulated datasets.

This is not only a toy problem. In twenty twenty-four, Brookshire and colleagues reviewed deep learning studies of clinical EEG in Frontiers in Neuroscience. They found that data from the same person often sat on both sides of the split, which can inflate the reported accuracy. Poldrack and colleagues made the broader point in twenty twenty: evidence of prediction needs data that were held out from the fitting.

If you followed the EEG series, this is the cross-validation by participant of episodes fourteen point one and fourteen point six.

To sum up. The training box turns the knobs. The validation box chooses. The test box is opened once. And when trials share an owner, split by person, not by trial. Next time: overfitting and underfitting, what happens when a model has too many knobs.

PLATE 1.45:15

Overfitting and underfitting

A stiff straight line underfits, wrong on old and new data alike, while a floppy curve with as many knobs as dots overfits: perfect on training dots, wild on fresh ones. Fresh error follows a U across flexibility. More data, a penalty on the knobs and early stopping help, yet very large networks show a second descent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the greenhouse. In a drawing office there is a tool called a flexible curve: a bendy ruler that holds whatever shape you press it into. Today we use one to draw through data, and to see the two ways a model can go wrong. By the end, you will know how to tell a model that learned from a model that memorized, and why that question is more surprising than it looks.

Here are twelve dots. Each one is a stimulus intensity, and the response it produced. These numbers are illustrative: a smooth S-shaped trend, plus some measurement noise. We want a curve that will be right about the next dot, not only about these twelve.

First, the stiff ruler. A straight line is a polynomial of degree one, with no bend at all. It misses the middle, where the dots clearly curve. Its typical miss is zero point fifteen on the twelve dots we drew through, and zero point fifteen on fresh dots too. This is underfitting. The model is too simple for the pattern, so it is wrong everywhere, on old data and on new.

Now allow a few bends: degree three. The curve follows the S. Its typical miss is zero point zero five on the twelve dots, and zero point zero eight on fresh ones. A little worse on fresh dots, which is normal. And zero point zero eight is exactly the size of the noise we added, so no curve could do much better.

Finally, the floppy ruler. Eleven bends, which makes twelve knobs for twelve dots. It passes exactly through every dot, so on the training dots the typical miss is zero. This is the trap that closed episode one point one. But between the dots, and near the ends, the curve swings wildly. On fresh dots the typical miss is zero point three, nearly twice as bad as the straight line. This is overfitting. The model has learned the noise, and the noise does not come back.

Now compare all the rulers, from one bend to eleven. The training miss only goes down, because the knobs were turned to make it smaller. The fresh miss goes down, then up. The best ruler sits at the bottom of that U, not at the far right. Training error is a poor judge. This is why episode one point three gave us boxes of data the knobs never saw.

There are three classic remedies. The first is more data. Give the floppy ruler sixty dots instead of twelve, and it can no longer swing: it has to stay close to many dots at once. Its typical miss on fresh dots falls from zero point three to zero point zero nine.

The second is regularization: make bends expensive. We add a penalty on how large the knobs may grow. The ruler still has eleven bends, but it spends them only when the data insist. We choose the strength of the penalty on the validation box, and the typical miss on fresh dots is about zero point zero nine.

The third is early stopping. Knobs are turned step by step, a method called gradient descent that we meet in chapter two. The training miss falls at every step. The fresh miss falls for a while, then turns around and climbs. After fifteen steps, chosen on the validation box, it is at zero point zero eight five. Run to the very end, it reaches zero point three. So we stop at the bottom.

Now a surprise. The story so far says that too many knobs means memorizing. Yet modern neural networks have millions or billions of knobs, far more than they have examples, and they fit their training data exactly. In twenty seventeen, Zhang and colleagues showed that such networks can even fit images with random labels. By the textbook, they should be terrible. And yet they often do well on new data.

In twenty nineteen, Belkin and colleagues proposed a picture that holds both stories: the double descent curve. As a model grows, the fresh error follows the familiar U. It peaks when the model has just enough knobs to pass through every training point. Then, as the model keeps growing, the error comes down again.

Here is a toy version. Twenty training points, and models with one to two hundred knobs. Among all the settings that pass through the dots, we always pick the one with the smallest knobs. The typical fresh error is lowest, at zero point one eight, with six knobs. It explodes at exactly twenty knobs, as many knobs as points. Then it falls back to zero point one nine at two hundred knobs. This is the median of three hundred simulated datasets. Nakkiran and colleagues reported the same shape in deep networks, as a function of model size and of training time. Why it happens is still an active research question. The peak is still a danger zone, and the fresh data are still the judge.

To sum up. Too few knobs underfits: wrong on old data and new. Too many knobs for too little data overfits: perfect on old data, wrong on new. Only fresh data can tell them apart. More data, a penalty on the knobs, and early stopping are the classic remedies. And very large models can surprise us with a second descent.
