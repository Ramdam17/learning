Autism

Case-control comparisons assume people within a diagnosis look alike, but normative studies of cortical thickness in autism find strong deviations spread over the cortex with minimal overlap between individuals. Group differences are small, and only a subgroup of males is atypical in any region. Stability over time, links to clinical profiles, site confounds and who is in the reference remain to be confirmed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked what a norm could be for things that are not one number. A network. A whole distribution. Today we come back to one simple measure, the thickness of the cortex, and we put the machinery to work on a clinical question. Welcome to the gallery of portraits. One person in each frame, never an average portrait.

The first room is about autism.

The question comes first. Do autistic people share a brain signature?

For a long time, the standard way to ask was a case-control comparison. Take a group of autistic people. Take a group of non-autistic people. Compare the two averages, region by region. If the averages differ, call it a signature.

That design makes a quiet assumption. It assumes that the people inside each group look alike. Chapter one showed what happens when they do not. An average can describe nobody.

And autism is a diagnosis that covers a lot of ground. The people who carry it vary widely, in how they present and in their biology. So the normative question is different. Not, do the groups differ on average? But, where does each person sit among people of the same age, and in which places, if any, do they stand out?

The portraits on the wall are simulated. They are not anyone's real scan. They only show the idea, and each one is labelled simulated.

Now, what has been found. Let us start with what has been replicated.

In twenty nineteen, Zabihi and colleagues published a study in Biological Psychiatry, Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging. The data came from the Longitudinal European Autism Project. There were two hundred and six typically developing people, and three hundred and twenty-one autistic people, aged six to thirty-one.

First, the authors built a normative model of cortical thickness from the typically developing people alone, with Gaussian process regression. It is the model of episode four point five, with its uncertainty. Then they scored every autistic person against that norm.

The group comparison found few differences. That is the old result. On average, not much.

But the individual maps told another story. Deviations were strong, and they were spread all over the cortex, with minimal overlap from one person to the next. One person was thinner here. Another was thicker there. And the fifteen most extreme people in the whole sample were all autistic.

The deviations were linked to symptom severity. But once the authors corrected for multiple comparisons, only the link with repetitive behaviours held.

The second room holds another study, another cohort, and another model.

In twenty twenty, Bethlehem and colleagues published in Communications Biology. They pooled the ABIDE data sets, which come from many sites. They kept males only, five to forty years old, several hundred in each group. They split the cortex into three hundred and eight regions. And they used LOESS, a local regression, instead of a Gaussian process.

A person counted as atypical in a region when their score was more than two standard deviations from the typical norm. By chance alone, five percent of people should cross that line. Across regions, the median was about seven point six percent of autistic males. Higher than chance, but far from everyone.

The title says it plainly. Age-atypical cortical thickness in a subgroup of males. Those people were almost always between six and twenty years old. The authors found their pattern somewhat consistent with the earlier study, in where the deviations fell.

One more room, a wider one. In twenty twenty-three, Segal and colleagues studied six conditions together. Autism was one of them. They used gray matter volume, in one thousand two hundred and ninety-four people with a diagnosis and one thousand four hundred and sixty-five matched controls. Any single region was affected in fewer than seven percent of people who share a diagnosis. Yet deviations kept landing on the same circuits and networks, up to fifty-six percent of the time. We will come back to that in episode ten point six.

So here is the stamp, as of November 2026. Level of evidence, replicated in several cohorts, with different models and different measures. Strong individual differences, with few places in common between people. A uniform group effect is not what these studies find.

Now the second half of the stamp. What remains to be confirmed.

First, stability. Is the person who looks atypical today still atypical next year? Both of these studies scanned each person once. Cross-sectional data cannot draw a trajectory, and the authors of both say so. Level of evidence, observed in single studies. Not yet tested over time.

Second, development and clinical profiles. Do the atypical subgroups match a clinical profile, or a stage of development? The links to symptom scales were modest, and in one of the studies partly exploratory. This is, for now, a hypothesis.

Third, the confounds. In the second study, the site alone explained about fifteen percent of the variance in cortical thickness. In the fMRI series, we met scanner effects. Head motion matters too. Both papers list confounds they cannot rule out.

And fourth, who was in the reference. One of the studies excluded people with an IQ below seventy. A norm built from some people may not describe all of them.

So, to sum up. In autism, normative models find deviations mostly one person at a time, in places that vary, rather than a uniform shift of the whole group. That much has been seen in several cohorts. Whether the subgroups are stable, and what they mean, is still to be confirmed.

PLATE 10.27:10

Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder

Normative models place each person with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder on a reference by age and sex, one piece of brain at a time. Deviations are common but fall in different places from person to person, so the average patient is a noninformative construct. Schizophrenia shows more extreme deviations than controls, bipolar disorder barely does, and no structural map yet predicts course.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, in the autism room of the gallery, we met a pattern. Normative models found deviations in some people, and the places changed from one person to the next. Today we walk into the two neighbouring rooms. Schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder.

Here is the clinical question. For decades, these disorders were studied by comparing two averages. Patients on one side, healthy controls on the other. The result was a map of what is, on average, different. But a map of the average patient is only useful if most patients look like it. So the question is this. Does the average patient exist? Or does each person deviate in their own way?

A normative model can answer, because it asks the question of one person at a time. Where does this person sit, compared with people of the same age and sex?

Let us look at the first big answer. In 2018, Thomas Wolfers and colleagues published it in JAMA Psychiatry. The data came from Oslo, in Norway. There were two hundred eighteen patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, and two hundred fifty-six healthy controls. The scans were structural brain images. The authors built a normative model of grey matter and white matter from the controls, using age and sex. Then they placed every person on it, one small piece of brain at a time.

Each piece of brain got a z-score. Remember from episode two point two that a z-score is a distance, in standard deviations, from what is expected. The authors called a piece extreme when its z-score went beyond two point six, in either direction. That is a cut-off that about one piece in two hundred crosses by chance, in each direction. In the Information series, this is the idea of surprise. The rarer the event, the more it tells you.

Now picture what we will draw. Two simulated patients, with the same diagnosis. This is illustrative, not a measurement. For the first one, a region at the front of the brain lights up in blue. For the second one, a region at the back. Same label, two different maps. Keep these two sheets in mind.

Here is what Wolfers and colleagues found. Deviations were common in both disorders. But they were very different from one person to the next. Only a few places had extreme deviations in more than two percent of patients. Mostly in the frontal and temporal parts of the brain, and in the cerebellum. Group differences were there too, in the frontal and temporal regions and the cerebellum for schizophrenia. But they described few individuals well. The authors wrote that the average patient is a noninformative construct.

So, what is replicated?

First, strong individual heterogeneity. In 2021, Lv and colleagues, in Molecular Psychiatry, worked with three hundred twenty-two people with schizophrenia. They used another normative model, and other measures, cortical thickness and white matter tracts. Even there, the group looked clearly different from controls. Yet no more than fifteen to twenty percent of patients stepped outside the normal range in any single tract or region.

In 2023, Segal and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience, went wider. Twelve hundred ninety-four people, with one of six diagnoses, among them schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, against fourteen hundred sixty-five matched controls. The same region deviated in fewer than seven percent of people with the same diagnosis. Three studies, one message. People with the same label rarely deviate in the same place.

Second, more deviations than in controls, at least for schizophrenia. In the Oslo data, extreme negative deviations in grey matter touched nought point nine percent of the brain in schizophrenia. In healthy controls, nought point two three. In Lv and colleagues, seventy-nine percent of patients had at least one region below the fifth centile. That sounds like a lot, but it is also true of fifty-nine percent of healthy people. Having one low region means little. Having many does.

And in 2022, the brain charts of episode eight point one placed these disorders on the lifespan centiles. Among the diagnoses compared, schizophrenia ranked third on a summary distance from controls, behind Alzheimer's disease and mild cognitive impairment.

Now, careful with the bipolar room. In the Oslo study, extreme negative deviations in bipolar disorder were nought point two four percent. Almost exactly the controls' value. The group differences of bipolar disorder were found mainly in the cerebellum. The story is not the same for both disorders.

So, what remains to be confirmed?

The big hope was that an individual map would predict something. How a person will evolve. Or how they will respond to a treatment. The structural scan studies we checked did not test this. The Oslo study linked its deviations to cognitive performance and to how long the illness had lasted. It was a snapshot in time, and the authors say plainly that a snapshot cannot tell cause from consequence. A deviation map describes. It does not yet predict.

There is one early signal, from a different kind of scan. In 2025, in Molecular Psychiatry, Giacomel and colleagues used dopamine PET. Referenced to a normative model, the signal in the striatum predicted treatment response, with an area under the curve between zero point seven seven and zero point eight three. It is one study, and it is not a structural map.

The same goes for sub-groups. Segal and colleagues found that scattered deviations often fall inside shared brain circuits, in up to fifty-six percent of cases. It is a lovely idea, that different places could mean the same circuit. But it comes from one study. And the authors of the brain charts wrote, in 2022, that their charts are not meant for the diagnosis of an individual patient in clinical practice.

So here is the stamp, as of November 2026. Replicated in several cohorts: strong individual heterogeneity, and more deviations than in controls for schizophrenia. Observed in one study each: deviations that converge on shared circuits, and, with dopamine PET, a prediction of treatment response. Still a hypothesis: structural maps that predict the course of the illness or the response to treatment.

All the patients we draw in this series are simulated. The deviation maps of real patients are, for good reasons, not ours to show.

So, to sum up. In schizophrenia, the average patient hides more than it shows. Many people deviate, but each in their own place, and no structural map has yet been shown to predict what comes next.

PLATE 10.35:30

ADHD

ADHD shows a small group effect, with slightly less grey matter in the cerebellum, temporal regions and hippocampus. Individual maps are scattered, and few locations are extreme in more than two percent of patients. A weak average can hide two worlds, everyone slightly off or a few far off, and only counts of extreme locations against chance tell them apart.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last room, we met schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. Deviations were common, but they rarely landed in the same place from one person to the next. Today we walk into a livelier room. The portraits here do not sit still. This is attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD.

First, the clinical question. ADHD is diagnosed from behaviour: inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity. People who receive the diagnosis differ from one another. So the question is the one this series keeps asking. Is there a typical ADHD brain? Or is each person atypical in a different way?

Here is the study that put the question to the test. Wolfers and colleagues, in Psychological Medicine, published in 2020. They worked with adults. One hundred and forty-six healthy adults served as the reference. A Gaussian process regression learned how brain volume changes with age and sex. Then one hundred and fifty-three adults with persistent ADHD were scored against that norm.

Notice what the score is. Not one number for the whole brain. A z-score at every small location of grey matter and white matter, for every person. A location counts as extreme when its z-score goes beyond plus or minus two point six.

Now, what holds up. Start with the group. When you average the patients, you do find something. Grey matter is a little smaller than expected in the cerebellum, in temporal regions, and in the hippocampus. That matches the older case-control literature. So the group effect is real, and modest.

Then look at the individuals. Patients did have somewhat more extreme negative deviations than healthy adults. But the percentages were tiny in both groups. And the places were scattered. The authors report that only a few locations were extreme in more than two percent of the patients. Nine of the ten most deviating patients had extreme values in the cerebellum. Yet in different parts of it, with no overlap.

That is the same pattern as in the last room. Many people with a diagnosis, each with their own map, and a group average that describes almost none of them. The authors put it plainly: the average ADHD patient has limited informative value.

Next, a bridge to a bigger picture. In 2023, Segal and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience, applied normative models to people with one of six conditions. ADHD, autism, bipolar disorder, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia. Their finding echoes the one we just saw. The same region was affected in fewer than seven percent of people with the same diagnosis. Yet deviations often fell in shared circuits, in up to fifty-six percent of cases. Where a deviation lands varies. The circuit it belongs to varies less.

Now to the moment that matters. A group result that looks weak can hide two very different worlds.

In the first world, almost everyone is a little off. The group mean moves slightly, and the effect is small for everyone.

In the second world, most patients sit right on the norm, and a few are very atypical. The group mean moves slightly too. Same average, completely different people.

An average cannot tell these worlds apart. Counts can. This is where the tools of episodes six three and six four come in. Count the extreme locations for each person, and compare that count with chance. At a threshold of two point six, and with perfectly Gaussian scores, a typical brain still crosses the line at about nine locations in a thousand. And fix the threshold before you look at the patients. Then draw the overlap map. If many patients are extreme in one place, the map lights up. If each one is extreme somewhere else, the map stays nearly empty, even when every person has a story.

The two imagined worlds are illustrative. It shows how to reason, not what ADHD looks like.

Last, the level of evidence, as of November 2026. A strong individual heterogeneity, with little overlap between patients, was observed in one study of adults, from one cohort. A similar spread, with convergence on shared circuits, was reported across six disorders in one later study. Newer work in children, published in 2026, scored patients against norms and found three subgroups, of which only some differences held in a second cohort. So the pattern keeps coming back, but with different measures and ages, and the adult result itself has not been repeated. Treat it as consistent, not settled.

What remains to be confirmed. Whether these maps are stable when the same person is scanned again. Whether the adult maps hold in children, where the norm moves fast. And whether a map tells you anything about symptoms, or about what will help. In the 2020 study, extreme deviations were not linked to symptom scores, to stimulant medication, or to comorbidity.

So, to sum up. In ADHD, the group effect is small, and the individual maps are scattered. The average patient says little, so count extreme locations against chance, and read the overlap map.

PLATE 10.45:16

Mood and anxiety disorders

In depression the average cortical gap is small, a tenth of a standard deviation, and patient and control distributions overlap by ninety percent or more. A normative study finds about a third of patients with an extreme deviation somewhere, scattered across regions, yet about thirty percent of controls have one too. Deviations track earlier onset and severity, and symptom severity in population cohorts.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we stood in the ADHD room. The group result was weak, and a few people sat far from the norm. Today we walk into the room of moods. Depression, and anxiety.

Chapter ten always asks three things, in the same order. The clinical question. What has been replicated. And what still has to be confirmed. At the end, we stamp the evidence with a date.

First, the clinical question. Does the brain of a person with depression or anxiety sit outside the range of the reference population? If it does, where? And in how many people?

Start with the group answer, because every normative study begins from it. In 2017, the ENIGMA consortium pooled scans from twenty cohorts around the world. Two thousand one hundred and forty-eight people with major depression, adults and adolescents, and seven thousand nine hundred and fifty-seven controls. In adults, the cortex was a little thinner, in the orbitofrontal cortex, the cingulate, the insula and the temporal lobes. The effects were between zero point one and zero point one four standard deviations. That is small.

A second study looked at one German cohort of eighteen hundred and nine people, with many kinds of scan. The overlap between patients and controls was between eighty-seven and ninety-five percent. A classifier reached an accuracy of fifty-four to fifty-six percent. Close to a coin toss.

Here is what such a gap looks like. This is illustrative, on our simulated index. Two bells, one for controls, one for patients, shifted by one tenth of a standard deviation. You cannot tell them apart by eye. And for a single person, the shift is invisible.

In the Information series, how far apart two distributions are is measured by a divergence. Here, the two are almost the same.

So the group answer is nearly empty. A normative model asks a different question. Maybe the average hides people who sit far from the norm, in different places.

The study to know here is a preprint from the ENIGMA depression group, led by Bayer, with Marquand and Schmaal as senior authors. They built the norm on three thousand one hundred and eighty-one healthy controls, from thirty-four sites. Then they scored three thousand six hundred and forty-five people with depression, and two thousand one hundred and nineteen new controls.

Again, the bells overlapped, by at least ninety-two percent. But look at individuals. About thirty-four and a half percent of patients had an extreme deviation in at least one region. And no more than twelve percent of patients shared an extreme deviation in the same region. Different people, different places. This map is a simulated patient, not a real one.

Careful, though. Thirty percent of the controls also had an extreme deviation somewhere. Extreme deviations are not a signature of depression. A norm has tails, and controls live in them too.

There is a link, still. Extreme thin deviations went with an earlier onset, and with more severe symptoms. That is the kind of result a normative model can add.

Now anxiety. A paper published in Molecular Psychiatry in 2026 used a norm learned by an autoencoder, on structural scans from two population cohorts. The German National Cohort, about twenty-nine thousand people. And UK Biobank, about twenty-five thousand. They studied symptoms, not diagnoses. Deviations grew with the severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms. The pattern was shared between the two, and it held in both cohorts. It was different from the pattern for heavy alcohol use.

So, where does the evidence stand? Here is the stamp. As of November 2026.

Replicated, in several large samples. The average differences are small. The cortex is slightly thinner. The overlap with controls is large.

Observed in one study. Extreme deviations scattered across many regions, a different set in each person, and a link with earlier onset and severity. And remember, this one is a preprint, in the version we read.

A hypothesis. A shared deviation pattern for depression and anxiety symptoms, seen in two population cohorts. That fits the cross-diagnosis question of episode ten point six.

What remains to be confirmed? Whether patients carry more extreme deviations than controls, by a margin that matters. Whether a person's deviations stay put over time. Whether any of this predicts a course or a response to treatment. The authors of the preprint note that their data are cross-sectional, and that the clinical details were limited. They also write that cortical thickness may not serve as a diagnostic marker.

So, to sum up. In mood disorders, the average gap is small and the overlap is large. Normative models show where individuals differ, but they have not yet shown a use in the clinic.

Child development and prematurity

After preterm birth, newborn brain charts place each baby by postmenstrual age rather than birthday. Preterm babies show deviations, often several at once but in different places, and earlier birth goes with lower centile scores. Links to later cognitive outcome are mixed, and the charts cover only ages around term.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we walked through the mood room. Depression and anxiety are common, varied, and often found together, and their average deviations are small. Today the gallery gives us a different kind of room. Not a diagnosis, but a moment in life. Early childhood, and a birth that came too soon.

We are back at the pediatrician's office. And here, the growth chart is hardly a metaphor. It is almost the real thing.

First, the clinical question. The World Health Organization calls a baby preterm when it is born alive before thirty-seven completed weeks of pregnancy. Under twenty-eight weeks is extremely preterm. From twenty-eight to under thirty-two is very preterm. As of November 2026, the WHO estimate is about thirteen point four million preterm births in the year 2020, more than one birth in ten.

Preterm-born children are at increased risk of lifelong neurodevelopmental difficulties. Yet they are far from alike. So the question is the one of this whole series. Where does this one baby's brain sit, among babies of the same age? And what does that position tell us?

Three things make this room harder than the others. The norm moves fast, because newborn brain growth is rapid and uneven. The data are scarcer. And scans are harder, because a newborn cannot be asked to hold still, and the consent comes from the parents. In the fMRI series, we saw how head motion corrupts a scan. In the EEG series, we saw how special populations raise the rejection rate. Both are at work here.

Now, what is replicated. Dimitrova and colleagues built a growth chart of the newborn brain. In 2020, they used diffusion imaging from two hundred and seventy babies born at term, and Gaussian process regression, to learn what typical looks like at each age. Then they placed eighty-two preterm babies against that chart. Each baby was scanned at term-equivalent age, around the time of a full-term birth.

Notice the age axis of this chart. It counts from the start of the pregnancy, not from the birthday. This is called postmenstrual age. A baby born at thirty weeks and scanned ten weeks later sits at the same place on the axis as a baby born at forty weeks. That is what lets a preterm baby be compared with babies born at term.

The result looks like chapter six. Preterm babies did show deviations. But the places differed from one baby to the next. In the most consistent region, around the ventricles, extreme values appeared in only thirteen of the eighty-two babies. A group average would have blurred all of this. The brain maps you see in this episode are simulated babies. The numbers I quote come from the papers.

In 2021, the same group repeated the idea on brain volumes. Two hundred and seventy-four term-born babies made the chart, and eighty-nine preterm babies were placed on it. Then came the real test. A second, independent set of two hundred and fifty-three preterm babies, scanned on a different scanner with different settings. Again, abnormalities were widespread, often several at once, and very different from baby to baby. They were linked to breathing support, nutrition, and birth weight.

The brain charts of 2022 add the wide view. Bethlehem and colleagues pooled one hundred twenty-three thousand, nine hundred and eighty-four scans, from one hundred and one thousand, four hundred and fifty-seven people. Ages run from one hundred fifteen days after conception to one hundred years. Where birth data were recorded, a later gestational age at birth went with higher centile scores.

A 2025 study in Nature Communications carried the idea further, to children and adults born preterm. Almost all of the preterm adults had at least one extreme region, yet no more than twenty-seven percent shared the same region. So the picture of many different brains held across ages.

So here is the stamp, as of November 2026. Replicated in more than one cohort: strong individual differences in the brains of babies born preterm, and a link between earlier birth and lower centile scores.

Now, what remains to be confirmed. The first thing is the future. Do these deviations predict how a child will do? In 2020, the whole-brain atypicality index was higher with more extreme prematurity, and it predicted poorer cognitive and language scores at eighteen months. But in the 2021 study of the cortex, regional measures were linked to age at birth, and not to development at eighteen months. The 2025 study agrees: in newborns, no link to the eighteen-month score; in children of about ten, surface area was linked to cognition and cortical thickness was not. So the link to later outcome is observed in studies, and mixed. That is the second stamp.

The second thing is the edge of the chart. That cortex chart covers babies scanned around term, between about thirty-seven and forty-four weeks. It says nothing about a baby scanned much earlier, or about a child of five. Later in childhood, big cohorts take over. As of November 2026, the ABCD study site says it invited eleven thousand, eight hundred and eighty children, aged nine and ten, across twenty-one sites. Between birth and nine, the gap is real. Chapter eleven shows what extrapolation does there. And to follow one child across those years, we need repeated scans, as we saw with trajectories.

The third thing is who is in the reference. The authors of the brain charts say their data lean toward European and North American populations, and that fetal and newborn ages are under-represented. They also note that more than one hundred scans give stable centiles for a new study. But those results do not immediately support the use of the charts on an individual clinical scan. Using a chart to guide care for one baby is, for now, still to be shown. That is the third stamp.

So, to sum up. After preterm birth, the chart shows many different brains, not one shifted brain, and the chart is only as good as the babies who built it.

PLATE 10.66:29

Across diagnoses

Diagnostic labels may not cut the brain at its joints. A meta-analysis found grey matter loss converging on the anterior cingulate and insula across six diagnoses, and a normative study found deviations landing in common circuits even when regions differ. Symptom dimensions can also be scored against a norm without any diagnosis.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last room, we followed children. Their norm moves fast, and every centile is a moving target. Today we walk to the middle of the gallery, and we take the labels off the doors.

Until now, each room had a diagnosis written above it. Autism. Schizophrenia. ADHD. But those names come from symptoms, not from the brain. So here is the clinical question. Do the labels cut the brain at its joints? Or do different disorders share something underneath?

A hint came long before normative models. In 2014, Caspi and colleagues followed a birth cohort, the Dunedin study, from adolescence to midlife, about twenty years. They found that one general factor described the data even better than separate families of disorders. They called it the p factor, by analogy with general intelligence. People with a higher score had more impairment in life, and a stronger family history.

That is a statement about symptoms. What does the brain say?

Let us start with the answer that came before normative models. In 2015, Goodkind and colleagues pooled one hundred and ninety-three studies of grey matter. Fifteen thousand, eight hundred and ninety-two people, across six diagnostic groups. Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, addiction, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and anxiety. Grey matter loss converged on three places. The dorsal anterior cingulate, and the insula on each side. Few effects were specific to one diagnosis. In healthy people, those regions form a tightly connected network at rest.

That is a group result, from case-control comparisons. It says that on average, something is shared. It cannot say whether each person carries it. For that, we need a norm.

Here is the study we met in the first chapter, and again in the ADHD room. Segal and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience, in 2023. They took one thousand, two hundred and ninety-four people with one of six conditions. ADHD, autism, bipolar disorder, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia. They compared them with one thousand, four hundred and sixty-five matched controls. The measure was grey matter volume in each region. And each person was scored against a normative model.

Now look at the overlap map. Patients have deviations. But the same region was affected in fewer than seven percent of the people with the same diagnosis. As maps, the patients barely overlap.

Then the authors changed the question. They asked where each deviation sits in the brain's wiring. They placed each one inside a functional circuit, or a network. Now the picture changes. Deviations fell within common circuits and networks in up to fifty-six percent of cases. Same diagnosis, different region, same circuit.

And one system was implicated across the disorders. The salience and ventral attention system. Other networks were tied to particular disorders.

Notice how the story fits together. The older meta-analysis and the normative study did not use the same method. One compared averages, the other scored individuals. Yet both point to a shared network, not to one diagnosis. Networks defined at rest are the kind we met in the fMRI series.

To picture it, here are three simulated patients. They are illustrative, not data from any study. Each has a different map. Their deviations land in different regions. Yet every one of those regions belongs to the same circuit. Redraw the picture at the circuit level, and the three portraits become one family.

Why is this a normative question? Because a case-control study starts from the label. It builds one group per diagnosis, and compares each with the controls. A norm starts from the person. Everyone is placed on the same reference, whatever box they were given. Then you can ask what the deviations share, after the fact. We will meet the risks of this later, in chapter eleven, when we look at how controls are chosen.

Now the most radical step. Do we need diagnoses at all? A normative model does not require them. It only needs a reference. So you can put symptoms on a dimension, and ask where the deviations go.

In 2021, Parkes and colleagues did exactly this, in the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort, a study of young people. They built normative models of cortical volume. They reduced one hundred and seventeen symptom measures to six dimensions. Overall psychopathology, anxious misery, externalizing, fear, and two kinds of psychosis symptoms. Then they asked which predicts symptoms better, raw volume, or deviation from the norm. Deviations predicted symptoms better, on data the model had never seen.

The dimension of overall psychopathology tied most strongly to deviations in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, the inferior temporal cortex, and the dorsal anterior cingulate. And a detail that matters. Depression and ADHD showed overlapping deviation effects. That overlap shrank when overall psychopathology was taken into account.

Now, the level of evidence, as of November 2026. Here is what is replicated. A shared network involvement across diagnoses was found by different methods, in different samples. Deviations are individual in their location, and shared in their circuit. That is solid enough to say aloud.

What remains to confirm. Segal and colleagues measured grey matter volume only, in adults. Whether other measures show the same circuits is open. Whether a shared circuit is a cause, a consequence, or an effect of treatment, we do not know. Motion in the scanner and medication could also leave their mark, and none of that is settled here. And we do not know yet if a person's circuit map tells you anything about what will help. This stamp covers the studies we looked at today. Newer ones may already have moved it.

So, to sum up. Take the labels off, and the deviations still do not land on the same region. They land on the same circuits.

Dementia · the contrast

In Alzheimer's disease, normative models of cortical thickness count outlier regions below the 1.96 z-score line for each person. Counts rise with disease stage, yet the patients differ widely, from one to 120 outlier regions in an amyloid-confirmed sample. The count grows over repeated scans, and a faster rise in mild impairment goes with higher progression risk, though so far in one research cohort.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we took the labels off the doors of the gallery, and looked across diagnoses. Today we walk to the very end of the corridor, to a room set apart. Dementia. It is here for a contrast.

Here is the clinical question. In Alzheimer's disease, can a normative model tell us where one person's brain departs from the norm, and does that departure say something about what comes next?

The method is the one you already know. A reference population. A model of cortical thickness by age and sex. A z-score for each region of each person.

Let us look at one well-known study. It is by Serena Verdi, James Cole and colleagues, published in the journal Neurology in 2023.

The reference was a hierarchical Bayesian regression, fitted on healthy people. Thirty-three thousand and seventy-two of them, from nine data sets. The hierarchy absorbs the differences between scanner sites, the way we saw in chapter five.

The patients came from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, called A D N I. Sixty-two sites. Cortical thickness in one hundred and forty-eight regions, for each person.

The authors called a region an outlier when its z-score fell below minus 1.96. That is the bottom two and a half percent of the reference. Then they simply counted the outlier regions for each person. One number per person, the total outlier count. Easy to read, and easy to compare between people.

Notice one choice. They counted only thinning. They looked at the low side because thinning is the signature of neurodegeneration. So when you hear that these deviations go one way, remember that the analysis was built to look that way.

In the Information series, a rare event is a surprising one. Counting outliers is counting surprises.

Now, what did they find? Two hundred and six patients with Alzheimer's disease had a median of twelve outlier regions out of one hundred and forty-eight. People with mild cognitive impairment, and healthy older controls, had fewer.

Group differences were clear. A group average, as in the case-control studies of chapter one, would stop right there. But a median is a summary of many different people.

Yet the median hides the point. The patients were also more different from each other than the other groups were. And the region with the most outliers, the parahippocampal gyrus, had them in only forty-seven percent of the patients. Even in dementia, no two maps are the same.

The maps you see on screen are simulated patients. They are drawn only to show this idea. They are not anyone's scan.

So here is the contrast with the psychiatric rooms. The diagnosis here rests on a biological marker. A second study looked at eighty-six patients from a memory clinic, every one with confirmed amyloid. Same model, same reference. The count of outlier regions went from one to one hundred and twenty out of one hundred and forty-eight. The median was twenty-one and a half.

That is a very wide range, within a single, biologically confirmed disease. Some patients looked almost like the reference. Others had most of the cortex far below it. A group average would have blurred all of them into one grey brain. The same group reported that people with dementia had more outlier regions than people with mild impairment. And that patients with a non-amnestic presentation had more than those with the usual memory-first picture. This is the study by Flavia Loreto and colleagues, in Alzheimer's and Dementia, Diagnosis, Assessment and Disease Monitoring, in 2024.

So heterogeneity exists here too. But it sits on a clearer background. The diagnosis rests on a marker, and the analysis follows one direction, thinning.

Then comes time. In 2024, Verdi and colleagues followed people over several visits. Three thousand, two hundred and thirty-three scans, from one thousand, one hundred and eighty-one people in A D N I. The number of outlier regions grew with time in Alzheimer's disease. It also grew in people with mild impairment who later developed the disease.

This is what a normative model brings to a degenerative disease. It gives every person their own baseline, the reference for their age and sex, and then lets us watch them move away from it. The question changes. It is no longer, are patients different from controls? It becomes, how far has this person drifted, and how fast?

And the pace mattered. In people with mild impairment, a faster rise in the count over the first year went with a higher risk of progressing to Alzheimer's disease.

The 2023 study already pointed that way. Each extra outlier region went with about three percent higher hazard of converting within three years.

Now the stamp, as of November 2026.

What is replicated. A large spread between patients, in two different cohorts, with more outlier regions as the disease advances. That is replicated in more than one study, though the studies share authors and a method.

What is observed in a single study. The link between a rising count and progression comes from one research cohort, A D N I. It has not yet been confirmed in independent cohorts.

What remains to be confirmed. Whether one person's map can guide care. Whether it can follow someone over time in the clinic, or select people for a trial. The authors call this a potential. It is not a result yet.

So, to sum up. In dementia, normative models show the same strong individual differences as in psychiatry, but against a clearer background, and with a count that rises as the disease advances.
