A map where far means easy to tell apart

Two bell curves are far apart when data can tell them apart. A straight ruler gives one for both pairs, yet guessing the source is right 69.1 percent of the time for the first pair and 52.0 for the second, needing seven observations against 657. The Fisher-Rao distance gives 0.980 and 0.100, and its map is the hyperbolic plane.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the optician's, the test is always the same. You sit in front of a chart, the optician flips two lenses, and asks: one, or two?

Your answer says how far apart the two lenses are, for you. Not in centimetres. In how easy they are to tell apart.

Today we measure the distance between two bell curves in exactly that way. Two bells are far apart when data can tell them apart. They are close when no amount of squinting helps.

Here is a map where every point is a bell curve. Go right, and the mean moves. Go up, and the standard deviation grows, so the bell gets wider.

Let us measure on this map the way we always did, with a straight ruler, the ruler of chapter two. The bell with mean zero and standard deviation one, and the bell with mean one and standard deviation one, are one unit apart.

Now two bells with standard deviation ten, one centred at zero and one centred at one. They are one unit apart too. The ruler says: same distance.

But look at the curves themselves. The first two are clearly different. The second two are almost the same curve.

So let us play the optician's game. I pick one of the two bells at random, and I draw one number from it. You only see the number. You guess which bell it came from.

With the first pair, you are right sixty-nine point one percent of the time. With the second pair, fifty-two point zero percent. Barely better than a coin.

Now allow more observations, so you can average them before you guess. How many do you need to reach ninety percent? Seven for the first pair. Six hundred fifty-seven for the second.

Same ruler distance. A hundred times more data. Widening the bells by ten asks for a hundred times more observations, because the need grows with the square of the width. The straight ruler is not counting what we care about.

This map needs a ruler that counts in steps you can tell apart. It exists. It is called the Fisher–Rao distance.

Harold Hotelling proposed it in nineteen twenty-nine, in a talk that was read out for him at a mathematics meeting. Only the abstract was ever printed, but the summary that was read out survives. It already pictures the normal curves as a surface of negative curvature. Rao published it in nineteen forty-five, in a paper that does not mention Hotelling.

The rule is local. A tiny step changes the mean by d mu, and the standard deviation by d sigma. Its squared length is d mu squared, plus two d sigma squared, all divided by sigma squared. This is per observation.

See the division by sigma squared. The higher you stand on the map, the cheaper a step becomes, because wide bells overlap a lot. One unit of mean is a small part of a wide bell.

These ellipses show it. Each one is the same distance from its centre, one half unit, per observation. Low on the map, they are small. High on the map, they are huge.

For our two pairs, the rule gives zero point nine eight zero, and zero point one zero zero. With a standard deviation of zero point one, the same shift of one costs five point five nine.

Here is a surprise. Shifting a bell by one standard deviation costs zero point nine eight zero. Doubling its standard deviation costs exactly the same.

And doubling costs the same wherever you start. From one to two, or from ten to twenty. Going up the map, the rule measures in ratios.

That is the idea of episode eight point two. For centred bells, its distance in ratios is this one times the square root of two. One point three eight six, against zero point nine eight zero.

Now look at the shape of the whole map. Where does it bend, and why? It is the space of episode nine point three, the hyperbolic plane. Nine point three drew it as a disc. Here is another map of the same space, a half plane, with the mean and the standard deviation as coordinates.

With this convention, its curvature is constant and negative, minus one half. The most familiar curve of statistics was hiding that geometry all along.

So shortest paths curve. Take the bell with mean zero, and the bell with mean ten, both with standard deviation one. Keep the standard deviation at one, and the trip measures ten.

The shortest path does something smarter. It widens the bell, because a wide bell is cheap to shift. It shifts it. Then it tightens it again. In the middle, the standard deviation reaches three point six seven. The trip measures five point five nine.

Move the second mean to a hundred, and the shortest path climbs to thirty-five point four. It measures twelve point zero five. Not a hundred.

Now the card of this distance. The object: a bell curve, a point on this map. The distance: the length of the shortest path, in steps you can tell apart, per observation.

What it ignores: the names we give the parameters. The next episode shows why.

What it costs: a formula, for bells in one dimension. For bells in several dimensions with different means, no general formula is known. A paper from twenty twenty still calls it an open problem.

And its trap: it is measured inside a family. Take again the bells with mean zero and mean ten. Inside the family of bells with standard deviation one, they are ten apart. Inside the family of all bells, five point five nine. Another family, another number. A bigger family, say all distributions at once, would give a third number. The distance belongs to the family, not only to the two bells.

Now the four rules. Never negative. Zero only for the same bell. The same in both directions. No shortcut. All four hold. It is the length of a shortest path on a curved surface, so it is a true distance.

To remember. On this map, far means easy to tell apart. The straight ruler said one for both pairs. The Fisher–Rao ruler says zero point nine eight and zero point one, and the data agree: seven observations against six hundred fifty-seven.

And the map is the hyperbolic plane of episode nine point three, hiding inside the most familiar curve of statistics.

The local ruler

Five divergences agreed on a fair coin against one giving fifty-one percent heads because up close they all read the Fisher information, which for a coin is one over p times one minus p. For larger steps they disagree. The Fisher-Rao distance measures a long way step by step: from always tails to always heads it is 3.14, finite where Kullback-Leibler is infinite.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the optician's. Lenses click into place, one after another, and the question never changes: can you tell the difference?

Episode five point five left a puzzle on the table.

Two coins. One is fair. The other gives heads fifty-one percent of the time. Half the chi-square, and four times the squared Hellinger distance, both gave zero point zero zero zero two zero, in natural units. Kullback-Leibler, and four times Jensen-Shannon, both gave zero point zero zero zero two nine bits. The same number, once you divide by the natural logarithm of two.

Five recipes, one answer. Why?

Today's answer is a single quantity: the Fisher information.

A coin has one dial, the chance of heads, which we call p. The Fisher information says how sharply data can pin that dial down. For a coin it is one divided by p times one minus p.

For the fair coin, it is four. For a coin that gives heads ninety percent of the time, eleven point one. At ninety-eight percent, fifty-one. The idea goes back to Fisher, in the early nineteen twenties. The Information series, in its episode twelve point two, shows where it comes from.

Here is the link. For a small step, every divergence of the family from episode five point five is worth half the Fisher information, times the square of the step. Up to a constant that depends on the units. This is a result you can read in Nielsen's introduction to information geometry, from twenty twenty.

Check it on the fair coin. The step is one point, zero point zero one. Half of four, times zero point zero one squared, is zero point zero zero zero two zero zero. Divide by the natural logarithm of two, and you get zero point zero zero zero two eight nine bits. Exactly the number from episode five point five.

So the divergences agree up close because they all read the same local ruler.

But this ruler is not the same everywhere. Take a step of one point, and measure it with the Fisher information of the place where you stand. Around fifty percent, the step has length zero point zero two zero zero. Around ninety percent, zero point zero three four one. Around ninety-eight percent, zero point zero eight three five. This length has no unit. It simply counts how far the step goes on the local ruler.

The same numbers set the precision of an estimate. Flip a coin a hundred times, and count the proportion of heads. Around fifty percent, that proportion wobbles by about zero point zero five. Around ninety-eight percent, by zero point zero one four. A simulation of twenty thousand runs agrees. Where the data pin the dial down well, a step of one point is long. Where they pin it down badly, the same step is short.

Now lay the whole line of coins flat, as a strip of a hundred tiles, one tile per point. Each tile is as long as its step. In the middle, zero point zero two. At the very ends, zero point two zero zero three, ten times more. Add the tiles up, and the line from always tails to always heads has length three point one four.

Now take a bigger step. From ninety-eight percent to ninety-nine.

Here the step is no longer small next to the scale on which the ruler changes. The divergences pull apart. Kullback-Leibler gives zero point zero zero five six bits. Half the chi-square, on the same scale, zero point zero zero seven three. Four times Jensen-Shannon, zero point zero zero five zero. The local ruler read at ninety-eight percent predicts zero point zero zero three seven. None of them is wrong. They stop agreeing when the ruler stops being constant.

So how do you measure a long way? Step by step, with the ruler of the place you are in, along the shortest path. That is the Fisher and Rao distance.

For histograms, it has a formula, already in Rao's paper of nineteen forty-five. Twice the angle from episode five point two. Our two histograms with three bins: the coefficient is zero point nine three two, the angle twenty-one point two degrees, the distance zero point seven three nine.

Between a fair coin and a coin at ninety percent: zero point nine two seven. Between a coin that always gives tails and a coin that always gives heads: three point one four. A finite distance, where the Kullback-Leibler divergence is infinite.

Now the distance card. The object: a family of distributions, with its parameters. The distance: the length of the shortest path, step by step, with the local ruler.

What does it ignore? The names we give to the parameters. Take a bell with a fixed centre. Double its standard deviation, from one to two, then from two to four. Each doubling costs zero point nine eight zero, with the convention of episode eleven point one, per observation. Now describe the same bells by their variance: one to four, then four to sixteen. Zero point nine eight zero again. On a straight ruler of the parameters, the steps would be one and two for the standard deviation, three and twelve for the variance. The Fisher and Rao distance does not care.

It also ignores something else. Merging bins can only make it shrink. On our histograms, merge the last two bins: zero point seven three nine becomes zero point six four four. In ten thousand random merges of six-bin histograms, simulated, it never once went up.

And when the merge loses nothing, it does not move at all. Cut one bin in two, in the same random proportion on both histograms, and the distance stays at zero point seven three nine.

Chentsov showed that this local ruler is the only one, up to a constant factor, with this property, for histograms with a finite number of bins. A proof published in twenty fifteen extends it much further.

That is why all the divergences agree up close. There is only one local ruler they could read.

What does it cost? For a model with many parameters, the Fisher information is a whole table of numbers, and it has to be estimated.

The trap: it depends on the noise model you assumed. Change the model, and you change the ruler.

To remember: a family of distributions has a local ruler, the Fisher information. It stretches from place to place. Up close, every divergence reads it. Far away, the distance is the length of the path.

One bowl, one divergence

Any smooth bowl defines a divergence: the gap between the bowl and a plank laid at the second point. The square gives the old ruler, entropy gives Kullback-Leibler, and minus the logarithm gives Itakura-Saito. Each keeps a mean that makes k-means work, a Pythagorean theorem and a noise model, so least squares, which assumes bell-shaped noise of the same size everywhere, is a choice.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the optician's shop. Chapter eleven asked when two distributions are far apart. Today we ask where a divergence comes from. There is one recipe, and it needs a bowl.

Take any bowl: a smooth curve that bends upward everywhere. Pick two points on it. At the second point, lay a straight plank that touches the bowl and never crosses it. Now look at the first point. There, the bowl rises above the plank. The height of that gap is the divergence from the first point to the second. The Soviet mathematician Bregman proposed it in nineteen sixty-seven.

Start with the simplest bowl, the square. Go from zero to two. The gap to the plank is four, which is exactly the square of the straight distance. The old ruler is the first member of the family.

Now bend the bowl another way. Take a coin, and plot, for each chance of tails, the entropy turned upside down, counted in bits. It is a bowl too. The fair coin sits at one half. The rigged coin of episode five point three gives tails nine times out of ten.

Lay the plank at the rigged coin, and read the gap at the fair coin. It is zero point seven four bits. That is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the fair coin to the rigged coin. Now lay the plank at the fair coin, where it is flat, and read the gap at the rigged coin. Zero point five three bits. Same bowl, same two coins, two directions, two numbers. They are the numbers of episode five point three.

A third bowl: minus the logarithm. Its gap is the Itakura-Saito divergence, the one that compared spectra in episode four point five. Between powers of one and two, it is zero point one nine, in natural units. Between powers of one hundred and two hundred, it is zero point one nine again. The picture is the same bowl, zoomed. Doubling costs the same wherever you start. This is a new pair, not the pair of episode four point five.

A fourth bowl is for matrices: minus the logarithm of the determinant. Its gap is twice the divergence of episode eight point five, counted in natural units. A trace and a determinant, and nothing else.

Four bowls, four divergences. None is symmetric, and none obeys the shortcut rule. Take Itakura-Saito. Go from one to ten, then from ten to a hundred. One point four, then one point four: two point eight in all. Straight from one to a hundred costs three point six. The detour is cheaper. Never negative, and zero only for the same point: those two rules hold for every bowl. The other two fall.

Yet every bowl keeps three things. The first is the mean.

Take three numbers: one, two and nine. Look for the centre that makes the sum of the divergences as small as possible. If the centre is the second point, the one where the plank is laid, the answer is the ordinary mean. Four. For the square, four. For the entropy, four. For minus the logarithm, four. Whatever the bowl. Banerjee and colleagues proved it in two thousand five. And under mild conditions, these are the only divergences with that property, by Banerjee, Guo and Wang, the same year. This is why the k-means algorithm runs unchanged with any of them.

Now put the centre on the other side, as the first point. The bowl matters again. The square still gives four. The entropy gives the geometric mean: two point six two. Minus the logarithm gives the harmonic mean: one point eight six. That is Nielsen and Nock, in two thousand nine. For distributions, the geometric mean must be rescaled to a total of one.

The second thing kept is a theorem of Pythagoras. Take a table with two rows and two columns: point four, point one, point one, point four. Choose another table with no dependence at all: rows of point eight and point two, columns of point seven and point three. The divergence from our table to that one is zero point seven three bits. It splits into two exact pieces. Zero point two eight bits is the divergence from our table to its own version with no dependence. That is the mutual information. And zero point four five bits is the rest of the way. The right angle sits at the version with no dependence. Csiszár proved it in nineteen seventy-five.

It holds for every table, and every table without dependence. So the mutual information is the gap from a table to the whole set of tables without dependence. It is a divergence from a set, not a distance between two points.

The third thing kept is a link with noise. Each bowl goes with a family of noise. The square goes with bell-shaped noise, the same size at every level. The entropy goes with counts, where the spread grows like the square root of the level. And minus the logarithm goes with noise that multiplies, like the noise in a power spectrum, a link that Févotte and colleagues made in two thousand nine.

Here are the three, simulated with seed one hundred thirteen. Four levels, fourteen draws at each level. On the left, the spread stays two. In the middle, it grows slowly. On the right, it grows in proportion to the level.

So here is the moment to say it plainly. Choosing a divergence is choosing a model of noise. Least squares is one bowl among others: the one that assumes bell-shaped noise of the same size everywhere. If your errors grow with the level, the square is not neutral. It is a choice.

One last place on the map. Episode five point five built a family from a ratio, a die and an average. Today we built another from a bowl, a plank and a gap. Between distributions, Kullback and Leibler is the only divergence that belongs to both. That is Jiao and colleagues, in two thousand fourteen, and Amari cites it too.

Now the card. The object: two points of a bowl, numbers, coins, spectra or matrices. The distance: the gap between the bowl and its plank. What it ignores depends on the bowl: minus the logarithm ignores a common scale. What it costs: one pass, the bowl and its slope at each pair. The trap comes twice. The direction, because the mean changes with the side of the centre. And the bowl, chosen by habit.

One bowl, one divergence. It keeps a mean, a Pythagorean triangle, and a model of noise. And it hides a choice you were already making.

Four straight lines

Halfway between two bells has four answers, one per straight line. For a narrow bell centred at minus two and a wide one at plus two, mixing heights gives two bumps, averaging logarithms gives a centre at minus 1.6, transport gives centre zero with width one, and the shortest path, length 3.72, gives minus one. Interpolating means nothing until you say on which line.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, every bowl gave us a divergence. Today we ask a simpler question. What lies halfway between two distributions?

Take the two bells of episode six point six. One is centred at minus two, and it is narrow: a standard deviation of one half. The other is centred at plus two, and it is wide: a standard deviation of one and a half.

Halfway sounds easy. It is not. We will find four answers, one for each way of walking in a straight line. And no two of them end up in the same place.

Answer one. Average the two curves, height by height. This is the mixture of episode six point six.

On the way, one bell fades out while the other fades in. At the halfway point there are two bumps. The mean is zero, and the standard deviation is two point two nine.

Look at the middle. The curve there has a height of zero point zero five five. The peaks stand at zero point four. The middle is about seven times lower. Almost nothing lives where we said halfway. This is not a bell any more.

Answer two. Take the logarithm of each curve. The logarithm of a bell is a parabola. Average the two parabolas, and you get a parabola again. Take the exponential, and scale it so the total is one. Before scaling, the total is only zero point one six.

The result is a bell. It is centred at minus one point six, with a standard deviation of zero point six seven. It is pulled toward the narrow bell. The reason is simple. This average weighs each bell by its precision, the inverse of its variance. The narrow bell has nine times the precision of the wide one.

These first two are the two straight lines of information geometry, in the sense of Amari and Nagaoka, in two thousand. One is straight when you add curves. The other is straight when you add logarithms. Each one is straight for its own rule.

They come as a pair, and the Kullback and Leibler divergence ties them together. Episode eleven point three gave a Pythagorean theorem. Its triangle has one side of each kind. Here it is, in bits.

Start from the two-bump mixture. Find the nearest bell, in the direction that keeps the average and the spread. It is centred at zero, with a width of two point two nine. From the mixture to that bell: about half a bit. From that bell to the bell of answer two, along an exponential line: about ten bits. And from the mixture straight to the bell of answer two: about ten and a half bits. The sides add up.

But which bell is nearest to the mixture? It depends on the direction, as in episode five point three. If the mixture comes first, the best bell is the one we just built. It covers everything, and its peak sits where there is little data. Fitting a model by maximum likelihood is this projection.

If the bell comes first, the best bell leans on one side. Here it is centred at zero point nine eight, with a width of two point zero two. It lies mostly over the wide bump, and it hardly covers the narrow one. There is a second, worse answer, sitting on the narrow bump, centred at minus one point nine four. Which one an optimizer finds depends on where it starts. The approximate inference methods called variational work in this direction.

Answer three. The transport line of episode six point six. The bell itself travels. Its centre moves in equal steps, and so does its width. A quarter of the way, it is centred at minus one, with a width of zero point seven five. At the halfway point, it is centred at zero, with a width of exactly one. McCann described this path in nineteen ninety-seven. Mass moves along straight lines.

Answer four. The shortest path of episode eleven point one. Here is the map of bells again. The mean runs along the bottom, and the standard deviation runs upward. The rule for one bell is: the square of a step is the square of the change in mean, plus two times the square of the change in width, all divided by the square of the width. That is per observation.

It is the hyperbolic plane of episode nine point three, drawn as another map. On this map, the shortest path between our two bells is an arc of a circle. Its length is three point seven two. It climbs to a width of one point eight four, higher than either bell, and comes back down. We widen, we shift, and we tighten. Halfway along, the bell is centred at minus one, with a width of one and a half.

Now put all four on the map. The transport line is a straight segment. The exponential line bends. The shortest path arcs above them. And the mixture line is not on the map at all. Its midpoint is not a bell.

Measure each path with the same ruler, the rule of episode eleven point one. The shortest path is three point seven two. The exponential line is four point three two. The transport line is four point six six. The mixture line cannot be measured, because it leaves the map.

Four lines, four midpoints. Minus one point six, zero, minus one, and no bell at all. They do not pass through the same middle.

You meet the first two every day. A forecast can be the average of two models in probabilities, or in log probabilities. Forecasters call these the linear pool and the logarithmic pool. Spectra can be averaged in power, or in decibels. Take powers one and one hundred. In power, the average is fifty point five. In decibels, they are zero and twenty, and the average is ten decibels, which is a power of ten. That is the geometric mean of episode eight point three.

What does each line cost? The mixture costs nothing, but it leaves the family of bells. The exponential line stays in the family, and costs a normalisation. The transport line costs a sort on a line, and an optimization elsewhere. The shortest path costs a formula, and for bells in several dimensions with different means, no general formula is known.

And the trap. Interpolating between two distributions means nothing until you say on which line.

To sum up. Episode six point six said that every distance has its own average. Here, every geometry has its own straight line. Between two distributions, there are at least four, and they do not pass through the same middle.

Steepest according to whom?

Ordinary gradient descent depends on how a bell is named. Fitting a bell to a target centred at three took 100 steps with the standard deviation, 81 with its logarithm and 1,031 with the variance. Measuring steps with the Fisher ruler, the natural gradient, gives the same first direction whatever the names, but only for small steps.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the optician's shop. We have a ruler for bells now. Today we put it to work, on a very ordinary job: fitting a bell by going downhill.

Here is the job. We start with a bell centered at zero, with a standard deviation of one. We want to reach a target bell, centered at three, with a standard deviation of zero point two. The loss is the divergence from the target to our bell. At the start it is eight point one two bits. We take steps downhill until it falls below zero point zero zero zero one four four bits.

Downhill. Which way is that? The usual answer is the gradient, the direction of steepest descent. But steepest needs a measure. Among all the steps of one fixed size, which one lowers the loss the most? That depends on how you measure the size of a step.

Picture every step of size one around a point. Measured in a straight line, they form a circle. Measured with another ruler, they form an ellipse, longer in the directions that count less. The steepest step is the one that reaches the lowest level of the loss. A circle and an ellipse touch different levels at different places.

Ordinary gradient descent measures it in a straight line, on the numbers you wrote down. So the answer depends on which numbers you wrote down. Let us try three descriptions of the very same bell.

First, the mean and the standard deviation. Second, the mean and the logarithm of the standard deviation. Third, the mean and the variance. Each is a perfectly good way to name a bell.

For each description, we give the ordinary method its best chance. We try a grid of step sizes, and keep the one that finishes fastest. With the standard deviation: one hundred steps. With its logarithm: eighty-one steps. With the variance: one thousand and thirty-one steps.

Same bell, same target, same stopping rule. Ten times more work, because of a name.

Look at the very first step. At the start the standard deviation is one, so the first two descriptions happen to agree. They point up and to the right, at seventy degrees on our map. The variance description points elsewhere, at thirty-four degrees. After that, all three paths part ways.

Here is the ruler again, per observation, on the map of bells. A small step of the mean counts as that step divided by the standard deviation. Near the top, where the standard deviation is one, a step of zero point one counts for zero point one. Near our target, where it is zero point two, the same step counts for zero point five. Five times more. The data fix a narrow bell much more sharply.

Now the other answer. Steepest, but with the step measured by the local ruler of episode eleven point two. A step is long when the data can tell its two ends apart. That is the Fisher information. The recipe is short. Take the ordinary slope, and multiply it by the inverse of the Fisher information. This is the natural gradient, from Amari, in nineteen ninety-eight.

Amari proved something more. When data arrive one at a time, the natural gradient is what he calls Fisher efficient: asymptotically, it does as well as the best estimate computed on all the data at once. He also suggested that the long plateaus that stall the training of neural networks might become less serious. That is a suggestion, not a theorem.

Its first step is the same in all three descriptions. It points at fifty-three degrees, whatever the names. The names have moved into the ruler, and the ruler cancels them.

Now count. With a step size of zero point five: seventeen steps, twenty-two steps, and fifteen steps. With a step five times smaller, zero point one: ninety-two, ninety-three, and ninety-one.

Look at the paths. With the small steps, the three paths almost lie on top of each other. The largest gap between them, at equal times, is about five hundredths on the map. With steps ten times smaller still, it falls to three thousandths.

With the large steps, they do not. After one step of zero point five, the standard deviation has jumped to three, to seven and a half, and to two and a quarter, depending on the description. That is not a detail. The sameness belongs to the smooth path you would follow with infinitely small steps. James Martens, in his review of the method, says the invariance will not strictly hold for finite steps. Believing it holds for big steps is the trap.

These counts come from one start, one target, and one grid. They illustrate. They do not measure a general gain.

Now the bill. The Fisher information is a table. Its side is the number of parameters. With a million parameters, that is a million million entries, a trillion. At four bytes each, four terabytes. And the table has to be inverted.

So nearly all the effort goes into approximations. One well-known family, from James Martens and Roger Grosse in twenty fifteen, approximates the table layer by layer, with a cost of a few times that of ordinary descent.

One shortcut is tempting. Replace the Fisher information with an average computed from the observed labels. It is cheap, and it is often called the natural gradient. Kunstner, Hennig and Balles showed, in twenty nineteen, that this empirical version does not generally capture second-order information. It is not a reliable approximation.

The idea traveled. In reinforcement learning, Sham Kakade applied the natural gradient to policies, in two thousand and one. Later, John Schulman and colleagues proposed a method they describe as similar to natural policy gradient methods. It caps each update by the divergence between the old policy and the new one. That is a neighbor, not the natural gradient itself. And mirror descent, which runs on the bowls of episode eleven point three, turns out to be a natural gradient seen from the dual side, a result of Raskutti and Mukherjee.

So here is the card. The object: a family of distributions with named parameters. The distance: steps measured with the Fisher ruler. What it ignores: the names of the parameters, for steps small enough. What it costs: a table the size of the parameters squared. Its trap: believing the invariance is automatic with large steps.

One last thing to keep. Steepest is a question with a second half, according to whom. Ordinary gradient descent hides a choice of geometry, and nobody made it on purpose.

Models as points

A fitted model is a point on a map with its own ruler. Established work includes Fechner's just-noticeable differences and neuron populations whose Fisher information sets a decoding floor: 6.0, 3.0 and 1.5 degrees for 8, 32 and 128 neurons. Untested tracks compare conditions by distances between fitted distributions. The key question is whether the family is right.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is the idea of this whole chapter, in one sentence. A fitted model is a point on a map, and the map has its own ruler.

This is the ideas episode of the chapter. We keep two columns apart. On one side, what is established in the literature. On the other, what is only worth trying.

Established, first. In 1860, Gustav Fechner proposed to build a scale of sensation by adding up just-noticeable differences. Take a light. Brighten it until you can just tell the difference. That is one step. Start again from there, and count the steps.

Suppose each step is ten percent brighter than the one before. This is illustrative. Going from 1 to 100 then takes 48 steps. The steps are not equal in size, they are equal in ratio, so the staircase is straight on a log axis.

Count steps you can tell apart: that is a path length, measured with a ruler that changes from place to place. It is the idea of this chapter, a century earlier. But a warning. Whether the brain really stores a logarithm is still open: the physiological search for it remains inconclusive, say Zhou, Duong and Simoncelli.

Second, neurons. A population of neurons responds to the orientation of a line, each with its own noise. The Fisher information of the population sets a floor on how finely any decoder can separate two nearby orientations. The Information series, episode 12.2, builds this up. Paradiso computed such limits in 1988, and Seung and Sompolinsky compared simple read-outs with the best possible one in 1993.

Here is a simulation, seed 116. With 8 neurons the floor is 6.0 degrees. With 32 neurons, 3.0. With 128 neurons, 1.5. A maximum-likelihood decoder, run on three thousand simulated trials, reaches 6.4, 3.0 and 1.5 degrees. Four times the neurons, half the error.

Brunel and Nadal showed in 1998 that, for a large population, the information the neurons carry about the stimulus is naturally related to the Fisher information. Zhou and colleagues use it to tie perceived intensity to discrimination: ratings follow the mean of an internal signal, and sensitivity follows its Fisher information. Kriegeskorte and Wei relate the tuning curves to the geometry of representations, with a caveat. Fisher information is local. It does not fix the whole geometry.

In a model of vision, Berardino and colleagues computed the directions where the Fisher information is largest and smallest. These are the distortions of an image that the model predicts to be most visible and least visible. They then compared them with human observers.

Last on this side: sloppiness. In many models with many parameters, the eigenvalues of the Fisher information spread over many orders of magnitude. A few combinations of parameters are pinned down by the data. The others, almost not at all. Gutenkunst and colleagues examined 17 models of biology. In 16 of them, the eigenvalues span more than six orders of magnitude. Machta and colleagues describe them as roughly evenly spread in the logarithm.

Here is a toy. A sum of four decaying exponentials has eight parameters. Its Fisher eigenvalues, computed, span 8.2 orders of magnitude. Illustrative, not a measurement of any real model.

Now the other column. These are tracks, not results. I have tested none of them on real data.

First track. Compare two conditions by the distance between the fitted distributions, not by the gap between their means. Illustrative reaction times: a gap of 50 milliseconds, with a spread of 50, is 0.98 on the map of bells, the same as one standard deviation in episode 11.1. With a spread of 200, the same gap is 0.25.

Second track. Before you interpret the parameters of a fitted model, look at the eigenvalues of its Fisher information. They say which combinations the data really fix.

Third track. Compare people by their fitted parameters with this ruler, not with a straight line, because a straight line depends on the units of each parameter. Illustrative: Rae, Ann and Ben, each with a mean and a spread. In milliseconds for both, Ben is nearer to Rae than Ann is. Count the mean in seconds and the spread in milliseconds, and Ann becomes the nearer. The Fisher-Rao distances do not care about units: Ben is at 0.66, Ann at 0.79, so Ben is nearer.

Fourth track. Choose the divergence of a fit from the noise in the data. Power spectra do not have bell-shaped noise of the same size everywhere. Squared error says that powers 1 and 2 differ by 1, and powers 100 and 200 by ten thousand. Itakura-Saito says 0.193 both times, the example of episode 11.3.

One question stands between the two columns. Is the family the right one? All this geometry lives inside a family. Take the two bells of episode 6.6, mixed. The mixture has standard deviation 2.29, and two humps. Fit one bell to each of two samples of 2000 points. The two fits are only 0.034 apart on the map. The ruler says the model is pinned down. Yet the divergence from the data to the best bell is 0.50 bits. The distance is precise, and it says nothing about the data.

So a fitted model is a point on a map, and the map has its own ruler. The distance between two models is not the distance between their parameters. It is the number of steps you can tell apart with data.
