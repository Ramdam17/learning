Sham, and why it leaks

A sham is the empty gift box of a stimulation study, built to feel like the real thing, yet each kind leaks: tilted TMS coils still stimulate, ramped currents are felt, tACS flickers in the eye, and ultrasound can be heard. A leaking blind turns a stimulation effect into an expectation effect, so guesses of participants and experimenters are reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every stimulation study hides a second question. Not only what the field does to the brain. Also what the participant, and the experimenter, believe it does. The tool we use to separate the two is called sham. It is a control built to feel exactly like the real thing. And, quite often, it does not.

Here is our picture. Two gift boxes, wrapped in identical paper, with identical ribbons. One holds a present. The other is empty. A sham is the empty box. Nobody should be able to tell which is which: not the person who opens it, and not the person who handed it over. That is blinding. A leak is anything that gives the box away. A rattle. A weight. A bent corner.

In stimulation, the present is the field in the brain. The wrapping is everything that comes with it: a sound, a tingle on the skin, a twitch of a muscle, a flicker of light. Those are the rattle. A good sham copies the rattle, and removes the present.

Start with TMS. The most common sham is a tilted coil: turned so that only one wing, or an edge, touches the scalp. It still clicks. But does it still stimulate? In 2001, Lisanby and colleagues measured the voltage inside the prefrontal cortex of a monkey. Three tilted arrangements cut it by about two thirds to nearly three quarters. A two-wing coil tilted by only forty-five degrees cut it by about a quarter. In volunteers, over the motor cortex, tilted sham was about half as effective as active TMS at producing motor evoked potentials. So a tilted coil can be a weak active condition, not an empty box.

The cleaner option is a dedicated sham coil, built with a magnetic shield that attenuates the field while the click remains. But the click is only one part of the rattle. Active TMS also twitches the muscles of the scalp, and a shielded coil does not. Some studies add weak electrical pulses on the scalp to copy that. Duecker and Sack made the point in 2015: a sham should match the whole experience, because every mismatch changes what participants believe.

Now transcranial current. Here the sham is a ramp. The current rises over several seconds, stays on for a short time, then falls back to zero. The idea is that the itch and tingle under the electrodes are felt mostly at the start, so the sham copies the beginning and then switches off. In 2006, Gandiga and colleagues reported that neither participants nor investigators could tell this sham from real tDCS.

Later studies found a different picture. At one milliamp, Ambrus and colleagues, in 2012, found that naive and experienced participants were fooled, but the investigators were not. At two milliamps, O'Connell and colleagues tested a hundred volunteers in 2012. Their guesses beat chance, and the assessor, who was supposed to be blind, noticed more redness under the electrode after real stimulation. In 2019, Greinacher and colleagues asked thirty-two naive adults, during the session, whether the current was on. For about five of the ten minutes, the answers for the two conditions did not overlap. The box rattled, even at one milliamp.

Alternating current adds one more leak. With tACS, people can see flickering light. Kanai and colleagues reported in 2008 that current over the back of the head worked best at beta frequencies in a lit room, and at alpha frequencies in the dark. Later work by Kar and Krekelberg suggests that this flicker arises in the retina, not in the cortex. Either way, the participant sees it, and a short sham gives only a short flicker.

Focused ultrasound has its own leak: sound. The usual sham is a transducer turned away or decoupled, so that nothing enters the head. But ultrasound pulsed at audible rates can be heard. In 2018, two groups reported widespread brain activity that came through the ear, not from the focus. Guo and colleagues worked in guinea pigs, and cutting the auditory nerves, or removing the fluid of the cochlea, abolished the activity. Sato and colleagues worked in mice. In humans, Braun and colleagues showed in 2020 that sound travels through the skull at the pulse rate, and that it can be masked.

In 2024, Kop and colleagues combined ultrasound with TMS. A protocol pulsed at one thousand hertz had repeatedly reduced motor cortex excitability. In four experiments, at three institutions, they replicated the effect. Then, with tightly matched controls, they showed that in their experiments the inhibition was driven by hearing, not by direct neuromodulation. Their conclusion: earlier work that used only a flip-over sham, with no active control, must be re-evaluated.

So far we have talked about the participant. The experimenter leaks too. Someone who knows the condition may speak differently, hold the coil differently, or read the redness on the skin. That is why a good trial uses a coded mode: a colleague sets the condition, and the experimenter sees only a code.

How do we know whether the blind held? We ask. At the end of the session: which condition do you think you had, and how sure are you? Then we report the answers. One common summary is the blinding index of Bang and colleagues, from 2004, computed in each group. Zero is consistent with perfect blinding. One means everyone guessed right. Minus one means everyone guessed wrong.

The end-of-study guess is itself imperfect. Turner and colleagues found, in 2021, that it did not track how sensitive participants really were to the stimulation, so they argue it may be a poor test on its own. Asking during the session, as Greinacher did, gives a fuller picture.

Here is the classic trap. A leaking blind turns a stimulation effect into an expectation effect. If people feel the real thing and expect to improve, and the sham feels different, the difference you measure may be a difference in feeling, not in the brain. A significant result is then no proof that the field did anything.

So what do we do? Choose the sham that matches the sensations of your technique. Mask the click with noise. Use an active control, which is the next episode. Ask the participants, and report what they said.

That gives a checklist you will meet again in episode thirteen point five. Which sham, and how does it copy sound, skin and sight? Who knew the condition? What did participants guess? And what was the blinding index?

To sum up. A sham is an empty box that must feel like the full one. A tilted coil, a ramped current, or a decoupled transducer each leave a rattle. The participant, and the experimenter, can hear it, feel it or see it. Ask who guessed, and report it.

Next: active controls. Stand-ins that feel the same, and do something different.

PLATE 9.25:27

Active controls

Sham asks whether the field did anything; active controls ask whether the effect belongs to this place, rhythm or direction of current, like understudies who each change one thing. The vertex is not inert, another frequency changes pulse count or duration, and reversing polarity gives a second active condition rather than a null. The control is chosen from the question and written down first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, sham was an empty box. It answers one question: did the field itself do anything? But a good study often asks a sharper one. Is the effect tied to this place, this rhythm, this direction of current? A sham cannot say. Duecker and Sack made the point in 2015: sham does not show that an effect is specific to a brain area or a moment, so it should complement other control strategies. This episode is about those strategies. They are called active controls.

Here is our picture. A theatre. On the stage, in the spotlight, stands the lead: the real protocol, on its marked spot. In the wings wait three understudies. Each one plays the same part, with exactly one thing changed. One stands somewhere else. One plays at another tempo. One walks the other way. They are real actors, with real stimulation. And each answers a different question.

The first understudy changes the place. The classic choice, in TMS, is the vertex: the top of the head, on the midline. The hope is that it gives the same click and the same feel on the scalp, while leaving the networks of the task alone.

Is the vertex really inert? In 2016, Jung and colleagues put TMS inside an MRI scanner. Thirty-two healthy adults. Single pulses at one hertz, twelve in each eleven-second block, at one hundred twenty percent of resting motor threshold. Twenty-one people were stimulated at the vertex, eleven over the left motor cortex. At the vertex, no region showed more activity. But many showed less. The decreases were widespread, and they included the default mode network: medial frontal cortex, the cingulate, the precuneus. The connectivity of that network did not change. The authors concluded that the vertex should not be seen as entirely inert, and that it may be a poor choice when the question touches the default mode network.

So a control site is not a blank. It is another target, with its own effects. Choose it for the question. And, as in the last episode, ask participants what they felt.

The second understudy changes the rhythm. If your claim is about a frequency, then the control is the same stimulation at a different frequency. In 2003, Klimesch and colleagues delivered rTMS over the right parietal and the mesial frontal cortex, just before a mental rotation task. The target train ran one hertz above each person's individual alpha frequency. The controls were a train three hertz below it, a train at twenty hertz, and a sham with a tilted coil. They reported that the train just above alpha improved performance, and increased the alpha desynchronisation that goes with the task.

Notice the price. At a lower rate, the same number of pulses takes longer. So either the pulse count or the duration must differ, and you have to say which one you matched.

The third understudy changes the direction. In tDCS, the obvious control looks like a mirror: anodal against cathodal. Anodal excites, cathodal inhibits. But the mirror is not a good one.

In 2012, Jacobson and colleagues pooled the literature. In motor studies, that pattern was common. In cognitive studies, anodal stimulation usually improved performance, but cathodal stimulation rarely produced inhibition. And in 2013, Batsikadze and colleagues applied two milliamps for twenty minutes over the motor cortex of fourteen volunteers. Both polarities increased motor evoked potentials. One milliamp of cathodal current, in a smaller group, lowered them. So reversing the polarity does not give you an opposite, or a null. It gives you a second active condition. With two electrodes, it also swaps what sits under the other one.

Now the costs. An active control takes more sessions, or more participants. And it does not replace sham. It answers a different question, and it brings its own unknowns.

Here is the classic trap: reading the control as zero. If target and control do not differ, what does that mean? Maybe neither did anything. Maybe both did the same thing, through a sound, through attention, through the default mode network. A difference can be inflated or shrunk by what the control did. Read the pattern, not just one contrast.

So what do we do? Choose the control from the question. Write it down before the first participant, with its parameters, and with the result that would count as specific. Preregistration, as Nosek and colleagues argued in 2018, separates a tested prediction from a story told after the fact. A control picked afterwards, because it gave the nicer contrast, is not a control.

That gives a checklist, which you will meet again in episode thirteen point five. Which question does this control answer? What does it change, and what does it keep? Is there evidence that it is not inert? Was it written down in advance?

To sum up. Sham asks whether the field did anything. Active controls ask whether the effect belongs to this place, this rhythm, this direction. None is neutral. Choose by the question, and write it down first.

Next: dose, target, timing, and brain state. The lines of the dose card that decide what you find.

PLATE 9.36:37

Dose, target, timing and brain state

A stimulation study has a dose card, like a theatre cue sheet, and each line needs its reference: intensity relative to a measured threshold and device, current with real contact area, a target with a reason. The line most often forgotten is what the participant and the brain are doing, since the same stimulation gives different results in different brain states.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In a theatre, the stage manager keeps a cue sheet. Every line says what the lights do, and when. Light twenty-four: spotlight, centre stage, on. A good sheet lets another crew run the same show. But look at what the sheet leaves out. It never says what is happening on the stage when the light comes on. And the same light, on a different scene, looks different.

A stimulation study has a cue sheet too. It is the dose card. Last time, understudies were the answer to a different question. This episode is about the card itself, one line at a time. Every value on it is fictional.

Technique. Repetitive TMS. Target. The hand area of the left motor cortex. Coil. A figure-eight coil, seventy millimetres across. Intensity. Eighty percent of resting motor threshold. Waveform. Biphasic. Pattern. Intermittent theta burst: bursts of three pulses at fifty hertz, five bursts per second, two seconds on and eight seconds off. Duration. About three minutes, six hundred pulses. Sessions. One.

Pause on intensity, because a bare number is not a dose. Most TMS machines set the pulse as a percentage of the machine's maximum. But two machines with the same setting can produce different pulses. So in 2012, Peterchev and colleagues asked authors to report the device and the coil, and to give the absolute setting next to the relative one. Their example: one hundred twenty percent of motor threshold, and what that is as a share of the machine's output.

Distance matters too. Stokes and colleagues, in 2007, placed spacers under a coil over the motor cortex. Each extra millimetre between coil and cortex needed about two point eight percent more machine output to reach the same threshold. So eighty percent of threshold is eighty percent of one person's threshold, measured at one place.

With current stimulation, the line reads differently. One milliamp is not enough. Peterchev's paper asks for the current, the duration, and the electrode's real contact area, because the saline can spread past the electrode. Current density, the current divided by the area, is only a summary. One milliamp through twenty-five square centimetres is the same as zero point zero four milliamps per square centimetre. Through thirty-five, it is lower. That is our own arithmetic. And chapter seven showed that even a fixed current gives a different field in each head. Evans and colleagues, in 2020, drew groups of fifteen heads and found group averages in the motor cortex ranging from zero point one five eight to zero point two one three volts per metre.

Now the target. Where does the coil go? A target needs a reason. For the hand area, the reason is a measurement: the spot where a pulse makes the hand muscle twitch. For other regions, authors have used the scalp positions of the ten-twenty EEG system, group coordinates, or the person's own scan. Sack and colleagues, in 2009, compared four ways to find the same parietal target. Targeting with the person's own functional MRI gave the largest effect on behaviour. The ten-twenty scalp position gave the smallest. The authors attribute this to how far the real stimulated site drifted from person to person. A field model, as in chapter seven, can then say what actually reaches the target.

Now the last line. Participant, during stimulation. On our card, it is empty. It is the line people forget most. And it is the one that can flip a result.

Silvanto and colleagues set the idea out in 2008, for TMS. The effect of a pulse depends on the initial activation state of the neurons it reaches. The same pulse is not the same input to a quiet network and to a busy one.

A real case. In 2008, Romei and colleagues sent identical TMS pulses over the visual cortex of blindfolded volunteers while recording EEG. When the alpha rhythm just before the pulse was weak, the pulse reliably produced a flash of light. When the alpha rhythm was strong, the same pulse often failed to produce one. Same coil, same intensity. A different moment, a different brain state.

For current stimulation, a real case too. In 2007, Antal and colleagues gave twelve healthy volunteers ten minutes of tDCS over the left motor cortex. Some sat still. Some did a cognitive task. Some squeezed a ball. The aftereffects were not the same. The cognitive task changed them compared with sitting still, and the hand exercise lowered excitability after both polarities. The authors concluded that the effect depends heavily on the state of the subject. Two studies with the same card, apart from this line, could have disagreed.

A review in 2022, by Bradley, Nydam, Dux and Mattingley, collected such results across sleep, anaesthesia, attention and working memory. Stimulation effects change with the state, and, for working memory, tend to be larger under high load.

And what about the moment just before? Episode eight point one asks whether the pulse can be timed to a rhythm. Here the question is plainer: what was the person doing, and what was the brain doing, before the pulse?

So what goes on the line? Rest or task. Eyes open or closed. What the instructions were. Whether the task was the same during and after. Whether brain activity was recorded. Peterchev and colleagues keep brain state outside the definition of dose, since the machine does not control it. But they list attention and neural oscillations among the factors that shape the response, and they ask authors to report them. Chipchase and colleagues made a checklist for single-pulse TMS studies of the motor system: twenty-seven items, from an international expert consensus. A checklist is longer than one card, but it keeps a forgotten line from slipping through. And an active control, from last time, must share this line too. Otherwise it is a different scene.

To sum up. A dose card is a cue sheet. Intensity needs its reference. A target needs a reason. And the line you forget, what the participant does, can change the result.

Next: how long do effects last? The curtain falls. Does the light stay?

PLATE 9.46:24

How long do effects last?

An effect has a time course, like fading applause, so minutes, an hour and a day are separate claims with different evidence. Most data come from healthy motor cortex, and spacing sessions or raising the dose can change an effect's direction, not just its length. One measurement cannot give a duration: several time points, sham, follow-up and a justified wash-out are needed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The curtain has fallen. The audience applauds, loud at first, then thinner, then silent. A stimulation effect behaves a little like that applause. This episode asks one plain question: how long does it last?

Follow one session along a timeline. During the stimulation. Just after it. One hour later. One day later. And after many sessions. For each moment, ask two things. What data exist? And how solid are they?

The first moment, during the stimulation, is left aside here, because the studies cited all measure after the current or the pulses stop. Start just after the session. This is the best-documented part of the timeline. Most of it comes from the motor cortex of healthy volunteers, read out as motor evoked potentials, a muscle twitch caused by a magnetic pulse. In 2000, Nitsche and Paulus applied weak direct current. Excitability changed by up to forty percent, and the change lasted several minutes after the current stopped. Varying the intensity and the duration controlled the strength and the length of the after-effect. In 2001 they reported an increase of about one hundred and fifty percent that lasted up to ninety minutes after the end of stimulation.

Now one hour. For magnetic stimulation, Huang and colleagues described theta burst in 2005. Earlier repetitive protocols rarely lasted longer than thirty minutes. Theta burst produced an effect after only twenty to one hundred and ninety seconds of stimulation. Depending on the protocol and on the report, the after-effect is described as lasting from about a quarter of an hour for the intermittent pattern to about an hour for the continuous one.

These durations are averages, and they do not hold for everyone. López-Alonso and colleagues tested fifty-six people with three protocols. Over the first hour, the group as a whole showed no significant effect, and only thirty-nine, forty-five and forty-three percent of people responded as expected. Wiethoff and colleagues gave ten minutes of two milliamps to fifty-three people and followed them for half an hour. About half had little or no response. Episode nine point five returns to this.

More current is not simply a longer effect. Batsikadze and colleagues gave two milliamps for twenty minutes. Both polarities increased the evoked potentials, while one milliamp of cathodal current decreased them. So a stronger or longer session can change the direction of an effect, not only its length.

Now one day. Here the data thin out. Several of the studies above stop within an hour or two. A few go further. Batsikadze and colleagues sampled up to the next evening. Reis and colleagues gave anodal tDCS during five days of practice on a motor skill task. Skill was still greater than with sham at three months. That is one task in healthy volunteers, and it measures behaviour, not excitability.

Then repeated sessions. Here, spacing matters. Monte-Silva and colleagues gave two tDCS sessions. When the second came three or twenty minutes after the first, during its after-effect, the enhancement was first reduced, then prolonged. With no gap, or with a gap of three or twenty-four hours, the effect was abolished or reversed. Tse and colleagues compared repeated theta burst blocks in fifteen people. A single block changed nothing in that sample. Blocks fifteen minutes apart increased the response for up to an hour, and blocks five minutes apart decreased it. Even then, only about half of the individuals facilitated after the fifteen-minute gap, and two in five were inhibited after the five-minute gap. Goldsworthy and colleagues found that a single continuous theta burst block was undone by a muscle contraction, while a spaced pair resisted it. And Alonzo and colleagues gave twelve volunteers tDCS either daily or every second day over five days. Daily sessions gave a larger increase, reflecting cumulative effects between sessions. All of this is healthy motor cortex, and small samples.

Now the key point. An after-effect measured at a single time point says nothing about its duration. One measurement ten minutes after stimulation shows that something happened. It cannot say whether the effect fades at fifteen minutes or lasts a day. To speak of duration, measure several times after stimulation, continue until the outcome returns to baseline, and take the same time points under sham.

The same rule protects against a second error. In a study with many sessions, a measurement made right after the last one may only show the fresh after-effect of that session. A follow-up, days after the last session, is what separates a cumulative change from a transient one. A transient effect reported as lasting is an easy mistake.

Wash-out follows the same logic. In a crossover design, each person receives active stimulation and sham in two periods. The wash-out is the pause between them, long enough for the first effect to be gone. Biabani and colleagues reviewed nineteen crossover studies of tDCS and motor learning. They found various lengths of wash-out, and asked for detailed guidelines. One stroke trial chose at least two weeks, citing an earlier report in which a single session had worn off by day eleven. Whatever the length, it should be justified from data on the outcome being measured, with a baseline check at the start of the second period, and the order of conditions randomised.

Treatment schedules, such as several sessions in one day, are a clinical question. Chapter twelve takes them up.

To sum up. An effect has a time course, not a single value. Minutes, an hour and a day are three different claims, with three different levels of evidence. Spacing changes the result. And one measurement cannot give a duration. So a line goes on the dose card and on the checklist: the time of each outcome measurement after stimulation, and the wash-out between sessions.

Next: responders, or noise? Some people go up, some go down, and the average barely moves. Is that the person, or the measurement?

PLATE 9.57:00

Responders, non-responders and regression to the mean

A spread of responses to stimulation is not a list of responder types. In a simulation where everyone has the same true effect, or none, noisy measurements still award gold stars that vanish on retest, and regression to the mean makes low baselines rise. Repeating measures, using sham inside the same person and fixing the responder definition beforehand protect against this.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a result you have probably seen. A group of people receive the same stimulation. Afterwards some show a bigger motor response, some a smaller one, and the average hardly moves. The tempting conclusion is that the brain comes in two kinds: responders and non-responders. This episode asks how much of that sorting is real, and how much is noise.

Our picture is an audition. A judge scores each singer twice, once before the song and once after. Nobody is coached in between. Then the judge hands a gold star to anyone whose second score jumped by twenty points or more. A gold star is our word for a responder.

First, what is documented. In 2013, Hamada and colleagues gave theta burst stimulation to the motor cortex of fifty-six healthy volunteers. The responses to both the excitatory and the inhibitory protocol were highly variable between individuals. Part of that variability tracked the latency of the motor evoked potentials, which hints at which circuits each pulse recruits.

In 2014, López-Alonso and colleagues tested fifty-six people with three protocols: paired associative stimulation, anodal tDCS, and intermittent theta burst. For the whole sample, none of them changed excitability significantly. A cluster analysis found two groups. But only thirty-nine, forty-five and forty-three percent of people responded as expected, in that order. The same year, Wiethoff and colleagues gave ten minutes of two-milliamp tDCS to fifty-three people. Anodal stimulation raised the motor evoked potentials on average, and cathodal did not. About half of the participants had little or no response.

These are careful studies. But a spread of responses is not yet a list of types of people. To see why, we simulate.

Forty simulated people. Each of them has exactly the same true change from stimulation: five points, on a scale where baseline is one hundred. Every measurement carries noise, with a standard deviation of twenty points. That value is our own choice, for illustration. So each person's measured change is five points, plus the difference between two noisy measurements.

The result looks like a real study. The average change is four and a half points. Twenty-three people go up and seventeen go down. Twelve people earn a gold star. Eight drop by twenty points or more. Yet in this simulation everyone responds in exactly the same way.

Now remove the stimulation. The true change is zero for everyone. Same people, same noise, one measurement before and one after. The average change is one point eight. Twenty go up, twenty go down. Eleven earn a gold star, and eleven drop by twenty points or more. The picture is the same. Nobody was stimulated.

If those stars were a trait of the person, they should come back. So we measure the same eleven again, again without stimulation. Only one of them earns a star a second time. As a group, their average change is minus seven point eight. Across all forty people, the changes in the two rounds are almost uncorrelated: about zero point one. The stars did not stick to the people, because they were noise.

The same mechanism has another name: regression to the mean. Bland and Altman wrote in 1994 that unless two measurements agree perfectly, people picked for an extreme first value will, on average, be less extreme the second time. In our simulation, the ten people with the lowest baseline scores, around seventy-three, rose by thirty-two points on average, with no stimulation at all. Across the forty, baseline and change are correlated at minus zero point six six. Recruit people with a low baseline, and you will see them improve.

How noisy are real measurements of plasticity? Jannati and colleagues tested twenty-eight adults twice, a median of nine and a half days apart, with continuous theta burst. They computed the intraclass correlation of the change in motor evoked potential. One means a perfectly repeatable measure. At best it reached zero point five three, at fifty minutes. At the other time points it was lower, down to minus zero point zero eight at sixty minutes. Boucher and colleagues, in 2021, tested twenty-four adults on two visits about a month apart. For intermittent theta burst, the correlation was negative at every time point. Their conclusion: no protocol showed consistent within-person modulation on retest. These are motor evoked potentials in two cohorts. Other measures may behave differently.

Does this mean that responders do not exist? No. Heads, tissues and brain states do differ, and Hamada's latency result is a hint of that. The claim is narrower. A single before-and-after difference cannot tell a stable non-responder from a person who had a noisy day. Stephen Senn made the point for drug trials in 2004. If seventy percent of patients improve in a single trial, that is as compatible with seventy percent always responding as with everyone responding seventy percent of the time. In 2016 he described how repeated periods in the same person, as in crossover and N-of-1 designs, let you estimate the personal part of a response. His conclusion was that the evidence for a strong personal element is often weaker than believed.

So three habits. First, repeat. Measure the baseline more than once, so you know the noise of one person. Repeat the stimulation too, so you can ask whether the same people respond again. Second, build a control inside the person. The same people also receive sham, on another day, as in episode nine point one. The spread you see under sham is your yardstick for what noise alone can produce. Third, write the definition in advance. Which measure, which time points, how large a change counts, and how many repeats. Pre-register it.

Here is the classic trap. You split people into responders and non-responders after the fact, then search for what predicts the split. You will always find something, because the stars were partly random. Test any predictor in a new sample, with the responder definition fixed beforehand.

To sum up. People do vary in how they respond to stimulation, and that variability is documented. But one before-and-after difference mixes the person with noise and with regression to the mean. A gold star from a single measurement is not yet a trait. Repeat the measures, control inside the person, and fix the definition first.

Next: power for stimulation studies. How many people do you need to see an effect this small through this much noise?

PLATE 9.68:35

Power for stimulation studies

Power is the chance of hearing a real whisper, and it depends on effect size, sample, noise and design. Simulations with a typical sample of twenty-two show low power for a realistic effect of about 0.2 standard deviations, and the few significant studies overshoot the truth fivefold and sometimes point the wrong way. Within-subject designs help if order and carry-over are controlled.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Imagine the back row of a theatre. On stage, an actor whispers one line. Do you hear it? A small audience, with a cough in every other row, will often hear nothing. And when a line does get heard, it is usually because that night the actor happened to be loud.

A stimulation study has the same shape. The whisper is the effect of the stimulation. The audience is the sample. Hearing the line is a significant result. The chance of hearing a whisper that is really there is called statistical power.

Power depends on four things: the size of the effect, the number of people, the noise, and the threshold for significance. Here the threshold is the usual five percent. A stagehand called Dice will run the studies for us. Everything on screen is simulated. The seed is ninety-six, and each setting is run five thousand times. Each simulated study draws its people, measures them, and runs a standard t test. The code is saved with the episode.

Dice needs a true effect. We try two, in units of standard deviations. The realistic one comes from a recent meta-analysis. In 2026, Wang and colleagues pooled twenty-three randomised trials of tDCS combined with cognitive training, in healthy adults, with working memory as the outcome. The pooled effect was a Hedges g of zero point one nine, with a confidence interval from zero point zero six to zero point three two. We round it to zero point two. That is one outcome and one kind of protocol. Other reviews find less. In 2015, Horvath and colleagues ran fifty-nine analyses of single-session tDCS and cognition in healthy adults. None was significant. So, depending on the study, a realistic effect runs from nothing to about zero point two.

The hoped-for effect is zero point six. It is the value that Minarik and colleagues assumed in 2016, when they planned a preregistered tDCS study. It is also close to the median effect size, zero point six one, reported in the studies that Mitra and colleagues reviewed in 2019.

How many people? Mitra and colleagues looked at brain stimulation studies in neuropsychiatric conditions. The mean total sample was twenty-two people, with a standard deviation of almost twenty-five. Dice uses twenty-two. They also estimated how much power those studies had against the effects of their own meta-analyses. The median was fifty percent overall, twenty-seven percent for tDCS, and seventy percent for TMS. In 2013, Button and colleagues did the same for neuroscience as a whole: a median power of twenty-one percent, across forty-nine meta-analyses and seven hundred and thirty studies. A reanalysis in 2017, by Nord and colleagues, found that many studies are indeed underpowered, some seriously, but that many have acceptable power. So the answer varies with the field and the question.

First design: between subjects. Eleven people receive active stimulation and eleven receive sham, each measured once. If the true effect is the hoped-for zero point six, Dice finds it in twenty-seven percent of the studies. If the true effect is the realistic zero point two, it finds it in seven percent. That is barely above the five percent of false alarms you would get with no effect at all.

The usual aim is eighty percent. For the hoped-for effect, that takes ninety people in total. For the realistic effect, it takes seven hundred and eighty-eight. Minarik's own calculation, one-tailed, gave seventy-two for the hoped-for effect.

Second design: within subjects. The same twenty-two people receive both active stimulation and sham, on different days. Each person is their own control, so their stable traits cancel out. How much this helps depends on the within-person correlation, which is how much a person's two measurements resemble each other. Dice assumes zero point five. That value is our own choice, and real values depend on the measure.

For the hoped-for effect, power rises from twenty-seven to seventy-six percent. For the realistic effect, from seven to fifteen. To reach eighty percent, the total falls from ninety to twenty-four people, and from seven hundred and eighty-eight to two hundred. With a correlation of zero point three, the realistic effect needs two hundred and seventy-eight people. With zero point seven, one hundred and twenty. Episode nine point five showed that the retest reliability of motor evoked potential changes can be low. A low correlation moves you toward the expensive end.

Turn the question around. What is the smallest effect that twenty-two people can find with eighty percent power? Between groups, one point two six standard deviations. Within the same people, zero point six three. Neither is close to zero point two.

Now the cost of low power. It is not only missed effects. Take the realistic effect, zero point two, and twenty-two people in two groups. Dice runs five thousand studies. Averaged over all of them, the estimate is zero point two one, which is right. But only seven percent of the studies reach significance. Suppose only those are published. Their mean estimate is one point zero one, five times the truth. The smallest significant estimate was zero point five six. A study this small can only cross the line when it overshoots. And nine percent of the significant results point the wrong way.

Gelman and Carlin named these errors of magnitude and of sign in 2014. Ioannidis described the same inflation in 2008, for newly discovered associations. In the within-subject design, the significant studies overshoot by a factor of two point six. Better, not cured. More people help: the overshoot is six and a half times with twelve people, three point eight with forty, two with one hundred and sixty, and one point four with four hundred.

This is one reason why a later, larger study often finds a smaller effect. Publication bias adds to it, and episode thirteen point four returns to that.

Crossover designs deserve their reputation, under two conditions. First, order. If everyone receives active stimulation first, practice or fatigue is mixed up with the stimulation. So randomise or counterbalance the order. Second, carry-over. The first condition may leave traces in the second. Episode nine point four covered the wash-out, and its length must be justified with data on your own outcome. Testing for carry-over first, and choosing the analysis afterwards, does not repair this. In 1989, Freeman showed that this two-stage procedure gives confidence intervals that cover the truth less often than they claim. Better to prevent carry-over by design. Our simulation has neither order effects nor carry-over, so its within-subject advantage is a best case.

The same reflexes appear in the robustness chapters of the fNIRS, EEG and fMRI series.

So, in practice. Take the effect size from a meta-analysis, or the smallest effect worth detecting, and not from your best pilot. Compute the sample for that effect. Say which effect your sample could actually detect. Take the correlation from earlier data. And write all of it down before the first participant, in a preregistration.

The classic trap is the lucky pilot. You collect twenty people, find a significant effect, and use its size to plan the next study. That size is one of the overshoots.

To sum up. Power depends on effect, sample, noise and design. With the effects that meta-analyses report for tDCS and the samples typical of the field, power is often low. Low power misses real effects, and exaggerates the ones it finds. Crossover helps, if order and carry-over are managed. And one line goes on the dose card and on the checklist: sample size justified by a power analysis with a realistic effect size, pre-registered.
