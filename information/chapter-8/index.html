Predictive dependence · Wiener's idea

A signal predicts another in the Granger sense when its past improves the prediction beyond the other's own past. Simulations show a real link cutting error by 29 percent, but a hidden common driver, like a church bell heard by two pigeons, produces an equally large false arrow that vanishes once the driver is included. The result is predictive dependence, not mechanism.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the pigeon loft. This is chapter 8, directed information.

So far, every dependence in this series was symmetric. The mutual information between two signals is the same in both directions. Now we ask a question with an arrow in it, does one signal help predict the other?

Here is the intuition. In our Pigeon Loft, two pigeons come home every evening, one from the north and one from the south. Night after night, the southern pigeon lands a few minutes after the northern one.

Did the northern pigeon carry a message that sent the southern one home? Maybe. Or maybe both left when the same church bell rang, and the northern route is simply shorter. Or the southern pigeon always rests on the same roof on its way, whatever the other one does. Arriving just after is not the same as carrying the letter.

Keep that picture. The whole chapter is about telling those stories apart, and about what can and cannot be told apart from timing alone.

The idea starts with Norbert Wiener in 1956. Wiener suggested that one signal could be called causal for another if knowing its past improved the prediction of the other, beyond what the other's own past already gave.

Clive Granger turned this into a practical test in 1969 in econometrics. Fit a model that predicts Y from its own past. Fit a second model that predicts Y from its own past and from the past of X. If the second model predicts significantly better, X is said to Granger cause Y.

The important word is beyond. The past of Y is always in the model. The question is never does X predict Y, which almost anything correlated would do. The question is does X add anything once Y's own history is known.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate three pairs of signals, 20,000 samples each, labelled simulated. For each pair, we fit the two prediction models with five past samples and compare their errors. We report the log of the ratio of the two error variances, which is Granger's measure, and the fraction by which the error drops.

In the first pair, x really drives y with a delay of two samples. Adding the past of X reduces the error in predicting Y by 29%. The log ratio is 0.35. In the other direction from Y to X, the log ratio is 0.0002, essentially nothing. The arrow is found and it points the right way.

In the second pair, X and Y are independent. Both log ratios are around 0.0001. Nothing to add, nothing found.

In the third pair, there is a hidden driver Z. It pushes X after one sample and Y after three samples. There is no link at all from X to Y. Yet the log ratio from X to Y is 0.34, with a 29% drop in error, as large as the real link in the first pair.

That is the church bell. X heard it first, so X's past announces what Y is about to do. The prediction is real. The mechanism is not.

Now add Z to both models so that the question becomes, does X add anything beyond Y's past and Z's past? The log ratio falls to 0.0001. Conditioning on the common driver removes the false arrow. Episode 8.8 comes back to this with whole networks.

So Granger causality and everything in this chapter that descends from it is a statement about prediction. It answers, does the past of X carry information about the future of Y that Y's own past does not? It does not answer, does X physically act on Y?

That is why, in this series, we will say predictive dependence rather than causality whenever we can. It is longer, but it is honest.

What is predictive dependence sensitive to?

First, what is in the model? A missing common driver creates false arrows as we just saw. Adding a variable can make an arrow vanish or appear.

Second timing. The method relies on one signal leading the other. If the measurement delays differ, for example a sensor that responds slowly or a filter that shifts one signal, the apparent order can flip. Episode 6.8 showed filters doing exactly that.

Third, the sampling rate. A real influence that acts faster than one sample looks instantaneous, and lagged models cannot see instantaneous effects. Sample too slowly and the arrow disappears.

Fourth, history length. With too few past samples of Y, Y's own past is poorly summarised, and X gets credit for information that Y already had. The U-curve of episode 7.2 applies here too.

And fifth, the model class. Granger's original test is linear. A nonlinear influence can escape it. Transfer entropy in the next episode removes that restriction.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study finds that activity in one region Granger causes activity in another, and writes that the first region drives the second. But the analysis included only those two regions. Any third source that reaches the first region earlier, such as a thalamic input, a stimulus, or a shared physiological rhythm would produce exactly the same result.

The safe sentence is narrower. The past of the first region improves the prediction of the second beyond the second region's own past in this model with these variables. Every word of that sentence matters.

Wiener's idea, made practical by Granger, X helps predict Y if its past improves the prediction beyond Y's own past.

On our simulations, a real link reduced the prediction error by 29%. Independent signals gave nothing. A hidden common driver produced a false arrow just as large, which vanished when the driver was added.

It is a statement about prediction, not mechanism. We call it predictive dependence.

Transfer entropy

Transfer entropy is the conditional mutual information between a source's past and a target's present, given the target's past: what the letter adds once the diary is read. It is never negative and carries an arrow. Lagged mutual information shows a false reverse arrow from the target's own memory, which conditioning removes, and surrogates are needed because the estimator is biased.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Transfer entropy. Does the source tell the target anything new?

Last time, Granger's question, does the past of X improve the prediction of Y beyond Y's own past? Granger answered it with linear prediction errors. Today we answer it with information, which removes the linear restriction.

Here is the intuition. At the pigeon loft, the keeper of station Y writes a diary every day. Each morning, a pigeon brings a letter from station X.

The keeper does not ask whether the letter is related to what happens today at station Y. Of course it is, both stations sit under the same sky. The keeper asks a sharper question, Once I have read my own diary, does the letter still tell me something about today that I could not have guessed?

If the letter only repeats what the diary already says, it carries nothing new. If it adds something, information was transferred, in the predictive sense of last episode.

That sharper question is transfer entropy. Thomas Schreiber introduced it in 2000 in Physical Review Letters under the title Measuring Information Transfer.

Here is the definition, built from pieces we already know. Transfer entropy from X to Y is the conditional mutual information between the past of X and the present of Y, given the past of Y.

Read it from the right. Given the past of Y, the diary is already read. The present of Y, today at station Y. The past of X, the letter. Conditional mutual information, what the letter adds once the diary is known.

There is a second way to read the same quantity, which explains its name. Transfer entropy is the drop in the entropy rate of Y when the past of X is added. Without the letter, today at station Y has a certain uncertainty given the diary. That is the entropy rate of chapter 7. With the letter that uncertainty is smaller. The difference in bits or nats is the transfer entropy. It is information about Y's next step that only X's past provides.

Two properties follow directly from chapter 4.

First, transfer entropy is never negative because a conditional mutual information is never negative.

Second, it is zero if and only if the present of Y is conditionally independent of the past of X, given the past of Y. No hidden linearity, no model class. If the letter adds anything at all, in any form, the transfer entropy is above zero.

And it has an arrow. Transfer entropy from X to Y and from Y to X are different quantities, built from different pasts and presents.

Now the calculation in one picture. Two pictures in fact, one exact one with a twist.

First the exact one in bits. Station X sends fair random bits, one per day. Station y copies yesterday's bit from X, but makes a mistake one time in 10. Station Y's own past says nothing about today because X's bits are random. The letter tells almost everything. The transfer entropy from X to Y is 1 bit minus the entropy of a 90-10 coin, about 0.53 bits.

Now the twist with continuous signals in NATS. We simulate two signals, 20,000 samples, labelled simulated. X is a slow, strongly autocorrelated process. Y depends on its own past and on the previous value of X. There is a link from X to Y and none from Y to X.

Before computing transfer entropy, let us try the naive approach. The lagged mutual information between the past of one signal and the present of the other, with no conditioning.

From X to Y, at a lag of one sample, the lagged mutual information is 0.77 nats. So far so good.

But from Y to X, the lagged mutual information is 0.36 nats. That is large, and there is no link from Y to X at all.

Here is Y. Yesterday's Y contains the day before's X, because X drives Y. And X is slow, so the day before's X predicts today's X. Yesterday's Y therefore predicts today's X through X itself. The arrow is false and it comes from the target's own memory.

Now compute transfer entropy with two past samples of the target. From X to Y, 0.27 nats. From Y to X, 0.00 to the second decimal. Conditioning on the past of X removed exactly what X already knew about itself.

A nearest neighbour estimate on 5,000 samples agrees. 0.28 nats from X to Y, and 0.01 from Y to X, the small positive bias that chapter 5 taught us to expect.

The lagged mutual information has a second problem. Compute it from X to Y at lags from 1 to 10. It is 0.77 at 1, still 0.75 at 2, and 0.34 at 5, although the real delay is exactly one sample. X's slowness smears the delay across many lags. Transfer entropy by conditioning on the target's past does not reward a source for repeating what the target already absorbed.

So everything happens in the conditioning on the target's past. Without it we measure shared history. With it we measure new information.

For Gaussian signals, transfer entropy and Granger's measure are closely related. Episode 8.3 makes that exact.

What is transfer entropy sensitive to?

First the target's history length. Too short and the target's own memory leaks into the transfer entropy, exactly as in our lagged mutual information. Too long and the estimator runs out of neighbours. The U-curve of episode 7.2 again.

Second, the source's history and the interaction delay. If the source's past is taken at the wrong lag, a real link can vanish. Episode 8.4 is about choosing delays.

Third, the estimator. Transfer entropy is a conditional mutual information, often in four or more dimensions. Every bias from chapter 5 is here, multiplied by the dimension. Kraskov-style estimators of conditional mutual information from Frenzel and Pompe are a common choice. Surrogates from chapter 6 are mandatory.

Fourth, everything that is not in the model. As with Granger, a hidden common driver can create transfer entropy without any link. Conditioning on that driver when it is measured is the remedy, and episode 8.8 extends it to networks.

Fifth, the sampling rate. An influence that acts within one sample cannot be seen by a measure built on the past, and very fine sampling makes the target's past so informative that little is left for the source. Transfer entropies are only comparable at the same sampling rate.

And sixth. The units. Here is good news. Transfer entropy is a conditional mutual information, so it does not change when you rescale either signal or apply any invertible transformation to the values. It is in bits or nats, and the unit is the log base, nothing else.

Now the classic pitfall.

A paper computes the lagged mutual information, or a lagged correlation between two regions, finds it larger in one direction, and reports directed information flow.

As we just saw, a lagged dependence without conditioning on the target's past can point the wrong way. In our simulation, the reverse-lagged mutual information was 0.36 nats, almost half of the forward one with no reverse link at all.

The second half of the pitfall is the opposite reflex, computing transfer entropy with a single past sample of the target because it is fast. That leaves most of the target's memory unconditioned, and the false arrow comes back in.

And a third, quieter version, reporting a transfer entropy value without a test, because the estimator is biased upward, a positive number on its own means nothing. In our simulation, the true reverse transfer entropy was 0, and the nearest neighbour estimate was still 0.01. Only a comparison with surrogates built so that they keep everything except the link being tested says whether a value is larger than what the estimator produces on its own.

The fix is the definition itself. Condition on enough of the target's past, choose that history with a criterion, and test against surrogates that keep each signal's own memory, such as the circular shifts of episode 6.3.

Transfer entropy from X to Y is the conditional mutual information between the past of X and the present of Y, given the past of Y.

It is never negative, it has a direction, and it is zero exactly when the past of X adds nothing once Y's past is known.

Transfer entropy meets Granger. When is the information-theoretic version worth it?

We now have two ways to ask whether the past of X helps predict Y beyond Y's own past. Granger's way with linear prediction errors. And Schreiber's way with transfer entropy. Today we put them side by side and find out when they agree and when they do not.

Here is the intuition. At the pigeon loft, two inspectors check whether station X's letters bring real news to station Y.

The first inspector uses a ruler. He measures how far off station Y's forecasts are, with and without the letters, and compares the averages. If a letter shifts the forecast up or down in a straight-line way, he sees it at once.

The second inspector reads everything. She asks whether the letters change anything at all about what station Y can expect, not just the average, but the spread, the shape, the odd combinations.

When the letters speak in straight lines, the two inspectors agree exactly. When the letters speak in riddles, only the second inspector hears them.

Here is the exact result. In 2009, Lionel Barnett, Adam Barrett, and Anil Seth showed that for jointly Gaussian variables, transfer entropy and Granger causality are equivalent. Precisely, if Granger causality is measured as the log of the ratio of the two prediction error variances, as in episode 8.1, then transfer entropy in nats equals exactly half of it.

The reason is short. For Gaussian variables, a conditional entropy is one-half the log of a conditional variance, plus a constant. Transfer entropy is a difference of two conditional entropies, so it becomes one-half the log of a ratio of variances, which is Granger's measure divided by two.

So for Gaussian data, Granger causality is not an approximation of transfer entropy. It is transfer entropy computed with the right estimator.

Now the calculation in one picture. Two systems.

The first is linear in Gaussian. X drives Y through its last two values, 50,000 samples labelled simulated. Granger's log ratio is 0.38. Half of that is 0.19 nats, and that is the transfer entropy. A nearest neighbour estimate on 8,000 samples gives 0.16, a little below, as expected for a conditional mutual information in four dimensions. For this system, the linear estimator is both exact and much more efficient.

The second system is nonlinear, a standard pair of Hénon maps a classic chaotic system where X drives Y through a product of the two signals. We vary the coupling strength from 0 to 0.6, 10,000 samples each, with a tiny measurement noise labelled simulated.

Granger's measure, with two past samples of each signal, barely moves. From 0 at no coupling, it reaches 0.06 at the strongest coupling. Half of that 0.03 nats is what the linear inspector hears.

Transfer entropy estimated with nearest neighbours tells a different story. At a coupling of 0.2, it is already 0.44 nats. At 0.4, 0.73. At 0.6, 0.81. Most of the coupling lives in the nonlinear product, where a straight line cannot reach. Here is why. The influence of X on Y is multiplied by Y itself. When Y is near zero, X has almost no effect. When Y is large, X has a strong effect, positive or negative, depending on the sign of Y. Averaged over everything, the best straight line through that relation is nearly flat. The information is all in the interaction, and a linear prediction of Y from X's past added to Y's past captures only a sliver of it.

And in the reverse direction from Y to X where there is no coupling, the transfer entropy estimate sits slightly below zero, around minus 0.03. At zero coupling, both directions give about minus 0.04. Those are not real negative informations. They are the bias of the nearest neighbour estimator on these smooth, nearly deterministic signals, the problem episode 5.8 warned about. We report them as they are and we compare against surrogates rather than truncating them at zero.

So the picture is clear. Transfer entropy brings something that Granger does not only when the dependence is nonlinear or non-Gaussian. When the data are close to Gaussian, Granger causality gives the same answer faster with less data and with well-known statistics.

That is the strong point of today and it cuts both ways. Do not use transfer entropy because it sounds more general. Use it when there is a reason to expect nonlinear dependence and check that it actually finds more than Granger.

What is the comparison sensitive to?

First how Gaussian the data really are. Brain signals after filtering are often close to Gaussian in their amplitude distribution, but their dependence can still be non-linear, for example through amplitude coupling between frequencies. Looking at the histograms is not enough. Comparing Granger and transfer entropy on the same data with the same history lengths is a direct test.

Second, the amount of data. Linear estimators need few samples. Nearest neighbour estimators of transfer entropy need many more, especially as the histories grow. With short recordings, a nonlinear estimator may find less than Granger, simply because it is noisier.

Third, the units. Granger's log ratio and transfer entropy differ by a factor of 2 in the Gaussian case. A paper that compares a Granger value with a transfer entropy value must state which is which, and in which log base. Some software reports Granger causality in other forms, such as F-statistics, which are not information at all.

And fourth, the history length, the delay, and the surrogates. Everything from the previous episodes applies to both methods equally. A fair comparison uses the same history, the same delay, and the same kind of surrogates for both. In particular, a linear model with 10 past samples and a nearest neighbour estimator with two are not answering the same question, even on the same recording.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study reports that transfer entropy revealed directed connectivity that Granger causality missed, and presents it as a nonlinear effect. But the two analyses used different history lengths, or different preprocessing, or different numbers of samples, or a different significance threshold. The difference may come from the pipeline, not from nonlinearity.

The fair test runs both methods on the same data, with the same embedding, the same surrogates, and the same correction for multiple comparisons. A good way to build that comparison is with the surrogates of episode 6.4 in their multivariate version from Prichard and Theiler, 1994. They keep the spectra of both signals and the cross-spectrum between them, which is everything a linear Gaussian model can see, and they destroy any non-linear structure. Then, if transfer entropy finds more, convert Granger's value to nats by dividing by two, and show that the gap is larger than the gap on Gaussian surrogates of the same data. Only then is nonlinear a conclusion rather than a label.

The opposite pitfall exists too, concluding that there is no directed dependence because Granger found none. Our Hénon maps had a strong coupling that Granger heard as a whisper.

For jointly Gaussian variables, transfer entropy in nats equals half of Granger's log ratio exactly. Barnett, Barrett, and Seth, 2009.

On our Gaussian system, Granger gave 0.38, so transfer entropy 0.19.

On coupled Hénon maps, Granger heard at most 0.03 nats of coupling, while transfer entropy found up to 0.81.

Non-uniform embedding builds the conditioning set one variable at a time, keeping dimension low, and delays should be reported in milliseconds.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Delays and embedding for transfer entropy. When did the letter leave?

Transfer entropy asks whether the past of a source adds information about the present of a target. But the past is vague. Which past samples of the source? Which past samples of the target? Today we make those choices explicit, because a wrong choice can make a real connection disappear.

Here is the intuition. At the pigeon loft, letters from station X do not arrive overnight. The route is long and the pigeons take six days.

Imagine the keeper at station Y checks only yesterday's letters for news about today. He finds nothing every time. Not because station X has nothing to say, but because what X wrote yesterday will only arrive next week. To hear X, the keeper must look at the postmark and match today with the letters posted six days ago.

That is the interaction delay. In the brain, signals take time to travel between regions. Axonal conduction, synapses, local processing. Tens of milliseconds are common between distant areas. At a sampling rate of 500 Hz, 20 ms is already 10 samples.

Standard transfer entropy with the source taken one sample in the past quietly assumes that the delay is one sample. If the true delay is longer and the source does not remember its own past for that long, the transfer entropy at one sample can be almost zero.

In 2013, Michael Wibral and colleagues proposed a simple fix. Add a delay parameter u and take the source at u samples in the past. Keep the target's own past conditioned at the step just before the present, as in standard transfer entropy. Then scan u.

That second instruction matters. It is tempting to shift the target's past along with the source, but then the target's most recent memory is no longer in the conditioning, and the source gets credit for what the target already knew. Wibral and colleagues call their choice self-prediction optimality. The target always predicts itself as well as it can, from its own most recent past, and the source must add something beyond that, whatever the delay.

They proved that for two coupled systems with a single interaction delay, this delayed transfer entropy reaches its maximum exactly at the true delay. Jakob Runge, in a published comment, stressed that this proof covers two coupled systems only, and that larger networks with indirect paths and shared drivers need a multivariate approach. Scanning works best when there is one dominant delay.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate a source X with a short memory and a target Y that depends on its own past at 1 and 3 samples and on X 6 samples earlier. 20,000 samples labelled simulated. Every value is in nats with a Gaussian estimator conditioning on 3 past samples of the target.

At a delay of one sample, the transfer entropy from X to Y is 0.0001 nats. For all practical purposes, nothing. A study that stops here concludes that X does not inform Y.

Now scan the delay. At 3 samples, 0.002. At 5, 0.029. At 6, 0.14. At 7, back down to 0.026. By 10, 0 again.

The curve has a sharp peak at 6 samples, the true delay. The small shoulders at 5 and 7 come from the source's own memory. X at 5 or 7 samples ago still carries a little of X at 6.

A nearest neighbour estimate on 5,000 samples agrees. 0.13 nats at the true delay and minus 0.002 at one sample, which is 0 plus the estimator's noise.

So a wrong delay can make a real connection invisible. Not weaker, invisible, at a value indistinguishable from zero. That is the strong point of today.

The second choice is the embedding, which past samples of the target and possibly several past samples of the source go into the conditioning.

The simplest option is uniform embedding. Take the last k samples of the target evenly spaced and the last l samples of the source. If we wanted to be safe and took 12 lags of each, plus the present, the estimator would work in 25 dimensions. No nearest neighbour estimator survives that with realistic data.

The alternative is non-uniform embedding, introduced for this purpose by Luca Faes and colleagues in 2011, and implemented in the IDTxl toolbox. Instead of taking every lag, you build the conditioning set one variable at a time.

Start with an empty set. Among all candidate past samples of the target and of the source, pick the one that adds the most information about the present of the target, given what is already selected. Test whether that gain is significant. If it is, keep it and repeat. If not, stop.

On our simulation, with 12 candidate lags for each signal, the first pick is the target 1 sample back, with a gain of 0.25 nats. The second pick is the source 6 samples back with 0.11 nats, the true delay found without scanning by hand. The third is the target 3 samples back, with 0.08 nats, the second piece of the target's own memory. The fourth candidate, the source 11 samples back, adds almost nothing, and the test stops the procedure there.

Three variables instead of 24. The selection recovered exactly the structure we built in, and it kept the estimator in 4 dimensions instead of 25.

In IDTxl, the selection uses surrogates at each step rather than the simple test we used here, and it separates the target's own past from the source's past, so that the final transfer entropy is computed with a target history chosen first. The logic is the same.

What are delays and embedding sensitive to?

First, the sampling rate. Delays are measured in samples, but they mean milliseconds. A delay of 6 samples at 250 Hz is 24 ms. At 1000 Hz, the same delay is 24 samples. Always report delays in time units and scan a range wide enough to contain plausible conduction times.

Second, filtering. Filters smear and shift timing. A zero-phase filter spreads each sample into its neighbours on both sides, which can broaden or shift the peak of a delay scan. A causal filter adds its own delay.

Third, the candidate range. Non-uniform embedding can only find lags you allowed. If the true delay is longer than the longest candidate, it is missed, and the source may look silent.

Fourth, the stopping rule. A loose threshold keeps noise variables and inflates the dimension. A strict one drops weak but real contributions. With many candidates, the threshold should account for the number of candidates tested at each step.

And fifth, multiple delays. If a source acts through two paths with different delays, a single peak can hide one of them. Non-uniform embedding can select several source lags, which handles this case more gracefully.

Now the classic pitfall.

A connectivity study computes transfer entropy between all pairs of sensors with the default settings of a toolbox. One past sample of the source, a few past samples of the target, and no delay scan. It reports that some pairs show no directed dependence.

But no transfer entropy at one sample is not no directed dependence. With conduction delays of tens of milliseconds and a sampling rate of a few hundred hertz, the relevant source lag can be 10 samples or more. The absence may only mean that the keeper read yesterday's mail.

The fix is to scan delays over a physiologically plausible range, or to let a non-uniform embedding choose source lags from that range, and to report the delays found in milliseconds along with the transfer entropy values. Showing the whole delay scan rather than only its maximum lets a reader see whether the peak is sharp, broad, or double.

Delayed transfer entropy, scanned over u, peaks at the true delay for a single coupling delay, as Wibral and colleagues proved. In our simulation, 0.14 nats at six samples and zero at one sample.

Directed information. The same idea with one extra term and that term is a trap.

Transfer entropy looks at the past of the source. In information theory, there is an older quantity that also looks at the present of the source. It is called directed information and it comes from the study of communication channels with feedback.

Here is the intuition. Two pigeon lofts stand side by side on the same roof. Every morning both keepers write down the weather.

Some of what loft Y writes can be predicted from what loft X wrote on previous days. That is the transfer we have been measuring.

But some of it simply matches what loft X writes on the same morning. Not because one keeper copies the other, but because they look at the same sky at the same moment. That shared present is not a message carried by a pigeon. Nothing travelled between the lofts, the sky reached both at once.

Directed information counts both. That is its strength in communication theory and its weakness on a scalp covered with sensors.

James Massey defined directed information in 1990 for channels with feedback. In words, the directed information from X to Y sums over time, the information that the source up to now, including its present, gives about the target's present, given the target's past.

Pierre-Olivier Amblard and Olivier Michel, in 2011, made the link with transfer entropy explicit. For stationary processes, the rate of directed information splits into two parts. The first is the transfer entropy, what the source's past adds. The second is an instantaneous term, the information shared by the two presents given both pasts.

That instantaneous term is symmetric. It is the same from x to y as from y to x. It has no arrow. It measures how much the two signals move together within the same sample, once everything in their past is accounted for.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate two sources labelled simulated. Source 1 drives source 2 with a delay of two samples. 40,000 samples. We compute everything with a Gaussian estimator and five past samples in nats.

On the sources themselves, the transfer entropy from 1 to 2 is 0.12 nats, and from 2 to 1, 0. The instantaneous term is 0 because nothing connects them within a sample. Directed information equals transfer entropy 0.12 forward 0 backward.

Now we do what the head does to brain sources. We record them through two sensors, and each sensor picks up a little of the other source. 20% in the first case, 40% in the second. That is volume conduction mixing at zero lag.

Symbolic transfer entropy and the many meanings of normalized.

Transfer entropy on continuous signals needs density estimators in several dimensions, with all the biases of chapter 5. One popular shortcut is to turn each signal into a short alphabet of shapes first. Today we see what that shortcut keeps, what it throws away, and why the word normalized needs a footnote every time.

Here is the intuition. At the pigeon loft, the letters have become too long to carry, so the stations agree on a code. Instead of writing the full weather readings, each station sends a small stamp that only says the shape of the last three readings. Rising, falling, peaked, dipped, or one of the in-between shapes.

With three readings, there are exactly six possible shapes. Six stamps. Light to carry, easy to count, and impossible to misread because of a miscalibrated barometer. Whatever the units, a rise is a rise.

But a stamp cannot say how much. A gentle rise and a storm surge get the same stamp.

These stamps are the ordinal patterns of Bandt and Pompe from episode 5.10. Matthäus Staniek and Klaus Lehnertz in 2008 computed transfer entropy on these symbol sequences instead of on the raw values and called it symbolic transfer entropy.

The recipe is simple. Replace each window of m samples in both signals by the rank order of its values. With m equal to 3, each signal becomes a sequence over 6 symbols. Then compute ordinary transfer entropy on the symbols by counting. Does the source's current pattern help predict the target's pattern a few steps ahead, beyond the target's current pattern?

Counting 6 symbols or 36 pairs or 216 triples is easy with a few thousand samples. No bandwidths, no neighbours.

Now the calculation in one picture. Two simulations labelled simulated 40,000 samples each, patterns of three samples and a prediction step of three samples. Values in bits.

Symbolic transfer entropy from X to Y is 0.17 bits. From Y to X, 0.007 bits. When we shuffle the source's symbols which destroys any link, the value is about 0.003 bits. That is the bias of counting on a finite sample.

Now we apply a monotone transformation to each signal, an exponential to X, a cube to Y. The raw values change completely. The symbolic transfer entropy does not move 0.17 bits to the fourth decimal. Rank orders are untouched by any increasing transformation, so the symbols are identical. That is the robustness the stamps promised. It is one reason symbolic measures are popular for EEG recorded with different amplifiers, gains, or references. The units drop out before anything is computed.

Symbolic transfer entropy from X to Y is 0.0028 bits. Shuffled, also 0.0028. Nothing. The stamp simply cannot carry it because a big wave and a small wave of the same shape get the same stamp.

So symbolic transfer entropy is robust to amplitude and blind to amplitude. Same property two sides.

Now the normalized versions and the trouble with the name.

UnCheol Lee, George Mashour and colleagues used a normalized symbolic transfer entropy, abbreviated NSTE, to study frontal parietal connectivity under anaesthesia, notably in a 2013 study of ketamine, propofol, and sevoflurane. Their definition subtracts the symbolic transfer entropy obtained with a shuffled source and divides the result by the entropy of the target's future pattern given its own past pattern. The result is a fraction, the share of the target's remaining uncertainty that the source explains. Because a shuffled value is subtracted, an estimate can come out slightly below zero when there is no link at all. The paper describes the measure as lying between zero and one. That holds for the quantity being estimated, not always for every estimate.

Other papers use other definitions under similar names. Some divide transfer entropy by the entropy of the target's future, without conditioning. Some only subtract the shuffled value, which keeps the result in bits. Marschinski and Kantz called that the effective transfer entropy in 2002. Some normalize by the maximum possible value, or by the sum of both directions.

On our first simulation, the same analysis gives 0.17 bits as raw symbolic transfer entropy, 0.17 bits as effective transfer entropy, and 0.067 as the Lee normalization, which is a fraction, not bits. All three are correct. They are simply not the same quantity, and they cannot be compared across papers without knowing which was used.

That is the strong point of today, read the method not the acronym.

What is symbolic transfer entropy sensitive to?

First, the pattern length and the delay between the samples of a pattern. Short patterns lose detail, long ones explode the alphabet since m samples give m factorial patterns. With m equal to 5, that is 120 symbols and over a million triples to count.

Second, the prediction step. How far ahead the target's pattern is taken matters as much as the interaction delay in episode 8.4. Lee and colleagues chose it from the lag of maximum cross-correlation.

Third, ties and flat signals. Rank orders are undefined when values repeat, which happens with quantized or clipped data. The tie rule must be stated.

Fourth, the amplitude in both directions, robust to it, blind to it. If the coupling of interest lives in amplitude, symbolic measures will miss it.

And fifth, the bias. Counting is simple but not unbiased. The shuffled value is never zero, and it depends on the number of samples. That is the reason for subtracting it, but the subtraction itself needs enough shuffles to be stable.

Information flows? Why predictive dependence is not transfer.

Transfer entropy is often described as information flowing from one signal to another. The picture is attractive, a pipe, an arrow, bits travelling. Today we test that picture on two small systems where we know everything, and it breaks twice.

Here is the intuition. At the pigeon loft, station Y keeps a torn half of a secret card in its drawer. Every day, the pigeon from station X brings the other torn half. Neither half means anything alone. Put together, they spell out exactly what station Y does tomorrow.

Did the letter from X carry the message? In one sense, yes. Without it, station Y could not know tomorrow. In another sense, no. The letter alone is noise. The message lives only in the combination of the letter and the drawer.

Transfer entropy credits all of it to the letter.

Here is the first system in bits computed exactly. Station X sends a fair random bit every day. Station Y's state tomorrow is the exclusive or of X's bit today and Y's own state today. If they agree tomorrow is zero, if they differ tomorrow is one.

Transfer entropy from X to Y is one full bit. Knowing Y's past, the letter from X tells you exactly what happens next.

But now compute the plain lagged mutual information between X's bit today and Y's state tomorrow, without conditioning on Y's past. It is zero. X's bit alone says nothing about Y's future. And Y's own past alone says nothing either. The active storage is zero.

Compare with a second system where station Y simply copies X's bit. Transfer entropy is again one bit. Lagged mutual information is also one bit. Here the letter carries the message on its own.

Two systems, the same transfer entropy of one bit, and completely different dynamics. A study that only reported transfer entropy would describe them identically, as one bit of information transferred from X to Y.

This is the critique published in 2016 by Ryan James, Nix Barnett, and James Crutchfield, under the title Information flows? A critique of transfer entropies. Transfer entropy, they showed, mixes two kinds of information. One part is unique to the source, what the source says on its own, as in the copy system. The other part is synergistic, what only the source and the target's past say together, as in the exclusive or system. Transfer entropy adds them up and cannot tell them apart.

We can make that exact with the partial information decomposition from episode 9.6. Decompose what the source's present and the target's present tell about the target's future. For the copy system, one bit is unique to the source, and there is no synergy. For the exclusive or system, there is nothing unique and nothing redundant, one full bit of synergy. In both cases, transfer entropy equals the unique part of the source plus the synergy. James and colleagues also built examples where this mixing makes transfer entropy point to the wrong source altogether.

So information flows from X to Y is a stronger claim than transfer entropy supports. The safer sentence is the one from episode 8.1. The past of X adds predictive information about Y beyond Y's past. Whether that information was carried by X alone or created by X meeting Y, transfer entropy does not say. The integrated information decomposition of episodes 9.11 and 9.12 was built in part to separate these modes.

Is this a curiosity of binary toys? Not really. Whenever the effect of one region depends on the current state of another, the information is partly synergistic. A modulatory input that opens a gate only when the target is already excited, an input whose sign depends on the target's phase, a burst that matters only in a particular state. All of these produce transfer entropy that is not carried by the source alone. In the brain, state-dependent effects are the rule rather than the exception.

Now the second break in the opposite direction.

Back at the loft, imagine that station X sends the same letter almost every day, 999 days out of a thousand. Station Y does exactly what the letter says.

If you intercepted the pigeon and swapped the letter, station Y would change its behaviour every single time. The causal effect of X on Y is total, but the transfer entropy from X to Y is only 0.011 bits. Because the letter almost never changes, it almost never brings news. Predictive information is about surprise, and a perfectly reliable but predictable cause produces almost none.

This distinction was made explicit by Joseph Lizier and Mikhail Prokopenko in 2010 in a paper called Differentiating Information Transfer and Causal Effect. Causal effect asks what happens if we intervene on the source. Information transfer asks how much the source's past helps predict the target in the system's normal behavior. A strong causal link can carry little information, and as we saw in episode 8.1, a large information transfer can exist without any causal link, through a hidden common driver.

So transfer entropy is neither a measure of flow nor a measure of causal strength. It is a measure of predictive dependence, with all the precision and all the limits of that phrase.

What does this depend on?

First the target's past. The synergy between source and target past is counted as transfer only because the target's past is conditioned on. Change the history length and the balance between unique and synergistic parts can shift.

Second, the regime of the system. A link that carries little information in one state because the source is predictable can carry a lot in another state when the source starts to vary. Transfer entropy measures use of a link, not the link.

Third, the predictability of the source. As the frozen station showed, a source that rarely changes produces little transfer entropy, whatever the strength of its influence. Comparing transfer entropy between conditions where the source varies more or less is comparing the source's variability as much as the link.

Fourth, the decomposition chosen. To separate unique and synergistic parts, a redundancy measure must be chosen, and the zoo of chapter 9 gives different answers on some systems. Our two examples are simple enough that the measures agree.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study reports that transfer entropy from region A to region B increased during a task, and concludes that region A sends more information to region B, or that the connection from A to B became stronger.

Neither follows. The increase could come from more synergy between A and B's own past, from A becoming less predictable while the connection stayed the same, or from a common input. A stronger connection might even reduce transfer entropy if it makes the target more predictable from its own past.

Each of these explanations predicts the same increase. Telling them apart takes more than one number. The source's own entropy in each condition, the lagged mutual information without conditioning, and ideally, a decomposition. The safe report is descriptive. The past of A became more informative about B's next state beyond B's own past in this condition. Mechanistic words such as sends, drives, or flows require interventions, models, or at least the decompositions of chapter 9.

In our examples, copy and exclusive or both gave one bit of transfer entropy, one bit unique in the first, one bit synergistic in the second.

Beyond pairs of signals. Multivariate transfer entropy and network inference.

So far, transfer entropy looked at two signals at a time. Brains have many. Today we see what goes wrong when a network is built from pairs, how conditioning on the other nodes fixes it and what that fix costs.

Here is the intuition. Five pigeon stations form a small network. Station A sends letters to station B. Station B, two days later, relays the news to station C. And a fourth station, D, sends letters both to A the next day and to a fifth station, E, three days later.

Now an inspector checks every pair of stations separately, asking only, does the past of one help predict the other?

A and C, yes of course. A's news reaches C through B. But there is no pigeon route from A to C. The inspector draws a link that does not exist. That is the relay.

A and E yes again. Both hear from D, A after one day and E after three. So A's past announces what E is about to receive. There is no route from A to E either. That is the common driver, the church bell of episode 8.1.

Checked pair by pair, a network of four routes can look like a network of eight or ten. An inspector who reads all five logbooks at once can do better.

The fix is to ask a better question. Not does A's past help predict C, but does A's past help predict C beyond C's own past and beyond the past of every other station? Once B's past is known, A adds nothing about C. Once D's past is known, A adds nothing about E. This is multivariate or conditional transfer entropy.

Now the calculation in one picture.

First, bivariate transfer entropy, each pair separately, conditioning only on the target's own past, with a correction for the 20 pairs tested.

All four true links are found every time, but so are false ones. With 300 samples, about three false links per network on average. With 1,000, about 6. With 10,000, about 7.

Notice the direction of that trend. More data makes it worse. The false links are not noise, they are real predictive dependencies created by the relay and the common driver. With more data, they simply become more significant.

Second, multivariate transfer entropy inferred the way the IDTxl toolbox does it, which Leonardo Novelli and colleagues described in 2019. For each target station, start with its own past. Then, among all past samples of all other stations, add the one that brings the most new information about the target, given everything already selected. Test it. Keep it if it passes and repeat. Stop when nothing passes. Then prune any selected variable that has become redundant, given the others selected after it, is tested again and removed if it no longer passes. In IDTxl, the target's own past is selected the same way before the sources so that its length does not have to be guessed.

The result, all four true links every time and almost no false ones, about one false link in every 10 or 20 networks at all three lengths. The relay link from A to C disappears because B's past is selected first. The link from A to E disappears because D's past is selected first. The few remaining false links in the longest recordings were reverse links such as E to D. The price of a test that is run many times on a network where every node has a long memory.

We also made one link weak to see whether the extra conditioning costs detection power. With a Gaussian estimator, it does not. With 150 samples, the weak link was found in 73% of networks by the multivariate method, against 40% by the bivariate one. Here conditioning helped. Conditioning on the right variables explains away part of the target's variability, which leaves less noise around a weak real link and makes it easier to see.

But that is because a Gaussian estimator handles extra dimensions cheaply. Nearest neighbour estimators do not.

Here is the cost measured. Take two variables with a true conditional mutual information of 0.2 nats, a thousand samples, and add conditioning variables that are pure noise, irrelevant to both. A nearest neighbour estimator gives 0.20 with one irrelevant variable, 0.17 with 4, 0.13 with 8, and 0.09 with 12. More than half of a real dependence vanishes under the weight of conditioning variables that should change nothing.

That is the strong point of today. Every conditioning variable costs data. Network inference is a compromise between two errors, false links from conditioning on too little and missed links from conditioning on too much with too little data.

The greedy approach is designed for that compromise. It does not condition on everything. It conditions only on what passes a test so the conditioning set stays small. That is what makes networks of dozens of nodes feasible with nonlinear estimators. Novelli and colleagues also use a hierarchy of statistical tests to control false positives across the whole network rather than link by link.

What is network inference sensitive to?

First, what is not measured? Multivariate transfer entropy can only condition on recorded signals. A hidden common driver, such as a deep source that no sensor sees, still creates false links. The method removes the false links it can explain, not the ones it cannot see. Recording more channels helps only if the hidden driver leaves a trace in them.

Second, the candidate set. Delays and lags not included as candidates cannot be selected. The maximum lag must cover plausible conduction times, as in episode 8.4.

Third, the estimator and the amount of data. As we just saw, nearest neighbour estimators lose sensitivity as the conditioning set grows. Short recordings with many nodes push the method towards missed links.

For the statistics, with many targets, candidates, and steps, the number of tests is large. Thresholds must be corrected or controlled through surrogates at each step, as in IDTxl, and the network-level false positive rate should be reported.

And fifth, volume conduction. On sensor level EEG, mixing creates instantaneous dependence, and as we saw in episode 8.5, distorts lagged ones. Conditioning on other sensors does not remove mixing. Source reconstruction first, then network inference is the safer order.

And sixth, stationarity. One network for a whole recording assumes that the links do not change during it. If they switch on and off, the inferred network is an average of several networks, and may match none of them. Time-resolved networks are the subject of chapter 11.

Local and specific transfer entropy. When and in which state.

Transfer entropy is an average. It tells you how much, on average, the past of a source improves the prediction of a target's next value. But coupling in real systems is rarely uniform. A brain region may drive another only in some states or only at some moments of a task. Today, two ways to unpack the average. Local transfer entropy one value per time step and specific transfer entropy one value per state. They look alike. They answer different questions, and only one of them can be trusted when the coupling is absent.

Here is the intuition. A carrier pigeon brings a message from the source to the target's loft every day. Two questions can be asked about one delivery. After reading it, did this particular message, compared with what the target would have guessed anyway, turn out to help or mislead about what happened next? That is local transfer entropy, a verdict after the fact. Before opening it, given the situation today, how much is a message from this sender expected to help? That is specific transfer entropy, a forecast made in advance from the state alone. The pigeon is the same in both cases. The questions are not, and neither are the answers.

Local transfer entropy was introduced by Joseph Lizier, Mikhail Prokopenko and Albert Zomaya in 2008. At each time step, it is the logarithm of a ratio, the probability of the target's actual next value given both pasts, divided by its probability given the target's own past only. Its average over time is the transfer entropy. Each local value can be positive, when the source made the actual outcome more likely or negative when the source made it less likely. Lizier and colleagues called the source misleading in that case.

Specific transfer entropy was introduced by David Darmon and Paul Rapp in 2017. Take the decomposition of the transfer entropy into an average over the current pasts of the target and the source and keep the inner part. For each pair of pasts, the expected local transfer entropy averaged over all the next values that could follow. That inner part is a Kullback-Leibler divergence between the prediction with the source and the prediction without it. So it is never negative and it depends only on the state, not on the outcome. Its average over states is, again, the transfer entropy.

Transfer entropy pitfalls in EEG. Six ways to find an arrow that is not there.

Everything in this chapter applies to any pair of signals. But scalp EEG adds its own traps because of how the signals are recorded. Today we go through six of them and simulate the three that surprise people most.

Here is the intuition. Two keepers at the pigeon loft watch the same sky, at the same moment, through two different windows. One window is clean. The other is fogged.

The keeper behind the fogged window writes a blurry record. If you try to predict tomorrow's blurry entry, the clean keeper's record helps a lot, more than the blurry keeper's own past, because the clean record says more precisely what the sky was doing. So the clean keeper appears to inform the fogged one. No letter ever travelled between them. They just see the same sky with different clarity. Clean the fogged window and the arrow disappears, swap the windows and it reverses.

That is the first trap and the strong point of today, unequal signal-to-noise ratios create a direction.

Let us simulate it. One source, a 10 Hz rhythm, labelled simulated. Two electrodes record it at zero lag, each with its own sensor noise. There is no interaction at all, a single source seen twice.

A symmetric, spurious dependence because each electrode's past is a noisy view of the same source, and the other electrode's past helps clean it up. The mechanism was analysed for Granger causality under measurement noise, for example by Nalatore, Ding, and Rangarajan in 2007, and Martin Vinck and colleagues showed in 2015 that linearly mixed noise, as produced by volume conduction, causes many false positives for standard-directed measures. On a scalp, electrodes over a strong source or with better contact have a better signal-to-noise ratio than others. They will tend to look like drivers.

The second trap is volume conduction itself, which we met in episode 8.5. A single source spreads instantly to many electrodes. That creates zero lag dependence, inflates the instantaneous term of directed information, and distorts lagged measures through the mechanism we just saw.

The third trap is the reference. Every EEG channel is a difference between an electrode and a reference. If the reference picks up its own activity, that activity is subtracted from every channel and becomes a shared signal.

We simulate two independent sources with no link. Raw, the transfer entropy between them, is about 0.0001 nats in both directions. Now we re-reference both to an active electrode that carries a third, independent rhythm. The transfer entropy rises to about 0.006 nats in both directions, dozens of times larger from nothing but the choice of reference. Small but with tens of thousands of samples, easily significant. Changing to an average reference or to a reference far from active tissue changes the shared signal but rarely removes it. The reference is part of the measurement, and it belongs in the methods section next to the transfer entropy.

The fourth trap is a common cause, a deep source, a stimulus, or a physiological rhythm such as breathing or the heartbeat that reaches several electrodes with different delays. As in episodes 8.1 and 8.8, the electrode reached first appears to drive the others.

The fifth trap is filtering, covered in episode 6.8. Zero-phase filters smear the future into the present and causal filters add delays. Either can create, hide, or flip an arrow.

Transfer entropies at different sampling rates are not comparable, and coarse sampling moves real influences into the instantaneous, directionless part.

What do these traps have in common? They all create predictive dependence that has nothing to do with communication between brain regions. And they are not rare edge cases. Every scalp recording has unequal signal quality, volume conduction, a reference, shared physiological inputs, filters, and a sampling rate.

So what can be done?

First, move from sensors to sources whenever possible with a source reconstruction method, and remember that reconstruction leaves some mixing behind.

Second, test against surrogates that keep each signal's own properties and against time reversed data. Haufe and colleagues and Vinck and colleagues showed that comparing the measure on original and time-reversed signals helps reject arrows created by mixing and noise.

Third, compare the directed measure with its instantaneous counterpart. If most of the dependence is at zero lag, mixing is the likely story.

Fourth, report signal quality per channel. A directed effect that always points from good channels to bad ones is suspicious.

And fifth, simulate. Build a model with your montage, your reference, your noise levels, and no interactions, and run your exact pipeline on it. Whatever arrows it finds are your floor.

There are also measures designed to ignore zero lag mixing, such as the phase slope index of Guido Nolte and colleagues from 2008. They help, but they are not a cure. Vinck and colleagues found model ensembles where the phase slope index also misreads the direction, even without noise. No single index replaces the checks above.

The fix is to check and report signal quality per region and per group and to show that the directed effect survives when signal quality is matched or controlled.

Information transfer across frequencies. Which rhythm sends and which rhythm receives.

Brain signals are made of rhythms, theta, alpha, beta, gamma, and more. It is natural to ask not only whether one region informs another, but at which frequency. Today we see why the obvious way to ask, filtering first, misleads and what the alternative looks like.

Here is the intuition. Next to the pigeon loft stands station X's bell tower. It has two bells, a big slow bell and a small fast one. Both ring all day mixed together. Station Y across the valley listens.

We know that station Y's activity follows something from station X. The question is which bell?

The obvious approach is to put a filter on both ears. Listen only to slow sounds at X and only to slow sounds at Y, then only to fast sounds at both and compare. That is what most early studies did. Band pass filter both signals into the same band, then compute transfer entropy.

There are two problems with that. First, filtering does things to time. As episode 6.8 showed, zero-phase filters smear the future into the present, and narrow filters stretch every signal's memory. Second, and more fundamentally, a rhythm in one region does not have to be received by the same rhythm in another. Nonlinear systems routinely transform frequencies. A slow bell can make the fast bell across the valley ring louder.

The alternative is to leave the signals untouched and to ask a different question. If I scramble only the slow bell of station X keeping everything else, does station Y become less predictable from X? If yes the slow bell was sending, Edoardo Pinzuti, Patricia Wollstadt, Michael Wibral and colleagues built a method on this idea in 2020. They use an invertible wavelet transform to create surrogate data in which only one frequency band of the source has been altered, and compare transfer entropy on the original data with transfer entropy on those surrogates. The original signals are never filtered.

Now the calculation in one picture.

Our circular shift of a filtered component is the simplest possible version. The published method alters wavelet coefficients, which keeps more of each band's own structure.

The sending frequency is identified without ever filtering the signals that go into the transfer entropy. That is the strong point of today.

Pinzuti and colleagues apply the same logic on the target side to find which frequencies receive. In their human magnetoencephalography data from temporal cortex, they report information sent from very high frequencies, above 100 Hz, and received both at similarly high frequencies and around 20 Hz. In ferrets, they report prefrontal cortex sending at 4 to 8 Hz to primary visual cortex, which receives above 125 Hz. A transfer from one rhythm to a very different one is exactly what a within-band analysis would miss entirely.

They also point out a deep difficulty. When several frequencies of the source carry the same information, scrambling any one of them may change nothing because the others still carry it. When they carry it only together, scrambling either one destroys it. Frequency-resolved transfer is a partial information decomposition problem in the sense of chapter 9, and it is not fully solved.

What is frequency-resolved transfer sensitive to?

First, the band definitions. A surrogate band that is too wide mixes rhythms, one that is too narrow leaves the sending rhythm partly intact.

Second, redundancy and synergy between bands, as just described. A null result for one band does not mean that band carries nothing.

Third, everything that affects transfer entropy, history length, delays, the estimator, and the number of surrogates.

And fourth, the frequency resolution of the data. Very slow rhythms need long recordings to contain enough cycles for an estimate.
