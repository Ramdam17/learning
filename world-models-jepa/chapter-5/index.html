PLATE 5.13:54

Supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised

The schoolyard has three corners. Supervised learning has a teacher sticking price tags on every picture; unsupervised learning, like children sorting marbles, finds structure without answers; self-supervised learning hides a piece of the data and predicts it, so the data holds its own answer. Language models and masked autoencoders rebuild what was hidden, whereas JEPA predicts a necklace describing it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the schoolyard. A model learns by guessing, and then something has to tell it how wrong it was. So today's question is simple: who gives the answers? The schoolyard has three corners, and each one is a way of learning. By the end of this video, you will know which corner JEPA plays in.

In the first corner stands a teacher, with a pile of pictures and a roll of price tags. For every picture, she writes the right answer on a tag and sticks it on: cat, dog, cat, bird. The model looks at a picture, makes a guess, and checks the tag. Guess, compare, adjust: it is the loop from chapter one, and the tag is what the loss is measured against. This is supervised learning. The answers come from a person.

It works very well, and it has a price. Someone had to write every single tag. ImageNet, the most famous example, was labelled with the help of crowd workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk. The challenge version has about one point two million training pictures, in a thousand classes. The full collection held over fourteen million labelled images in August twenty fourteen. Every one of those tags was a human decision. The next episode is all about that bill.

In the second corner, there is no teacher and there are no tags. A group of children is handed a bag of marbles, and no instructions. They start sorting anyway: small ones here, big ones there, the red ones together. Nobody said what the piles should be. This is unsupervised learning. The model looks for structure in the data alone: groups, directions, a shorter way to describe the whole bag.

If you followed the EEG series, you have met this corner already. Independent component analysis, in episode 7.11, separates mixed signals into sources, without being told what the sources are. That is its strength, and also its limit. With no answer to check against, nothing tells you whether the piles you found are the piles you cared about.

In the third corner, the children play a guessing game. One child covers a word in a sentence with a paper flap: the cat sat on the, blank. Everyone guesses. Mat. Then the flap is lifted, and the hidden word is right there. Nobody wrote a tag. The sentence held its own answer. This is self-supervised learning.

Here is the whole trick. Take a piece of the data and hide it. Predict it from the rest. Then lift the flap and compare. The question is made out of the data, and so is the answer. Cover a word in a sentence, a patch of a picture, or the end of a video clip: the answer is always whatever was under the flap. The data is already there, so the supply of questions is nearly endless, and it costs nobody an afternoon.

You have already seen this game played at scale. Large language models are trained to predict the next word. GPT, in twenty eighteen, was an early example. BERT, the same year, hid fifteen percent of the words in each sequence and learned to fill them in. For images, the masked autoencoder, in twenty twenty-one, hides seventy-five percent of the patches of a picture, and learns to paint back the missing pixels.

Now look at what these models predict: words, and pixels. They try to rebuild exactly what was under the flap. JEPA plays the same game with one big difference. It hides a part, but it does not rebuild that part. It predicts a necklace that describes it. Why that changes so much is the story of chapter eleven. For now, remember the game, and remember who lifts the flap.

To sum up. Supervised learning gets its answers from a teacher. Unsupervised learning has no answers, and looks for structure. Self-supervised learning builds its answers out of the data itself, by hiding a piece and predicting it. Next time: the price of labels, and what a tag really costs.

PLATE 5.26:02

The price of labels

Every label is a person's decision, and decisions cost time. ImageNet needed millions of crowd votes, a night of sleep takes one to two hours of expert scoring, and seizure EEG takes about an hour of work per recording hour. Experts also disagree, and LeCun's cake argues that most learning should come from data carrying its own answers.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the schoolyard. Last time we met TAG, the little price tag that gets stuck on a piece of data. Today we ask what TAG really costs. Behind every label there is a person who looked, decided, and wrote something down. That takes time, and time is the price. By the end of this video you will know where labels get expensive, and why some researchers want to need fewer of them.

Start with pictures. ImageNet was introduced in two thousand nine by Jia Deng and colleagues, at a computer vision conference. That first version held three point two million images, sorted into five thousand two hundred and forty-seven categories. Every candidate image was shown to people on Amazon Mechanical Turk, an online marketplace for small paid tasks, and they answered one question: is the thing really in this picture? One person is not enough, because people make mistakes. So several people vote on each image, and the hard categories need more votes than the easy ones.

How many people? A twenty nineteen paper that studied ImageNet reports over forty-nine thousand workers, from a hundred and sixty-seven countries. It cites a twenty seventeen talk by Fei-Fei Li and Jia Deng for that figure. Now some arithmetic. By August twenty fourteen, the dataset held fourteen million, one hundred ninety-seven thousand, one hundred and twenty-two annotated images. Suppose each image got only three votes. That is more than forty-two million human decisions. Three is our own illustrative number, since the real count changed from one category to the next.

Now a night of sleep. A sleep lab records brain waves, eye movements and muscle tone, all night long. To turn that into a sleep chart, an expert cuts the recording into thirty-second epochs, and gives each epoch one label: wake, N one, N two, N three, or REM. That is the convention of the manual of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine. Eight hours at thirty seconds each is nine hundred and sixty epochs. One night, nine hundred and sixty labels.

How long does that take? A benchmark study, published in twenty twenty-three, says a technologist spends about one to two hours scoring one recording. In that study, the measured average was seventy-one minutes. So take a thousand nights, as an illustration. At one to two hours each, that is a thousand to two thousand hours of expert time.

Seizures are harder still, because they are rare. The Temple University Hospital seizure corpus, from twenty seventeen, is the largest open collection of its kind. It holds about five hundred and four hours of EEG, and seizures fill only thirty-six of them, around seven percent. Its authors write that the expertise is in short supply, and that earlier attempts with panels of experts, or with crowdsourcing, were not productive. So they trained undergraduate students to mark when each seizure starts, when it stops, and what type it is. Their team reported, in twenty twenty, a rule of thumb: about one hour of human work for each hour of EEG. For the whole corpus, that is roughly five hundred hours of labor, and it does not count the files that were screened and set aside.

But the price is not only money and hours. Experts disagree. In twenty thirteen, the sleep society's reliability program compared more than twenty-five hundred scorers on the same epochs. On average, a scorer agreed with the majority eighty-two point six percent of the time. For stage N one, it was only sixty-three percent. For EEG, a twenty twenty-three study asked thirty experts to classify ten-second segments, seizures and look-alike patterns included. Two experts agreed with a kappa of zero point four two, which the authors call moderate. Even ImageNet's authors report workers who kept confusing a seal with a sea otter, or a banjo with a guitar.

So a label is not a fact about the data. It is one person's decision, at one moment, under one rulebook. And, as reasoning rather than measurement, a label can only name what the rulebook has a word for. A sleeper halfway between two stages still gets exactly one of them.

This is the cost that Yann LeCun put on a slide. In his December twenty sixteen keynote at NIPS, called Predictive Learning, he pictured intelligence as a cake. The bulk of the cake is unsupervised learning. The icing is supervised learning. The cherry on top is reinforcement learning.

In February twenty nineteen, at a chip-design conference, he redrew it with one word changed. The cake became self-supervised learning, and the cherry became pure reinforcement learning. His argument is about how much information each example gives the machine. On that slide, reinforcement learning gives a few bits, for some samples. Supervised learning gives ten to ten thousand bits per sample. Self-supervised learning offers millions of bits per sample.

This is a position, not a measurement. Nobody has weighed the layers. Some reinforcement learning researchers replied that their field deserves more than one cherry, and we will meet reinforcement learning properly in chapter nine. What the cake gives us is a question: if the data can write its own answers, how much of the bill can we avoid?

If you followed the EEG series, episode 4.8 drew the sleep chart, and the spindles that scorers look for in stage N two.

To sum up. A label costs a person's time: millions of votes for ImageNet, one to two hours for a night of sleep, about an hour for each hour of seizure EEG. Labels also carry disagreement, and one person's choices. LeCun's cake is a bet that most of learning should come from data that carries its own answers. Next time: pretext tasks.

PLATE 5.36:15

Pretext tasks

A pretext task is a game made from the data so the answer is free: predict an image's rotation, unshuffle jigsaw tiles, repaint colors from a gray photo, or fill masked words. The game decides what features must notice, and a game with a shortcut teaches the shortcut. The patch-position task was solved through chromatic aberration in the camera lens.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The schoolyard has a new thing today: a book as big as a desk, and it is a book of games. Last time, we saw what labels cost, because somebody has to write every one. So here is today's question. If the data must write its own answers, what game should it play? By the end of this video, you will know four such games, and the trap hidden inside them.

In this series, such a game has a name: a pretext task. It is a puzzle made out of the data itself, so the answer comes for free. Nobody cares how well the network plays. What we keep is the representation it builds along the way. The game is only a pretext, and the necklace is the prize.

Open the book to the first page. A photograph, turned. Is it upright, or turned by a quarter, a half, or three quarters? Gidaris and colleagues made this game in twenty eighteen. Every image is rotated by zero, ninety, one hundred eighty, or two hundred seventy degrees, and the network must say which. Four answers, so a blind guess is right one time in four. Their argument is that to tell which way is up, a network must find the objects, and know how they usually stand.

Second page: a jigsaw. In twenty sixteen, Noroozi and Favaro cut a picture into a three by three grid, shuffled the nine tiles, and asked for the shuffle. Nine tiles can be arranged in nine factorial ways: three hundred sixty-two thousand, eight hundred eighty. The network is not asked to choose among all of them. Their main set holds one thousand orderings, picked to be far apart. Why? Because two orderings that differ by a single swap are impossible to tell apart if the swapped tiles look alike. Their conclusion: a good self-supervised task is neither simple nor ambiguous.

Third page: a black-and-white photograph. Zhang, Isola and Efros, also in twenty sixteen, keep the lightness of a photo and hide its colors. The network must paint them back. The colors are the answer, and the photo already holds them. Many answers are plausible, since the balls on a lawn could be any color. So the network does not pick one color. It spreads its guess over three hundred thirteen color bins.

Fourth page: a sentence with holes. This is the guessing game from episode five point one. In twenty nineteen, Devlin and colleagues trained BERT by hiding fifteen percent of the tokens. Take an illustrative sentence of twenty tokens. Fifteen percent of twenty is three. Three blanks, and the model fills them in.

Now, the trap. The book has one more page, the page that started this family of games. In twenty fifteen, Doersch, Gupta and Efros cut two patches out of a picture: one in the middle, and one of its eight neighbors. The network must say where the second patch sits. Above, below, to a side, or in a corner. The authors hoped that to answer, it would have to recognize objects and their parts. But a network wants to win the game. It does not care what we hoped.

The authors saw it coming. Edges and textures running from one patch into the next would give the answer away. So they left a gap between the patches, about half a patch wide, and shifted each patch at random, by up to seven pixels. It was not enough. For some images, the network still won, and the authors were surprised. They traced it to chromatic aberration. A lens does not focus every color at exactly the same place. In some cameras, the green channel is shrunk a little toward the center of the picture. A network can detect the gap between green and magenta, and learn where in the picture a patch comes from. Once it knows where each patch sits relative to the lens, the relative position is easy. It never needs to look at an object.

The fix was to take the color away, in two ways. One shifts green and magenta toward gray. The other keeps one color channel at random, and fills the other two with noise. To check the diagnosis, they trained a network to guess where a patch came from in the image, with no neighbor at all. On some images, it did far better than chance. Noroozi and Favaro met the same shortcut, and used the same kind of cure: they jitter the color channels, use gray images, and leave gaps between the tiles.

So what is the lesson? A pretext task makes the label for free, but it does not make the lesson for free. The task decides which features pay off. When a game can be won with a low-level cue, like an edge or a color fringe, the network is likely to learn that cue. Choosing the game is choosing what the features must notice, and what they may ignore. You met this kind of choice in the episode on invariance and equivariance: every invariance is a choice. A pretext task is the same choice, made one step earlier. The rotation game, for instance, only works if the features keep the orientation, which an invariant feature would throw away.

There is a second limit, and it has nothing to do with the game. In twenty nineteen, Kolesnikov, Zhai and Beyer tested several of these methods on several network architectures. They found that the quality of the features depends strongly on the architecture, and that the ranking of the methods changed from one architecture to another. With the right network, even the old patch-position game of Doersch and colleagues beat every published self-supervised result of the time. So when we compare two games, the network is part of the answer.

Where does this lead? The authors of SimCLR, in episode seven point two, describe these hand-made games as relying on heuristics, which could limit how general the features are. Chapter seven picks up that thread. Instead of a puzzle, it asks for matching necklaces from two altered views of one picture. That is the start of contrastive methods, and of the joint-embedding family that JEPA belongs to.

To sum up. A pretext task is a game made from the data, so the answer is free: turn it, shuffle it, bleach it, or hide a word. The game decides what is learned, and a game with a shortcut teaches the shortcut. Doersch and colleagues found theirs in a camera lens. Next time: autoencoders.
