PLATE 3.15:42

Weak points: bridges and cut vertices

Harris and Ross's 1955 railway report asked where a network breaks most cheaply. A bridge is an edge on no loop whose removal disconnects the graph, and a cut vertex does the same for a node. In a ten-village valley, one road out of thirteen is a bridge, and it is the widest. Fragility comes from position, not strength.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter three. Cutting and flowing. We are standing in a pumping station, and we are not here for the pumps.

Pinned to the notice board is a report. It is dated the twenty-fourth of October, nineteen fifty-five. Its authors are Harris and Ross. It is a memorandum of the RAND Corporation, written for the United States Air Force. It was marked secret, and it stayed that way until nineteen ninety-nine.

Its subject is the railways between the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe. How many tons can this network carry? And the question the Air Force cared about most: where could it be cut most cheaply?

That does not look like a graph. It looks like a map of railways. The picture here is a small invented stand-in with nine railway districts, so it is marked illustrative.

Harris and Ross made one clever choice. A district becomes a vertex. A line between two neighbouring districts becomes an edge. Their real model had forty-four vertices and one hundred and five edges.

Now let the map melt away. And there is the hidden graph.

So where would you cut a graph? Start with the smallest cut of all, a single link. Remove it, and see whether the network falls apart. Today's question is simple. Where is a network weak?

Let us practise somewhere calmer. A mountain valley, after a storm. Ten villages, thirteen roads. This valley is invented.

The storm may close any road. Will every village still reach every other? Let us test them all, one by one. Close a road, and look.

Twelve times, nothing happens. Every village keeps a way to every other. But close this road, the six-lane motorway into the side valley, and three villages are cut off from the other seven.

An edge whose removal disconnects a graph has a name. It is called a bridge. You may also read isthmus, or cut edge. Do not confuse it with a bridge of stone. In graph words, a bridge is this picture: one edge, and a piece of the graph on each side that can no longer reach the other.

This valley has one bridge, out of thirteen roads. Why this road, and not the others?

Look at what the others sit on. Each one belongs to a loop. Close one road of a loop, and traffic simply goes round the other way. A bridge is an edge that lies on no loop at all.

So the footpath across the western loop, the thinnest road on the map, is not a bridge. And the motorway, the widest road on the map, is one.

That is the strong idea of this episode. Fragility comes from position, not from strength. A six-lane motorway can be a bridge. A footpath may not be one. You cannot read how robust a network is from the strength of its links. You read it from its shape. Chapter nine returns to this, when it compares random failures with targeted attacks.

Now close a village instead of a road. A landslide buries the pass village, E. Take away E, and its four roads go with it. The valley breaks in two. A loop of three villages on one side, six villages on the other.

A vertex whose removal disconnects a graph is called a cut vertex, or an articulation point.

Notice something odd. No single road at E is a bridge. E sits between two loops, and each of its roads belongs to a loop. Yet E is a weak point all the same. So a network has two kinds of weak points. A road, or a village.

Our valley has three cut vertices. The pass village E, and the two ends of the motorway.

How weak is the whole network? Count the roads. What is the smallest number of roads whose closing splits the graph? That number is called the edge connectivity. For our valley, it is one.

Now the council builds a second road into the side valley. Fourteen roads. The motorway is no longer a bridge, and the valley has no bridge left. Close any one road, and everyone still connects. Now the edge connectivity is two. It takes two closed roads to cut someone off.

Yet the pass village has not changed. Without E, the western loop is still cut off. Edge connectivity counts roads. It cannot see this weak point.

Back to Königsberg. Seven bridges of stone. How many are bridges in graph words? We removed them one at a time, on the computer. The four lands always stayed connected. None of them is a bridge. The island has two bridges to each bank and one to the eastern land, and the eastern land has one to each bank. Its edge connectivity is three. It takes three of the seven to cut off a land. The word is the same. The idea is not.

Now a real network. The marriages between fifteen families of fifteenth-century Florence. Twenty marriages. Five of them are bridges. Four families are cut vertices. Take away the Medici, and the network falls into three pieces, of eleven families, two families, and one. Chapter six comes back to this, and asks what it means for the Medici.

Back to the report on the notice board. In our schematic railway, there is no bridge at all. Cutting it needs two links, and there are four such cuts, one at each corner. A real cutter wants more than a count of links. A cut should be weighed by what its links can carry. The next episodes ask exactly that, and so did Harris and Ross.

To sum up. An edge on no loop is a bridge. Pull it out, and the graph falls in two. A cut vertex is a vertex that does the same. Edge connectivity is the smallest number of edges that must go. And weakness comes from position, not from strength.

Next time, we put water in the pipes. Pipes and flows.

PLATE 3.24:42

Pipes and flows

A sorting hall with belts of limited capacity shows how to find the largest flow from source to sink. Find a path with room, push what its tightest belt allows, and repeat; when stuck, send flow back along a belt. The example reaches seven parcels per minute. Conservation holds at every table, and the method fits water, trains and data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We are back in the pumping station. The secret report is still pinned to the board. Harris and Ross, 24 October 1955. How many tons could the railways carry? Their answer was a number. One hundred and sixty-three thousand tons, flowing from the western Soviet Union to Eastern Europe. Their capacities were appraised for twenty-four-hour periods. And their diagram points to a place they called the bottleneck.

Their method did not promise the best answer. They pushed trains greedily through the map. A method that promises the best flow came a few weeks later. Two mathematicians at RAND, Ford and Fulkerson, wrote it down in a report dated 29 December 1955. Years afterwards, they said that Harris and General Ross had posed the problem to them, in the spring of 1955. Today we learn that method, on a small example.

Forget the railways, and forget water. Picture a sorting hall. Parcels come in at the source, S. They leave at the sink, T. In between sit four tables, and belts carry parcels from table to table. Each belt runs one way, so it is an arrow. And each belt has a capacity: the most parcels per minute that it can carry. The thicker the belt, the bigger the capacity. This hall is invented, and its numbers are illustrative.

Here is the question. How many parcels per minute can we send from S to T? Count the belts that leave S, and you get eight. Sum the belts that enter T, and you get nine. Neither is the answer. Somewhere in the middle, the belts are too thin.

So let us grow a flow, one path at a time. The idea of Ford and Fulkerson is simple. First, find a path from S to T where every belt has room left. Then push as much as the tightest belt allows. After that, look again.

The first path is S, A, B, D, T. Its tightest belt is A to B, with room for one. So push one parcel. That belt is now full.

Now check the tables. At table A, one parcel comes in, and one goes out. At table B, the same. And at table D. This is conservation. What enters a table also leaves it. No parcel piles up, and none appears from nowhere. Only S makes parcels, and only T takes them away. The value of a flow is what leaves S. Right now, it is one.

Is there another path? Yes: S, A, C, T. The tightest belt is S to A, with one place left. Push one more. Now S to A is full, and the flow is two.

Then S, B, D, T has room. Here the tightest belt is B to D, with room for four. Push four. Six parcels per minute now flow.

Now the belt from S to A is full. The belt from S to B has room, but B can only pass parcels to D, and that belt is full. No path with room is left. Is six the best we can do?

It is not. There is one more move. We may send flow back. Take the belt from A to B. It carries the parcel of our first push. Let us take that parcel back. Instead, A sends it to C. And table B gets a fresh parcel straight from S, to fill the place that was left. Nothing piles up, and nothing is lost. The path is S, B, A, C, T, with the middle step going against the belt. Push one. Seven parcels now flow, one more than before.

Afterwards, A to B is empty again, as if we had never used it. A choice from the first step has been undone. So no choice has to be right the first time.

We took the paths in one order. Another order pushes other amounts, and ends on the same seven. With whole-number capacities, the method always stops, and the answer never changes.

Search one last time. S to B has one place left. But B to D is full, and B has nothing to send back. The tables we can still reach are S and B. The search stops. Seven parcels per minute is the best flow.

Those two tables, S and B, hold a secret. It waits for next time.

To sum up. A belt has a capacity. A flow respects every capacity, and keeps conservation at every table. Find a path with room, push what it allows, and repeat. When you are stuck, you may send flow back. When no path is left, the flow is the largest possible. The same picture fits water, trains, roads and data cables. One line of NetworkX does all of it, and it returns seven for this hall. Later, in chapter four, this same method will pair people with tasks.

Next time, we ask where the bottleneck is. The bottleneck theorem.

PLATE 3.34:29

The bottleneck theorem

A cut is a fence separating source from sink, and its capacity adds up the belts crossing from the source side. No flow can beat any cut. The max-flow min-cut theorem says the largest flow equals the smallest cut: seven in the sorting hall. When the search stops, the tables it reached mark the bottleneck, as in the railway report.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We are back in the pumping station. The secret report is still pinned to the board. Harris and Ross did not ask how much the railways could carry. They asked where the network breaks most easily. The historian Alexander Schrijver puts it plainly: their interest was a minimum cut, not a maximum flow. Their diagram marks one place, and calls it the bottleneck. Today we learn what a cut is, and why it answers their question.

Go back to the sorting hall from last time. Same belts, same capacities. Now take a marker, and draw a fence across the floor. The source S stands on one side. The sink T stands on the other. Every parcel must cross the fence. And it can only cross where a belt crosses it, through a gate.

A fence has a capacity. Add up the capacities of its gates. Only the belts that go from the S side to the T side count. Take a fence around S alone. It has two gates: two plus six. Eight.

Now a fence around everything except T. Again two gates: three plus six. Nine. These are the numbers from last time, the belts that leave S, and the belts that enter T.

And now a fence around S and A. Three gates: six, one and three. Ten.

Let us put last time's flow back on the belts. Seven parcels per minute cross each of these fences. The first fence has one place to spare. The second has two. The third has three. A fence with no place to spare would be a bottleneck.

Why do fences matter? Because every parcel must cross one. So no flow can be bigger than the capacity of any fence. A fence of eight allows at most eight. And a fence of nine allows at most nine. The smallest fence gives the strictest limit.

This hall has four tables, and each of them stands on one side of the fence or the other. So there are sixteen fences in all. Each bar is the capacity of one of them.

The smallest is seven. No flow can ever beat it. And last time, we pushed seven parcels per minute. The flow and the smallest fence meet.

This is the max-flow min-cut theorem. In any network with capacities, the largest flow equals the capacity of the smallest cut. One half is easy: a flow cannot beat any cut. The deep half says that the limit is reached. Some flow fills the smallest cut completely.

The theorem was published in 1956, and twice, independently. Ford and Fulkerson wrote Maximal flow through a network, in the Canadian Journal of Mathematics. Elias, Feinstein and Shannon wrote A note on the maximum flow through a network, in the I R E Transactions on Information Theory.

That last name is Claude Shannon, the founder of information theory. A communication channel has a capacity too, and the Information series meets it again.

Now the secret. When the search stopped last time, it had reached two tables: S and B. Draw the fence around exactly those two. Which belts cross it? S to A, and B to D. Both are full. Two plus five is seven. That is the smallest fence.

The search found it for free. Why are those two belts full? If one had room, the search would have crossed it, and reached another table. And the belt from A to B crosses the other way, so it does not count. It carries nothing. If it carried a parcel, the search would have sent it back, and reached A.

So every gate is full, and nothing comes back. The flow equals the fence. There is nothing more to compute. The method that finds the largest flow also marks the bottleneck.

Now back to the report. It carries two numbers: a flow of one hundred and sixty-three thousand tons, and a cut of one hundred and sixty-three thousand tons. They match. So no better flow exists, and no cheaper cut exists. One calculation answered both questions: how much can pass, and where to cut. Their question was the second.

One line of NetworkX returns both. The call minimum cut gives seven, and the two sides of the fence.

To sum up. A cut is a fence that separates S from T. Its capacity adds up the belts that cross from the S side to the T side. No flow beats any cut. The largest flow equals the smallest cut. And when the search stops, the tables it reached mark the bottleneck.

PLATE 3.44:50

Cutting in two: smallest and largest cuts

Minimum cuts can outline objects: pixels become nodes, weights reflect colour similarity, and the cheapest cut between scribbled object and background traces the cup. With no terminals, the minimum cut isolates one pixel, so the normalized cut divides by size. Smallest cuts are fast to find, but the maximum cut is one of Karp's 21 hard problems.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We are back in the pumping station. Last time, we found the smallest cut between a source and a sink. Today that cut gets a new job, and then it meets its opposite.

Here is a photograph. An espresso cup on a saucer, on a wooden table. We shrink it to thirty-two by twenty-six pixels. That makes eight hundred and thirty-two of them.

Every pixel becomes a node. Every pixel is tied to the neighbour on its left, right, above and below. That makes sixteen hundred and six edges.

Now give each edge a weight. If two neighbours have almost the same colour, the weight is high. A hundred at most. If their colours are far apart, the weight drops to one. So cutting between two similar pixels is expensive. Cutting where the colour jumps is cheap.

A cut that goes through cheap edges follows the places where the picture changes. That is an outline. But a cut needs two sides to separate.

So we add two special nodes: object, and background. The user paints a scribble on the cup, and a frame around the picture. Thirty-one pixels are marked as object, and one hundred and twelve as background. Each marked pixel is tied to its special node by an edge that nothing can cut.

Now ask the same question as last time. What is the cheapest cut between object and background? It is a min cut, and a maximum flow finds it.

The cut costs nine hundred and eighty. It crosses ninety-eight edges, and the average weight of a cut edge is ten. The average edge of the whole picture is fifty-seven. The cut runs along the cheap edges, and it traces the cup and the saucer. Three hundred and ninety-nine pixels end up inside.

Greig, Porteous and Seheult showed in 1989 that min cuts solve some image problems exactly. They worked on binary images. Boykov and Jolly turned it into an interactive tool in 2001. You scribble, and the cut does the rest.

Now the trap. Remove the two special nodes and the scribbles. Ask for the smallest cut of the picture, with no source and no sink.

The answer costs three. And it separates a single pixel, on the top edge, from the other eight hundred and thirty-one. A small piece is always cheap to detach, because it has so few edges to cut.

Shi and Malik fixed this in 2000. Divide the cost of the cut by the size of each piece, the total weight of edges that touch it. This ratio is the normalized cut. For the single pixel it comes to one. For the outline of the cup it comes to point zero two one.

We only evaluated those two cuts. Finding the very best normalized cut is a hard problem, and a later chapter returns to it.

Now the opposite question. Instead of the smallest cut, look for the largest.

Six guests are invited to dinner. Nine pairs of them are feuding. We have two tables. A feud is cut when the two guests sit at different tables. We want to cut as many feuds as possible. This is the maximum cut.

Try it. We can split six guests in thirty-two ways, so a computer checks them all. The best split cuts seven of the nine feuds, and three splits reach it.

Why not nine? Look at three guests who all feud with each other, a triangle. Whatever we do, two of them share a table. At best, two of the three feuds are cut. That is the odd cycle again, from episode one point four.

Small dinners are easy. But with thirty guests, there are five hundred and thirty-seven million splits to check. And each new guest doubles the number.

Compare with the smallest cut of the same dinner. It costs three, and a flow finds it at once. For the smallest cut, we have a fast method. For the largest, nobody has found one.

In 1972, Richard Karp listed twenty-one problems, and showed that they are hard in the same way. A fast method for any one of them would give a fast method for all. Maximum cut is the last on his list.

Nobody has proved that no fast method exists, and finding one would settle a famous open question. So the honest summary is that after more than fifty years, nobody has found one.

Two questions that look alike have opposite difficulties. The Quantum Computing series uses the maximum cut as its example, in episode thirteen point three.

To sum up. Give each edge a weight for how alike its ends are. The smallest cut between object and background draws an outline. With no terminals, the smallest cut isolates one pixel, and the normalized cut divides by size. Smallest is easy. Largest is one of Karp's twenty-one.

Next time, a different kind of question. Can we give every node a label, so that no two neighbours share one?
