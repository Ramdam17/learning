PLATE 10.17:08

When the outcome is yes or no: logistic regression

When the outcome is yes or no, a straight line predicts impossible probabilities such as minus 17 percent. Converting probability to odds, then taking the logarithm, gives log-odds, where the S becomes a straight line. Maximum likelihood fits it, climbing step by step. Logistic regression predicts a probability, not a yes or no; a cutoff makes the decision.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the lighthouse. The lamp is on, or it is off.

Many questions in science are like that. A student passes or fails. A seed germinates, or it does not. A patient responds, or does not. Until now, the outcome was a number on a scale. Today it is a yes or a no, and a straight line has a problem with that. Let us see which one.

Here is a simulated exam. 40 students, and for each, the hours of study and the result. Up means pass, which we code as 1. Down means fail, which is 0. 13 students passed, and 27 failed. The dots sit on two shelves, because nothing lives in between. Students who studied longer pass more often, but not always.

Try what we know. Draw the least squares line through the 0s and the 1s, as in 9.1. It rises, and that looks reasonable, until we read it. At zero hours, the line says minus 0.17. At 10 hours, it says 1.11. A probability of minus 17 percent does not exist. Neither does 111 percent. This is no corner case. The line drops below zero at 1.3 hours, and 11 of our 40 students sit where it is negative. A straight line has no reason to stop at 0 or at 1.

So we want a curve that flattens before it reaches the shelves. An S. At very low hours, an extra hour of study changes little. In the middle, it changes a lot. At very high hours, it changes little again.

But which S? We need a recipe that turns a straight line into an S, and the recipe is a change of scale. Start with a probability. It is stuck between 0 and 1. Convert it to odds: the chance that it happens, divided by the chance that it does not. A probability of 10 percent is odds of 0.11, which is 1 to 9. 50 percent is odds of 1. 90 percent is odds of 9. And 99 percent is odds of 99.

Odds are not stuck at 1 any more. They run from 0 up to infinity. But the bottom is still squeezed against 0. So take the logarithm. Odds of 0.11 become minus 2.20. Odds of 1 become 0. Odds of 9 become plus 2.20. And odds of 99 become 4.60. This is the log-odds. It runs from minus infinity to plus infinity, with no wall on either side. In 1944, Joseph Berkson called it the logit.

Now watch the same curve change scale. Stretch the vertical axis from probability to log-odds, and the S unrolls into a straight line. The model is now a plain line: log-odds equals a, plus b times hours. Our fit gives minus 5.78, plus 1.09 per hour. Each extra hour adds 1.09 to the log-odds, wherever you start. On the probability scale, the same hour moves the chance a lot in the middle, and hardly at all near the shelves. And the dots on the shelves? On this scale, 0 and 1 are infinitely far away, so they fly off the plot. That is why we cannot fit this line by least squares.

Instead we use maximum likelihood, from chapter 5. Ask each student: how much probability did your curve give to what really happened? A student who studied 2.55 hours and passed. The curve gave that outcome 5 percent. That is the score. A student who failed gets one minus the height of the curve. Multiply the 40 scores together, and you have the likelihood. Take the logarithm, and the product becomes a sum.

Try three curves. Too flat, and the log-likelihood is minus 16.22. Too steep, minus 12.67. Shifted, minus 16.89. The curve with the log-likelihood closest to zero wins. There is no formula for the best curve, so the computer climbs. It starts from a flat curve at one half, with a log-likelihood of minus 27.73. After one step, it is minus 11.94. Then minus 9.71, minus 9.24, and after five steps, minus 9.20. That is the top. Read it: after 4 hours of study, the chance of passing is 19 percent. After 7 hours, it is 86 percent.

The same recipe fits any yes or no. A seed germinates, or it does not. Here are 45 simulated seeds, with the temperature of their tray. At 24 degrees, the curve reads 83 percent. A patient responds, or does not, as the dose rises. 50 simulated patients. At 60 milligrams, the curve reads 78 percent. A customer buys, or does not, as the price rises. This curve falls. 60 simulated customers. At 15 euros, it reads 76 percent. A bird nests, or does not, at some distance from water. 50 simulated sites. At 150 metres, the curve reads 89 percent. Only 10 of those 50 sites held a nest: a rare outcome works the same way. Four fields, one recipe: a straight line on the log-odds scale, and an S in probability.

Back at the lighthouse, a question. If we ran the exam study again with other students, would we get the same curve? Guess before you see. With 20 students per study, how different could the curves be?

Here are 100 simulated studies of 20 students. The curves fan out. At 4 hours, the true chance is 31 percent, and 95 percent of the studies say something between 1 and 66 percent. In 144 of 2,000 studies, the hours split the passers from the failers perfectly, and the curve has no top: its slope runs off to infinity. With 80 students, the fan narrows: 15 to 46 percent. With 320 students, 23 to 38 percent. As in 9.1, more data means less wobble, and a curve wobbles just like a line. Even with 320 students, the band is still wide: one yes or no carries little information about a probability.

A common misreading. Logistic regression predicts yes or no. It does not. It predicts a probability. A yes or a no appears only when you add a cutoff. Say pass at 50 percent: that is 5.31 hours. But if you want to flag every student above 20 percent, the cutoff is 4.03 hours. Same model, different decisions. The cost of each kind of error sets the cutoff, as in 6.4.

So here is the idea. We never model the yes or the no. We model the probability of yes, on a scale where effects can add up.

Remember: a yes or no is modelled through its probability, and on the log-odds scale, the model is a straight line. Next time, in 10.2: what a logistic coefficient really says.

PLATE 10.27:22

Reading a logistic model

A logistic coefficient is a change in log-odds, and its exponential is an odds ratio, constant across starting points. The effect on the chance is not: an odds ratio of 2 adds 1.92 points from 2 percent but 16.67 from 50 percent. Common outcomes make odds ratios overstate gains. Translate back into probabilities, average marginal effects, and always say per what unit.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the lighthouse. Last time we drew the S. Today we read it.

A lighthouse keeper writes down, night after night, how far he can see, and whether he spotted the ship. Here are 300 simulated nights. The fit has one coefficient: 0.854 per kilometre of visibility. What does that number say? Each extra kilometre adds 0.854 to the log-odds of seeing the ship. Exponentiate it, and each extra kilometre multiplies the odds by 2.35. At 1 kilometre, the keeper sees the ship 35 percent of the time. At 3 kilometres, 75 percent.

Now a model with two predictors. A simulated exam, 400 students. For each, the hours of study, and whether the student had tutoring, yes or no. 184 passed. The truth, which only the simulation knows, is 0.5 per hour, and 0.9 for tutoring.

The fit, with 95 percent intervals. Hours: 0.446, from 0.354 to 0.538. Tutoring: 0.861, from 0.358 to 1.364. Both intervals contain the truth. Each coefficient is a change in log-odds, with the other predictor held fixed, as in 9.4.

Exponentiate each one, interval ends included, and you get an odds ratio. Hours: 1.56, from 1.42 to 1.71. Tutoring: 2.37, from 1.43 to 3.91. So each extra hour multiplies the odds of passing by 1.56, and tutoring multiplies them by 2.37. The odds, not the chance.

Why does that matter? Take an odds ratio of exactly 2, and apply it at five starting points. From 2 percent, the odds double, and the chance goes to 3.92 percent. About 4. That is 1.92 points more, a risk ratio of 1.96. From 10 percent, the chance goes to 18.18. From 25 percent, to 40. From 50 percent, to 66.67, about 67. That is 16.67 points more, but a risk ratio of only 1.33. From 80 percent, to 88.89: just 8.89 points, a risk ratio of 1.11.

On the log-odds scale, every step has the same length. On the probability scale, the steps shrink near 0 and near 1. The odds ratio is constant. The effect on the chance is not.

Here is a rare side effect, against dose. 6,000 simulated patients. Each 10 milligrams multiplies the odds by 1.98, close to 2. At 20 milligrams, the risk is 2.1 percent. At 30 milligrams, 4.0 percent. Twice the risk, almost, because the event is rare.

When the event is common, that stops. In 8.2, 72 of 120 treated patients improved, against 28 of 80 controls. The risk ratio was 1.71, and the odds ratio 2.79. For a rare event, 2 in 100 against 1 in 100, the risk ratio is 2.00 and the odds ratio 2.02. Now keep the risk ratio at 1.5, and raise the starting risk. From 1 percent, the odds ratio is 1.51. From 20 percent, 1.71. From 40 percent, 2.25.

A common outcome: recovering from a cold within a week, against hours of sleep. 800 simulated people. At 6 hours, 62 percent recover. At 8 hours, 80 percent. Two more hours of sleep multiply the odds by 2.51. But the risk ratio is 1.30. Read the 2.51 as two and a half times as likely, and you overstate the gain. The chance is 1.3 times higher, which is 18 points.

So always translate the model back into probabilities, at values that make sense. Back to the exam. The quartiles of the hours are 2.7, 5.1 and 8.1. Without tutoring, the predicted chance of passing is 16, 35 and 68 percent. With tutoring, 31, 57 and 84. The same odds ratio of 2.37 gives gains of 15, 21 and 15 points.

Guess before you see: does one more hour add the same number of points everywhere? For a student without tutoring, one more hour adds 7 points from the lower quartile, 11 from the median, and 9 from the upper quartile. The coefficient is 0.446 each time. The points are not.

Average the tutoring change over all 400 students, and you get 15.1 points. For one more hour, the average is 7.9 points. This is the average change in probability, also called the average marginal effect. It is one number, in the unit people care about. The coefficient, 0.861, could never have told you that.

Now a trap: the unit. A disease against age, in years, for 5,000 simulated adults. Per year, the coefficient is 0.0777, and the odds ratio 1.08. That looks like nothing. Count in decades, and the coefficient is 0.777, and the odds ratio 2.17, from 1.94 to 2.44. Same model, same data. Only the unit changed. Weight does the same. For a newborn, the odds ratio is 0.9988 per gram, and 0.31 per kilogram. Always say per what.

One last surprise. In the same adults, smoking has an odds ratio of 2.74, and the model adds it to the log-odds at every age. At 40, the predicted risk is 0.7 percent for non-smokers, and 2.0 percent for smokers: 1.3 points. At 70, it is 7.1 percent against 17.2: ten points. The model has no interaction, and yet the effect in points is eight times bigger.

Refit with an age by smoking product term, which lets the odds ratio change with age. It is 2.60 at 40, and 2.76 at 70, close to the 2.74 before. The product term, per decade, is 0.020, with an interval from minus 0.207 to 0.247, which includes zero. An interaction can appear on one scale, and not on the other. So say which scale.

Remember: a coefficient is a change in log-odds, and its exponential is an odds ratio.

The odds ratio is constant, but the effect on the chance is not, so translate the model back into probabilities.

PLATE 10.37:18

Counting events: Poisson regression and overdispersion

Counts need a log link so predictions never go negative; each step of fog multiplies ships per night by a rate ratio of 0.70. Exposure time goes in as an offset, otherwise the effect can come out backwards. Counts often vary more than Poisson allows; with variance near three times the mean, false alarms reach 24.8 percent. Use negative binomial or quasi-Poisson.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the lighthouse. Last time the outcome was yes or no. Today we count.

The keeper now writes down how many ships pass each night, against the fog. Here are 200 simulated nights. Fog runs from 0, clear, to 10, thick. Draw a straight line through them, and it crosses zero at a fog of 8.3. At 10, it predicts minus 1.2 ships. A count cannot be negative.

So model the logarithm of the expected count instead. This is the log link. The Poisson fit gives a coefficient of minus 0.354 per step of fog, and the curve never touches zero. On a clear night, 8.86 ships are expected. One step of fog, 6.22. Two steps, 4.37. Three, 3.07. Each step multiplies the count by the same number. Exponentiate the coefficient, and you get 0.70, a rate ratio. Read it like an odds ratio: each step of fog multiplies the ships per night by 0.70, at every fog level.

Now two keepers count birds from the gallery. The first keeper made 60 watches of about 2.3 hours. The second keeper made 60 watches of about 6.0 hours. The second logged 811 birds in all, the first only 400. Compare the average count per watch: the second keeper's is 2.03 times bigger, and so is the Poisson rate ratio. But the second keeper watched longer. Per hour, the first saw 2.85 birds, and the second saw 2.25.

The fix is an offset. Add the logarithm of the hours to the model, and it compares birds per hour. Now the rate ratio is 0.79. The second keeper sees 21 percent fewer birds per hour. The truth, which only the simulation knows, is 0.8. Without the offset, the model had the effect backwards. Always count per hour, per page, or per year of follow-up.

Next question: how much do counts spread? In a Poisson count, the variance equals the mean. Here are typos on one page, for 2,000 writers in each group. On the left, every writer has the same habits. On the right, some writers are careful and some are not, but the average is the same.

Look at eight writers from each group, then at all of them. On the left, the mean is 3.9, and the variance is 4.0. On the right, the mean is also 3.9, but the variance is 11.3. That is 2.9 times the mean. On the right, 12 percent of the pages are clean, against 2 percent on the left. And 7 percent have ten typos or more, against 1 percent. The writers differ, and the Poisson law does not know it. This is overdispersion: a variance above the mean.

The Poisson standard error is built on a variance equal to the mean. If the real variance is larger, the estimate wobbles more than the model thinks. The interval is too narrow, and the p-value too bold.

How bad? Seizures per month, 50 patients in each arm, and no true effect. At the 5 percent level, one study in 20 should cry wolf. We run 5,000 simulated studies. Guess before you see: when the variance is about three times the mean, how often does the Poisson test cry wolf?

24.8 percent. One study in four. With no overdispersion, it is 5.1 percent, as promised. With a mild spread, 10.2 percent. With a strong spread, 51.4 percent: more than one study in two. And more data does not help. With 500 patients per arm, it is still 27.0 percent.

Look at the standard errors. The Poisson standard error is 0.100. But the 5,000 estimates actually spread by 0.173. The model claims a wobble 1.7 times too small. And 1.7 is the square root of the variance-to-mean ratio, 2.93.

There are two remedies. The first is the negative binomial law. Each writer has their own rate, and it estimates the spread of those rates with one extra number, the dispersion. Greenwood and Yule studied repeated accidents with this idea in 1920.

The second is quasi-Poisson. Keep the Poisson fit, estimate how far the variance sits above the mean, and multiply the standard error by the square root of that factor. Wedderburn introduced quasi-likelihood in 1974.

Back to the 5,000 studies. Quasi-Poisson cries wolf 5.1 to 5.6 percent of the time, whatever the spread. The negative binomial, 4.8 to 5.8. And both standard errors are close to the true wobble: 0.172 and 0.171, against 0.173.

One study in detail. 100 patients per arm, seizures per month, simulated. The Poisson rate ratio is 0.97, with an interval from 0.85 to 1.10. The corrected interval runs from 0.71 to 1.33: about 2.5 times wider. The truth, 0.85, falls a hair below the Poisson interval, and well inside the corrected one. The Poisson p-value is 0.65, the corrected one 0.85.

Here are six simulated studies. For each, the rate ratio, the variance-to-mean ratio, and the p-value three ways.

Ships against fog: the variance-to-mean ratio is 1.09. Emergency admissions against temperature, 365 days: 1.94. Both p-values are tiny. Seizures: 6.02. Typos by writer, 40 pages each: 2.70, and the p-values are 0.58 and 0.74. Goals per match, home against away: 1.07, so all three agree. Birds per hour, with the offset: 4.06. The Poisson p-value is below one in a thousand. Quasi-Poisson says 0.055, the negative binomial 0.017.

Counts in practice very often vary more than a Poisson law allows, because people, days and places differ. Ignore it, and you manufacture significance.

If many writers never make a typo, zero-inflated models exist. If the same people are counted again and again, mixed models in 11.4 handle it.

Remember: a Poisson model with a log link gives rate ratios, and the exposure goes in as an offset.

Always compare the variance with the mean. If it is larger, use the negative binomial or quasi-Poisson, or your standard errors are too small.

PLATE 10.47:20

The generalized linear model

A generalized linear model has three ingredients: a linear predictor, a link function and a family, like pressure, a fitting and a nozzle. The same line pushed through identity, logit and log links gives a mean, a probability and a rate. Choose the family from how the data are produced, then check it by simulating fake data from the fitted model.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the lighthouse, for the last time in this chapter. For three episodes we used three different models: a line, an S curve, and a log curve. Today we show that they are one machine, with three settings.

In 1972, Nelder and Wedderburn published a paper called Generalized linear models. Its recipe has three ingredients.

The first is a linear predictor: a plain line, a plus b times x, built from what we know. The second is a link function: a fitting that turns the predictor into the average outcome the model expects. The third is a family: a law that says how real outcomes scatter around that average.

Picture a water line. The predictor is the pressure. The link is a fitting on the pipe. The family is the nozzle, which sprays the drops we call data.

Now keep the predictor fixed: minus 1, plus 0.5 times x. At x equal to 4, it gives 1. Push that 1 through three fittings. With the identity link, nothing changes: the mean is 1. With the logit link of 10.1, we get a probability: 0.73. With the log link of 10.3, we get a rate: 2.72.

Same line, same coefficients, three kinds of answer. At x equal to 0, the predictor is minus 1. The mean is minus 1, but the probability is 0.27, and the rate is 0.37.

That is why the link exists. A mean can be any number. A probability must stay between 0 and 1. A rate must stay above zero. The link keeps the answer where it can live.

Now look back at what we did. In 9.1, plants: growth against fertiliser. The family is normal, the link is identity. Simulated, 40 plants: the slope is 1.32 centimetres per gram, and the truth is 1.5.

In 10.1 and 10.2, exam results: pass or fail against hours. The family is binomial, the link is logit. Simulated, 80 students: 0.73 per hour, against a truth of 0.6.

In 10.3, ships per night against fog. The family is Poisson, the link is log. Simulated, 100 nights: minus 0.24 per step of fog, against a truth of minus 0.25.

Three models, three settings. And one fitting routine: Nelder and Wedderburn showed that one iterative weighted regression fits models of this whole kind.

So which setting do we pick? Not by habit. By how the data are produced.

A height in centimetres is a measurement on a scale, symmetric around its average: normal, with an identity link. A reaction time is positive, with a long tail of slow trials: gamma, usually with a log link. Pass or fail is a yes or no: binomial, logit. Ships per night are a count with no upper limit: Poisson, log.

Correct answers out of 20 questions are a count out of a fixed number of trials. That is still binomial, and the 20 goes in as the number of trials. Simulated, 60 people: the fit gives 0.55 per hour of practice, an odds ratio of 1.73.

Waiting times and rainfall on a wet day are positive and skewed: gamma again. Seizures per month are counts: Poisson, or negative binomial.

What if we pick the wrong family? Let us measure it, three times, with 10,000 simulated studies each.

Case one. Calls to a help line per hour, over 60 hours, falling as the night goes on. Fit a straight line, and it dips below zero in every one of the 10,000 studies.

Take hour 9, where the true average is 0.13 calls. The line's 95 percent interval contains it in only 31.6 percent of studies. The Poisson interval contains it in 95.2. And the line believes its estimate wobbles 1.3 times more than it really does.

Case two. Pass or fail, 60 students, hours of study from 0 to 10. Fit a straight line, and it leaves the range from 0 to 1 in 86.9 percent of the studies. Guess before you see: at 1 hour of study, where the true chance is 8.3 percent, will the line's interval fail?

It does not. Both intervals contain the truth in about 96 percent of studies: 96.0 for the line, 96.1 for the logistic curve. I expected it to fail there, and it did not. But the line's interval is 1.5 times wider, and the line itself predicts probabilities that cannot exist.

Case three. Reaction times, 40 people, hours awake from 0 to 8. The truth is gamma with a log link. At 8 hours, the true average is 679 milliseconds. The normal model's interval contains it in 87.1 percent of studies. The gamma model's, in 93.7. The normal model thinks its estimate is steadier than it is, and its interval is 28 percent narrower.

How do we find out, with real data? We simulate.

Fit the model. Let it produce 1,000 fake data sets, with the same x values. Compare a feature of the real data with the same feature in the fake ones: the share of zeros, the variance, the largest value.

First, 120 simulated nights of ships, fitted as Poisson. In the real data, 34.2 percent of nights have no ship. In the fake data sets, between 24.2 and 38.3. The variance is 3.30, against 2.42 to 4.54. The largest count is 7, against 6 to 12. The model could have made these data. It passes.

Now seizures per month, 120 simulated patients with a wide spread, fitted as Poisson. In the real data, 30.0 percent of patients have no seizure. In the fake data sets, between 1.7 and 9.2. The variance is 19.9, against 2.5 to 4.5. The largest count is 32, against 7 to 12. The model could not have made these data. It fails.

That is the overdispersion of 10.3, found without a formula. The Bayesian version of this check is called a posterior predictive check, in chapter 16. And if many zeros are the problem, zero-inflated models exist.

Here is the idea. Do not ask which regression you always run. Ask how the numbers were produced. A measurement on a scale: normal, or gamma if it is positive and skewed. A yes or no: binomial. A count: Poisson. Then simulate from the fitted model, and see if it could have made your data.

Remember: a generalized linear model is a family, a link and a linear predictor. Choose the family from how the data are produced, and check the model by simulating from it.
