What is a world model?

A world model can mean two independent things. One is a runnable function from a state and an action to the next state, like a vending machine fed its own output to imagine rollouts, and the other is the structure a model holds inside, like a floor plan. A toy lookup table shows how missing data becomes a wall in imagination.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the scale model. On this table sits a miniature of the room next door: little walls, a little floor, and a little ROVI. Before ROVI drives across the real room, it pushes its small copy across the table and watches what happens. That table is the subject of this whole chapter: the world model. In episode nine point three, ROVI rehearsed in its head. Today we ask what, exactly, it was rehearsing with.

Start with the simplest answer. Remember the words from episode nine point one: a state, and an action. A world model takes the state ROVI is in now, and the action it is about to take, and gives back the state that comes next. In symbols, the next state equals f of the state and the action.

Think of a vending machine. You slide in a card that says where you are, and a second card that says what you do. You turn the crank, and a third card drops into the tray: where you would be. Nothing in the real room has moved. The machine is a function with knobs, as in episode one point one, and its knobs are tuned from what really happened.

Now feed the machine its own output. Take the card from the tray, slide in a new action card, and crank again. That is an imagined rollout, from episode nine point three, and the raw material of planning, search and model-predictive control.

This idea is much older than deep learning. In nineteen forty-three, the Cambridge psychologist Kenneth Craik published a book called The Nature of Explanation. He argued that thought works like a model: a working system that has the same relation-structure as the process it imitates. A tide predictor made of pulleys and levers looks nothing like the sea, yet it moves the way the tide does. And engineers tested the Queen Mary as a model in a tank, because trying plans on a real liner would cost far more. For Craik, the nervous system lets us try alternatives on a cheaper scale, before paying for them in the world. Psychologists came to call such things mental models.

In reinforcement learning, the idea became an algorithm. In nineteen ninety, Richard Sutton presented Dyna at the International Conference on Machine Learning, and a short overview followed in nineteen ninety-one. A Dyna agent runs a single process that alternates between the world and a learned model of it. It learns from the moves it really makes, and from the moves its model imagines. In twenty eighteen, David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber published a paper with the plain title World Models, which we meet in the next episode. From Craik to Ha, seventy-five years, and one idea: try it in the model first.

Now a toy, illustrative, so we can watch a learned machine fail. ROVI lives in a corridor of ten cells, and has two actions: one step left, or one step right. Walls stop it at both ends. That makes twenty pairs of state and action. Suppose ROVI has seen only fourteen of those pairs, and writes each one in a table. For a pair it has never seen, the table guesses that nothing happens.

Ask the table about all twenty pairs, and it is wrong on six: a one-step error of thirty percent. Now chain it. Averaged over every start and every sequence of moves, after two steps forty-five percent of the imagined endings are wrong. After three steps, fifty-nine percent. After five, seventy-two. After eight, seventy-nine.

Look at one rollout. Start at cell two and go right, eight times. In the real corridor, ROVI walks to the far wall. In the table, it stalls at cell three, because that pair was never seen. A hole in the data became a wall in the imagination. Errors in a rollout compound, as in episode nine point three, here through missing data.

That was the first meaning of world model: a learned simulator. Give it an action, and it returns what comes next. But in papers, the same two words are often used for something else.

Pin a floor plan beside the vending machine. It shows which rooms connect, where the walls are, which doors lead where. You cannot crank it. It does not say what happens after an action. It says how the world is put together. This is the second meaning: an internal representation of the structure of the world. It is what people ask about when they say, what does this model know?

The two meanings are independent. Our table runs, but it holds no plan of the corridor, only memorised entries. A floor plan can be perfect, and still unable to predict a single step. Many arguments about large models mix them up: one side shows that a network predicts well, the other asks what it holds inside.

So this series keeps them apart. When we say world model, we mean something you can run: a state and an action go in, a next state comes out, possibly in an abstract space instead of pixels, as in episode six point five. When we mean what a model knows, we will say representation.

Here is where the debate stands, as of November twenty twenty-six, with one result on each side. In twenty twenty-two, Kenneth Li and colleagues trained a small network of the GPT kind only to predict legal moves in Othello, without ever showing it the rules or the board. Inside, they found a representation of the board, and when they edited it, the moves the model predicted changed to match. Their reading: predicting moves can lead a network to build something like a board.

In twenty twenty-four, Keyon Vafa and colleagues asked a sharper question. For worlds governed by fixed rules, such as navigation, games and logic puzzles, they built tests of whether a model has recovered the rules. Models did well on the usual tests. On the new ones, their implicit world models were far less coherent than they appeared, and that made them fragile when the task changed slightly. Their reading: good predictions do not prove a coherent model. Both are about the second meaning, and the question is open. We return to it in chapter thirteen.

The chapter goes on with Ha and Schmidhuber's dream, then Dreamer, then MuZero, a model that predicts only what matters for winning. And in chapter eleven, JEPA asks what such a model should predict at all.

To sum up. A world model, first, is a learned function from a state and an action to the next state, which ROVI can run in imagination. The idea runs from Craik's mental models, to Sutton's Dyna, to Ha and Schmidhuber's World Models. Second, the same words can mean the structure a model holds inside. The two meanings are independent, and this series will always say which one it uses. Next time: Ha and Schmidhuber, learning inside a dream.

Ha & Schmidhuber: learning inside a dream

A driver learns inside a dream made of three parts: a variational autoencoder compresses each frame into a necklace, a recurrent network predicts the next necklace as a mixture, and a tiny linear controller trained by evolution reads both and acts. Trained entirely in the dream, it works in the real game, provided the dream stays uncertain enough that the agent cannot exploit its flaws.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the scale model. In the last episode, What is a world model, we separated two meanings of those words. Today we meet the first famous example of the first meaning: a learned simulator that an agent can use. A small driver learns to race without touching the real game while it learns. It practises inside a dream that its own model makes up. By the end of this video you will know the three parts of that model, what each one learns, and the trick that stops the driver from cheating.

Picture a snow globe. Inside the glass sits a tiny road and a tiny car. Now suppose nobody built that road. Under the base, a machine keeps drawing the next scene, one moment after another, from what it remembers of the real game. A driver who steers the car inside the globe never touches the real game. Yet if the dream is faithful enough, what the driver learns carries over.

That is the idea of a paper by David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber, titled World Models. It appeared on arXiv in March twenty eighteen, with an interactive version at a website called worldmodels dot github dot io. A conference version followed at NeurIPS that year, under the title Recurrent World Models Facilitate Policy Evolution. The authors say the idea has roots: the paper distills a series of papers between nineteen ninety and twenty fifteen, among them Schmidhuber's nineteen ninety report Making the World Differentiable, about a recurrent network that models its world so that a controller can plan. We take these dates from the paper's own reference list.

Now the architecture, in one picture. It has three parts, called V, M and C.

V is for vision. V is a variational autoencoder, the one from episode six point two. Each frame of the game is resized to sixty-four by sixty-four pixels and goes into the encoder. Out comes a necklace, z. In the car race, z has thirty-two beads. In the second game, it has sixty-four. V is trained first, on ten thousand rollouts of a player that moves at random.

M is for memory. M is a recurrent network, an L S T M, with two hundred and fifty-six hidden units in the car race. Its job is to predict the next necklace, from the current necklace and the action taken. But the world is not always predictable. A monster may shoot, or may wait. So M does not give one guess. It gives a mixture of five Gaussian bumps, and the next necklace is drawn from that mixture. The authors call this an M D N R N N.

Like the autoregressive models of episode six point three, M predicts the next piece from what came before. Only here the piece is a necklace, not a pixel. Look where its scale sits: M compares its ghost necklace with the real next necklace, in representation space.

C is for controller, and it is almost nothing. C is one linear layer. It reads the current necklace z and M's hidden state h, glued end to end, and writes the action. In the car race there are three actions: steering, gas and brake. So that is three times thirty-two plus two hundred and fifty-six, plus three biases. Eight hundred and sixty-seven numbers. The vision part has more than four million parameters, and M more than four hundred thousand. C is less than two hundredths of one percent of the agent.

Why so small? Because the reward only ever reaches C, and a tiny controller can be trained without backpropagation. The authors use an evolution strategy called C M A E S. Imagine sixty-four slightly different controllers, each driving sixteen times. The best are kept, and the next batch is drawn around them. It is a cousin of the cross-entropy method of episode nine point four, except that it selects controllers, not plans. And C does not search through imagined futures: it reads h, which already holds what M expects, and acts at once.

So what does this system learn? First, a car race called Car Racing: a top-down track, new at every trial. The game counts it as solved at an average of nine hundred points over a hundred consecutive trials. If C sees only z, and not h, it scores six hundred and thirty-two, plus or minus two hundred and fifty-one, and the car wobbles. Give C both z and h: nine hundred and six, plus or minus twenty-one. The authors describe it as the first known solution.

But there, the dream only fed C its inputs. The real trick comes in a second game: Viz Doom, the Take Cover scenario. Monsters on the far wall shoot fireballs, and the agent slides left or right. There is no score. The reward is the number of time steps the agent stays alive, up to twenty-one hundred, about a minute. Solved means above seven hundred and fifty steps on average.

Here M predicts one more thing: whether the agent dies in the next frame. That is all a game needs, a next frame and a game over. So V and M, wrapped together, behave like the game itself. The controller trains inside that wrapper, in latent space, with no real pixels at all. Then it is moved to the real game. At the best setting, it survives about nine hundred steps in the dream, and one thousand and ninety-two, plus or minus five hundred and fifty-six, in the real game. A dream, harder than reality, made a good teacher.

Now the classic pitfall. The controller does not know that it is dreaming. It only knows that some moves earn a high score. And M is only an approximation, so some moves exploit its flaws. In the first experiments, the agent found a way to move so that the monsters never shot. Even when a fireball was forming, it seemed to put it out, as if it had superpowers. Inside the globe, that worked. In the real game, it does not exist. The authors call it cheating the world model.

The remedy is uncertainty. Tau is the temperature of M's mixture. Set tau to point one, and the dream becomes almost deterministic. The paper reports that the monsters then never shoot, whatever the agent does. A controller trained there survives twenty-one hundred steps in the dream, most of the time. In the real game it survives one hundred and ninety-three. A random player survives two hundred and ten.

Turn tau up to one point oh, and the dream shakes: about eleven hundred steps in the dream, eight hundred and sixty-eight in reality. At one point three, seven hundred and fifty-three in reality, with less risk. Too much noise, and the dream is too hard to learn from. So tau is a dial between realism and exploitability.

One limit is worth keeping. V and M were trained without knowing the reward, and only on random play. They keep what helps rebuild and predict frames, which may not be what helps drive. What a representation should keep comes back, with a different answer, in chapter eleven.

To sum up. V compresses each frame into a necklace. M predicts the next necklace, as a mixture, in representation space. C, a tiny linear controller trained by evolution, reads both and acts. Trained inside the dream and moved to the real game, it worked, as long as the dream stayed uncertain enough not to be exploited. Next time: Dreamer, behaviour learned in imagination.

Dreamer: behaviour learned in imagination

Dreamer trains an actor and a critic entirely inside imagined latent trajectories, like a pilot in a flight simulator with an examiner. Its world model pairs a deterministic memory with a stochastic necklace, and gradients of learned values flow back through imagined steps to the actor. One configuration handles over one hundred and fifty tasks, though its decoder still reconstructs pixels.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the scale model. In the last episode, Ha and Schmidhuber: learning inside a dream, a small driver practised inside a dream. Today an agent learns all of its behaviour inside imagined futures. By the end of this video, you will know the parts of Dreamer, what it learned to do, and the question it leaves open.

Picture a flight simulator. A pilot sits in a cockpit, hands on the stick. The windscreen shows a world, but no plane is flying. A crash costs nothing. Beside the pilot sits an examiner with a scoreboard. After every manoeuvre, the examiner says how well it went.

Now give this cockpit to ROVI. The pilot is the actor, the policy from episode nine point two. The examiner is the critic, the value function from the same episode. And the windscreen is a world model, showing the futures that the model imagines. That is the whole idea of Dreamer. The actor practises, the critic scores, and all of it happens in trajectories imagined in latent space.

Dreamer has a short family history, all led by Hafner. First came PlaNet, on arXiv in November twenty eighteen, then at the International Conference on Machine Learning in twenty nineteen. PlaNet introduced the recurrent state space model, the R S S M: a latent model with a deterministic part and a stochastic part. PlaNet acted by planning. At every step it ran the cross entropy method of episode nine point four inside its model.

Then came Dreamer, by Hafner, Lillicrap, Ba and Norouzi, on arXiv in December twenty nineteen, and at the International Conference on Learning Representations in twenty twenty. Its title is Dream to Control. Dreamer keeps the model of PlaNet and drops the search. Instead it trains an actor, which proposes the action directly, and a critic.

How does the actor learn from dreams? The model is unrolled for a short horizon, about fifteen steps, starting from states that were really seen. Every step of the dream is differentiable. So the gradient of the learned value flows back through the imagined steps, all the way to the actor. The paper calls this backpropagating the gradients of learned state values through imagined trajectories.

Now the architecture, in one picture. A frame comes in on the left, in pixel space. The encoder, in blue, turns it into a necklace, z. Beside it runs a recurrent memory, h. The pair h and z is the model state: together they form the R S S M. Then the decoder, in pink, paints the frame back from h and z, and a scale in pixel space compares the painting with the real frame. Finally, the actor and the critic live in representation space. They never see a picture. They read necklaces.

Let us open the R S S M. The part h is deterministic. It is a recurrent state that remembers the past, updated from the previous h, the previous z and the action. The part z is stochastic: a random necklace, drawn at each step, which keeps several futures open, as in episode eight point three.

There are two ways to get z. With a real frame, the encoder writes a posterior. Without a frame, the model predicts z from h alone: the prior. In imagination there is no frame, so the model uses the prior. Training pulls the two together with a K L loss, the same kind of price as in the variational autoencoder of episode six point two.

Training alternates between two phases. In the first, the agent acts in the real world and stores what happens. The world model learns from that replay: it reconstructs the frames, predicts the rewards, and keeps the prior close to the posterior.

In the second phase, nothing real is touched. Starting from replayed states, the model imagines a trajectory. At each step the actor picks an action, and the model returns the next state and a predicted reward. The critic scores where the dream ends. The actor moves to raise that score, and the critic moves to match it. The real world supplies the experience. The dream supplies the practice.

Here is a toy case, illustrative. Four imagined steps, predicted rewards zero, zero, one, zero, and a discount of zero point nine. The rewards add up to zero point eight one. At the end, the critic says the state is worth five. Discounted four times, that is three point two eight. The score is four point zero nine. Rewards that were dreamed, plus the value beyond.

The recipe was refined twice. DreamerV2, at the International Conference on Learning Representations in twenty twenty one, replaced the Gaussian latents with discrete ones. Its paper claims the first agent to reach human level on fifty five Atari games, learning inside a world model.

DreamerV3, by Hafner, Pasukonis, Ba and Lillicrap, appeared on arXiv in January twenty twenty three. The journal version came out in Nature in April twenty twenty five, titled Mastering Diverse Control Tasks through World Models.

So what does it learn? The headline is generality. DreamerV3 is evaluated on eight domains, with over one hundred and fifty tasks, among them Atari, ProcGen, DMLab and the Control Suite. All of them use one configuration, with the same hyperparameters. The authors report that it beats specialised methods tuned for each benchmark.

The most famous test is Minecraft. To get a diamond, the player must find a chain of twelve items from sparse rewards, in a world generated anew each time. Dreamer used its default settings. It started from scratch, with no human demonstrations and no curriculum. All the Dreamer agents that were trained found diamonds within one hundred million steps. In the paper's table, that took eight point nine days on one graphics card. The other methods tested reached the iron pickaxe, but none found a diamond.

A preprint called Dreamer 4, by Hafner, Yan and Lillicrap, followed in September twenty twenty five. It reports diamonds from offline data alone, with no game interaction during training. I found no peer reviewed version as of November twenty twenty six, so take it as a claim.

Now the classic pitfall. Look at the pink decoder again. Dreamer learns its latent by reconstructing pixels. Episode six point five asked whether predicting every pixel is worth its cost, and the question comes back here. DreamerV3 even tested it. In its ablation, the authors write that performance rests predominantly on the unsupervised reconstruction loss of the world model, unlike most prior algorithms, which rely on reward and value gradients.

So here, pixels help. But a pixel loss pays for every leaf and every shadow. Whether a good latent can be learned without painting the frame is the question chapter eleven takes up, with JEPA. And episode ten point four will show another answer: a model that predicts only what matters for winning.

To sum up. Dreamer trains an actor and a critic entirely inside imagined latent trajectories. Its world model is an R S S M, a deterministic memory plus a stochastic necklace, with a decoder that still reconstructs the frames. DreamerV3 handles over one hundred and fifty tasks with one configuration, and collects diamonds in Minecraft without human data. The pixel question stays open. MuZero: a model that predicts only what matters

MuZero's model predicts neither the next image nor the true state, only reward, value and policy, read through three windows of a sealed jar, its hidden state. Representation, dynamics and prediction functions let a tree search run in hidden space without being given the rules. It matched AlphaZero on board games and led on Atari; its hidden state is meaningful only for planning.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the scale model. Last time, Dreamer learned its behaviour in imagined futures, but its decoder still repainted every frame. Today we meet a model that paints nothing at all. By the end of this video, you will know its three parts, what it learned to play, and why its inner state cannot be read as a picture.

Start with a question. To win a game, what must your model of the game predict? The next image? The true state of the board? The answer of MuZero is neither. It predicts only three things. The reward you collect right now. The value, which says how good the position is in the long run. And the policy, which says which moves deserve your attention. Reward, value and policy: the three things of episode nine point two.

Here is our picture. A jar, sealed and opaque, standing on the table. We cannot see inside. But the jar has three little windows. One shows a coin: the reward. One shows a gauge: the value. One shows a bar for each move: the policy. That jar is a hidden state. Everything the model knows about a position lives inside it, and the only way to look is through the windows.

The model is MuZero, by Schrittwieser, Antonoglou, Hubert, Silver and colleagues at DeepMind. The preprint appeared on arXiv in November twenty nineteen, under the title Mastering Atari, Go, Chess and Shogi by Planning with a Learned Model. The journal version came out in Nature in December twenty twenty. It builds on AlphaZero. AlphaZero searched a tree of moves, but it was handed a simulator that knew the rules of the game. MuZero keeps the search, and replaces the simulator with a model that it learns itself.

Now the architecture, in one picture. There are three functions. The representation function, h, reads the observation, a Go board or an Atari screen, and writes the first hidden state, s zero. That is the first jar. The dynamics function, g, takes a jar and an action, and returns two things: an immediate reward, and a new jar. And the prediction function, f, looks at a jar and fills in its windows: a policy and a value. All three are neural networks. In the paper, the dynamics function is deterministic. Stochastic transitions are left for future work.

Now put the search of episode nine point four to work. The root of the tree is the first jar, made from the real board. Each branch is a candidate move. To follow a branch, the search hands the jar and the move to g, and gets back a new jar and a reward. Then f reads the windows of the new jar. The whole tree grows in hidden space. No board is ever drawn, and no rule is ever consulted. Finally the agent plays a move, drawn in proportion to how often the search visited each branch. In the paper, board games use eight hundred simulations per move, and Atari uses fifty.

One honest detail. At the root, the real game still tells the agent which moves are legal. Inside the tree, nothing is masked. The paper says that the network quickly learns not to predict moves that never occur in its training games.

How does it learn? The agent plays, using the search, and stores its games. Then it takes a stored game and unrolls the model for five steps, using the moves that were really played. At each step it compares the three windows with three targets. The reward target is the reward that was really observed. The value target is the return that was really collected over the next few steps, plus the search's own estimate beyond. The policy target is what the search decided. One loss adds up the three errors, and the gradient flows back through all five steps, into all three functions.

Here is a toy case, illustrative. After fifty simulations, the search visited four moves thirty, twelve, five and three times. That gives a policy target of zero point six, zero point two four, zero point one and zero point zero six. The policy window of the model currently says zero point four, zero point three, zero point two and zero point one. The cross entropy is one point six four bits. The best it could reach is one point five one bits, the entropy of the target. The search teaches the policy window, and the policy window guides the search. That loop is the whole engine.

So what does it learn? Go, chess and shogi, without being given the rules. The paper measures it against AlphaZero, which was given the rules, using Elo ratings. In all three games, MuZero matched AlphaZero. In Go, it slightly exceeded it, with sixteen residual blocks per evaluation where AlphaZero used twenty.

Atari is the harder test, because the screens are visually rich, and model-based methods had lagged there. On all fifty seven games, MuZero set a new state of the art, for both the mean and the median score, normalised to human testers. In the paper's table one, the median is two thousand and forty one percent, against nineteen hundred and twenty one percent for R2D2, the previous best. MuZero beat R2D2 on forty two of the fifty seven games, and the earlier model-based agent SimPLe on every game.

Now the classic pitfall. Open a jar. What do you see? Nothing you could call a picture. The paper is explicit. There is no requirement that the hidden state can rebuild the observation, and none that it matches the true state of the game. It is free to represent whatever helps predict values and policies. So the hidden state only has meaning for planning. Hand it to a decoder, and you have no reason to expect a board or a screen.

And a second pitfall: without reward, nothing is learned. By design, all three targets come from observed rewards, or from a search that relies on values learned from them. In a game that never pays, the windows have nothing to learn from.

Here is the bridge to JEPA. Both refuse to predict pixels. Both predict inside an abstract space. Dreamer repainted every frame, and the ablation of episode ten point three showed that its reconstruction loss carried most of the performance. MuZero shows another route: a latent trained with no decoder at all.

But watch what differs. MuZero's latent is shaped only by reward, value and policy targets. It keeps what helps win this game, and nothing else. JEPA trains its latent differently. It predicts the representation of a hidden part of the input, and it uses no reward. So one latent asks, what helps me act? The other asks, what can be predicted? Chapter eleven takes up that second question.

To sum up. MuZero's model predicts neither the image nor the true state, only reward, value and policy. Three functions, representation, dynamics and prediction, run a tree search inside hidden space. It matched AlphaZero in Go, chess and shogi without being given the rules, and set a new state of the art on fifty seven Atari games. Its hidden state is meaningful only for planning, and without reward nothing is learned. JEPA also predicts in an abstract space, but with no reward. Video models as « world simulators »

Video models are called world simulators, but a clip generator only makes a flipbook: one future for one prompt. An agent needs an action-conditioned model, a joystick on the book, so it can compare the futures of several candidate actions. The evidence for includes real-time interactive worlds and robot and driving uses; the evidence against includes physics benchmarks, compute cost and the pixel question.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the scale model. Last time, MuZero predicted only what matters for winning, and painted nothing at all. Today the question runs the other way. Companies have trained models on enormous amounts of video, and some of them say these models are simulators of the world. By the end of this video you will have a dated timeline, one distinction that matters for any agent, and the evidence for and against, given equal weight.

Start with the dates. In February twenty twenty-four, OpenAI published a report called Video generation models as world simulators, about its model Sora. The report says that scaling video generation models is a promising path towards general purpose simulators of the physical world. That is OpenAI's claim. The same month, Google DeepMind posted a paper called Genie: eleven billion parameters, trained on unlabelled internet videos, with a latent action model that lets a user act frame by frame. In December twenty twenty-four came Genie 2, which DeepMind says makes playable three-dimensional worlds, consistent for up to a minute, with most examples lasting ten to twenty seconds.

In August twenty twenty-five, Genie 3. DeepMind says it runs in real time at twenty-four frames per second and seven hundred and twenty pixels, stays consistent for a few minutes, and remembers back about one minute. In December, the company Runway announced GWM-1, a model it says responds to camera pose, robot pose and audio. In January twenty twenty-six, Google opened Project Genie to AI Ultra subscribers in the United States, with each generation capped at sixty seconds.

Then the timeline gets a gap. On March twenty-fourth, OpenAI gave notice that it would discontinue Sora. The app and website closed on April twenty-sixth, and the video API ends on September twenty-fourth. On May thirty-first, NVIDIA announced Cosmos 3, an open model that, the company says, understands and generates text, images, video, sound and actions. And on October twenty-seventh, DeepMind announced Genie 4, a research preview for trusted testers, with native audio and steering by text. Now the distinction. Picture a flipbook. A clip generator makes one. You write a prompt, and out comes a stack of pages, each a little different from the last. You can flip it as often as you like, but every page is already drawn. The only thing you chose was the prompt, before the book existed. You cannot reach in and change what happens on page ten.

Now fix a joystick to that book. At every page, someone pushes a direction, and only then is the next page drawn. That is an action-conditioned model, the function of episode ten point one: a state and an action go in, the next state comes out. DeepMind describes Genie 2 this way: at each step, a person or an agent provides a keyboard and mouse action, and the model simulates the next observation. Genie learned its actions without any labels. And Runway lists camera pose and robot pose as inputs.

Why does this matter for an agent? Think of ROVI in episode nine point four, fanning out ghost paths. To score them, it needs one starting state, several candidate actions, and a different future for each. MuZero's dynamics function in episode ten point four takes an action. So does Dreamer's imagination in ten point three. A flipbook gives one future for one prompt. It cannot say what happens if ROVI turns left instead of right. With three actions and three steps there are twenty-seven futures to compare, and a clip offers one. A clip can still be useful, but it is not a simulator the agent can question.

So here is the case for. First, coherent, real-time worlds. In August twenty twenty-five, DeepMind reported twenty-four frames per second, and consistency for a few minutes. Second, robots. In December twenty twenty-five, the Gemini Robotics team fine-tuned a Veo video model to take robot actions, and used it to evaluate eight policies on five tasks, checked against more than sixteen hundred real trials. Third, driving. In February twenty twenty-six, Waymo said its simulator, built on Genie 3, follows specific driving inputs and produces camera and lidar data. These are the groups' own reports about their own systems, and the robotics paper itself says that contact-rich interactions, especially with small objects, remain a challenge.

Now the case against, starting with physics. In January twenty twenty-five, Motamed and colleagues released Physics-IQ: three hundred and ninety-six real videos of physical events, in sixty-six scenarios. Each model is compared with real recordings of the same physical events. A score of one hundred means matching what a second real recording of the same event would show. In the paper's Table 1, the best model scores twenty-nine point five, Runway's Gen-3 twenty-two point eight, and Sora ten point zero. The authors conclude that visual realism does not imply physical understanding.

Other benchmarks agree. PhyGenBench, in October twenty twenty-four: the best average is Gen-3, at zero point five one out of one. VideoPhy-2, in March twenty twenty-five: the best model, Wan 2.1, produces a video that is both on prompt and physically sensible in twenty-one point nine percent of the hard cases. And for interactive models, WorldRoamBench, in June twenty twenty-six: its authors say none of the ten or more models they tested reliably satisfies all four dimensions, and even the best achieves only moderate scores.

The interactive models list their own limits. For Genie 3, DeepMind says the range of actions an agent can take directly is constrained. Project Genie's page says characters can respond more slowly to control, and that some Genie 3 features announced in August were not yet included. For Genie 4, DeepMind says details and object permanence can still drift over time.

Is the gap closing? A revised Physics-IQ Verified appeared in June twenty twenty-six, with a different scale, so its numbers cannot be compared with the paper's. On its leaderboard, as of October twelfth, plain image-to-video models run from Sora 2 at twenty-six point five to Cosmos 3 Super at forty-two point seven. Rewriting the prompts and picking the best of eight attempts lifts the top image-to-video entry to fifty-five point one, and the top entry on the multi-frame track to sixty-six point one. Many entries are contributed through the benchmark's repository, so they are reported by their authors. Scores have risen, and even the best are still well below one hundred.

Second argument against: cost. In June twenty twenty-five, the V-JEPA 2 authors compared planning with an action-conditioned Cosmos model to their own latent one. Four minutes per action, against sixteen seconds. These are the authors of the competitor, in their setup. Project Genie's sixty-second cap and Waymo's remark that long simulations are harder to compute and keep stable point the same way.

Third: pixels. In February twenty twenty-four, Yann LeCun was reported as calling the generation of pixels, to model the world for action, wasteful and doomed to failure, because prediction is too uncertain for continuous sensory data. The other side has evidence too. In episode ten point three, DreamerV3's ablation found that its pixel reconstruction loss carried most of the performance. The question stays open, and chapter eleven takes it up.

One last point. The line between clip and action is blurring. Cosmos 3 lists actions among its outputs, and Genie 4 takes keyboard controls. But the Genie 4 announcement gives no resolution, frame rate or duration and lists no benchmark, and no independent evaluation had surfaced by mid-November.

To sum up. Since February twenty twenty-four, video models have been called world simulators. A clip generator makes a flipbook; an action-conditioned model adds the joystick an agent needs. For: real-time worlds, and robot and driving uses, by the groups' own reports. Against: physics benchmarks, compute cost, and pixels. Next time: episode eleven point one, LeCun's 2022 proposal.
