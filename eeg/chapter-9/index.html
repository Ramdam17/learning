ERSP · Power over time and frequency

A time-frequency map is a musical score for the brain, showing power by frequency over time. Each trial is convolved with Morlet wavelets and power is taken before averaging, so rhythm changes with varying phase survive. The number of cycles sets the time-frequency trade-off and edges are unreliable, while the one-over-f slope calls for a baseline.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter nine: time-frequency analysis. Our first piece is the E-R-S-P, the event-related spectral perturbation. Power, laid out over time and frequency.

In chapter eight, we averaged voltages. That keeps only what is locked in phase to the event. But a lot of the brain's response is a change in rhythm: alpha that drops when you open your eyes, beta that dips when you move. To see those changes, we need to look at power, frequency by frequency, moment by moment.

Picture a musical score. Time runs from left to right. Pitch runs from bottom to top. Each note tells you which pitch plays, when it starts, and how long it lasts. A time-frequency map is a score for the brain: frequency from bottom to top, time from left to right, and colour for how loud each frequency is at each moment.

Here is how we build it.

Step one: take one epoch, from one channel.

Step two: convolve it with a family of wavelets, one per frequency, as we saw in episode three point nine. A Morlet wavelet at ten hertz picks out the ten-hertz activity, moment by moment. Repeat for every frequency, from, say, three to forty hertz.

Step three: from each wavelet output, take the power: the squared amplitude. This gives one row of the map per frequency. The phase is set aside.

Step four: do this for every trial, and average the power maps across trials.

The order matters. We compute power first, on each trial, and average second. Power is always positive, so rhythms that change in strength survive the averaging, even if their phase differs from trial to trial. This is what averaging voltages could not do, and episode nine point three explores it in detail.

The term E-R-S-P was introduced by Scott Makeig in 1993. Today the same idea goes by many names: time-frequency representation, T-F-R, spectrogram, or simply a time-frequency map.

Now, how to read the map. A warm patch, between eight and twelve hertz, from zero to five hundred milliseconds, means alpha power increased at that time. A cool patch, between fifteen and twenty-five hertz, around a movement, means beta power decreased. Read it like the score: which frequency plays, when, and for how long.

Three choices shape the map.

First, the number of cycles in each wavelet. Few cycles give good timing but blurry frequency. Many cycles give sharp frequency but blurry timing. A common compromise lets the number of cycles grow with frequency, for example half the frequency in hertz, so that low and high frequencies are treated more evenly. This is the time-frequency trade-off from episode two point seven.

Second, the frequency range and spacing. Logarithmic spacing gives more rows at low frequencies, where the bands are narrow, and fewer at high frequencies.

Third, the edges. A wavelet needs data on both sides. Near the start and end of the epoch, the map is unreliable, and more so at low frequencies, where wavelets are long. This region is sometimes called the cone of influence. So cut epochs longer than the window you want to interpret, and crop the edges after the transform.

There is one problem with the raw map. Low frequencies have much more power than high frequencies: the one over f slope from chapter four. On a raw map, everything above twenty hertz looks dark, and only the slope is visible. To see changes, we need a baseline. That is the next episode.

In M N E, you can compute the map with epochs dot compute T-F-R, using the Morlet method, a list of frequencies and a number of cycles. The result can be averaged across trials and plotted per channel.

To recap. A time-frequency map shows power over time and frequency, like a musical score. We convolve each trial with wavelets, take the power, then average across trials. Power first, average second, so non-phase-locked changes survive. The number of cycles sets the time-frequency trade-off, and edges are unreliable, especially at low frequencies. And the raw map is dominated by one over f, so it needs a baseline.

Next time: baselines and normalization.

Baselines and normalization

A raw time-frequency map mostly shows the one-over-f slope, so each frequency is compared with its own pre-event power, like comparing an orchestra with the hall's background. Decibels are symmetric, percent and ratio are not, and z-scores measure change against baseline variability. Baselines can be contaminated, edge-affected, too short or different across conditions, and the method should be chosen before looking.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Baselines and normalization. The raw time-frequency map shows mostly one thing: the one over f slope. To see what the event changed, we compare each moment to a quiet period before it.

Think of a concert hall before the music starts. People chat, chairs creak, the air conditioning hums. That background is louder in some registers than in others. To hear what the orchestra adds, you compare each moment of the concert to the hall's own background, register by register.

That is the idea of a baseline. For each frequency separately, we compute the average power in a window before the event, for example from minus five hundred to minus one hundred milliseconds. Then we express every point of the map relative to that average, at the same frequency.

Because the comparison is done frequency by frequency, the one over f slope disappears. A ten-hertz increase and a forty-hertz increase become comparable, even though the raw power at forty hertz is much smaller.

There are several ways to express the change. Here is the same map, shown four ways.

Decibels: ten times the logarithm of the power divided by the baseline power. Zero means no change. Plus three decibels means power doubled; minus three means it halved. Decibels treat increases and decreases symmetrically, which is why they are popular.

Percent change: the power minus the baseline, divided by the baseline, times one hundred. Intuitive, but not symmetric: power can rise by several hundred per cent, but never fall by more than one hundred.

Ratio: the power divided by the baseline. One means no change. Simple, but also asymmetric.

Z-score: the power minus the baseline mean, divided by the standard deviation of the baseline. It tells you how unusual each value is compared to the baseline's own variability. It can be computed on single trials, before averaging, which makes it more robust to a few extreme trials, as Grandchamp and Delorme discussed in 2011.

In M N E, the apply baseline method offers these modes: mean, ratio, log ratio, percent, z-score, and z log ratio. Choose one before looking at the data, and report it.

Now, the traps.

Trap one: a contaminated baseline. If the previous trial's response is still going on, or the participant is already anticipating the next stimulus, the baseline is not quiet. Every change after the event is then measured against the wrong reference. A strong alpha decrease in the baseline will make a normal post-stimulus period look like an alpha increase.

Trap two: edge effects in the baseline. As we saw last episode, the start of the epoch is unreliable, especially at low frequencies. If the baseline window sits too close to the start of the epoch, it is computed from distorted values. Make the epoch long enough so that the baseline sits well inside it.

Trap three: a baseline that is too short. At four hertz, one cycle lasts two hundred and fifty milliseconds. A baseline of two hundred milliseconds does not even contain one full cycle. Aim for a baseline that covers several cycles of the lowest frequency you care about, or accept that low frequencies will be noisy.

Trap four: different baselines per condition. If each condition is normalized to its own baseline, and the baselines differ, the normalized maps can differ even when the post-stimulus power is identical. A common alternative is to use a baseline averaged across conditions. Either way, check the raw baseline power in each condition.

One last point: the baseline does not always have to be before the stimulus. For a task with continuous rhythms, or for a movement that is preceded by preparation, a separate rest period can be a better reference. What matters is that it is quiet, relevant, and chosen in advance.

To recap. A baseline removes the one over f slope by comparing each frequency to its own pre-event level. Decibels are symmetric; percent and ratio are not; z-scores measure change against baseline variability and can be computed on single trials. Watch for contaminated, edge-affected, too-short, and condition-specific baselines. And choose the method before you look.

Next time: evoked versus induced activity.

PLATE 9.34:09

Evoked vs induced

A violin tremolo that starts after the beat at a different bow position each night cancels when many nights are overlaid, while a chord on the beat adds up. Evoked activity is time-locked and phase-locked; induced activity is time-locked only. Averaging voltages first keeps evoked power, power first keeps total power, and subtracting the ERP from each trial estimates the induced part.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Evoked versus induced. Two kinds of brain responses that look the same on a single trial, and behave completely differently when you average.

Imagine an orchestra that plays a short chord exactly on the conductor's beat, every time. Now imagine a section of violins that starts a tremolo after the beat, every time, but each night at a slightly different point in their bow stroke. Both happen after the beat. But if you overlay recordings from many nights, the chord adds up perfectly, while the tremolo's waves fall on top of each other at random and cancel.

In EEG, the chord is evoked activity: time-locked and phase-locked to the event. Every trial has the same waveform at the same latency, with the same phase. The tremolo is induced activity: time-locked, because it reliably appears after the event, but not phase-locked, because its phase varies from trial to trial.

Catherine Tallon-Baudry and Olivier Bertrand made this distinction central in a 1999 review on gamma oscillations, and it is now standard.

Now, two ways to average.

Way one: average first, then transform. We average the voltages across trials, which gives the E-R-P. Then we compute the time-frequency map of that E-R-P. Evoked activity survives the voltage averaging. Induced activity cancels out before the transform ever sees it. This map shows evoked power only.

Way two: transform first, then average. We compute the time-frequency map of each trial, take the power, and average the power maps, as in episode nine point one. Power ignores phase, so both evoked and induced activity survive. This map shows total power.

So the same data give two different maps, depending only on the order of the two operations. Averaging first loses the induced activity.

How do we get the induced part alone? A common approach: subtract the E-R-P from every single trial, then compute the time-frequency maps of the residuals, and average their power. What is left is the part of the power that is not explained by the average waveform. In M N E, epochs have a subtract evoked method that does exactly this, before computing the T-F-R.

Why does it matter? Because evoked and induced activity can reflect different processes. A sharp, early, phase-locked response often reflects the arrival of the stimulus in sensory cortex. Later, induced changes in alpha, beta or gamma power often reflect processes whose timing varies from trial to trial, like attention, memory, or preparing a movement.

A few cautions.

The subtraction assumes the E-R-P is identical on every trial. If its amplitude or latency varies, as it usually does, the subtraction is imperfect. Part of the evoked response remains in every trial, and appears in the induced map, especially at low frequencies where the E-R-P has most of its power. David, Kilner and Friston discussed these subtleties in 2006.

Low-frequency power increases in a total power map are often largely evoked: they are the E-R-P itself, seen in frequency. Before interpreting a theta increase as an oscillation, check how much of it is in the evoked map.

And subtract the E-R-P computed in the same condition, and, if you can, the same participant, because the average waveform differs between them.

To recap. Evoked activity is time-locked and phase-locked; induced activity is time-locked but not phase-locked. Averaging voltages first keeps only the evoked part. Computing power first, then averaging, keeps both: total power. Subtracting the E-R-P from each trial estimates the induced part, but variable E-R-Ps leak into it, especially at low frequencies.

Next time: inter-trial coherence, which measures phase consistency directly.

PLATE 9.44:01

Inter-trial coherence · Phase consistency

Inter-trial coherence averages one unit arrow per trial, built from the phase alone; the length of the mean arrow, from zero to one, measures phase consistency. It ignores amplitude, so a phase reset can raise it without any power change. Pure noise gives about 0.9 over the square root of the trial count, so trial numbers must be equalized and values compared with chance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Inter-trial coherence. A measure of one thing only: does the phase line up across trials?

Last time, we saw that evoked activity is phase-locked, and induced activity is not. Inter-trial coherence, or I-T-C, measures phase locking directly, frequency by frequency, moment by moment. You may also meet it as the phase-locking factor, or inter-trial phase clustering.

Think of the musicians of an orchestra, each bowing their violin. At a given moment, each bow is somewhere in its back-and-forth cycle. If they all play in sync, every bow points the same way at the same time. If they play freely, the bows point every which way.

Here is the computation. Pick one frequency and one moment. On each trial, the wavelet gives a complex number. Keep only its phase: draw a small arrow of length one, pointing in the direction of that phase. One arrow per trial.

Now average all the arrows, head to tail, and divide by the number of trials. If the arrows all point in the same direction, the average arrow is long, close to one. If they point in all directions, they cancel, and the average arrow is short, close to zero.

The I-T-C is the length of that average arrow. It runs from zero, no phase consistency, to one, perfect consistency. Compute it for every frequency and every moment, and you get an I-T-C map, the phase twin of the power map.

Notice what I-T-C ignores: the amplitude. Each arrow has length one, whatever the power on that trial. So I-T-C can be high even when power does not change. A stimulus can reset the phase of an ongoing rhythm without making it stronger. And power can increase without any phase locking, as with induced activity.

But here is the trap: the number of trials.

With few trials, even random phases rarely cancel perfectly. With ten trials pointing at random, the average arrow is typically around zero point three long. With a hundred random trials, around zero point zero nine. With a thousand, around zero point zero three. On average, the I-T-C of pure noise is close to zero point nine divided by the square root of the number of trials.

So an I-T-C of zero point three means nothing with ten trials, and a lot with five hundred. And two conditions with different numbers of trials will have different I-T-C values even if their phase locking is identical. This is the same bias we met for the phase-locking value in episode five point three.

What to do about it? Equalize the number of trials across conditions, for example by randomly sub-sampling the larger condition. Compare to what random phases would give for the same number of trials. The Rayleigh test does this analytically: it asks whether the arrows are more concentrated than chance. Or shuffle trials or time points to build a null distribution.

A second caution: when power is very low, the phase is poorly defined, because noise dominates. Phase estimates at frequencies or moments with almost no signal are close to random. Interpret I-T-C where there is some power to measure.

In M N E, the time-frequency functions can return the I-T-C together with the power, by setting return I-T-C to true.

To recap. I-T-C measures phase consistency across trials: one unit arrow per trial, averaged, and the length of the average is the I-T-C. It ignores amplitude, so a phase reset can raise I-T-C without raising power. It is biased by the number of trials: about zero point nine over the square root of N for pure noise. Equalize trial counts and compare to chance. And phases are unreliable where power is very low.

Next time: event-related desynchronization and synchronization, and motor imagery.

PLATE 9.54:40

ERD/ERS and motor imagery

Preparing or imagining a movement lowers mu and beta power over the opposite motor cortex (event-related desynchronization), and beta rebounds afterwards (synchronization), both as percent change from a quiet reference. Imagining the left or right hand shifts power at C4 or C3, so a classifier can steer a cursor: the basis of motor imagery brain-computer interfaces.

TRANSCRIPTEN

E-R-D and E-R-S. Event-related desynchronization and synchronization, and how imagining a movement can move a cursor.

When you prepare and make a movement, the mu and beta rhythms over the motor cortex drop in power. When the movement ends, beta comes back, often stronger than before. These two changes have names.

E-R-D, event-related desynchronization: a decrease in power in a frequency band, relative to a reference period. The name comes from the idea that the neurons stop firing together, so their rhythm fades. Gert Pfurtscheller introduced the measure with Aranibar in 1977.

E-R-S, event-related synchronization: an increase in power relative to the reference. After a movement, the beta rhythm often shows a burst of E-R-S, called the post-movement beta rebound.

Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva summarised the principles in a classic review in 1999. The formula is simple: power during the event, minus power in the reference period, divided by the reference power, times one hundred. Negative values are E-R-D, positive values are E-R-S. If this looks familiar, it is the percent change from episode nine point two, applied to a band.

Think of a choir humming softly before a concert. When the soloist on the right starts to sing, the singers on the left side of the stage fall silent to listen. When the solo ends, they come back louder than before.

In the brain, the stage is crossed. Moving the right hand produces the strongest E-R-D over the left motor cortex, around electrode C3. Moving the left hand produces it over the right motor cortex, around C4. This is called contralateral dominance.

Now the remarkable part: you do not need to move. Simply imagining a movement of the right hand produces a similar, usually weaker, E-R-D over the left motor cortex. Imagining the left hand affects the right side. Imagining the feet affects the midline, around Cz.

This is the basis of motor imagery brain-computer interfaces. A person imagines moving the left or the right hand. A classifier looks at mu and beta power over C3 and C4, and decides which side shows more E-R-D. The decision moves a cursor, a wheelchair, or a robotic arm. The Graz group, led by Pfurtscheller, built some of the first systems of this kind in the nineteen-nineties.

Let's look at a time-frequency map from C3, for imagined right-hand movement. From the cue onwards, a blue patch appears in the mu band around ten hertz, and in the beta band around twenty hertz: E-R-D. After the imagery ends, a red patch in the beta band: the rebound, E-R-S. At C4, the same patches are weaker. Subtract C4 from C3, and the lateralization stands out.

A few practical points. The frequencies vary between people: the most reactive mu and beta bands should be found individually, as in chapter four. The reference period must be quiet, without movement or preparation. Not everyone produces a clear E-R-D during imagery: in brain-computer interface research, a substantial minority of users struggle to control a motor imagery system, at least at first. And muscle activity, even small, contaminates the beta band, so check the E-M-G and the topography.

A good open dataset to practise with is the EEG Motor Movement and Imagery dataset on PhysioNet, available in M N E through the E-E-G-B-C-I module. It contains real and imagined hand and foot movements from over one hundred volunteers.

Finally, a classifier does not have to use only C3 and C4. Spatial filters that maximise the difference in power between two conditions, called common spatial patterns, do this much better. We meet them in episode fourteen point two.

To recap. E-R-D is a power decrease, E-R-S a power increase, relative to a reference, in percent. Movement produces mu and beta E-R-D over the contralateral motor cortex, followed by a beta rebound. Imagining the movement produces a similar, weaker pattern. This lateralization is the basis of motor imagery brain-computer interfaces. Find the reactive bands individually, keep the reference quiet, and watch for muscle activity.

Phase–amplitude coupling

Phase-amplitude coupling means the amplitude of a fast rhythm follows the phase of a slow one, like violins swelling on the first beat of a waltz, as with theta and gamma. Slow phase and fast envelope are extracted, then dependence is quantified with the mean vector length or modulation index. Sharp waveforms, narrow filters and artifacts create false coupling.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Phase-amplitude coupling. When a slow rhythm sets the tempo for a fast one.

So far, we have looked at each frequency on its own. But rhythms can interact. One of the most studied interactions is phase-amplitude coupling, or P-A-C: the amplitude of a fast rhythm goes up and down with the phase of a slow rhythm.

Picture a waltz. The bass plays slowly: one, two, three. On every "one", the violins swell; on "two" and "three", they soften. The violins are fast, but their loudness follows the slow beat of the bass. That is phase-amplitude coupling.

In the brain, a classic example is theta and gamma. In recordings from the human cortex, Ryan Canolty and colleagues reported in 2006 that gamma amplitude was strongest at a particular phase of the theta rhythm. Similar couplings have been described between alpha and gamma, and between delta and faster rhythms during sleep.

Here is how we measure it.

Step one: band-pass filter the signal around the slow frequency, for example theta, and extract its phase with the Hilbert transform, as in episode three point ten.

Step two: band-pass filter around the fast frequency, for example gamma, and extract its amplitude envelope.

Step three: ask whether the fast amplitude depends on the slow phase.

There are two popular ways to answer.

The mean vector length, used by Canolty and colleagues. At each time point, draw an arrow pointing in the direction of the slow phase, with a length equal to the fast amplitude. Average all the arrows. If the fast amplitude is larger at some phases than others, the arrows do not cancel, and the average is long.

The modulation index, proposed by Adriano Tort and colleagues in 2010. Divide the slow cycle into bins, often eighteen of twenty degrees each. In each bin, compute the average fast amplitude. If there is no coupling, the amplitude is the same in every bin: a flat histogram. If there is coupling, the histogram has a hump. The modulation index measures how far the histogram is from flat, using an information-theoretic distance.

You can repeat this for many pairs of slow and fast frequencies, and draw a comodulogram: slow frequency on one axis, fast frequency on the other, and colour for the coupling strength.

Now, P-A-C is famous for its pitfalls. Aru and colleagues reviewed them in 2015. Here are the main ones.

Pitfall one: non-sinusoidal waveforms. As we saw in episode four point twelve, a rhythm with sharp peaks contains harmonics. Those sharp edges create high-frequency power at a fixed phase of the slow rhythm. The measure reports coupling, but there is only one rhythm with an unusual shape. Look at the raw waveform, and use tools like bycycle to check the shape.

Pitfall two: the filter bandwidth. If the fast amplitude really is modulated at the slow frequency, its spectrum has sidebands at the fast frequency plus and minus the slow frequency. The band-pass filter for the fast rhythm must be wide enough to include them, at least twice the slow frequency. A filter that is too narrow removes the very coupling you are looking for.

Pitfall three: no real slow oscillation. If there is no clear peak at the slow frequency, its "phase" is mostly noise, and coupling values are hard to interpret. Check the spectrum first.

Pitfall four: artifacts. Eye movements, muscle bursts and sharp transients produce broadband power locked to slow deflections. Clean the data carefully, and inspect the topography.

And always compare to chance. A common approach is to shift the amplitude time series by a random amount relative to the phase time series, recompute the coupling many times, and see where the real value falls in that surrogate distribution.

In Python, the tensorpac and pactools packages implement these methods, with surrogates and comodulograms.

To recap. Phase-amplitude coupling means the amplitude of a fast rhythm follows the phase of a slow one, like violins swelling on the first beat of a waltz. We extract the slow phase and the fast amplitude, then measure their dependence with the mean vector length or the modulation index. A comodulogram shows many frequency pairs at once. Beware of non-sinusoidal waveforms, narrow filters, absent slow rhythms and artifacts, and always compare to surrogates.
