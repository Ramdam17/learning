Seven bridges, one walk

Königsberg has seven bridges, and nobody can cross each exactly once. Euler's key move was to forget the shape of the city and keep only which lands each bridge connects: four points, seven lines. Every point touches an odd number of bridges, which blocks the walk. Points joined by lines are a graph, found in stations, friendships and neurons.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the seventeen thirties, the Prussian city of Königsberg sat on the river Pregel. The river split around two islands, and seven bridges tied the city together.

Here is the puzzle. Can you take a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once? You may start anywhere and end anywhere. You may not swim, and you may not cross a bridge twice.

Try it. Start on the north bank. Cross to the island, come back, go east, return to the island, then south, then east again. Six bridges, and you are stuck. One bridge is left over. Start somewhere else, and the same thing happens. Search every possible walk, and the best you can do is six bridges out of seven.

The question reached Leonhard Euler, a young mathematician at the Academy of Saint Petersburg. He was told that nobody had found such a walk, and nobody had shown that none exists. In a letter, he called the question "so banal", and yet worthy of attention, because neither geometry, nor algebra, nor even the art of counting was enough to solve it.

His key move was to stop looking at the city. The shape of the island does not matter. The length of a bridge does not matter. Whether the streets are straight or crooked does not matter. Only one thing does: which pieces of land each bridge connects.

So let the city melt. Shrink each piece of land to a single point. Stretch each bridge into a line. Four points, seven lines. Nothing about the walk has changed, and now you can see the whole problem at once.

A small confession about that picture. Euler never drew it. His own figure was a map of the city, with the lands lettered A to D and the bridges a to g. The version with points and lines appears much later, in 1892, in a book of mathematical recreations by Walter William Rouse Ball.

Now count the lines at each point. The island touches five bridges. The other three lands touch three each. Five, three, three, three: every land touches an odd number of bridges. Euler showed that this alone makes the walk impossible. He presented his solution to the Academy on the twenty-sixth of August, 1735, and it was printed in 1741. Why do odd numbers forbid the walk? Keep that question in mind. We will prove it together in chapter five.

Points joined by lines have a name: a graph. And once you have seen one, you start to see them everywhere. Stations, and the tracks between them. Friends, and who knows whom. Neurons, and the synapses that join them. Exams that cannot share a time slot. Wires that meet in an electric circuit. None of these looks like a graph at first. Every one of them is.

That is what this series is about. Each chapter opens with a problem that does not look like a graph. Then we find the hidden graph, and we ask it questions. Which point matters most? Where does the network break? How does something spread across it? To follow the main path, school mathematics is all you need.

So here is the one idea to keep: forget the shape, and keep what touches what.

Next time, we give the picture its words: dots and lines.
