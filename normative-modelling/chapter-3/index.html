A distribution, not a number

At one age, a population hands over a whole distribution rather than a single number. Mean, median and standard deviation summarise its shape, and a Gaussian bell is only one possible shape. On simulated data the Gaussian misplaces the 2.5th and 97.5th centile lines, where a clinic looks, so a normative model must predict a full distribution.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we saw that a norm is always the norm of someone. A reference population, chosen by somebody, for some purpose. Today we ask a simpler question. What is a norm made of? What does a population actually hand us?

Welcome to the pumpkin fair. Pumpkins of every size, lined up on shelves. They are only our decor. The numbers in this episode come from a simulated brain measure, a made-up cortical index in arbitrary units. Every point on screen is labelled simulated.

Now line them up by value, from low to high. Stack the ones that fall close together. A histogram appears. Most people pile up near the middle, a few sit far out on the right. Now smooth the stack into a curve. That curve is a density. The area under it is one, and the height tells you where people are most likely to be.

So here is the first idea. At one age, there is no single value. There is a whole shape.

We can describe that shape with a few numbers. The mean is the balance point. For these forty-two people, the mean is one hundred point four. The median is the middle person, half below and half above. It is ninety-nine point seven. The standard deviation measures the typical distance from the mean. Here it is four point nine.

Mean, median and standard deviation. Three numbers. Useful, but they are a summary, not the shape itself.

Now let us lay a Gaussian over the histogram. A Gaussian is the classic bell. It is symmetric, and it is fully described by two numbers, its mean and its standard deviation. We give it the same mean and the same spread as our people.

It fits well in the middle. But look at the right side. The highest person is at one hundred fourteen, nearly three standard deviations above the mean. The bell is a little too polite there.

With only forty-two people, the edges are noisy. So here we cheat, in a good way. This is a simulation, so we know the true shape at age forty, at site A. The true mean is ninety-nine point six, and the true standard deviation is four point eight. The distribution leans to the right. Its mean sits a little above its median, which is ninety-eight point seven.

Now draw the lines that a Gaussian would put at the 2.5th and the 97.5th centiles. As we saw in the growth chart chapter, those two lines should hold ninety-five percent of people between them. A Gaussian puts them at ninety point two, and one hundred nine. The true lines are at ninety-two point six, and one hundred eleven point one.

Both lines are shifted. The bell is in the wrong place for the people at the edges. And the edges are exactly where a clinic looks.

In the Information series, entropy tells us how uncertain we are about the next draw from a distribution. A wide, spread-out shape is a more uncertain one. That is a good way to think about a norm. A norm is not a number. It is a shape that says how uncertain we are about a person.

Here is the key moment. A normative model does not predict a value. It predicts a distribution. When we ask where a person stands, we are asking where that person falls inside a shape.

If we reported only the mean, we could say how far a person is from the middle. But we could not say whether that distance is ordinary or rare. To say that, we need the spread, and the skew, and the tails. We need the whole shape.

And this shape is not fixed. At age eight it is one shape. At age eighty it is another. The rest of this chapter is about describing that shape correctly, at every age.

So, to sum up. At each age, a population gives us a distribution, not a number. Mean, median and standard deviation summarise it, and a Gaussian is only one possible shape.

Conditional mean, conditional variance

Slicing the data by age gives a different distribution in each slice, with its own centre and its own width. The centre is the conditional mean, and the width is the conditional variance, and each follows its own curve. The same nine-unit gap is a rare z-score of minus 2.3 at age twenty-five but an ordinary minus 1.3 at seventy-five.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that a normative model does not predict a number. It predicts a distribution. But that was a distribution at one age. Today we ask what happens to it when the age changes.

Back to the pumpkin fair, but seen from above. Age runs along the bottom, from eight to eighty. These dots are a simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. It is one simulated site, site A, with two hundred and forty people. Each dot is one person.

Last time, we lined up the pumpkins of a single age on a shelf. Now the shelf is the whole fair. Let us cut it into slices, one slice for each stretch of age.

Take the slice from twenty to thirty. Thirty-four people. Turn it on its side, and stack them. A histogram appears. Its middle sits near one hundred and two. Its spread, the standard deviation, is three point four.

Now take the slice from sixty to seventy. Thirty-eight people. Turn it on its side. Its middle has slid down, to about ninety-four. And it is wider. The spread is six point four. Almost twice as wide as the young slice.

Two slices. Two bells. One has moved, and the other has widened. Cut the fair into every slice you like, and the same thing happens. The middles line up along one curve. The widths line up along another.

Let us give this a name. We call the age x, and the index y. In a slice, there is no single distribution of y. There is one distribution for each age. We write it p of y given x. The bar reads: among the people whose age is x.

Its centre has a name. The conditional mean. It is the average of y among people of age x, and we write it the expectation of y given x. Look at the word given. It says that the answer depends on x.

Its width has a name too. The conditional variance. It is the average squared distance between y and that conditional mean, again among people of age x. We write it the variance of y given x. Take its square root, and you get the conditional standard deviation, the width you just saw in each slice.

Conditional only means given the age. Nothing more mysterious than that.

One note on the slicing. It is a picture, not the method. A slice of thirty people gives a wobbly estimate, and a slice that is too wide mixes ages, so that the slope of the curve inflates the width. The models of chapter four will estimate both curves without cutting anything.

Here is the key moment. These are two different curves, and a normative model must estimate both.

In this simulation we know the truth, because we built it. The conditional mean rises fast in childhood, from one hundred and twenty-two at age eight, to a plateau near one hundred. Then it declines, down to eighty-seven point five at eighty. The conditional standard deviation tells another story. It grows steadily, from three at age eight, to seven at eighty. The middle goes up, then down. The spread only goes up.

Why does the second curve matter? Meet two people. One is twenty-five, the other seventy-five. Each sits nine units below the middle of people their age. Same distance.

At twenty-five, the true spread is about three point nine. Nine units is more than two standard deviations. The z-score is minus two point three. This person is unusual.

At seventy-five, the true spread is about six point seven. Nine units is only one point three standard deviations. The z-score is minus one point three. This person is well inside the crowd.

Same distance from the middle. Two very different verdicts. A model that gives only the first curve sees nine and nine. It cannot tell the two apart. It cannot say whether a person is unusual.

So the z-score of episode two point two gets a more careful form. The person's value, minus the conditional mean, divided by the conditional standard deviation. Both are functions of age. The normative model of Marquand and colleagues, in 2016, was built this way. For each person, it predicts an expected value and an expected level of variation.

So, to sum up. At each age there is a distribution. Its middle is the conditional mean, its width is the conditional variance, and each moves along its own curve. A normative model needs both.

PLATE 3.35:04

Regression, the line through the clouds

Regression finds the curve through the middle of the cloud by minimising squared residuals, written as y equals X times beta plus epsilon. Adding columns to X, such as age squared and cubed, bends the curve without changing the method. Too many powers make the edges swing wildly, which motivates local B-spline columns for the data-poor ends of the age range.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we sliced the pumpkin fair by age. Each slice had its own mean and its own spread. Two curves, and a normative model has to estimate both. Today we draw the first one. A line through the clouds.

Here is the fair, from above. Each dot is a simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units, against age. Two hundred and forty people, all from one site, aged from about nine to about seventy-seven. Every dot is simulated. None of it is a real person.

The cloud is wide. At any age, people differ by several units. But the cloud also leans. Younger people sit higher, older people sit lower. We want one curve that follows the middle of the cloud.

Start with the simplest curve. A straight line. Two numbers describe it. A starting height, and a slope. Move the line up and down, tilt it, and it passes close to some dots and far from others.

How do we pick the best line? Look at one dot. Draw a short vertical stroke from the dot down to the line. That stroke is the residual. It is what the line fails to explain for that person.

Now square every residual, and add them up. The best line is the one that makes this sum as small as possible. This is called least squares. The French mathematician Legendre published it in eighteen oh five, in an appendix about the orbits of comets.

Here is the fitted line. At age thirty it sits near one hundred and two. At age sixty it sits near ninety-five. And the typical residual is about five units. Nobody lies exactly on the line, and nobody should.

Now we write the whole thing as one equation.

y equals X times beta, plus epsilon.

Let us read it term by term. Small y is the list of measurements, one per person. Beta is the list of numbers we want to find. Epsilon is the leftover, the residuals, the part that is not the line. And capital X is a table, with one row per person.

For a straight line, X has two columns. A column of ones, and a column holding each person's age. Multiply the table by beta, and you get the line's height for every person at once.

Now look at the line again. It is straight, but the cloud is not. Young people rise quickly, then the cloud flattens, then it declines. Because this fit is simulated, we know the true curve. At age ten, the true middle is about one hundred and sixteen. The line says one hundred and seven. It misses by nine units, in the young people, and a little in the oldest.

So we bend the curve. How? Add a column to X. A column holding age squared. Then another, with age cubed. The curve now has room to bend.

Here is the key moment. This curve is no longer straight in age. But the equation did not change. It is still y equals X times beta, plus epsilon. The curve is nonlinear in age, and still linear in beta. We did not change the method. We only changed the columns of X.

That is why the same least squares works for any such curve. Same recipe, richer table.

Let us watch what the extra columns buy. With the cubic, the fit at age ten moves up to about one hundred and eleven, closer to the truth of one hundred and sixteen. At age eighty, the line says ninety, and the cubic says eighty-eight, close to the true eighty-seven and a half.

Notice what did not move. The typical residual stays close to five units. The cloud is wide, and no curve can make people alike. The curve only decides where the middle is.

Why not keep going? Add a column for every power, up to the tenth. The curve will wiggle through the cloud. Here is what happens. In the middle, it looks fine. But at the edges, it swings. At age ten it overshoots, at about one hundred and twenty-two. And at age eighty, where this site has nobody, it plunges to about sixty-two. The true value is near eighty-seven.

A high-degree polynomial is flexible everywhere at once. One person at one edge can bend the curve at the other edge. And a normative model lives at the edges, because that is where unusual people are. We will meet this trap again in chapter eleven.

The fix is not a new method. It is the same idea, with better columns. Columns that are local. Each one is a small bump, active in only one stretch of age. Those are the B-splines of chapter four.

A word on names. The word regression itself comes from Galton. In eighteen eighty-six, he described the heights of children moving back toward the average. The name stuck.

So, to sum up. A regression finds the best curve by minimising squared residuals. Changing the columns of X bends the curve, without changing the method. And too many powers of age make the edges swing.

When the spread changes: heteroscedasticity

The spread of people grows with age, making the cloud a trumpet, a pattern called heteroscedasticity. A single constant spread gives bands too wide for the young and too narrow for the old, so false deviations pile up at old ages unseen. The fix lets the spread be a curve of age too, with its own model.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we drew a line through the clouds, and then we bent it, so that it follows the middle of the crowd as age goes by. A line gives us the middle. Today we look at the edges. And the edges depend on how wide the crowd is, at each age.

Back at the pumpkin fair. The pumpkins are only decor. The dots are a simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. It is simulated site A, two hundred and forty people, from age eight to age eighty. Every dot is labelled simulated.

Statisticians have a long word for this. Heteroscedasticity. Karl Pearson coined it in nineteen oh five, from Greek words for different, and scatter. Its opposite is homoscedasticity, the same scatter everywhere. A long word, for a simple idea. The spread depends on x.

Now let us see what happens when a model ignores it. Take the plain model of the last episodes. A curve for the middle, and one single spread for everybody. To be fair to it, we give it the true curve for the middle. Only the spread can be wrong.

Now compare the band with the truth. At age eight, ten units is more than three true standard deviations. The band is far too wide. At age eighty, it is only one point four. The band is far too narrow. It is right at one age only, near forty.

The rate of false deviations depends on age, and nobody sees it.

One more hint, for the next episode. All five of those people sit above the band, none below. The trumpet is not the only thing bending the bell.

How do we spot this in our own data? Plot the size of the residuals against age. If the dots fan out like a trumpet, the spread is changing.

And how do we fix it? We let the spread be a curve too. Sigma of x. Then the score becomes y, minus the predicted value, divided by sigma of x. A different ruler at each age. One simple way is to model the logarithm of the spread, which keeps it positive. This is the second curve of episode three point two, the conditional variance, now doing real work.

Not every model can do this. The basic Bayesian linear regression of chapter four has one constant spread, and has to be helped. Models of the GAMLSS family, introduced by Rigby and Stasinopoulos in two thousand and five, let every parameter of the distribution depend on age, the spread included. We meet them in episode four point seven.

A bridge, for those who followed the Information series. A wider shape has more entropy. The old distribution is more uncertain than the young one, and a fair score has to know it.

So, to sum up. The spread between people grows with age. One constant spread is too wide for the young and too narrow for the old, so false deviations pile up at old ages, unseen. A norm needs two curves, the middle and the spread.

Skew and tails: skewness and kurtosis

A bell can lean and have heavy tails, measured by skewness and excess kurtosis. Kurtosis is best read as tail extremity, not peak sharpness. On a skewed distribution a z-score of two does not have the same rarity on both sides, so a Gaussian band flags people too often on one side and hides unusual people on the other.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that the spread of people grows with age. A band that was too wide for the young, and too narrow for the old. And we left a hint. Every person outside the band sat above it. None sat below. Today we find out why. The bell does not only get wider. It leans.

Back at the pumpkin fair. The pumpkins are only decor. The dots are a simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. Every dot is labelled simulated.

Take one age, the oldest, eighty. Stack the people on a shelf, in order of their value. Most pile up on the left. A few giants stretch the pile out to the right. A long tail on one side, a short tail on the other. The giants drag the mean toward them. In simulated site A, the mean is eighty-seven point five. The median is eighty-five point seven.

So the mean and the standard deviation are not enough. Two crowds can share both, and still look nothing alike. We need two more numbers, for the shape. Skewness, and kurtosis.

First, skewness. Turn each person into a z-score. Their value, minus the mean, divided by the standard deviation. Now cube every z-score, and take the average.

Why the cube? Because a cube keeps the sign. On a symmetric bell, the positives and the negatives cancel, and the average is zero. A Gaussian has a skewness of zero. A positive skewness means a long tail on the right. A negative one, a long tail on the left.

In this simulation, we know the truth. The skewness is zero point seven one at age eight, and one point two two at age eighty. The lean grows with age, like the spread. The noise comes from a family of shapes called sinh-arcsinh, described by Jones and Pewsey in two thousand and nine. One knob sets the lean, another the weight of the tails. We meet it again in episode four point four.

Now, kurtosis. Same recipe, one power higher. Raise each z-score to the fourth power, and take the average. For a Gaussian, the answer is three. Most people subtract three, so that the Gaussian sits at zero. That is excess kurtosis, and it is the one this series uses. When you read a paper, check which convention it follows.

You may have read that kurtosis measures how pointy the peak is. In two thousand and fourteen, the statistician Peter Westfall argued that this is a trap. Its only clear meaning is the extremity of the tails. The fourth power makes far-away people count for a great deal. A person at four standard deviations adds two hundred and fifty-six. A person at one adds one. Positive excess kurtosis means more people far from the mean than a Gaussian predicts.

An illustrative example, not data. A Student t with five degrees of freedom, scaled to the same standard deviation as a Gaussian. Its excess kurtosis is six. Beyond three standard deviations above the mean, the Gaussian puts about one person in seven hundred and forty. This shape puts about one in a hundred and seventy. Four times as many. In the middle, the two bells look almost alike. The difference lives in the tails.

In our simulation, the true excess kurtosis is zero point seven six at age eight, and one point seven six at age eighty. Slightly heavy tails. Take the one hundred and twenty-eight people aged sixty or older, from the three simulated sites, each scored against the true curves of their own site. The sample skewness is one point zero seven, close to the truth. The excess kurtosis is one point one, rougher than the truth, because a few people decide it.

Here is the key moment. The deviations that matter are the extreme ones, far out in a tail. And that is exactly where the Gaussian is least reliable.

Go back to age eighty, and read a z-score of plus two. On a Gaussian, that is the ninety-seven point seven centile, with about one person in forty-four above it. Here, it is the ninety-five point one centile. About one person in twenty lies above it. Twice as many as we think.

Now a z-score of minus two. Here, about one person in fifteen thousand lies below it. Almost nobody gets there.

With the centiles themselves, it is the same story. The two point five centile sits only one point three standard deviations below the mean, at seventy-eight point four. The ninety-seven point five centile sits two point five standard deviations above, at one hundred and five. A Gaussian band would run from seventy-three point eight to one hundred and one point two. The true band is shifted to the right, and it is not symmetric.

Among our one hundred and twenty-eight older people, five are above plus two, and none below. The same lean as last time. On the right, people are flagged too often. On the left, a truly unusual person hides inside the band.

So what can we do? Two roads. We can bend the scale first, until the bell is symmetric, and then compute the z-score. That is the warped model, in episode four point four. Or we can let the shape itself depend on age, not only the middle and the spread. Models of the GAMLSS family do that, in episode four point seven.

So, to sum up. Skewness measures the lean of the bell, and kurtosis the weight of its tails. A z-score of two does not have the same rarity on both sides of a skewed distribution.

Two kinds of uncertainty

Two kinds of uncertainty look alike on a chart. The yellow band is real variability between people, which more data never removes, while the blue band is what the model does not know about the curve and shrinks as data are added. A new person's variance is the sum of the two, and an honest z-score divides by both.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we looked at the shape of the bell. Skew, and heavy tails. Whatever the shape, we treated the spread of people at one age as a single thing. Today we split it in two. Two kinds of uncertainty, that look alike on a chart, and are not alike at all.

Back to the pumpkin fair. These dots are a simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. It is one simulated site, called site A, with two hundred and forty people, from children to people in their seventies. Each dot is one person.

Let us fit a smooth curve through the middle of the cloud. To keep the picture clean, we use a plain curve, with one constant spread. We know from episode three point four that the real spread grows with age. We set that aside for now, so that we can look at one thing at a time.

Around the curve, a first band appears. Pale yellow. It is about ten units above and below the curve, wide enough to hold ninety-five percent of the people. Most dots sit inside it. A few stray outside.

Now bring more pumpkins. Double the crowd. Ten times the crowd. The yellow band does not shrink. It stays about ten units wide. People did not become more alike because we measured more of them.

That band is the first kind of uncertainty. Aleatoric uncertainty. It is the real variability between people. It belongs to the population, not to us. Measuring better, or measuring more, will describe it better, but it will never make it go away.

Now a second question. Never mind the people. How sure are we about the curve itself?

A new band appears, blue, with dashes, and MIST drifts in with it. This one is not about people. It is about our estimate of the middle.

Look at age forty. Twenty-six people in site A are within three years of it. Here the curve is pinned down well, to about one unit either way. The blue band is thin.

Now look at age seventy-eight. Only five people are within three years of it. The curve is far less sure of itself there. About three units either way. The blue band opens up, and MIST thickens.

The same happens at age ten, where only three people are close by. The blue band is wide on both edges, and thin in the middle. It follows the data. Where the data thin out, the band opens.

And this band does shrink. Add pumpkins at the edges, and it closes. This is the second kind of uncertainty. Epistemic uncertainty. It is what the model does not know, for lack of data. A simple test, from the engineering literature: if more data could reduce it, it is epistemic.

Let us give the two bands their proper names in statistics.

The blue band is a confidence interval, for the mean. It answers one question. Where is the average person of this age?

The yellow band is a prediction interval, for a new observation. It answers another. Where will the next person of this age land?

The prediction interval is the wider one, always, because it has to carry both uncertainties. And they add up, as variances. Written out, the variance of a new person is sigma squared, the spread between people, plus s squared of x, the doubt about the curve at that age.

Let us read it on the fair. At age forty, the confidence interval is about one unit either way. The prediction interval is almost ten. Ten squared is a hundred. One squared is one. A hundred and one has nearly the same square root, so the blue barely moves the total.

At age seventy-eight, the confidence interval is about three units either way, and the prediction interval is about ten. Even at the edge, the yellow band dominates.

So does the blue band matter? Yes, and here is when. It shrinks like one over the square root of the number of people. With thousands of people in the reference, it nearly vanishes. With a few dozen, for example at a small new site, it grows, and it can no longer be ignored. We will meet that case in chapter five.

Here is the key moment. A z-score has the form: the person's value, minus the predicted value, divided by a spread. Which spread? The honest one divides by both. The square root of sigma squared plus s squared of x.

The normative modelling paper of Marquand and colleagues, in 2016, builds the individual score in the same spirit. It puts the variance of the reference data and the predictive variance of the model together, in one denominator.

Watch what changes. At age seventy-eight, the honest denominator is about four percent larger than the one that only looks at people. Small, but it always pushes in the same direction. A person at the edge of the data looks a little less extreme than the naive score says. And that is fair, because we know a little less there.

One warning. The blue band measures only the doubt that the model can express. If the curve has the wrong shape, the blue band does not know. It can be thin, and wrong. The band tells us how much the data constrain the model, not whether the model is right. That is the job of chapter seven.

A bridge, for those who followed the Information series. There, the entropy of a prediction splits into two parts. The expected entropy of the model's predictions, which is the aleatoric part. And the mutual information between the parameters and the prediction, which is the epistemic part. Same split, in the language of information.

In practice, both bands come out of one object, the posterior predictive distribution. Episode three point seven shows how, with a single picture.

So, to sum up. The yellow band is the variability between people, and no amount of data removes it. The blue band is what the model does not know, and more data shrinks it. An honest z-score divides by both.

Bayes in one picture

A prior fan of possible lines is multiplied by the likelihood of each arriving dot, and the posterior fan tightens around the lines the data prefer. Letting every line vote gives the posterior predictive distribution, which carries the spread between people and the doubt about the curve together. With few data the prior matters, so it must be chosen and reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we split the uncertainty in two. A yellow band for the variability between people, and a blue band for what the model does not know. We ended with a promise. Both bands come out of one object, the posterior predictive distribution. Today we build that object, with a single picture.

Now multiply. For each line, take how plausible it was before, and multiply by how well it explains the dot. Lines that were plausible and fit well become more plausible. Lines that were plausible but fit badly fade. In words, prior times likelihood gives posterior.

The posterior is the same fan, redrawn. After one pumpkin, it has barely changed. One dot cannot say much.

Pumpkins keep arriving. A second. A fifth. Twenty. After each one, the old posterior becomes the new prior, and the fan redraws itself. The lines bunch around the ones that the data like. After the whole crowd of two hundred and forty people from site A, the fan is a thin ribbon.

Here is the rule that makes this work, written in plain symbols. The posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior. Nothing else. For a straight line with Gaussian noise and a Gaussian prior, the posterior turns out to be a Gaussian too, so the whole fan can be written with two ingredients: a best guess for the coefficients, and how far they can wander around it.

Now the question that matters for normative modelling. A new pumpkin is coming. Where will it land?

Take one line from the fan. It says: the new pumpkin lands near me, give or take the spread between people. Another line says the same, around a slightly different place. We do not know which line is the right one, so we do not choose. We let every line vote, each one weighted by its posterior. The result is a distribution for the new pumpkin. That is the posterior predictive distribution.

Look at what it is made of. Its centre is the average of the lines. Its width has two parts. The first part is the spread between people, the noise around each line. That is the yellow band. The second part is the disagreement between the lines in the fan. That is the blue band.

Written out, the variance of a new pumpkin at age x is sigma squared, plus s squared of x. It is the same sum as last time. But now we can see where it comes from. Sigma squared is the noise that every line carries. S squared of x is how far the lines disagree at that age. For a linear model with Gaussian noise, this sum is a textbook result.

Here is the key moment. We never added the two uncertainties by hand. The posterior predictive distribution carries both of them from the start. Averaging over the lines gives the blue part. The noise around each line gives the yellow part. That is why several models of chapter four are Bayesian. They do not need a separate recipe for the honest z-score. It comes with the distribution.

Add data at the edges, and the fan pinches there, so the blue band closes. The yellow band does not move.

One more thing, and it is a decision, not a detail. The prior. Run the picture again with two priors, and only five pumpkins. With a wide, vague prior, the fan follows those five dots almost wherever they lead. With a narrow prior, tightly centred on a flat line, the fan stays flat, and five dots can barely tilt it. Same pumpkins, two different answers.

Now go back to the full crowd of two hundred and forty. The two fans nearly merge. With enough data, the likelihood wins, and the prior is forgotten. But a small site, like the new site we will meet in chapter five, has few pumpkins. There, the prior is a real part of the model. So it must be chosen on purpose, and reported.

A bridge, for those who followed the Information series. How much did the data change our mind? That can be measured. It is the divergence between the posterior and the prior, the information that the pumpkins gave us. Few dots, little information. Many dots, much.

One warning. The fan can be thin and the model can still be wrong. If the true curve bends, and we draw a straight line, the posterior will be confident about the best straight line, not about the truth. Whether the model is right is the job of chapter seven.

So, to sum up. The prior is what we believe before the data. The likelihood is what the data say. Together they give the posterior, a fan of plausible lines that tightens as pumpkins arrive. Letting every line vote gives the posterior predictive distribution, which carries the spread between people and the doubt about the curve in one object.
