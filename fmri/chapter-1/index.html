PLATE 01:09

Teaser · A magnet that watches you think

A giant magnet lines up hydrogen nuclei, a radio pulse tips them over, and the faint signal they emit becomes an image. When a finger taps, fresh blood rushes to the motor cortex and the signal rises by a few percent: the BOLD effect. Functional MRI sees the whole brain, deep structures included, but it is slow and indirect.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Lie down. Slide in. Hold still. All around you, a magnet about 60,000 times stronger than the Earth's field is quietly humming.

Inside your head, billions of hydrogen nuclei spin like tiny tops. The magnet lines them up. A pulse of radio waves tips them over. As they settle back, they send out a faint signal, and we turn it into an image.

Now tap your finger. A few seconds later, fresh blood rushes to your motor cortex. Oxygen-poor blood blurs the signal. Fresh blood washes it out so the signal rises by just a few percent. That's BOLD.

This is functional MRI. It sees the whole brain, deep structures included, down to a few millimeters. But it is slow, it is indirect, and between the scanner and the final map there are many choices.

In this series, we follow the signal all the way. From spin to image to brain map to a result you can trust. Why it works, what it measures, how to clean it, and how not to fool yourself.

Ready? Let's step inside the magnet.

PLATE 1.13:13

The scanner, exploded

The scanner is taken apart ring by ring like nesting dolls. A superconducting magnet, cooled with liquid helium, stays on permanently at about 60,000 times the Earth's field. Inside sit shim coils, three gradient coils that give each position its own field strength and make the noise, a radio coil that sends pulses and a head coil that listens.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the magnet. Today we take the scanner apart one ring at a time, like a set of nesting dolls. Each ring has a job, and knowing these jobs will explain a lot of what you see later in your data.

From the outside, an MRI scanner looks like a giant donut. Under the plastic cover, the biggest ring is the main magnet. It is a coil of niobium titanium wire, many kilometers long, wound around the bore. Cooled with liquid helium to about 4 Kelvin, around minus 269 degrees Celsius, the wire becomes superconducting. The current flows with no resistance at all. So once the magnet is charged, it stays on. Day and night, even when nobody is scanning.

Its field is called B0. A research scanner typically runs at 3 Tesla. The Earth's field, the one that moves your compass, is about 50 micro Tesla. So the magnet is roughly 60,000 times stronger. That is why nothing made of iron ever enters the room.

Around the coil sits a giant vacuum flask, the cryostat, that keeps the helium cold. If the coil ever warms up and loses superconductivity, the helium boils off all at once. This is called a quench. The gas is vented out of the building through a dedicated pipe.

Just inside the magnet sit the shim coils. They fine-tune the field because the signal we measure is very sensitive to tiny bumps in it. Before every session, the scanner runs a quick shimming step around the participant's head since the head itself bends the field a little.

Next ring is the gradient coils. There are three of them, one for each direction, X, Y, and Z. They are ordinary copper coils at room temperature. Each one adds a small linear slope to the main field. That slope gives every position in the brain its own field strength, and so its own identity. We will see how in episode 1.5, switching these coils on and off very fast makes the famous knocking and buzzing. It is loud, so everyone in the scanner wears ear protection.

Inside the gradients comes the radio frequency coil. It sends short pulses of radio waves that tip the hydrogen spins over. Then closest to your head sits the head coil, an array of many small receiving antennas. Each one listens best to the brain region right next to it. Used together, they boost the signal and let us image faster.

Finally, the whole scanner lives in a shielded room. A copper cage hidden in the walls keeps radio stations and phones from leaking into the tiny brain signal. The computer and the console stay outside. Inside, the participant sees the task through a small mirror and answers with a button box. A technician watches through a window and talks through an intercom. And the participant always holds a squeeze ball to call for help at any moment.

So from the outside in. A superconducting magnet that never sleeps. Three gradient coils that make the noise. A radio coil that sends the pulses. And a head coil that listens. Next time we leave the hardware and travel back in time, with a short history of magnetic brains.

PLATE 1.24:08

A short history of magnetic brains

A carousel ride through a century of discoveries leads to functional MRI. Blood-flow control (1890), nuclear magnetic resonance (1946), gradient-based imaging by Lauterbur and Mansfield, and Ogawa's observation that deoxygenated blood darkens images come together. A first injected-contrast brain map in 1991 is followed in 1992 by human BOLD studies, where the blood itself becomes the tracer.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Hop in the carousel. Each seat is a moment in the history of functional MRI. The ride takes about a hundred years from a simple idea about blood to the scanners we use today.

First seat 1890. Two physiologists, Charles Roy and Charles Sherrington, suggest that the brain adjusts its own blood supply. When a region works harder, more blood flows to it. Keep this idea in your pocket. It will come back at the very end of the ride.

Next seat, the 1930s. In New York, Isidor Rabi measures the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei using beams of molecules. Nuclei behave like tiny magnets. He receives the Nobel Prize in physics in 1944.

1946. Two teams, Felix Bloch at Stanford and Edward Purcell at Harvard, independently discover nuclear magnetic resonance in ordinary matter. Put the sample in a magnet, send the right radio wave and the nuclei answer back. They share the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1952. For two decades, this resonance is used to study molecules, not to make pictures.

1973. The chemist Paul Lauterbur has a simple and brilliant idea. Add a gradient to the magnetic field so that each position resonates at its own frequency. Then position becomes something you can measure. His first image, published in Nature, shows two small tubes of water. He calls the method zeugmatography, from a Greek word for joining together. Luckily the name did not stick.

Meanwhile, in Nottingham, the physicist Peter Mansfield works out how to analyze these gradient signals quickly. In 1977, his team images a human finger. He also proposes echoplanar imaging, a way to capture a whole image in a fraction of a second. It becomes practical in hospitals about a decade later. In 2003, Lauterbur and Mansfield share the Nobel Prize in Medicine.

Through the 1980s, MRI spreads through hospitals, mostly to look at anatomy. But brain function still belongs to another technique, positron emission tomography, which needs a radioactive tracer.

1990. At Bell Laboratories, Seiji Ogawa scans rats in a 7-Tesla magnet. He notices that blood vessels look darker when the blood carries less oxygen. Deoxygenated blood disturbs the magnetic field around it. He calls this contrast blood oxygenation level dependent. BOLD for short.

1991. In Boston, Jack Belliveau and his colleagues inject a contrast agent and watch it flow through the visual cortex while volunteers look at a flashing checkerboard. It is the first functional map of the human brain made with MRI. It makes the cover of Science, but it still needs an injection.

1992. Three teams publish human BOLD studies within a few months. Kenneth Kwong and colleagues in Boston, Ogawa with the Minnesota team at 4 Tesla, and Peter Bandettini and colleagues in Milwaukee. No injection, no radioactivity. The blood itself becomes the tracer. Remember Roy and Sherrington? Their idea about blood flow, a century old, is now something we can watch.

From there, the carousel speeds up. Faster sequences, more channels in the head coil, several slices excited at once. In 2017, the first 7-Tesla scanner is cleared for clinical use in the United States. And research magnets now go above 10 Tesla. Huge open data sets shared by thousands of participants change how studies are done.

So the ride in one breath. Blood follows brain activity. Nuclei are tiny magnets that resonate. Gradients turn resonance into images. Echo planar imaging makes them fast. And oxygen in the blood makes them functional.

Next time we shrink down to the size of a nucleus and watch the spinning tops themselves.

PLATE 1.33:36

Spinning tops · Spins and precession

Hydrogen protons act as tiny spinning tops that are also magnets. In a strong field a slight excess lines up and forms a net magnetization, which precesses at the Larmor frequency, about 128 MHz at 3 Tesla. A radio pulse at exactly that frequency, like pushing a swing at the right rhythm, tips it over and produces the signal that every image starts from.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we said that nuclei behave like tiny magnets. Today, we zoom in on one of them and watch it dance. Our dancer is the nucleus of hydrogen, a single proton. It is everywhere in the brain, mostly in water. A single gram of water holds about 67,000 billion billion of them.

A proton has a property called spin. Think of it as a tiny spinning top that is also a magnet, with a north pole and a south pole. Outside the scanner, these tops point in every direction at random. Their little magnetic fields cancel out and the brain as a whole is not magnetic.

Now slide into the magnet. Inside B0, the tops feel a pull. A few more of them settle along the field than against it. Very few. At 3 Tesla, only about 10 extra protons per million. But there are so many protons that those few add up to a small net magnetization pointing along the field. We draw it as one big arrow. From now on, that arrow is what we follow.

Here is the surprise. The tops do not simply line up and stop. Like a real top tilted on a table, they wobble around the field direction. Their axis traces a cone again and again. This motion is called precession. Think of the swing ride at the fair, where the chairs fly round and round the central pole.

How fast do they go round? The Larmor equation tells us. The frequency equals a constant, called the gyromagnetic ratio, times the field strength. For hydrogen, the constant is about 42.6 MHz per Tesla. So at 3 Tesla, protons precess about 128 million times per second. Double the field and they turn twice as fast. That frequency falls in the radio range, close to FM radio stations. This is why MRI talks to the body with radio waves.

So how do we tip the big arrow over? We send a radio pulse, a second, small magnetic field that rotates at exactly the Larmor frequency. Think of pushing a child on a swing. Push at the right rhythm and each push adds up. Push at the wrong rhythm and almost nothing happens. That is resonance. Only the protons that turn at the pulse's frequency respond.

During the pulse, the big arrow spirals away from the field. A short pulse tips it a little. A longer or stronger pulse tips it further. Tip it by 90 degrees and the arrow now lies flat, turning in the horizontal plane. This angle is called the flip angle.

A magnet turning in a plane is exactly what an antenna can hear. As the arrow sweeps past the receiving coils, it induces a tiny oscillating current, a radio signal at the Larmor frequency. That is the raw signal of MRI. Every image in this series starts right here.

One honest note, real protons are quantum objects and a single proton does not really wobble like a toy. But the net magnetization of billions of them behaves exactly like our classical arrow. So the picture is safe to use.

To sum up, hydrogen protons are tiny magnets. In the field, a tiny excess lines up and forms a net magnetization. It precesses at the Larmor frequency, about 128 MHz at 3 Tesla. A radio pulse at that exact frequency tips it over, and the tipped arrow sends back a signal. Next time, we watch what happens after the pulse when the arrow finds its way home. Relaxation.

PLATE 1.43:34

Relaxation · T1, T2 and T2*

After a radio pulse, the tipped magnetization relaxes in two ways. T1 is the slow regrowth along the field that gives anatomical contrast, while T2 is the loss of signal as spins drift out of step like horses in a carousel race. Field bumps make this fading faster, which defines T2 star, and because deoxygenated blood adds bumps, it is the door into brain function.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a radio pulse tipped our big arrow into the horizontal plane, where it sent out its radio signal. But the song does not last. Two things happen at the same time, and they have names — T1 and T2. Today we watch both. And we meet a third one, T2 star, the one that makes functional MRI possible.

Start with T1. After the pulse, the spins slowly hand their energy back to the molecules around them, and the arrow stands up again along B0. Like a roly-poly toy that you push over, and that rocks back upright all on its own. The recovery follows a smooth curve. T1 is the time it takes to get about 63% of the way back.

Different tissues recover at different speeds. At 3 Tesla, white matter takes roughly 800 milliseconds, gray matter about 1.3 seconds, and the fluid around the brain several seconds. Take a picture before everyone has recovered, and white matter looks bright while fluid looks dark. That is a T1-weighted image, the classic anatomical scan of every fMRI session.

Now T2. Right after the pulse, all the spins turn together, in step, like horses in a carousel race that start side by side. But each spin feels slightly different neighbors, and so turns at a slightly different speed. Little by little, they drift apart. Some run ahead, others fall behind. As they spread around the circle, their small arrows cancel out, and the signal in the plane fades away. T2 is the time constant of that fading.

T2 is much faster than T1. In brain tissue, it is in the range of tens of milliseconds, and much longer in fluid. Take a picture at the right moment, and the fluid shines brightly. That is a T2-weighted image.

But real magnets are never perfectly even. Tiny bumps in the field from the hardware from air in the sinuses from the tissue itself make the spins drift apart even faster. The total fading with these bumps included is called T2 star. It is always shorter than T2. In gray matter at 3 Tesla, T2 star is roughly 40 to 50 milliseconds.

Why should we care? Because blood that carries less oxygen brings a little magnetic bump of its own. More deoxygenated blood means more bumps, faster dephasing, a shorter T2 star, and a slightly darker voxel. When fresh, oxygen-rich blood washes in, T2 star gets a little longer and the voxel gets a little brighter. That tiny change is the BOLD signal. This is why functional images are T2 star weighted. At 3 Tesla, they are read out around 30 milliseconds after the pulse, close to the T2 star of gray matter, where this difference is easiest to see.

Keep in mind that both clocks run at once. The arrow shrinks in the plane through T2 star and grows back along the field through T1. The signal in the plane is always gone long before the arrow is fully upright again.

So to sum up, T1, the arrow stands back up in about a second and gives us anatomy. T2, the spins fall off step in tens of milliseconds. T2 star, the same thing, only faster because of bumps in the field and sensitive to oxygen in the blood. That last one is our door into brain function. Next time, we find out where the signal comes from by painting the brain with gradients in k-space.

PLATE 1.54:06

Painting with gradients · k-space

Gradients turn position into pitch. One gradient selects a slice, one gives each column its own frequency and one gives each row its own phase. The measurements fill a grid called k-space, pictured as a quilt of stripe patterns, and a Fourier transform turns it into an image: the centre carries contrast, the edges carry detail.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a puzzle. Inside the scanner, every proton in the head precesses at the same Larmor frequency. So when they send their signal back, we hear one big choir all singing the same note. A beautiful sound, but it tells us nothing about where each voice comes from. To make an image, we need to give every position its own voice. That is the job of the gradients.

Remember the Larmor equation. Frequency equals gamma times the field. A gradient adds a gentle slope to the field, so the field and therefore the frequency now depends on position. A little lower on one side, a little higher on the other. Position becomes pitch. We use this trick three times, once for each direction.

Step 1. Choose a slice. While the radio pulses on, we switch on a gradient along the length of the body. Now only one thin slab has exactly the right frequency to resonate with the pulse. Only those protons tip over. It is like cutting one slice from a loaf of bread. The rest of the loaf stays untouched. A steeper gradient or a narrower pulse gives a thinner slice.

Step 2. Listen along one direction. While we record the signal, we switch on a second gradient from left to right. Now the left side sings low and the right side sings high, like the keys of a piano. The signal we record is a chord. If we split that chord into its notes, each note tells us how much signal comes from each column. This is called frequency encoding.

Step 3. The last direction, from front to back. Here we use a brief gradient before recording. It does not change the pitch for long, but it nudges each row a little ahead or behind in its rotation. This shift is called phase. We then repeat the whole measurement many times, each time with a slightly stronger nudge. This is called phase encoding.

Now, where do these measurements go? Not straight into the picture. Each recording fills one line of a grid. Line after line the grid fills up. This grid is called k-space. Think of it as a quilt. Each patch of the quilt holds one ingredient of the image. A pattern of stripes with a certain direction and a certain fineness. Near the center of the quilt, the stripes are broad and gentle. Out at the edges they are fine and tightly packed.

To get the picture, we add up all these stripe patterns, each with its own strength. Mathematically, this is a two-dimensional Fourier transform. And notice something surprising. Every single patch of the quilt contributes a little to every pixel of the image. There is no one-to-one match between a point in k-space and a point in the brain.

Let us try an experiment. Keep only the center of k-space and throw away the edges. The image comes back blurry, but its brightness and contrast are right. Now do the opposite. Keep only the edges. We get the outlines, the fine details with almost no contrast at all. The center carries the contrast. The edges carry the detail.

Two more rules fall out of the quilt. How far out we go in k-space sets the resolution. The further we go, the finer the details. And how closely the lines are packed sets the field of view. If the lines are too far apart, the picture becomes too small for the head, and the part that sticks out wraps around onto the other side. This is called aliasing or wraparound.

There is one catch. In classic imaging, we collect one line of k-space after each radio pulse. And for a good image, we need many lines. That takes time, often minutes for a single picture. Far too slow to follow a brain at work.

So to sum up. Gradients turn position into frequency and phase. One gradient picks the slice. One gives each column its own pitch. One gives each row its own phase. The measurements fill a quilt called k-space, and a Fourier transform turns it into an image. The center gives contrast, the edges give detail. Next time, we meet the trick that fills the whole quilt in one go. Echo-planar imaging.

PLATE 1.64:12

EPI · A brain every two seconds

Echo-planar imaging fills the whole of k-space in one zigzag ride after a single radio pulse, giving a slice in tens of milliseconds and a whole brain every few seconds, with T2 star weighting built in. Multiband excitation adds speed. The price is distortion and signal dropout near the sinuses and ear canals: a stretched image can be straightened, but a lost signal cannot.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we built an image line by line, one line of k-space after each radio pulse. Beautiful pictures but minutes per image. A brain at work changes within seconds. We need something much faster. Enter Echo-planar imaging or EPI — Peter Mansfield's idea from the late 1970s and the workhorse of almost every fMRI study today.

The idea is simple and a little wild. Send one radio pulse. Then, before the signal fades away, race through the whole of k-space in a single ride. The readout gradient flips back and forth, left, right, left, right, and between each pass, a tiny blip of phase encoding moves us down to the next line. The path looks like a zigzag. Picture a roller coaster swooping across the quilt from top to bottom in one go.

How fast is that? One slice takes a few tens of milliseconds. Then the scanner moves to the next slice and the next until the whole brain is covered. With classic single band EPI, a whole brain at 3 or 4 millimeter voxels takes about 2 to 3 seconds. That time is called the repetition time or TR. It is our sampling rate. One brain volume per TR.

And there is a bonus. Because the ride happens a few tens of milliseconds after the pulse, while the spins are drifting out of step, EPI images are naturally T2 star weighted. Exactly what we need to see the BOLD effect.

Can we go faster still? Yes. Multiband imaging, also called simultaneous multislice, excites several slices at the same time, with one combined pulse. Their signals overlap, and the many small antennas of the head coil help to pull them apart again. Picture several roller coasters, riding on parallel tracks all at once. The Human Connectome Project, for example, used a multiband factor of 8, and acquired the whole brain at 2 millimeter voxels in 720 milliseconds.

But speed has a price, and it is the most important lesson of this episode. Remember the tiny bumps in the field. Near the sinuses and the ear canals, air sits right next to tissue, and the field is badly bent. EPI is very sensitive to this, because the zigzag ride is slow along the phase direction. Small field errors pile up line after line, and pixels end up in the wrong place. The image stretches or squeezes, like your reflection in a funhouse mirror. This is called geometric distortion.

Worse, in the same places, the spins dephase so quickly that the signal is simply gone before we read it. These are signal dropouts. Two regions suffer most, the bottom of the frontal lobe just above the eyes called the orbitofrontal cortex and the inner part of the temporal lobes near the ear canals. Both matter for emotion, reward, and memory. So if your hypothesis lies there, plan for it from the start.

Engineers have a few tricks. Thinner slices, a shorter echo time, smart slice angles, and faster readouts with parallel imaging all reduce the damage. We also collect a field map or two images with opposite phase directions so that distortions can be corrected later. We will see how in the preprocessing chapter. But remember one rule, a stretched image can be straightened, a lost signal cannot be brought back.

Multiband has its own small costs too. Slices excited together can leak into each other a little, and the signal per image is lower. As always in MRI, every choice is a trade-off.

So to sum up, EPI reads a whole slice in one zigzag ride after a single pulse. A whole brain every 1 to 3 seconds, naturally sensitive to BOLD. Multiband reads several slices at once to go even faster. And the price, distortions and dropouts near air, especially in orbitofrontal and medial-temporal regions. That closes our first chapter, inside the magnet. Next time we start chapter 2 and follow the blood. Blood as a contrast agent, BOLD.
