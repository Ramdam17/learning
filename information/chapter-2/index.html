Surprise · Why log(1/p)

Surprise measures how unexpected an event is from its probability alone. Requiring that surprises of independent events add, while rarer events surprise more, forces the logarithm: surprise equals log of 1 over p, in bits for base 2 and nats for base e. It depends on the model, explodes for events judged impossible, and says nothing about meaning.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the Post Office. This is Chapter 2, and our new home is a telegraph office from the age of Morse code. Messages arrive, some dull, some astonishing. Today we put a number on how astonishing a message is. That number is called surprise, and every quantity in information theory is built from it.

Let's start with intuition. Two telegrams arrive. The first says, the sun rose this morning. The second says, it snowed in July. Which one tells you more?

The second obviously, not because snow is more important than sunrise, but because it was less expected. A message that you could have predicted tells you almost nothing new. A message that you thought nearly impossible tells you a lot.

So surprise should depend on probability and only on probability. The rarer the event, the larger the surprise. An event that is certain with probability 1 should carry no surprise at all.

That still leaves many candidates. 1 over p. 1 minus p. Here is the property that settles it.

Take two independent events. A coin lands heads with probability one-half, and a die shows a six with probability one-sixth. The probability that both happen is the product, one-twelfth. Now, how surprised should you be to learn both? It seems reasonable to ask that the surprise of two independent pieces of news be the sum of their separate surprises. Learning both is just learning one, then the other.

So we need a function where multiplying probabilities means adding surprises. There is essentially one such function, the logarithm. The log of a product is the sum of the logs. Add the requirement that rarer events are more surprising, and that small changes in probability produce small changes in surprise, and you are left with one family. The surprise of an event with probability p is the logarithm of 1 over p, equivalently minus the log of p.

That is the definition. Surprise equals log of 1 over p. Some authors call it self-information or surprisal. Same quantity.

Claude Shannon set out the requirements that lead to this logarithmic form in his 1948 paper. A Mathematical Theory of Communication, in the form of a theorem about entropy, the average surprise we meet next episode. Aleksandr Khinchin gave a more complete and rigorous axiomatic proof a few years later, in 1953. The logarithm is not a convention someone liked. It is what the requirements force.

Now the calculation in one picture. Probability on the horizontal axis from 0 to 1. Surprise on the vertical axis. At p equal to 1, the surprise is 0. At 1 half it is 1. At 1 quarter 2. At 1 eighth 3. Each halving of the probability adds exactly 1 unit of surprise. As p approaches 0, the surprise climbs without limit. A few landmarks are worth remembering. 1 chance in 10, about 3.3 bits. 1 in 100, about 6.6 bits. 1 in 1000, about 10 bits. Every factor of 10 in rarity adds about 3.3 bits.

What unit? That depends on the base of the logarithm. With log base 2, the unit is the bit. One bit is the surprise of an event with probability one half, a fair coin landing heads. Shannon credited the word bit, short for binary digit, to John Tukey.

With the natural logarithm base e, the unit is the nat. One nat is the surprise of an event with probability one over e, about 37%. To convert, one nat is about 1.443 bits and one bit is about 0.693 nats. Both are correct. They are the same quantity measured with different rulers, like centimeters and inches.

In this series, we use bits by default and nats whenever the formulas are naturally written with the natural logarithm. The token in the corner of the screen tells you which one is in use. Always check it. A number of information without its unit is like a distance without metres or miles. Impossible to compare with anything else.

Let's try the coin and the die. Heads log base 2 of 2 1 bit. A 6 log base 2 of 6 about 2.585 bits. Both together, log base 2 of 12 about 3.585 bits. 1 plus 2.585. The surprises add exactly as required. Stamp by stamp the two telegrams cost exactly what the pair costs, no more and no less.

Now what is surprise sensitive to? Three things.

First, it is sensitive only to probability. Two events with the same probability carry the same surprise, whatever they mean, whatever their consequences. A rare word and a rare earthquake, if equally probable, are equally surprising in this sense. We come back to this in episode 2.5, Information is Not Meaning.

Second, it is extremely sensitive at the rare end. Going from a probability of 1 in a thousand to 1 in a million adds about 10 bits. When probabilities are estimated from data, the rarest events have the least reliable probabilities and therefore the least reliable surprises.

Third, it depends on the model. A probability is always a probability under some model of the world, a jar, a distribution, an assumption. Change the model and the same event gets a different surprise. Snow in July is astonishing in Paris and ordinary in parts of the southern hemisphere, where July is winter.

Here is the classic pitfall. Suppose your model assigns probability zero to something, and then it happens. The surprise is the log of one over zero, infinite. In practice, an estimate built from a finite sample will assign zero probability to anything it has never seen. One unseen event, and your computed surprise explodes. Real data are full of such events. This is why careful work never lets an estimated probability sit at exactly zero, a theme that returns with divergences in Chapter 3 and with estimators in Chapter 5.

A final remark for neuroscientists. Many experiments manipulate how probable a stimulus is in an oddball design, for instance. The surprise of each stimulus under the experimenter's model can then be computed exactly, as minus log p. Whether the brain computes anything like it is a separate, empirical question, and episode 2.5 takes it up carefully.

The companion notebook lets you compute surprises in bits and in nats, check that they add for independent events, and watch what happens when an estimated probability hits zero.

To recap, surprise measures how unexpected an event is, using only its probability. The requirement that independent surprises add forces the logarithm. Surprise is log of 1 over p. With base 2 it is measured in bits, with base e in nats, and 1 nat is about 1.44 bits. Each halving of probability adds 1 bit. Surprise depends on the model, explodes for events judged impossible, and says nothing about meaning.

Entropy · The average surprise

Entropy is the average surprise: the sum of p times log of 1 over p, or the average number of well-chosen yes-or-no questions needed to find an outcome. A fair coin has 1 bit, a fair die about 2.585, a 90-10 coin about 0.47. It peaks at the uniform distribution, ignores the values themselves, and is underestimated from small samples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the post office, last time we measured the surprise of a single telegram, log of 1 over its probability. But a telegraph line carries many messages, some dull, some astonishing. Today we ask, on average how surprising is the next message? The answer is entropy.

Start with intuition and a game. 20 questions — I think of something and you may only ask yes or no questions. How many questions do you need, on average, to find the answer?

If I flip a fair coin, you need exactly one question. Was it heads? If I pick one of eight equally likely boxes, you need three. Is it in the left half? Then in which half of that half? Then which of the last two? Three halvings, three questions.

Now suppose I pick one of six faces of a fair die. Six is not a power of two, so you cannot halve perfectly every time. But over many rounds, cleverly grouping several rounds together, the average number of questions per round approaches log base 2 of 6, about 2.585.

Entropy is that number. The average number of well-chosen yes or no questions needed to pin down the outcome. Equivalently the average surprise.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Take each possible outcome. Compute its surprise, log of 1 over p. Weighted by how often it happens, its probability p. Add everything up. Entropy written H of X equals the sum over outcomes of p times log of 1 over p. In words, the expected surprise.

Let's do it for a coin. Two outcomes, each with probability 1 half, each with a surprise of 1 bit. 1 half times 1 plus 1 half times 1, 1 bit. In nats, the same coin has the natural log of 2, about 0.693 nats.

A fair die. Six outcomes, each with probability 1 sixth, each with a surprise of log base 2 of 6. The average is the same, about 2.585 bits.

Now a biased coin that lands heads 90% of the time. Heads carries little surprise, log of 1 over 0.9 about 0.152 bits. Tails carries a lot, log of 10 about 3.32 bits. But tails is rare. Weight each by its probability. 0.9 times 0.152 plus 0.1 times 3.32. About 0.469 bits. Less than half a bit.

That matches intuition. You can bet on heads and be right 9 times of 10. There is little uncertainty left to resolve. Compare that with a fair coin, where every toss is a full bit of news.

Plot the entropy of a coin as its bias goes from 0 to 1. At 0, the coin always lands tails, entropy 0. At 1, it always lands heads, entropy 0. In between, the curve rises to a single peak, exactly one bit at a fair coin. This curve is called the binary entropy function, and you will see it again and again.

Two properties follow, and they hold for any number of outcomes. Entropy is never negative, and it is zero only when one outcome is certain. And for a fixed number of outcomes n, entropy is largest when all outcomes are equally likely, where it equals log of n. A fair die is the most uncertain six-sided die there is.

Back to the lab. In episode 1.1, alpha power was high 60% of the time and low 40%. Its entropy is about 0.971 bits. Close to 1 bit because 60-40 is close to a fair coin. If alpha were high 99% of the time, the entropy would drop to about 0.08 bits. Hardly anything left to learn.

Experimenters control entropy directly. An oddball design with a standard tone 80% of the time and a deviant 20% has a stimulus entropy of about 0.72 bits. A design with four equally likely stimuli has exactly two bits. Before asking how much the brain learns about a stimulus, it helps to know how much there was to learn. For discrete stimuli, the stimulus entropy is a ceiling on the information any response can carry about them. A point we make precise in Chapter 4.

Now, what is entropy sensitive to?

First, only to the probabilities. Shuffle the labels of the outcomes, and the entropy does not change. A die with faces 1 to 6 or faces painted 6 colors has the same entropy. Entropy does not know about distances between values. 70 and 71 beats per minute are as different, for entropy, as 70 and 140.

Second, it is sensitive to how many categories you use. Cut a continuous signal into 2 bins, and its entropy is at most 1 bit. Cut the same Gaussian signal into 1,000 equal width bins, and its entropy rises to about 9 bits. Same signal, different number. The entropy of a discretized signal describes your discretization as much as your signal. We take this up properly with differential entropy in episode 2.6.

Third, it is sensitive to the sample. Estimated from data, entropy tends to come out too low because a finite sample misses rare outcomes and looks more concentrated than the truth. The smaller the sample relative to the number of categories, the stronger this downward bias.

Here is the classic pitfall, which combines the last two. Two conditions are compared, and one has a higher entropy. Is it more irregular or more complex? Before concluding, check three things. Same number of bins or the comparison is meaningless. Same number of samples or the bias differs. Same preprocessing, because filtering and rescaling change the histogram. A difference in entropy that disappears when these are equalized, was never about the brain.

And a word of warning about the name. Entropy here is a property of a probability distribution. It is not disorder in a physical sense, not complexity, and not information about anything in particular. It is the average surprise of the next observation, under a given model. Calling it complexity or chaos invites conclusions it cannot support.

The companion notebook computes entropies in bits and nats, draws the binary entropy curve, shows that the uniform distribution wins, and measures the downward bias of the plug-in estimate as the sample shrinks.

To recap, entropy is the expected surprise, the sum of p times log of 1 over p. It is the average number of well-chosen yes or no questions needed to find the outcome. A fair coin has 1 bit, a fair die about 2.585 bits, a 90-10 coin about 0.47 bits. Entropy is 0 for a certain outcome and maximal log of n for n equally likely outcomes. It ignores the values themselves, depends on the number of categories, and is underestimated from small samples.

Entropy as compression · Source coding

Entropy is the limit of lossless compression. Short codewords for frequent symbols, as in Morse code, and Huffman's merging of the two rarest symbols give the best symbol-by-symbol prefix code, never below the entropy and within one bit of it. Coding in longer blocks approaches the entropy, while a code built for the wrong probabilities pays an extra cost.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the telegraph desk with a practical question. Every message we send costs time on the line. How short can we make our messages, on average, without losing anything? The answer is one of the great results of information theory, and it gives entropy a concrete meaning. Entropy is a limit on compression.

Start with intuition and a code you already know. Morse code turns letters into dots and dashes. Look at which letters get the shortest codes. E, the most common letter in English, is a single dot. T, also very common, is a single dash. Rare letters like Q or Z get four symbols each.

That is the whole idea. Give short codes to frequent symbols and long codes to rare ones. On average, messages get shorter. Alfred Vail, Morse's partner, is usually credited with this design, reportedly after counting the metal type in a newspaper printer's cases. Today's international code is a later revision, but it kept the idea. Nearly a century later, Claude Shannon showed exactly how far this idea can go.

Let's set up the problem. We have a source that emits symbols, each with a known probability. We want to assign each symbol a string of bits, its codeword. Two requirements. First, no information is lost. The receiver must recover the exact message. Second, the stream must be readable without separators. The simplest way to guarantee this is a prefix code. No codeword is the beginning of another one. Then reading bit by bit, the receiver always knows when a codeword ends.

Now the calculation in one picture. Four symbols. A with probability one half, B one quarter, C and D one eighth each. Their entropy is 1.75 bits.

Here is a prefix code. A gets zero, B gets one zero, C gets one one zero, D gets one one one. No codeword starts another. The average length, one half times one bit plus one quarter times two bits plus 1 eighth times 3 plus 1 eighth times 3. 1.75 bits. Exactly the entropy. Not a single bit is wasted.

Notice the pattern. Each codeword length equals the surprise of its symbol. A has a surprise of 1 bit and gets 1 bit. C has a surprise of 3 bits and gets 3 bits. When every probability is a power of one half, the surprise is a whole number and the code can match it perfectly.

How do we build such a code in general? David Huffman found the answer in 1952, as a student. Take the two least probable symbols. Merge them into one node whose probability is their sum. Repeat until a single node remains. Then read the tree from the root, each left branch adds a zero, each right branch adds a one. The result is the best possible prefix code for those probabilities, symbol by symbol.

Let's run it on a less tidy source. Four symbols with probabilities 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1. Merge 0.1 and 0.2, 0.3. Merge that with the other 0.3, 0.6. Merge with 0.4, 1. The code lengths come out as 1, 2, 3, and 3 bits. Average length 1.9 bits.

The entropy of this source is about 1.846 bits. The code is slightly longer. It has to be, code lengths are whole numbers and the surprises here are not.

That brings us to Shannon's source coding theorem. For any lossless prefix code, the average length can never be smaller than the entropy. And the best code like Huffman's gets within one bit of it, the average length is at least the entropy and less than the entropy plus one.

And there is more. Encode symbols in blocks two or ten or a hundred at a time, and the extra cost per symbol shrinks toward zero. In the limit, the average number of bits per symbol approaches the entropy, but never goes below it. The entropy is a floor that no lossless code can break.

So entropy has a meaning you could test in a lab with a stopwatch. It is the minimum average number of bits per symbol needed to describe a source without loss.

Now what is this sensitive to?

First, the probabilities must be right. A code built for the wrong probabilities pays a penalty. Use a code designed for one distribution on messages from another, and the average length grows. The extra bits on average have a name, the Kullback-Leibler divergence. That is the subject of Chapter 3.

Second, the symbols you choose. Code letters one by one, and you ignore that Q is almost always followed by U. Code pairs or words, and the dependencies between neighbours are exploited. The true limit for a source with memory is not the entropy of single symbols but the entropy rate, the new information per symbol once the past is known. That idea returns in Chapter 7.

Third, losslessness. All of this is about exact reconstruction. Lossy compression, like a JPEG image, can go far below the entropy by throwing away detail. That is a different theory, rate distortion theory, which we only mention here.

Here is the classic pitfall in neuroscience. Compression programs like ZIP or the Lempel-Ziv algorithm are sometimes used to measure the complexity of a brain signal. The compressed size really is related to entropy, but in a specific way. For long, stationary sequences, the compression ratio of the Lempel-Ziv algorithm approaches the entropy rate. On short or nonstationary recordings, and after a choice of threshold to turn the signal into symbols, the number you get depends heavily on those choices. Chapter 10 treats Lempel-Ziv complexity in detail. For now, compressibility is a property of a sequence under a given coding scheme, not a direct readout of how complex a brain is.

One more connection. The idea that the nervous system might encode frequent inputs efficiently, reducing redundancy, was proposed by Horace Barlow in 1961, under the name of efficient coding. It is a hypothesis about brains, inspired by the mathematics of coding, and it has been tested in many sensory systems. We will come back to coding in neurons in Chapter 12.

The companion notebook builds Huffman codes for the two sources in this episode, checks that the average length sits between the entropy and the entropy plus one, and shows the length shrinking toward the entropy as symbols are coded in blocks.

To recap, give short codes to frequent symbols. A prefix code can be read without separators. Huffman's algorithm builds the best symbol-by-symbol prefix code by repeatedly merging the two rarest symbols. Shannon's source coding theorem, no lossless code beats the entropy on average, and the best ones come within one bit or arbitrarily close with long blocks. Entropy is therefore a compression limit measured in bits per symbol.

Joint and conditional entropy · The chain rule

The entropy of a pair equals the entropy of the first variable plus the entropy of the second given the first, in either order and for any number of variables. With an alpha power and heart rate table, 0.971 plus 0.704 gives the joint 1.675 bits. Conditioning never raises entropy on average, though a single observation can increase uncertainty.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the post office where telegrams now arrive in pairs, today we measure the uncertainty of two variables at once, and we meet the rule that holds the whole theory together — the chain rule of entropy.

Start with intuition. Imagine two telegrams sent one after the other. How much does it cost in bits to send both? You could send the first, which costs its entropy, then send the second. But if the second is partly predictable from the first, you do not need to pay its full price. You only pay for what is still uncertain once the first has arrived.

That leftover uncertainty has a name, conditional entropy. And the idea fits in one line. The uncertainty of the pair equals the uncertainty of the first, plus the uncertainty of the second given the first.

Let's define the pieces. The joint entropy, H of X and Y, is the entropy of the joint distribution, the average surprise of the pair computed over every cell of the table. Nothing new, just entropy applied to pairs.

The conditional entropy, H of Y given X, is built in two steps. For each value of X, take the row of the table for that value, rescaled as in episode 1.3, and compute the entropy of Y within that row. Then average those entropies, weighting each row by how often it occurs.

Now the calculation in one picture, using our illustrative table from chapter 1. Alpha power high or low. Heart rate high or low. Cells 45, 15, 5, and 35%.

The entropy of alpha alone, about 0.971 bits from its 60-40 split. The joint entropy of the four cells, about 1.675 bits.

Now heart rate given alpha. When alpha is high, the row rescales to 75 and 25%, an entropy of about 0.811 bits. When alpha is low, it rescales to 12.5 and 87.5%, about 0.544 bits. Weight the first by 0.6 and the second by 0.4. The conditional entropy is about 0.704 bits.

Check the chain rule. 0.971 plus 0.704 gives 1.675. Exactly the joint entropy. The first telegram plus what remains of the second equals the pair.

The rule works in either order. The joint entropy also equals the entropy of heart rate plus the entropy of alpha given heart rate. The split changes, the total does not. Pay for heart rate first, then for what alpha adds, and the receipt still totals 1.675 bits.

And it extends to any number of variables. The entropy of three variables equals the entropy of the first, plus the second given the first, plus the third given the first two. One term at a time, each paying only for what is new.

Now compare with the independent table from episode 1.2, 30, 30, 20, 20%. There, knowing alpha changes nothing about heart rate. The conditional entropy of heart rate equals its full entropy, 1 bit. And the joint entropy is simply the sum, 0.971 plus 1, 1.971 bits. Independence means the entropies add.

In the dependent table, heart rate given alpha was 0.704 bits instead of 1. Knowing alpha removed about 0.3 bits of uncertainty about heart rate. Hold on to that difference. Next chapter it gets a name, mutual information.

A picture often helps here. Draw two overlapping circles, one for the entropy of alpha, one for the entropy of heart rate. The part of the heart rate circle outside the overlap is the conditional entropy of heart rate given alpha. The part of the alpha circle outside the overlap is alpha given heart rate. The overlap itself is the shared part, the mutual information. The whole shape is the joint entropy. For two variables this picture is exact. For three it breaks down in an instructive way, as episode 4.7 will show.

Here is the most important inequality of the episode. Conditioning cannot increase entropy on average. The conditional entropy of Y given X is always less than or equal to the entropy of Y, with equality exactly when the two are independent. Learning something can only help on average.

But read the words on average carefully. A particular observation can make you more uncertain, not less.

Here is an illustrative example. A variable Y is 0 90% of the time. Its entropy is low, about 0.47 bits. Most of the time, a second variable X is 0, and then Y is even more predictable. Its entropy in that row is about 0.31 bits. But 10% of the time, X is 1, and in that row Y is a fair coin, 1 full bit.

So when you observe X equal to 1, your uncertainty about Y jumps from 0.47 to 1 bit. The observation made things worse. Averaged over both rows, though, the conditional entropy is about 0.38 bits, still below 0.47. The inequality holds on average and only on average.

This distinction between an average and a single observation will come back. In Chapter 7, local information measures look at individual moments in time, and some of those moments are misleading in exactly this way.

What is all this sensitive to? Everything we said about entropy carries over with a twist. Conditional entropy needs the distribution of Y within every row of the table. With more variables, the rows multiply, and each row holds fewer samples. Estimated conditional entropies are therefore even more biased than plain ones, and the bias grows with every variable you condition on.

Here is the classic pitfall that follows. People sometimes estimate H of Y given X as H of X and Y minus H of X, using two separate estimates. Algebraically, that is correct. Statistically, each estimate has its own bias, and they do not cancel automatically, especially when the joint table has many nearly empty cells. Use the same estimator, the same binning, and the same samples for every term, and remember that the difference of two biased numbers can be badly biased.

The companion notebook computes every number in this episode, verifies the chain rule in both orders, and builds the example where one observation increases uncertainty while the average still decreases.

To recap, joint entropy is the entropy of a pair. Conditional entropy is the average entropy of Y within each row, weighted by how often the row occurs. The chain rule, H of X and Y equals H of X plus H of Y given X in any order for any number of variables. Conditioning never increases entropy on average, with equality only under independence. But a single observation can increase uncertainty.

PLATE 2.59:18

Information is not meaning

Shannon's information measures probability, not meaning: a yes and a no telegram carry the same bits. Every surprise is defined under a model, so the question is always whose probabilities. In oddball experiments, the P300 and mismatch negativity may track surprise under some participant model, but that link must be tested against alternatives rather than assumed from the shared word.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the post office, for an episode with fewer formulas and more care. Two telegrams arrive. One says the answer is yes. The other says the answer is no. If both answers were equally likely, both telegrams carry exactly the same surprise. One bit each. Yet they mean opposite things, and one of them might change your life.

That is the point of today. Information in Shannon's sense is not meaning.

Shannon said so himself on the first page of his 1948 paper. Messages often have meaning, he wrote, but the semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem. The engineer's job is to reproduce the message exactly, whatever it says. A telegraph line that delivers yes or no correctly has done its work, regardless of what the answer means to anyone.

A year later, Warren Weaver spelled this out. He described three levels of communication problems. Level A, the technical problem, how accurately can the symbols be transmitted? Level B, the semantic problem, how precisely do the symbols convey the intended meaning? Level C, the effectiveness problem, how well does the received meaning change behaviour in the intended way? Shannon's theory addresses level A. Weaver argued that it has implications for the other two, since meaning cannot survive a channel that garbles the symbols. But it does not measure meaning.

Let's make the distinction concrete. Surprise is log of 1 over p. It depends on the probability of the message under a model, and on nothing else. Two consequences follow.

First, two messages with the same probability carry the same information, whatever they say. Yes and no. A birthday greeting and an alarm. Equal probabilities equal bits. The postman weighs them the same.

Second, the same message can carry very different amounts of information under different models. The bits are not in the message. They are in the relation between the message and the probabilities someone assigned beforehand.

So the essential question is always, whose probabilities? Under which model?

This matters enormously in neuroscience, where surprise is everywhere. Take the oddball paradigm. A participant hears a stream of tones. Most are standard, say 80%. A few are deviant, 20%. Deviants evoke characteristic brain responses. The mismatch negativity, described by Risto Näätänen and colleagues in 1978. And the P300, a positive wave around 300 milliseconds, reported by Samuel Sutton and colleagues in 1965, whose amplitude grows as the eliciting stimulus becomes rarer.

These responses are often described as responses to surprise, and under the experimenter's model, the deviant really is more surprising, log of 1 over 0.2, about 2.32 bits, against about 0.32 bits for a standard.

But notice which probabilities we used, the global frequencies chosen by the experimenter. The participant does not have access to those numbers. The participant has a sequence of tones, a memory of the recent ones, and perhaps instructions. Their expectations may follow a different model entirely.

This was shown long ago. In 1976, Kenneth Squires, Christopher Wickens, Nancy Squires, and Emanuel Donchin showed that P300 amplitude depends not only on the global probability of a stimulus, but also on the specific sequence of the preceding stimuli. A deviant that breaks a long run of standards evokes a larger response than one that follows another deviant. They modelled expectancy as depending on a decaying memory of past events, on the structure of the sequence, and on global probability.

So which surprise does the P300 track? Global probability, local sequence, some learned model, each gives a different surprise for the very same tone. Later work compared such models trial by trial. For instance, Rogier Mars and colleagues in 2008 related single-trial EEG fluctuations to the surprise of an ideal observer that learns stimulus probabilities as the experiment unfolds. Other models with different memory assumptions fit some datasets better. The question is still being refined. What matters for us is the method. Several explicit models of expectation compared on the same single trials rather than one model assumed from the start.

Now an example where statistical rarity and the participant's expectation come apart completely. Suppose deviants are rare overall but they occur in a fixed, announced pattern, every fifth tone and the participant knows it. Under a model of global frequencies, every deviant is surprising. Under the participant's model, every deviant is perfectly expected, and it is a missing deviant that would be surprising. The reverse also happens. A statistically common event can be unexpected for a participant who holds a wrong belief.

Here is the general lesson, which Paul Rapp has emphasised in his teaching. Shannon's surprise is a quantity defined within a statistical model. The brain's response to an event is a physiological measurement. The two can be correlated in a given paradigm, and that correlation can be very informative. But the correlation has to be demonstrated by specifying a model of the participant's expectations and testing it against alternatives. It cannot be assumed by vocabulary simply because both are called surprise.

There is a second trap of vocabulary. Bayesian surprise, defined by Laurent Itti and Pierre Baldi, measures how much an observation changes beliefs, as a divergence between the beliefs after and before. Variational free energy in the work of Karl Friston is a bound related to Shannon's surprise under a generative model. These are different quantities with different assumptions, even though all three are called surprise. Episode 3.5 lays them side by side.

What is this sensitive to in practice? To the model first and always. Write down which probabilities you use to compute a surprise. Global frequencies, a sliding window, an ideal Bayesian learner, or a model fitted to behaviour. Different choices give different regressors for the same trials.

Second to what counts as an event. Is the event the tone, the tone given the last three tones, or the tone given the instruction? Changing the definition of the random variable changes the probabilities, as episode 1.1 warned.

Here is the classic pitfall. A paper reports that a brain response increases with the surprise of the stimulus, and concludes that the brain computes Shannon information. The analysis, though, used only the experimenter's global probabilities, and never compared them with simpler explanations, such as adaptation to repeated stimuli or the time since the last deviant. Rare stimuli are also, almost by construction, stimuli that have not been heard recently. Rarity and recency are confounded unless the design separates them.

Designs can separate them. Sequences can share the same global probabilities while differing in their local structure, as in the Squires study. Probabilities can change across blocks so that a learning observer and a fixed frequency model make different predictions. And a model of expectation can be fitted to the participants' own predictions or reaction times, then tested on the brain data. Each of these turns a vocabulary claim into a testable one.

So the right claim is narrower and stronger. Under Model M, single trial responses track surprise better than under models A and B. That is a result about the brain in a model. It is not a property of Shannon's theory.

The same humility applies to every information value in this series. When we compute how many bits one brain's signal carries about another or about a stimulus, we describe a statistical relation measured under a model and an estimator. It tells us that knowing one reduces uncertainty about the other. It does not tell us what the brain means by it or whether the brain uses it at all.

To recap, Shannon's information measures probability, not meaning. Two equally probable messages carry the same bits, whatever they say. Weaver's three levels, technical semantic effectiveness, Shannon's theory addresses the first. Every surprise is defined under a model, so always ask whose probabilities. Brain responses to rare events like the P300 and the mismatch negativity can track surprise under some model, but that link must be tested against alternatives, not assumed from the word surprise.

Differential entropy · Entropy for continuous signals

Binning a Gaussian into ever finer bins makes its entropy grow without limit, as a fixed number minus the log of the bin width. That fixed part is the differential entropy, an entropy relative to a unit. It depends on scale, can be negative, and is one half log of 2 pi e sigma squared for a Gaussian.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the post office where a new kind of message arrives. Not letters from a finite alphabet, but a continuous voltage on the line like an EEG. Can we give it an entropy? The natural answer has a twist, and that twist explains a lot of confusion in the literature. Today, differential entropy.

Start with intuition. Entropy counts, on average, how many yes or no questions you need to pin down a value. For a die about 2.6. Now take a continuous voltage. To pin it down exactly with infinitely many decimals, you would need infinitely many questions. So the honest entropy of a continuous variable is infinite.

That is not a useful number. But there is a useful finite part hiding inside it. Let's find it.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Take a standard Gaussian mean zero, standard deviation one. Cut its axis into bins of width delta, turn it into a discrete variable, and compute the ordinary entropy of the bins.

With bins of width one, the entropy is about 2.1 bits. Width one-half about 3.1 bits. Width one-tenth about 5.37 bits. Width one-hundredth about 8.69 bits. Width one-thousandth, about 12.01 bits.

Each time the bins shrink by a factor of 10, the entropy grows by about 3.32 bits, which is the log base 2 of 10. It never stops growing. In the limit of infinitely fine bins, it diverges, as intuition said.

But look at the patterns. The entropy of the bin variable is, very nearly, a fixed number minus the log of the bin width.

Write it as, H of X delta is approximately h of X minus log delta. The part that grows without limit is minus log delta, and it depends only on how finely you chop the axis. The fixed part h of X depends only on the distribution. That fixed part is called the differential entropy. It is the part of the cost that belongs to the signal, not to your ruler.

For our standard Gaussian, the differential entropy is about 2.047 bits. Add the log base 2 of 10 for bins of width 1 tenth, 5.37. Add the log base 2 of 100 for width 1 hundredth, 8.69. The approximation becomes exact as the bins shrink.

The formula for differential entropy looks like a direct copy of the discrete one. Replace the sum with an integral and the probabilities with the density. h equals minus the integral of p of x times log p of x. And for a Gaussian with standard deviation sigma, it has a closed form, one half of the log of 2 pi e sigma squared. With sigma equal to 1, that is one half log base 2 of 2 pi e, about 2.047 bits or about 1.419 nats.

So far so good, but the derivation tells us what differential entropy is, and what it is not.

It is not the entropy of the continuous variable. That is infinite. It is the finite remainder after we subtract the term that depends on resolution. It is a relative quantity, the entropy of the binned variable measured relative to the bin width.

Shannon himself flagged this in 1948. For continuous distributions, he noted, entropy is measured relative to an assumed standard, the coordinate system chosen. Change the coordinates and the entropy changes with them.

That has consequences and here is the first. Differential entropy depends on the scale of the variable. Double the standard deviation of a Gaussian and its differential entropy increases by exactly 1 bit. Multiply it by 10 and it increases by log base 2 of 10, about 3.32 bits. A spread out distribution needs more questions to locate a value to a given precision, so this part makes intuitive sense.

The second consequence is less intuitive, differential entropy can be negative. For a Gaussian, it is zero when σ equals 1 over the square root of 2πe, about 0.242. Any narrower Gaussian has a negative differential entropy. A standard deviation of 0.1 gives about minus 1.27 bits.

A negative entropy sounds absurd. It is not, once you remember what h is, entropy relative to a bin width of one unit. A very narrow distribution needs fewer than zero questions, relative to unit-wide bins, because it fits inside a single bin with room to spare. Change the unit and the sign can flip.

That brings us to the third consequence, which is the subject of the next episode. Since h depends on the unit, the same EEG has a different differential entropy in microvolts and in volts. We will see that it shifts by about 13.8 nats. For a Gaussian voltage with a spread of 10 microvolts, the differential entropy is about 5.37 bits when measured in microvolts and about minus 14.56 bits when measured in volts. Same voltage, same brain. Only the ruler changed.

What is differential entropy sensitive to? To scale first, any rescaling of the variable adds the log of the scale factor. To the shape of the distribution, of course. For a fixed variance, a Gaussian has the largest differential entropy of all, a fact we use in Episode 2.8. And when estimated from data to the estimator, histograms, kernel densities, and nearest neighbour methods each bring their own bias, which Chapter 5 covers in detail.

Here is the classic pitfall. Someone discretizes a continuous signal into bins, computes the entropy of the bins, and reports it as the entropy of the signal. That number, as we just saw, is h of X minus log delta. It contains a term that depends on nothing but the bin width chosen by the analyst. Change the bin width and the reported entropy changes, sometimes by many bits, with the data untouched. When comparing two groups, two conditions, or two studies, the bins must be identical, and even then, differences in signal scale shift the result.

The companion notebook reproduces the staircase, binned entropy against bin width, the straight line of slope minus 1 in log of delta, and the plateau that reveals h. It checks the Gaussian formula numerically and shows the negative entropy of a narrow Gaussian.

To recap, a continuous variable has infinite entropy, pinning it down exactly takes infinitely many questions. Binning it at width delta gives an entropy of about h of X minus log delta. The finite part h is the differential entropy, minus the integral of p log p. For a Gaussian it is one half log of two pi e sigma squared. It is relative to the unit, grows with scale, can be negative, and should always be reported with its unit and its base.

The traps of differential entropy

Differential entropy carries six traps: negative values, change of unit, z-scoring, amplitude passing for complexity, nonlinear transformations, and preprocessing. Switching an EEG from microvolts to volts shifts it by about 13.8 nats, and for near-Gaussian data it is just the log of the standard deviation. Mutual information escapes these traps because the shifts cancel under invertible transformations.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the post office with a warning posted on the wall. Last time we met differential entropy, the finite part of the entropy of a continuous signal. Today we walk through its traps one by one because each of them has caught published work. And at the end, we see why mutual information walks past all of them unharmed.

First the intuition to keep in mind. Differential entropy is an entropy measured relative to a ruler. The ruler is the unit of the variable. Change the ruler and the number changes. Almost every trap follows from forgetting the ruler.

Trap number one, negative entropy. For a Gaussian, the differential entropy is one half the log of two pi e sigma squared. It crosses zero when sigma equals about 0.242 and becomes negative below. A Gaussian with a standard deviation of 0.1 has a differential entropy of about minus 1.27 bits. In nats about minus 0.88.

Nothing is wrong with the mathematics. The formula is exact, but a negative value cannot be read as a number of questions or as an amount of uncertainty in an absolute sense. It is only meaningful relative to the unit. If someone tells you that a signal has an entropy of minus 3 nats, your first question should be, in which unit was the signal expressed?

Trap number two, changing the unit. Here is the calculation in one picture. Take an EEG voltage. Express it in microvolts, then express the very same samples in volts. Every value is divided by 1 million. The density, as we saw in episode 1.5, is multiplied by 1 million.

The rule for differential entropy is simple and exact. If you multiply a variable by a constant A, its differential entropy increases by the log of the absolute value of A. Going from microvolts to volts multiplies by one millionth. So the differential entropy drops by the log of one million. In nats that is about 13.8. In bits about 19.9.

Same brain. Same recording. Same physiology. Only the export settings changed. A difference of almost 14 nats, created by a choice of unit in the export settings. Exported in millivolts instead, the shift would be the log of 1000, about 6.9 nats. Every choice of unit gives its own number.

Trap number 3, a consequence of the second, normalization. Many pipelines z-score each channel, dividing by its standard deviation. After z-scoring, every channel has a standard deviation of 1. If the data were Gaussian, every channel would then have exactly the same differential entropy, about 1.419 nats, whatever the brain was doing. Any difference that remains reflects only departures from Gaussian shape.

So z-scoring before computing differential entropy removes the scale, but it also removes the information about scale. And not z-scoring leaves the scale in, so that differences in amplitude become differences in entropy. You must decide which question you are asking, about the shape of the distribution or about its spread.

Trap number 4, amplitude disguised as complexity. Suppose two groups are compared, and one shows a lower differential entropy. Before concluding that its brain activity is more regular or less complex, check its amplitude. For a Gaussian, halving the standard deviation lowers the differential entropy by exactly one bit, with no change in shape at all. Lower amplitudes arise for many reasons. Skull thickness, electrode impedance, age, medication, the reference. A difference in entropy may be nothing more than a difference in variance.

Trap number five, other transformations. Changes of unit are the simplest case. Any smooth, invertible transformation changes the differential entropy too. Take the logarithm of a power value or apply a nonlinearity. The differential entropy shifts by the average log of how much the transformation stretches the axis. A log transformed power spectrum and a raw one have different differential entropies for the same data.

Trap number six, preprocessing. Filtering removes variance so it lowers the differential entropy. Rereferencing, artifact rejection, and resampling all change the distribution of values. Two studies that report the differential entropy of the same kind of signal with different pipelines are not reporting comparable numbers.

Here is a way to see how much of differential entropy can be plain variance. For a Gaussian, the differential entropy in nats equals the natural log of the standard deviation plus a constant, about 1.419. That is all. For data that are close to Gaussian, differential entropy is the log of the standard deviation, in disguise. Before interpreting it as anything deeper, compute the log standard deviation of the same data and compare. If the two tell the same story, the entropy has added nothing.

Let's practise on a fictional abstract. Patients showed lower EEG entropy than controls, minus 2.1 against minus 1.6 nats, suggesting reduced neural complexity. Read it with this episode in mind. Negative values, so this is differential entropy and the unit matters. Which unit? Not stated. Were channels z-scored? Not stated. Did the groups differ in amplitude? A difference of 0.5 nats is exactly what a ratio of standard deviations of about 1.65 would produce in Gaussian data, with no change in shape at all. Until those questions are answered, the abstract supports a claim about variance, not about complexity.

So what should a report contain? The unit of the signal, the logarithm base, the preprocessing, whether and how the data were normalized, the estimator and its settings, and the amplitude of the signals in each group. With those, a reader can tell spread from shape.

So when is a difference in differential entropy meaningful? When the two quantities are measured in the same unit after the same preprocessing, and the question is explicitly about spread and shape together. A useful fact, if two signals are rescaled by the same factor, the difference between their differential entropies does not change, because the log of the factor cancels. Comparisons within the same unit with matched pipelines are safe. Comparisons across units, normalizations or pipelines are not.

Now the good news. Mutual information escapes all of these traps.

Here is why in one line. Mutual information can be written as the sum of the two individual differential entropies, minus their joint differential entropy. Multiply X by a. The entropy of X goes up by the log of a. The joint entropy of X and Y also goes up by the log of a. The two shifts cancel. Mutual information does not move.

The same cancellation happens for any smooth, invertible transformation of either variable, applied separately. Microvolts or volts, raw or log transformed, z-scored or not. The mutual information between two signals is the same, as long as the transformation can be undone. And it is never negative. It has a meaning that does not depend on the ruler, how much knowing one reduces uncertainty about the other. One caution, this invariance belongs to the quantity. Some estimators still need their inputs on comparable scales, a practical detail that Chapter 5 returns to.

This is one of the main reasons the series puts mutual information at its center. Chapter 4 proves the invariance carefully, and Chapter 5 builds an estimator, the Gaussian copula, directly on top of it.

The companion notebook reproduces every number of this episode. The negative entropy of a narrow Gaussian, the shift of about 13.8 nats between microvolts and volts, the effect of z-scoring, and the invariance of mutual information under the same transformations.

To recap, differential entropy is entropy relative to a unit. It can be negative. Changing from microvolts to volts shifts it by the log of 1 million, about 13.8 nats. Z-scoring, filtering, log transforms, and amplitude differences all change it. Differences are meaningful only in the same unit with the same pipeline. Mutual information built from differences of entropies cancels the ruler and is invariant to any invertible transformation of each variable.

The Gaussian · Maximum entropy and closed forms

Among all distributions with a given variance, the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy, which is why maximum entropy picks it when only mean and variance are known. Its closed forms, including the determinant formula for several variables, give entropy and mutual information from covariance alone. For non-Gaussian data they give an upper bound on entropy and miss any dependence that is not correlation.

Back at the post office where one shape of message keeps turning up — the bell curve. The Gaussian is everywhere in this series, in formulas, in estimators, in decompositions. Today we see why. It is not only common. Part of the reason it is common is the central limit theorem. A sum of many small, independent contributions tends toward a Gaussian. A scalp EEG voltage is a sum of activity from many sources, and its distribution is often close to Gaussian, though not always. Strong rhythmic bursts and artifacts can bend it. It is the most uncertain distribution you can have once you fix the variance, and that makes it uniquely useful.

Start with intuition. Suppose all you know about a signal is its mean and its variance. Nothing else. Which distribution should you assume? A good principle is to assume as little extra structure as possible: choose the distribution with the largest entropy among all those that match what you know. That is the principle of maximum entropy championed by the physicist Edwin Jaynes in 1957.

And here is the result. Among all continuous distributions on the real line with a given variance, the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy. Any other shape with the same variance has strictly less.

Let's check it in one picture. Three distributions, all with variance 1. A uniform distribution, a flat box. A Laplace distribution, a sharp peak with heavy tails. And a Gaussian. Their differential entropies in nats. About 1.242 for the uniform, about 1.347 for the Laplace, and about 1.419 for the Gaussian. The Gaussian wins.

In bits about 1.79, 1.94, and 2.05. Same ranking.

Why does this matter? Because it turns the Gaussian into a reference. For any signal with variance σ², the Gaussian formula, ½ log of 2πe σ² gives an upper bound on its differential entropy. A real signal can only fall below that line. The gap measures how far its shape is from Gaussian.

Now the multivariate version, which is where the Gaussian becomes truly powerful. For a vector of d variables that are jointly Gaussian with covariance matrix sigma, the differential entropy has a closed form, one half the log of 2 pi e to the power d times the determinant of σ.

The determinant measures the volume of the cloud. Correlated variables squeeze the cloud into a thin ellipse with a small determinant. Independent variables with the same variances fill a round cloud with a larger determinant.

Take two standard Gaussian variables. If they are independent, the joint entropy is twice 1.419, about 2.838 nats. If their correlation is 0.8, the determinant drops from 1 to 0.36, and the joint entropy drops to about 2.327 nats.

The difference about 0.511 nats is exactly what the two variables share. It is the mutual information of a bivariate Gaussian, minus one-half log of one minus rho squared, the formula of episode 4.2. Here it falls straight out of the determinant.

So the Gaussian gives closed forms for entropy, joint entropy, conditional entropy, and mutual information, all from covariance matrices. No histograms, no bins, no neighbours. Just linear algebra.

And a matching maximum entropy result holds in many dimensions too. Among all distributions with a given covariance matrix, the jointly Gaussian one has the largest joint entropy. That leads to a practical consequence that shows up all over this series.

If you assume your data are Gaussian when they are not, the Gaussian formulas give you the entropies of a Gaussian with the same covariance. For entropy, that is an upper bound. For mutual information, once each variable has first been transformed to have a Gaussian distribution, the Gaussian formula gives a lower bound on the true value. It only sees the part of the dependence that survives as correlation. Chapter 5 uses exactly this logic for the Gaussian copula estimator, and Chapter 9 for Gaussian versions of information decompositions, which are only computable in closed form because of these formulas.

What is this sensitive to? First, to the variance itself. The Gaussian entropy is just the log of the standard deviation plus a constant, so all the scale traps of the previous episode apply. Second to the covariance estimate. With many variables and few samples, sample covariance matrices are poorly conditioned. Their determinants are tiny or unstable, and the log determinant can swing wildly. Regularization or shrinkage is often needed.

Here is the classic pitfall. The Gaussian formula is applied to data with heavy tails, strong skew, or nonlinear dependence, and the result is reported as the entropy or the mutual information of the data. It is not. It is the entropy or the information of a Gaussian that shares only the covariance of the data. For entropy, the true value is lower. For dependence, anything nonlinear like the parabola of episode 1.4 is invisible. Its correlation is zero so its Gaussian mutual information is zero too.

To recap, at fixed variance, the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy. The maximum entropy principle picks it when only the mean and variance are known. Its entropy is ½ log of 2 pi e sigma squared, and in d dimensions ½ log of 2 pi e to the d times the determinant of the covariance. These closed forms give entropies and mutual information from covariances alone. Applied to non-Gaussian data, they describe a Gaussian with the same covariance, an upper bound for entropy and blind to any dependence that is not carried by correlation.

Other entropies · Rényi and Tsallis

Rényi entropies form a family indexed by alpha: zero counts possible outcomes, one gives Shannon's entropy, two gives the collision entropy, and infinity gives the min-entropy. Tsallis entropies are a related family. Both lack Shannon's chain rule, so plugging them into the identities that build mutual information or decompositions gives numbers that are not mutual information.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last visit to the post office for this chapter. Shannon's entropy is not the only way to measure uncertainty. There is a whole family of entropies, with a dial that changes how much weight rare and common outcomes receive. You will meet them in the literature so let's see what they are and why they cannot be mixed freely with Shannon's.

Start with intuition. Shannon's entropy averages surprise, weighting every outcome by its probability. But you might care about other things. How likely are two independent draws to land on the same outcome? How surprising is the single most probable outcome? How many outcomes are possible at all? Each question leads to a different entropy.

In 1961, the mathematician Alfréd Rényi gathered these into one family, indexed by a number alpha. The Rényi entropy of order alpha is 1 over 1 minus alpha, times the log of the sum of each probability raised to the power alpha.

Here is the calculation in one picture, with the telegraph source from episode 2.3, 1 half, 1 quarter, 1 eighth, 1 eighth.

Turn the dial to alpha equals zero. Every possible outcome counts the same, however rare. The result is the log of the number of possible outcomes, here log base 2 of 4, 2 bits. This is called the Hartley entropy.

Turn it toward alpha equals 1. The formula looks undefined there but its limit exists and it is exactly Shannon's entropy, 1.75 bits.

Alpha equals 2. The sum of squared probabilities is the chance that two independent draws give the same outcome, the collision probability. The Rényi entropy of order 2 is minus the log of that chance. Here the collision probability is 0.34375 and the entropy is about 1.54 bits. It is sometimes called the collision entropy.

Turn the dial all the way up toward infinity. Only the most probable outcome matters. The result is minus the log of the largest probability, here 1 bit. This is called the min-entropy and it matters in cryptography, where an attacker always guesses the most likely value.

Plot the whole family against alpha. 2, 1.87, 1.75, 1.54, and down to 1. The curve never increases. Low orders emphasize rare outcomes. High orders emphasize common ones. All the members agree only for a uniform distribution, where every one of them equals the log of the number of outcomes.

A second family is often encountered in physics and some neuroscience papers: the Tsallis entropy, introduced by Constantino Tsallis in 1988. With index q, it equals 1 minus the sum of probabilities to the power q, divided by q minus 1. As q goes to 1, it too becomes Shannon's entropy in nats. For q equal to 2, our source gives 0.656. Tsallis and Rényi entropies of the same order are related by a monotone function, so they rank distributions identically. They differ in how they combine.

And that is the key difference with Shannon. What are these entropies sensitive to? To the order you choose first. Different orders can rank two distributions differently because they weight the tails differently. Always report the order.

Second to how they combine. Rényi entropies still add for independent variables. The entropy of a pair of independent variables is the sum of their entropies. Tsallis entropies do not even do that; they pick up an extra cross term, and neither family obeys Shannon's chain rule in general. The entropy of a pair is not simply the entropy of the first plus a well-defined conditional entropy of the second. Several competing definitions of conditional Rényi entropy exist, and they do not agree.

Here is the classic pitfall. Someone computes a Rényi or Tsallis entropy, then builds mutual information, conditional information, or a decomposition by plugging it into Shannon's identities. For example, a sum of two entropies minus a joint entropy. The result is not mutual information, and it can behave strangely. It can be negative or fail to vanish for independent variables in the Tsallis case. Everything in this series from mutual information to transfer entropy to synergy rests on Shannon's chain rule. Generalized entropies are useful measures of spread and concentration in their own right, but they do not slot into those decompositions.

To recap, Rényi entropies form a family indexed by alpha. Alpha equals zero counts possible outcomes, one gives Shannon, two gives the collision entropy, infinity gives the min-entropy. The spectrum never increases with alpha. Tsallis entropies are a related family. They are useful descriptions of a distribution, but they lack Shannon's chain rule, so they cannot be mixed into mutual information or information decompositions.
