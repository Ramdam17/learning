PLATE 13.16:59

Neighbours in time: autocorrelation

Measurements in a row resemble their neighbours, and the autocorrelation function shows how far that memory reaches. A smooth signal has lag-one autocorrelation of 0.94, while white noise has 0.08. Sampling a slow process a hundred times faster left the spread of the average unchanged at 0.39, since 12,000 points held about seven independent ones.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the tide-gauge hut. Inside, a float rides the water, and a pen draws its height on a roll of paper. Chapter thirteen is about measurements in a row, and today's question looks innocent. If we measure every second for a thousand seconds, do we hold a thousand pieces of information? Guess before you see.

Here is a signal, simulated: one value per second, in pink. Under it, a blue copy on tracing paper, slid sideways by one second. Where the signal is high, its slid copy is high as well. Guess before you see: if we plot each value against the next one, will the dots scatter, or line up? Each second gives one dot: the value now, across, and the value one second later, up. The dots line up along a diagonal. The strength of that line is called the autocorrelation at lag one, and here it is 0.94. Neighbours in time resemble each other.

Compare it with white noise, simulated too: values that know nothing about each other, with the same spread. Slide the copy, and nothing matches. The dots make a round cloud, and the autocorrelation at lag one is 0.08, close to zero. This is the ingredient we start from: a signal with no memory at all. It is also the reference we compare every other signal with.

One lag is not the whole story. TICK slides the copy two seconds, then five, then twenty. For the smooth signal, the line loosens: 0.90 at lag two, 0.75 at lag five, and 0.12 at lag twenty. Now put every lag in one picture, the autocorrelation function. Each bar is one lag. For white noise, the bars stay inside a narrow band, plus or minus 0.10, which is what chance alone gives with 400 values. Out of 40 lags, 37 sit inside the band, and three poke out by chance, as a band that holds 95 percent allows. For the smooth signal, the bars decay slowly, and they cross one half at lag ten.

Can we manufacture autocorrelation? Take the very same white noise, and replace each value by the average of the last k values. For k equal to 3, the trace is a little smoother, and the autocorrelation at lag one jumps to 0.70. Guess before you see: for k equal to 30, how high does it go? It reaches 0.97. For k equal to 10, it is 0.91. The bars fall in a straight line, down to zero at lag k, which is the exact result for this filter. Nothing was added. Neighbours simply share most of the same raw values. Many smooth signals are noise that was filtered by a sensor, or by a body.

Now a trap. A round cloud at lag one does not prove independence. Here is a simulated tide: one clean wave, about twelve and a half hours long, read every three hours. The lag-one cloud is round, with an autocorrelation of 0.05. But at lag two, the dots sit on a line sloping down, at minus 0.99. Six hours apart, the tide is almost exactly at the opposite height. The bars swing up and down like the tide itself. That is why we read every lag, not one.

Now the question from the hut. Take a slow process, with a memory of about ten seconds, and record it for 120 seconds. Sample it once a second, and we hold 120 values. Ten times a second: 1,200. A hundred times a second: 12,000. Same recording, a hundred times more points. Watch the lag-one autocorrelation as we sample faster: 0.86 at one point per second, 0.985 at ten, 0.999 at a hundred. The neighbours are closer and closer to copies. We want the average of the recording. Guess before you see: with a hundred times more points, how much narrower is the spread of that average?

Simulate 4,000 recordings, and keep the average of each. At one point per second, the spread of the average is 0.39. At ten per second, 0.39. At a hundred per second, 0.39. A hundred times more points, and the spread did not move. If every point were new information, it would have shrunk to 0.09, then 0.03, then 0.01. At 12,000 points, the spread is 43 times wider than that promise. Those 12,000 points are worth about seven independent measurements. Seven, out of twelve thousand.

Why? The new points fall between old ones, and neighbours are almost copies. Sampling faster does not create information. It records the same slow movement more finely. What helps is time. Record ten times longer, still once a second: 1,200 points, and the spread falls to 0.13, as if we had about sixty independent measurements. The same 1,200 points, sampled ten times a second, gave 0.39.

The same memory hides in many places. Guess before you see: which one forgets in the fewest steps? Each series next is simulated, with an assumed persistence. Daily temperature, as a departure from the seasonal average: the autocorrelation falls below one half after two days. A heart rate, one value per second: thirteen seconds. The power of an EEG alpha rhythm, ten values per second: eight tenths of a second. A river level, one value a day: seven days. Pupil size, thirty values per second: about half a second. Notice the units. A step is a second for the heart, and a day for the river, so the memory is counted in steps, and a step means whatever the sensor says. A pupil recorded at sixty hertz for ten seconds gives 600 points, but only a handful of slow movements.

Now two series with no memory. Dice rolls: the bars sit near zero, and a few poke out by chance. Radioactive counts in one-second windows: decays are independent events, so the counts are too, and the bars stay inside the band.

This should sound familiar. In 11.1, the neurons of one animal resembled each other, and ten animals were not five hundred. In 11.2, the trials of one person did. Neighbours in time are the same story: what resembles its neighbours is worth less than its count. We will count it properly in 13.4.

Back in the hut, the pen keeps drawing. Remember: measurements in a row resemble their neighbours, the autocorrelation function says how far that memory reaches, and sampling faster does not create information. Next time: stationarity, trends and drifts, when the average itself moves.

PLATE 13.27:20

Stationarity, trends and drifts

A stationary series keeps its average, spread and memory across windows. A heating sensor shows a staircase of window averages, and a random walk wanders without any slope built in. A straight-line test finds a slope in 87.4 percent of walks. Removing a line fixes the sensor, taking differences fixes the walk, and numbers on a walk describe only the window.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tide-gauge hut, the pen has been drawing for months. If we cut a long record into pieces, does each piece tell the same story? Guess before you see.

A series is called stationary when its average, its spread and its autocorrelation do not change over time. Here is one, simulated: 300 points, with some memory but no drift. Cut it into six windows of 50 points. TICK hops a frame along the series, and each hop leaves a footprint: the average of the window as a dot, the standard deviation as a bar. The six averages sit between minus 0.29 and 0.55, and the six standard deviations between 0.90 and 1.08. The trail of footprints is flat. That is what stationary looks like.

Now a slow sensor that heats up, simulated: a steady climb, plus noise. Same frame, same hops. The standard deviations barely move, between 0.94 and 1.20. But the averages climb, from 0.30 to 5.71. The trail is a staircase. The average is moving, so this series is not stationary. An EEG baseline that drifts over a session does the same, and so do reaction times when people tire.

The third series is a random walk, simulated: each value is the previous one plus a fresh random step. A stock price is the classic example. There is no slope inside it, only steps. Yet the window averages wander, from 8.37 up to 22.29 and back down to 15.67. Nobody built that staircase. The standard deviations jump around too, from 1.60 to 5.48.

Is that one unlucky walk? Simulate 2,000 walks, and keep the average of each window. Guess before you see: does the spread of that average stay the same from window to window? For a stationary series, it does: 0.28 in the first window, 0.27 in the sixth. For walks, it grows: 4.17 in the first window, and 16.64 in the sixth.

Three more walks, drawn in order. Two fall steadily: their window averages go from 1.00 down to minus 30.61, and from 0.15 down to minus 18.20. The third wanders between 4.85 and 10.93. Three walks, three different trends, and none of them was put there.

A walk drifts in one direction for long stretches, so it looks like a trend. Fit a straight line, and run the textbook test of the slope at 5 percent. On the three walks we just saw, it calls two significant. On 5,000 simulated walks of 100 points, the test finds a significant slope in 87.4 percent of them. On white noise, it does so in 4.9 percent, as promised. A walk has no slope, so each of those is a false alarm. This is not the test of the next episode, where two walks meet.

There are two gestures to deal with a series like this. The first is to remove the trend: fit a straight line, and subtract it. On the heating sensor, it works. The window averages of what is left stay between minus 0.17 and 0.18. On the walk, it does not. The line removed a slope that was never there, and what is left still wanders: its window averages go from minus 5.40 to 5.38. Over 2,000 walks, the lag-one autocorrelation of the leftover is 0.96. The memory is untouched.

The second gesture is to take differences: each value minus the one before. For the walk, the differences are just the random steps we put in. Their lag-one autocorrelation is 0.01, near zero. The walk is gone, and independent steps remain. Guess before you see: what happens to the sensor? Its climb becomes a constant. But its differences are not independent. The lag-one autocorrelation is minus 0.44 here, and minus 0.50 on average over 2,000 sensors. The same noise value is subtracted in one difference, and added in the next, so neighbours pull in opposite directions.

How different is a walk from a stationary series with a long memory? Turn the memory knob, and watch the spread of the sixth window's average over 2,000 series. Memory 0: 0.14. Memory 0.6: 0.28. Memory 0.9: 0.55. Memory 0.99: 0.95. A walk, which is memory 1: 16.64. The spread creeps up, and then the average of a walk breaks loose. So the question is whether the memory is exactly one. Dickey and Fuller published a test for it in 1979. Its null hypothesis is that the series has a unit root, as a random walk does. We only name it here.

Six more series, all simulated, with made-up units. Guess before you see: which one has a stable average? Compare the first third with the last third of each. An EEG baseline over a session: 5.9 to 9.9 microvolts. A body-weight diary: 71.7 to 71.8 kilograms. A sensor that heats up: 20.5 to 22.5 degrees. A stock price: 99.6 to 113.1. A river level with a seasonal swing: 2.6 to 1.4 meters. Reaction times as people tire: 411.2 to 455.6 milliseconds. Only the diary holds still. The river's average moves with the seasons, a case we leave for another day. Built like this, the three climbers ask for a line to remove, the stock price asks for differences, and the diary asks for nothing.

Now the strong moment. A random walk has no stable average. Take our walk again. In its six windows, the average ranges from 8.37 to 22.29, and the standard deviation from 1.60 to 5.48. Even the lag-one autocorrelation moves, from 0.80 to 0.96, against 0.97 for the whole walk. Each window describes itself faithfully. But none of them describes the process, because the process has no fixed average to describe. The mean, the standard deviation and the correlations computed on a walk describe the window we looked at.

Two walks side by side will correlate by accident, and that is the next episode. How many independent points a series really holds comes in 13.4, and modelling its memory in 13.5.

Back in the hut, the pen keeps drawing. Remember: a stationary series keeps its average, its spread and its memory, a trend or a walk does not, and the numbers computed on a walk describe the window we looked at. Next time: two smooth series will correlate.

PLATE 13.37:13

Two smooth series will correlate

Two random walks with nothing in common often look strongly correlated. Of 10,000 pairs of 100-point walks, 40 percent go beyond 0.5 in size, and the textbook test calls 76 percent significant. With more data it climbs to 92 percent. Yule described this nonsense correlation in 1926, so compare against unrelated pairs, not the textbook formula.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tide-gauge hut. Two floats sit in two harbours that never met, and each pen draws its own line. Today's claim sounds like a prank: two smooth series with nothing to do with each other will often look strongly correlated. Guess before you see: if two series are truly unrelated, how big will their correlation be?

First, the smooth series. A random walk starts at zero, and at every step it adds a random step to the previous value. That is all. Watch the first walk being built: each step is a small arrow added at the end of the line. It is smooth because each value is the previous one plus a small change, so neighbours resemble each other, as in 13.1. The lag-one autocorrelation of these walks averages 0.93.

Here is a second walk, built the same way, with other random steps. The two walks are independent by construction. The correlation of this pair is 0.07, close to zero, as we guessed. Now three more pairs. Their correlations are 0.48, 0.25, and minus 0.30.

Now the fair. We hang 24 pairs on a wall, each pair in its own frame, each curve rescaled to the same height. LENS reads the correlation of every frame, and we pin a rosette on every frame where the correlation is stronger than 0.5, in either direction. Ten frames out of 24 win a rosette. Ten out of 24, from pairs that were built to be unrelated.

Guess before you see: out of 10,000 pairs, what share would win a rosette?

First the case the textbook has in mind: 10,000 pairs of white noise, 100 points each. The correlations stay narrow, around zero, and the textbook formula, in blue, fits the pile. Beyond plus or minus 0.5: not one pair in 10,000.

Now Nil opens the curtain on 10,000 pairs of random walks, also 100 points each, drawn on the same scale. The pile is very wide. Beyond plus or minus 0.5: 40 percent of the pairs. Beyond plus or minus 0.8: 7 percent. Strong correlations between unrelated smooth series are frequent.

A tempting misreading: unrelated series average a correlation of zero, so a big one must signal a link. The average is indeed zero: 0.00. But zero is only the centre of the pile, not where the pairs sit. The typical size of the correlation, ignoring the sign, is 0.42.

This is old news. In 1926, the statistician Udny Yule described nonsense correlations between time series. His example used real data: the share of marriages held in the Church of England, and the death rate, in England and Wales, from 1866 to 1911. Over 46 years, their correlation was 0.95. And in 1974, Clive Granger and Paul Newbold showed by simulation that regressing one random walk on another gets called significant far too often.

Now the strong moment: Take the first frame of the wall that won a rosette. Its correlation is minus 0.60. As a description, that number is not wrong. The two curves did move in opposite directions.

What is wrong is the p-value. The textbook formula counts 100 independent points, and answers: p below 0.0001. But the true curtain, built from pairs of independent random walks, puts the flag well inside the pile: p equals 0.28. The textbook curtain is about five times too narrow.

Why? A random walk drifts for long stretches in one direction. In 100 points, each walk makes only a few slow swings, and two walks with a few swings each easily line up, or oppose each other. A hundred points, but a handful of independent pieces of information.

How often does the textbook test cry wolf? A test at 5 percent should be wrong one time in twenty. For white noise, it is. For random walks of 100 points, 76 percent of the pairs are declared significant.

Guess before you see: with more data, does that false-alarm rate fall? Try 50 points, 200, and 1,000. At 50, it is 66 percent. At 200, 84 percent. At 1,000, 92 percent. It does not fall. It climbs. The walks wander further, and the textbook threshold keeps shrinking.

How smooth is smooth? Turn one knob: the memory of the series, from zero to one. With no memory, the false-alarm rate is 5 percent. At 0.5, it is 13 percent. At 0.9, 51 percent. At 0.99, 73 percent. And for a full random walk, 76 percent.

The same trap hides in many fields. Each pair next is simulated: two independent random walks, and we kept the first draw whose correlation was beyond 0.7 in size, after two to eight tries. Two people's heart rates, recorded in separate rooms: 0.82. Two stock prices over a year: 0.74. The alpha power of the EEG of two people who never met, as in a hyperscanning control: minus 0.73. Two rivers in two countries: minus 0.86. The weekly sales of two unrelated shops: minus 0.87. Every textbook p-value is far below 0.0001. And the real case again: Yule's marriages and deaths, 0.95.

Imagine a report: the slow signals of two people correlated at minus 0.73, p below 0.0001. A careful reader asks: compared with what? Among unrelated random walks of 100 points, 17 percent of the pairs go beyond 0.7 in size. The honest comparison is the curtain of unrelated pairs, not the textbook formula.

One more distinction. If two series climb because of a real shared cause, like two cities warming with the planet, the correlation is real too. The p-value cannot tell a shared cause from two independent wanderings. Only what we know about the data can.

So what do we keep? The correlation stays, as a description of the window we looked at. What we drop is its textbook p-value. Three remedies come next: count the points differently, in 13.4; whiten each series, in 13.5; and shuffle in a way that keeps the memory, in 14.5.

Remember: two smooth series will correlate by accident, often strongly. The correlation describes the window, but its textbook p-value counts points that are not independent. Next time: the effective sample size, in 13.4.

PLATE 13.47:28

Effective sample size

A thousand points with memory 0.9 are worth only about 53 independent ones, since one independent point stands for 19 neighbours. Sampling faster adds nothing. Every formula containing n, the standard error, interval, t test and p-value, needs this effective size instead. With it, an interval that covered the true mean 33.1 percent of the time covers 93.6.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tide-gauge hut. Last time, three remedies were announced, and the first was to count differently. A thousand readings in a row: how many independent pieces of information do they really hold? Guess before you see: each value is 0.9 times the previous one, plus fresh noise. How many of the thousand points are worth counting?

Simulate it. Fifty thousand series of a thousand points, first with no memory at all. The average of each series falls as a tile in a pile. The textbook promises a spread of sigma over the square root of n, which is 0.032. The measured spread is 0.032.

Now the same, with memory 0.9. The series itself is wider: sigma is 2.29, so the promise is 0.073. The measured spread of the pile is 0.31. That is 4.3 times wider than promised.

Which number of independent points would give a spread of 0.31? Solve for n, and the answer is 53.2.

Now the formula. For a series where each value is the memory times the previous value plus noise, the effective size for the mean is n times one minus the memory, over one plus the memory. Here, 1,000 times 0.1, over 1.9. That is 52.6, exact arithmetic. One independent point is worth 19 neighbours. Be clear about what this is: a large-sample approximation for a first-order autoregressive series.

Here is what 19 means. Lay the thousand values out as a contact sheet, like a photographer's burst, each frame shaded by its value. With no memory, the sheet looks like confetti. With memory 0.9, neighbours share a shade. As an illustration, circle every 19 frames: about 53 circles, about 53 independent shots.

Turn the memory knob. With no memory, a thousand points are worth about a thousand. At 0.3, 532. At 0.6, 253. At 0.9, 53.2. At 0.95, 26.2. Guess before you see: at 0.99, how many? The simulation says 5.6, and the formula says 5.0. The simulation is 11 percent above the formula.

Does the formula fail on short records? Keep memory 0.9 and shorten the series. At 1,000 points, the simulation is 1 percent above the formula. At 300 points, 3 percent. At 100 points, 10 percent. At 30 points, the formula says 1.6 and the simulation says 2.2. The smaller the effective size, the rougher the formula.

Now sample faster. One slow signal, simulated, recorded for 100 seconds, once, twice, five and ten times a second. That is 100, 200, 500 and 1,000 points. The spread of the average, in units of the signal's own spread, is 0.42 each time. The textbook promise falls from 0.10 to 0.032. The effective size is 5.6 every time. More points between the same readings are not more information, as in 13.1. The tide gauge shows it too: read every minute for a month, 43,200 points, or every hour, 720 points. With an illustrative memory, both are worth 43.2.

The same arithmetic on records you know. The memories are illustrative, and the arithmetic is exact. EEG band power, 250 samples a second for ten seconds: 2,500 points, worth 12.6. A mood diary, 90 days: 30.0. Body temperature, one reading a minute for a day: 1,440 points, worth 7.2. Heart rate, once a second for an hour: 3,600 points, worth 92.3. A slow fMRI-like signal, 300 volumes: 33.3. Monthly sales over five years: 60 points, worth 10.6.

Back to the false alarms of 13.3. Two independent series, both with memory 0.9, 100 points each. In nineteen ninety-nine, Bretherton and colleagues gave the effective size for a correlation: n times one minus r1 times r2, over one plus r1 times r2, where r1 and r2 are the two memories. Here, 100 times 0.19, over 1.81: 10.5.

The textbook test at 5 percent, with n equal to 100, declares 50.4 percent of 10,000 simulated pairs significant. In 13.3 it was 51, from another batch of pairs. Now replace n by 10.5. The cutoff for the correlation moves from 0.20 to 0.62. The pile's own cutoff is 0.52. With the true memories, the corrected test declares 1.5 percent: too careful, not exactly 5.

In real life, we do not know the memories. Estimate each from its series, and the corrected test declares 4.9 percent. That is close to 5, but partly by luck: the estimated memory averages 0.85, below 0.9, which loosens the test.

Only one series smooth? Memories 0.9 and 0: the product is zero, the effective size stays 100, and the textbook test is right at 5.0 percent. Memories 0.5 and 0.5: the effective size is 60, the textbook test declares 12.4 percent, and the corrected one, with the true memories, 4.5. Memories 0.9 and 0.5: the effective size is 37.9, textbook 20.9 percent, corrected 3.6. The product of the two memories is what matters.

Now an interval for a mean, with memory 0.9 and 1,000 points. The usual 95 percent interval covers the true mean in 33.1 percent of 10,000 series. Put the estimated effective size in the formula, and it covers 93.6 percent. Of the 30 intervals drawn, the usual one covers 13, and the corrected one 29.

Now the strong moment. Every formula with n inside, the standard error, the interval, the t test, the p-value, needs the effective size instead. On a smooth signal, it is a small fraction of the points. With memory 0.99, a thousand points are worth about five: 5.0 by the formula, 5.6 in the simulation. The formulas cannot see the memory unless we tell them. Fitting a model of it comes in 13.5, and shuffling in a way that keeps it in 14.5.

Back in the hut. Remember: neighbours that resemble each other are worth less than their count, so replace n by the effective size, a large-sample approximation. Next time: autoregressive models and prewhitening.

PLATE 13.56:53

Autoregressive models and prewhitening

An autoregressive model captures memory: today is the echo, 0.9 times yesterday, plus fresh noise, like a river after rain. Subtracting the echo leaves residuals close to white noise. Prewhitening two unrelated smooth series cuts false correlation from about half the pairs to 5.2 percent. It keeps real delayed links but removes a slow signal two series genuinely share.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tide-gauge hut. Last time we counted the points differently. Today is the second remedy: take the memory out before comparing. To do that, we first need a model of the memory.

Here is a simulated series, built with a memory of 0.9. Each value is made of two parts. The echo is 0.9 times yesterday's value, and the fresh part is a new random draw. Stack them, and you get today's value. Think of a river after rain: tomorrow's level is 0.9 of today's, plus whatever the sky adds. This recipe is called an autoregressive model of order one: today is regressed on yesterday, and it looks one step back.

In real life nobody tells us the memory, so we estimate it. Plot each value against the one before, and draw the least-squares line from chapter 9. Its slope is the memory: 0.87 here, a little under the true 0.9. Guess before you see: repeat this on ten thousand new series, where do the estimates land? They average 0.90, and wander by 0.014. With only a hundred points they average 0.86 and wander by 0.06, so short series underestimate the memory.

Now subtract the echo from every value. What remains is the fresh part: the residuals. The series was smooth, and the residuals jump around like noise. Check it with the autocorrelation bars from 13.1. The series has a lag-one autocorrelation of 0.87, and every bar sticks out of the chance band. The residuals have 0.02, and one bar in sixteen leaves the band, about what chance alone gives. Replacing a series by its residuals is called prewhitening, because the residuals are close to white noise. Be careful what this means: white residuals do not say the series was random. They say the model has taken the predictable part.

Memory lives everywhere. Daily temperature remembers yesterday's weather. An EEG band-power envelope drifts slowly. Heart rate over a night changes gently. Monthly unemployment moves like a slow tide, a river stays high for days after the rain, and the tide gauge surge lingers for hours. These six records are simulated, with memories I invented. The temperature fit finds 0.75 where I used 0.7, because a year is a short record. The EEG envelope fit finds 0.95, the river 0.89. In each one the fit finds the memory again, and the residuals lose their lag-one autocorrelation. All but one: the tide record keeps 0.08, just outside its band, because its memory estimate came out a little low. In fMRI, the software SPM whitens the noise by default with an order-one model, and the fMRI series covers that in its own video.

One step back is not always enough. A slow oscillation depends on the last two values. In 1927 Yule used two values like this, with dice for the fresh noise, to describe sunspot numbers. Here is a simulated oscillation, built with two memories, 1.6 and minus 0.8. Guess before you see: fit order one, and how many of twenty bars leave the band? Twelve. The residuals still carry structure. Fit order two, and none do. The fit finds 1.61 and minus 0.81. Order three adds nothing: the size of the residuals goes 1.65, then 0.97, then 0.97. So choosing the order is a judgement, helped by these checks. Raise it until the residuals look white, and stop. Box and Jenkins wrote a book on these models in 1970.

Now the situation from 13.3: two unrelated smooth series, memory 0.9 each, a hundred points. The textbook test calls them correlated in 49 percent of the pairs, about half again, though nothing links them. Here is one such pair: the correlation is 0.46. Whiten each series with its own estimated memory, and compare the residuals: the correlation is 0.10. Guess before you see: over ten thousand pairs, how many are now declared correlated? The answer is 5.2 percent, close to the 5 we asked for. The spread of the correlations shrinks from 0.27 to 0.10.

Does whitening destroy real links? Let one series drive another, with a delay of three steps. The raw cross-correlation is a broad hump: about 16 of the 20 other delays leave the band as well. After whitening there is one clear bar, at delay three, and only about one other delay leaves the band. The link survives: 0.50 before, 0.51 after. The raw peak was also at delay three in 97 percent of the runs, so the hump was not wrong, only blurry. After whitening, it is 100 percent.

Whitening is not free. Take unrelated oscillating series. Whiten them with order one, and 35 percent of the pairs are still declared correlated, against 39 percent raw. With order two, 4.9 percent. The residuals of an order-one fit are still slow waves, and a wrong order leaves the false alarms.

And sometimes the slow shared part is exactly what you want to study. Two series share a slow signal, plus their own noise. The average correlation is 0.68 raw, and 0.20 after whitening. The share of pairs declared correlated falls from 100 percent to 78. Whitening removes what is predictable, and a slow shared signal is predictable. If that is your question, do not whiten, or say what you removed.

Now the strong moment. Modelling the dependence turns a dependent series into residuals that are roughly independent, and on those, the classical tools are valid again. That is the whole idea: predict, subtract, compare what is left. And the question has changed: we no longer compare the series, we compare what each one has that is unpredictable. Of the three remedies announced in 13.3, two are done: counting differently, and whitening. The third keeps the memory and shuffles around it, and that is chapter 14.

Back in the hut. Remember: model the memory, then compare what it cannot predict. Order one fits many records, order two fits oscillations, and the residuals tell you whether the model is enough. Next time: a shuffle is a null hypothesis.
