A mean without addition

A mean can be defined with no addition, as the point minimising summed squared distances. On wagons at 0, 1, 3, 7 and 8.5 the squared version gives 3.90, the plain-rope median and medoid give 3. A far point drags the mean by 50 when sent to 1,000. On a ring of radius 5, the mean sits in the empty hole.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Take the mean. You learned it with an addition: add everything up, divide by how many.

But what do you add when the things are angles? Or ellipses? Or trees? Nothing adds up there.

There is another way to say what a mean is, and it needs no addition at all. It needs only a distance.

We met the idea in episode 1.6, as a pin that minimises squared distances. Now we go all the way with it.

Picture a railyard. Five wagons stand on one straight track, at 0, 1, 3, 7 and 8.5. They are the same five points that episode 15.6 turned into a tree. We tie a rope from each wagon to a single knot, and we ask where the knot should rest.

First, let every rope be a spring. A spring pulls harder the farther it stretches. The knot settles where the total of the squared stretches is smallest. Slide it along the track and watch the total: it falls, reaches a lowest point, and climbs again.

That lowest point is at 3.90, and the total there is 55.20. It is exactly the ordinary mean. So here is the definition that needs no addition: the mean is the point with the smallest sum of squared distances to the data. It is called the Fréchet mean, after Maurice Fréchet, who studied averages in spaces that only have a distance.

Now take away the squares. Use plain ropes, which pull with the same force however long they are. A far wagon no longer pulls harder than a near one. The total length of rope is 14.50 at 3, and a little more, 15.40, at 3.90. So the knot settles at 3. That is the median.

And if the knot must sit on a wagon, we pick the wagon with the smallest total distance to all the others. It is the wagon at 3, again. This one is called the medoid: the most central point that was actually observed. Because it is always one of your data points, it works with nothing but a table of distances.

The table of distances also tells you how spread out the data are. Square the ten distances between pairs and add them up: you get 276. Divide by five, and you get 55.20.

That is exactly the sum of squared gaps to the mean, 55.20. We measured the spread without ever placing the mean. Episode 17.5 will use this.

The square has a price. Take twenty simulated points, and send one of them far away: to 10, then 100, then 1,000. Averaged over a thousand draws, the mean moves by 0.50, then 5, then 50. It follows the wild point, in proportion.

The knot with plain ropes moves by 0.11, however far the wild point goes. The medoid moves by 0.13 or 0.14. Squaring gives the far point a louder voice.

The plain-rope problem is old. Fermat posed it. Torricelli solved the case of three points. And in 1937, Weiszfeld gave a way to reach the answer step by step: move the knot, re-weigh the ropes, and repeat.

The answer is called the geometric median. It holds up well against bad data: it only runs away when about half of the points are wrong. That value, one half, goes back to Lopuhaä and Rousseeuw in 1991. The exact count for a small sample is still discussed, so we say about half.

It also exists on curved spaces. Fletcher and colleagues used it to average brain images, so that one odd image would not drag the result.

Now a warning: a mean is not always one point.

On a circle, the flat recipe already fails: the plain average of 350 and 10 is 180, the opposite side of the dial. The distance version asks for the direction with the smallest squared gap to the data.

Take two directions, 350 degrees and 10 degrees. There is one mean: 0 degrees, the middle of the short way round.

Take 0 and 180 degrees. Now there are two means, at 90 and 270, and they are equally good. Four directions in a cross give four means: 45, 135, 225 and 315 degrees. Episode 10.3 showed this for angles, and episode 14.5 for persistence diagrams.

The flat recipe has the same flaw. If the data go around a circle, the average of their coordinates falls inside it, where nobody stands. A mean can land where there is nothing. Here are 500 simulated points on a ring of radius 5. The mean sits at the centre, in the hole. The nearest real point is 4.02 away.

The medoid stays among the data, at radius 4.08, at the inner edge of the ring. But nothing marks that spot as special. The mean is nowhere near anyone.

One more case. Here are 500 random points in 1,000 columns, simulated. Each point is between 29.7 and 34.0 from the mean. The two closest points are 40.5 apart.

So the mean is nearer to every point than any point is to another. It is the popular neighbour of episode 2.6, and it looks like nobody.

This matters beyond toy examples. Normative Modelling 9.6 asks for norms of things that are not one number. A centre defined by a distance is where such a norm can start.

Let us fill in the card. The object: anything with a distance. The distance: the one you choose. What it ignores: whatever the distance ignores. Change the distance, and the mean changes, as in episode 8.3.

What it costs: a formula when the space is flat, a step by step search when it is not, and every pair of points for the medoid. Its trap: a mean always comes out.

So before you use one, ask two questions. Is it unique? And is there data around it?

A mean needs no addition, only a distance. But it always comes out, even in the middle of a ring where nobody lives.

Groups always come out

Ask for groups and you always get them. Sixty simulated signals split by baseline with the straight line and by shape with one minus correlation, two bits apart in variation of information. A single round cloud still yields two to six groups, but the gap statistic says one group. Cutting a cloud then testing the difference makes the test say yes in all 200 draws.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Ask a yard for groups, and it will hand you groups. Any yard, any wagons.

Last time we found a centre. Now we form groups. Here, clustering means exactly that: forming groups. All it needs is a table of distances.

Two things decide the groups before the data have a say. The first is the distance.

Take sixty simulated signals. Two shapes, three cycles or five, and two baseline levels, low or high. Fifteen signals of each kind. We ask for two groups.

With the straight line, the two groups are the two baseline levels. The agreement with the baseline is 1.00, and with the shape, minus 0.02. Zero means no better than chance.

Now use one minus correlation. Note that this is not a distance in the strict sense: episode 2.3 showed why. With it, the two groups are the two shapes. The agreement with the shape is 1.00, and with the baseline, minus 0.02.

Same sixty signals. Two answers, both perfectly sharp.

How far apart are the two groupings? Episode 15.7 gave us a distance for that, the variation of information, in bits. A bit is what you learn from the answer to a question with two equally likely outcomes.

This distance counts, in bits, what is still unknown about an object's group in one grouping once you know its group in the other, and the same the other way round. It is zero for identical groupings.

Here it is 2.00 bits. Knowing one grouping tells you nothing about the other, and one bit is missing in each direction. The Information series defines these quantities in episodes 2.2 and 2.4.

The second thing is the merging rule of the tree. A tree starts with every point alone, and keeps merging the two closest groups. But what is the distance between two groups?

You can take their nearest members. Or the average over all pairs. Or their farthest members. Or Ward's rule, the merge that adds the least squared spread.

Two rings, one inside the other. Nearest members find them both: agreement 1.00. The other three rules cut the rings like a cake: from 0.11 to 0.21.

Two clouds joined by a bridge of points. Now it is the opposite. Nearest members follow the bridge, and score 0.00. The other three score 0.97 to 0.98. These are averages over ten simulated draws.

So the distance and the rule decide. Now the fact that gives this episode its title: groups always come out.

One round cloud of 200 simulated points. No groups at all. We ask for two groups, then three, then six. We get them every time.

The silhouette score tells you how good they are. It was proposed by Rousseeuw in 1987. For each point, it compares the average distance to its own group with the average distance to the next closest group. It runs from minus one to one, and gets close to one when groups are tight and well apart.

On the round cloud, in two columns, it is 0.33 to 0.35, for any number of groups from two to six. In ten columns, it is 0.08 to 0.09. On a uniform square, it climbs to between 0.37 and 0.42. These are averages over twenty draws.

A rule of thumb, often attributed to Kaufman and Rousseeuw's book of 1990, would call these values weak structure, possibly artificial. The boundaries vary between sources.

So we need checks. There are three.

First check: compare with data that have no structure, of the same size. This is the gap statistic of Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001. On the round cloud, its answer is a single group, in 20 draws out of 20, in two columns and in ten.

Second check: form the groups again on half of the data, and see whether they come out the same. Two random halves of the round cloud agree at 0.26 on average, from 0.01 to 0.65. Two real groups, four apart, agree at 0.98.

But a cloud three times longer than wide, with no groups, agrees at 0.77. Stability measures the symmetry of the cloud, not the number of groups. This is the warning of Ben-David, von Luxburg and Pál, in 2006.

The third check is the trap. We cut the round cloud in two with k-means. Then we test whether the two groups differ.

With 200 points, the shuffle test on the table of distances says yes in every one of the 200 draws. Its floor is 0.005, since it shuffles only 199 times.

Column by column, the t-tests are just as sure. In ten columns, with 200 points, on average two thirds of the columns come out different at the five percent level, where five percent is the floor when nothing differs. And the smallest p-value of the column tests has a median of ten to the minus seventeen.

We tested a difference we had just made. The cut looks for the largest gap, and then the test finds a gap.

Cutting on one half of the data and testing on the other does not repair it. With 200 points, the shuffle test says yes again, 100 percent. With 40 points the defect is less complete: 94 percent in two columns, 63 percent in ten.

Tests that account for the cut do exist. Gao, Bean and Witten, in 2024, built one for trees. Zhang, Kamath and Tsay, in 2019, built one for single-cell data.

A case mattered in psychiatry. In 2017, Drysdale and colleagues announced four subtypes of depression, read from brain scans.

In 2019, Dinga and colleagues ran the same kind of analysis on 187 people. The links between brain and symptoms were 0.99 and 0.97 inside the sample, and 0.07 and minus 0.07 outside it. A single round cloud could not be ruled out. They did find a best solution with three groups. Neither the links nor the groups held up in the tests.

Now the card. What it ignores: whatever the distance ignores, and whatever the merging rule ignores. What it costs: all the pairs, and a control against data with no structure. The trap: testing the groups on the data that made them.

And the trap behind that one: believing there are true groups, whatever they are for. Hennig, in 2015, asks for the purpose to be stated first.

Groups always come out. Only a control tells you whether they were there. For the groups in a graph, the Graph Theory series asks it in episode 8.5.

A map from a table

A table of distances alone can draw a map. Ten Québec cities come out with a largest error of 0.3 kilometres, since the check finds no negative axis. Ten world cities have four negative axes and a 1,809 kilometre average error. Stress rates six structureless objects as excellent at 1.9 percent, and a saturating distance bends a line into a horseshoe.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Open a road atlas. In one corner there is a little triangle of numbers: the distance between every pair of cities, and nothing else. No coordinates. No map.

Can you draw the map from that corner alone? This is the third gesture of the chapter: draw a map. And like the other gestures, it always returns something.

Take ten cities of Québec: Montréal, Québec, Sherbrooke, Trois-Rivières, Saguenay, Gatineau, Rimouski, Val-d'Or, Gaspé and Sept-Îles. Their distances run from 114 to 983 kilometres. Here is the table. Darker means farther.

The recipe is a direct calculation, from Torgerson in 1952. Square every distance. From the squares, recover the dot products of the cities, measured from their common centre. Then take the axes of that new table, the strongest first. Two axes are enough.

And here is the map, drawn without a single coordinate. The largest gap between a distance on the map and a distance in the table is 0.3 kilometres.

It comes out turned, and it might come out flipped. The table knows nothing about north, and nothing about mirrors. We turned it to match the real map, only so that you can compare.

Why does it work? The calculation is exact when the distances are straight lines in a flat space. And a check tells you so before you draw anything: no axis may have a negative squared length. That criterion goes back to Schoenberg in 1935, and to Young and Householder in 1938.

For Québec, two axes carry 85 and 15 per cent, and nothing is negative to speak of. The map is almost exact, not quite exact. The Earth is curved, and that is where the 0.3 kilometres come from.

Now the same calculation for ten cities of the world: Montréal, Paris, Tokyo, Sydney, São Paulo, Cape Town, Cairo, Mumbai, Los Angeles and Reykjavik. Their distances run from 2,233 to 18,535 kilometres.

A map comes out, as it always does. But the average error is now 1,809 kilometres, about 20 per cent of a distance. Cairo and Los Angeles are 12,201 kilometres apart on the globe. On the map, they are 6,605.

And the check said so in advance. Four axes have a negative squared length: minus 9.3, minus 5.8, minus 1.0 and minus 0.2 per cent of the total. No flat space, in any number of dimensions, gives these distances. That is episode 9.1, read in a table.

The same warning rings whenever distances are not straight lines of a flat space. A small graph of five nodes, measured in steps, has a negative axis too.

There is a second family of maps. They keep only the order of the distances: the nearest pair stays the nearest, and so on. This is due to Shepard in 1962 and Kruskal in 1964. There is no direct formula here: the computer improves the map step by step.

Their error is called the stress. It is the misfit between the map's distances and the closest numbers that keep the order of the table.

Kruskal gave a rule of thumb, from his experience in 1964. A stress of 20 per cent is poor. 10 per cent is fair. 5 is good, and 2.5 is excellent. The grid has been used everywhere. Mair, Groenen and de Leeuw call that mechanical use problematic.

So test it on objects with no structure at all: random points in ten columns. Map them in two dimensions, and average over ten draws. With 6 objects, the stress is 1.9 per cent: excellent. With 10 objects, 12.5. With 40, 27. With 80, 30 per cent. Nothing changed but the number of objects.

Stress grows with the number of objects, as Mair, Groenen and de Leeuw point out, and the grid does not account for it. Six objects with no structure at all earn an excellent map.

So a score of a map is only read against the score of objects with no structure, in the same number. And more axes lower it: ten such objects, in one, two and three dimensions, give 36.9, 12.5 and 4.9 per cent.

One last trap: the horseshoe. Fifty objects stand on one line, at 0, 1, 2, up to 49. Their distances are the gaps between them, except that a distance saturates: beyond 10, everything is simply far.

The map bends the line. The two ends, the farthest objects there are, land 3.7 apart, while each of them is 8.0 from the middle. The first three axes share 27, 24 and 18 per cent, for an object that has one dimension.

Diaconis, Goel and Holmes explained it in 2008. A horseshoe appears when only the small distances are reliable. They showed it on the 2005 votes of the American House of Representatives: 401 representatives.

Saturating distances are common in this series: the total variation of episode 5.1, the root of the Jensen-Shannon divergence of episode 5.4. Episode 9.6 gave a remedy: replace the large distances by paths of small steps.

Let us fill in the card. The object: a table of all the pairs. The distance: whichever you chose. What it ignores: the position, the orientation and the mirror of the map. Its axes mean nothing by themselves.

What it costs: the decomposition of a table of all pairs. Its trap: reading a shape that comes from the method. A curve on the map may be a straight line and a distance that saturates.

So a map always comes out. Look for negative axes. Read the stress against objects with no structure. And distrust every curve.

A map needs only a table. But it always comes out, even for six objects with no structure, and then it is called excellent.

Ask the neighbours

A new wagon is sent where its closest example stands. With the best possible error at 15.9 percent, one neighbour is stuck near 22.5, while about the square root of examples gets 16.0. The triangle shortcut skips nearly all candidates in two columns but only 0.4 percent in 100. As a norm, a ring's centre looks most normal by the mean, strangest by neighbours.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A new wagon rolls into the yard. Nobody knows which siding it belongs to.

It has no label. There is no rulebook and no model. There are only the wagons already sorted, and a way to measure how far the newcomer is from each of them.

So the clerk does the simplest thing there is. Find the closest wagon, and send the newcomer where it stands.

That is the nearest neighbour rule. It needs two things only: examples, and a distance. It is the fourth gesture of this chapter: ask the neighbours.

Here is a test we can score. Two kinds of wagon, blue and pink, along one column. The two kinds are centred two standard deviations apart, and they overlap.

Even the best possible rule, which knows exactly how both kinds are spread, gets 15.9 percent of the newcomers wrong. Nobody can do better than that.

In 1967, Cover and Hart proved something striking about the lazy rule. With infinitely many examples, its error is never more than twice the best one. Here the sharper bound is 26.7 percent, and the rule's true limit is 22.5. Read it carefully: the guarantee is about the limit, with endless examples. It says nothing about the examples you hold.

So we measure. Twenty simulated draws each. With 20 examples, one neighbour is wrong 23.6 percent of the time. With 100, 22.6. With 1,000, 22.1. With 10,000, 22.5. It hardly improves, because it has already reached its limit.

One neighbour listens to one voice, and that voice is noisy. So let the clerk ask several, and take a vote. We use about the square root of the number of examples: 5, 11, 33 and 101 neighbours. The error falls to 18.3 percent, then 16.8, then 16.0 and 16.0. That is almost the best possible rule, 15.9, reached from examples alone.

The second use is searching. Not deciding a label, only fetching the closest wagon. Among 5,000 points, that costs 5,000 distances for every question.

The triangle rule saves some of them. Keep twenty landmark points, and the distance from each landmark to every point. Now suppose the question is 10 away from a landmark, and a candidate is 2 away from the same landmark. Then the candidate is at least 8 away from the question. If we already hold a closer wagon, we never measure this one.

In two columns, the rule rules out nearly all the candidates: 100 percent, rounded. In 5 columns, 99.9 percent. In 10, 93.3. In 20, only 11.0. In 50 and in 100 columns, 0.4 percent. These are simulated: 5,000 points, 20 landmarks, 200 questions.

Why does the shortcut collapse? This is episode 2.6 again. Divide the farthest point's distance by the nearest point's. It is 264 in two columns, then 13.8, 5.2, 2.9, 1.9 and 1.6. When the farthest is hardly farther than the nearest, no bound can tell them apart.

Indexes that cut the space into pieces fare no better. Weber, Schek and Blott showed in 1998 that beyond around ten dimensions, on uniform data, such indexes lose to plain reading of everything.

And the rule needs a true triangle inequality. Without it, the shortcut is not allowed. The lamp for the fourth rule goes out, and you pay for it here.

The third use is saying whether something is normal. Back to the ring of episode 17.1: 500 simulated points, around radius 5.

One way to build a norm is the distance to the mean, in units of the cloud's own spread. A typical point of the ring sits at 1.41, and 99 percent of the points are below 1.62.

Now try the centre of the ring. It lies 0.04 from the mean: the most normal point there could be. But ask its neighbours. Its fifth neighbour is 4.22 away, while 99 percent of the real points have a fifth neighbour within 0.69.

Judged by the mean, the most normal. Judged by the neighbours, the strangest.

A point on the ring: 1.44, and 0.46. Both agree it is normal. A point at radius 8: 2.30, and 2.87. Both flag it.

The distance to the mean assumes a full cloud. The distance to the neighbours assumes no shape at all, and it needs only a table of distances, no columns. Normative Modelling 9.6 asks for norms of things that are not one number. This is one way to start.

Did anyone publish such a norm? For this episode we searched again, narrowed to that meaning: a norm built on a table of distances, read from neighbours. The search made for this episode found nothing. It found a close cousin: a recent preprint, not yet peer reviewed, that judges brain structure measurements by the density of their neighbours. But it works from columns, with the plain Euclidean distance.

The distance to the fifth neighbour is also an old outlier score for any data, from Ramaswamy and colleagues in 2000. And comparing one person to a group of matrices does exist, differently: by unfolding the matrices around their mean. That was Varoquaux and colleagues in 2010, and episode 8.8.

Let us fill in the card. The object: examples, and a distance. The distance: the one you choose. What it ignores: whatever the distance ignores, and nothing else. No model is added.

What it costs: keeping every example, and measuring a distance to each one for every question. Its trap comes in two parts.

First, the popular neighbours of episode 2.6, studied by Radovanović, Nanopoulos and Ivanović in 2010. In many columns, a few points become the neighbours of almost everyone, and they vote in every poll.

Second, the number of neighbours. Try 1, 5, 11, and keep the one that looks best on your test, and the choice is no longer a measurement. Choose the number of neighbours, and the distance, before you see the result.

Ask the neighbours, and they always answer. Even in the middle of a ring where nobody lives.

Testing by shuffling

Do two groups differ, given only a table of distances? Square and add the distances to get a pseudo-F, then shuffle labels 199 times. A p-value of 0.76 appears for thirty points with no difference. The test confuses place and spread: a small group twice as spread says different in 37.8 percent. Shuffling trials instead of people gives 79 percent false alarms.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two groups of objects, and one table with the distance between every pair. Do the groups really differ?

This is the fifth and last gesture of the chapter: testing a difference. Like the other four, it needs no columns. It needs only the table.

Picture the railyard. Thirty wagons, fifteen blue and fifteen pink. The colour is the group. Dice works the top of the hump, where a wagon can be sent down any siding.

First, read the table twice. In episode 17.1 you saw that the spread of a cloud can be read from its table: square every distance between pairs, add them up, and divide by the number of objects. Do it for all thirty wagons together. Then do it again inside each group, and add the two results. The ratio between what the groups explain and what remains is the statistic of the test. It is a pseudo-F. It is the one used by PERMANOVA, the test Anderson described in 2001. The identity behind it is classical, and goes back to Gower, in 1966.

Let us check it on thirty points in seven columns. The spread is 188.40 from the coordinates, and 188.40 from the table alone. The ratio is 0.6267 either way. The table is enough.

Is 0.6267 large? Here comes the idea of the Statistics series, in episodes 14.1 and 14.2: shuffle the labels. Send the wagons over the hump again, so that the colours land on other wagons. The table does not change, because the distances belong to the wagons, not to the colours. Only the groups change. Compute the ratio again, and again. Do it 199 times.

Each shuffle gives a ratio that luck alone could build. Ours sits in the middle of the pile. Three quarters of the shuffles do at least as well, so the p-value is 0.76. Nothing here, and that is right: these thirty points were drawn with no difference at all.

Keep one number in mind. When nothing differs, a test at the five percent level still says different in about five draws out of a hundred. That is the floor. Every share you will see today is read against it.

Now, three things decide what this test can tell you. The first is the distance.

Take two groups of twenty covariance matrices, with eight channels. Their sources of variance go from 100 down to 0.01. In one group, multiply the weakest source by 1.5. With the straight line, the test says different in 6.5 percent of 200 simulated draws. That is no better than with no difference at all, where it says 5.5 percent. With the distance in ratios, it says different every time: 100 percent.

Now multiply the strongest source by 1.15 instead. The straight line sees it every time, 100 percent. The distance in ratios sees it in 61.5 percent. Those are the cards of episodes 8.1 and 8.2, turned into test results. No test on a table sees what the distance has erased.

The second thing is that the test mixes up place and spread.

Take two groups with the same centre, one of 10 points and one of 40, in five columns. If both have the same spread, the test says different in 5.3 percent of 1,000 draws. Now make the small group twice as spread out. The test says the groups differ in 37.8 percent of draws, though the centres are the same. Make the large group twice as spread out instead, and it says so in 0.1 percent: it has gone blind.

With 25 points in each group, the problem fades. One group twice as spread out: 5.1 percent. Three times: 5.4 percent. Anderson and Walsh studied this in 2013, and found that with balanced groups PERMANOVA is largely unaffected, while two other tests, ANOSIM and the Mantel test, are very sensitive. We have only read their abstract. The numbers here are ours.

So a test of the spread always goes with the first one, as Anderson proposed in 2006. On 300 of those draws, it is positive in 100 percent of the unequal cases, and in 3.7 percent when the spreads are equal.

Now the two traps. The first one: choosing the distance after you have seen the p-value.

Take data with no difference at all, two groups of twenty, ten positive columns. Try eight distances: the straight line, city-block, Chebyshev, cosine, correlation, Canberra, Bray-Curtis, and the standardised line. Each one alone says different in 4.1 to 5.0 percent of 1,000 draws. Fine. But keep the smallest p-value of the first two, and it says different in 5.7 percent. Of four: 9.7 percent. Of eight: 13.0 percent.

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn counted this kind of freedom in 2011. Four of them combined gave 60.7 percent false positives. Alone, they gave 9.5, 7.7, 11.7 and 12.6 percent. Their freedoms are not distances, and the eight-distance figures are ours. The rule is the same. Choose the distance before you look.

The second trap: shuffling the wrong unit. Statistics 14.4 asks the question: which unit do we shuffle? The answer is the unit that was drawn at random.

Two groups of 10 people, 20 trials each, and no difference between the groups. The people differ from each other by half the noise of a trial. Shuffle the trials one by one, and the test says the groups differ in 79 percent of the draws. Shuffle the people, and it says so in 5.6 percent. Without any difference between people, both give about five percent. When the people differ by as much as the noise of a trial, shuffling trials says different in 95 percent of draws, and shuffling people in 6.4 percent.

The trials of one person resemble each other. They are not independent draws. Shuffled one by one, the differences between people pass for a difference between groups. We shuffle what we drew at random. Here, that is the person.

Here is the card of this gesture. The object: a table of distances, and a label on each object. The distance: the ratio of what the groups explain to what remains, and the p-value comes from the shuffles. What it ignores: whatever the distance has erased. What it costs: the table once, then shuffles that are almost free. Its trap: choosing the distance after the p-value, and shuffling trials when the unit is the person.

Like a mean, a map and a tree, a test always returns something. A p-value always comes out. Whether it can be trusted depends on the distance, the spread and the unit.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Sixteen chapters built distances. This one said what to do with a table of them.

Five gestures are enough. Find a centre. Form groups. Draw a map. Ask the neighbours. Test a difference. Each one works from the table alone. And each one has the same defect: it always returns something, even on data with no structure. Each has a check that says so.

Today we ask a different question. Where have these gestures been used on brain signals? What has held up? And what is still only a lead?

We sort the claims on two sidings of the same railyard. On the left siding, a wagon is established. It is coupled to its train, painted solid blue, and it carries a plate that says how we read its source: the full text, the abstract, or only part of the text. On the right siding, the wagons are only outlines. They are leads. Nobody has loaded them, and nothing on them was tested.

First wagon. Forming groups. EEG microstates are groups of scalp maps. Pascual-Marqui, Michel and Lehmann described the method in 1995. It is a modified k-means, where each group has an orientation. Later descriptions say that the distance is blind to the sign, so a map and its mirror image count as the same. Michel and Koenig reviewed the field in 2018. We read both abstracts. The sign comes from the later descriptions.

Second wagon. The centre. In brain-computer interfaces, Barachant and colleagues, in 2012, classified covariance matrices by their distance to the mean of each class. The distance and the mean are the Riemannian ones of chapter 8. This simple method gave results comparable to the reference method. The gain, from 65.1 to 70.2 percent, belongs to a second method, a linear classifier in the tangent space. Congedo, Barachant and Bhatia reviewed the field in 2017. We read the abstracts.

Third wagon. The map and the tree. Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini proposed in 2008 to compare brain responses through a table of dissimilarities, and to draw it as a map. The same year, Kriegeskorte and colleagues read the table of 92 images as a tree, in people and in monkeys. We read both papers in part.

Fourth wagon. The test. Anderson, in 2001, published a test that needs only a table of distances. The groups are compared by shuffling, and the distance can be any one you choose. It is called PERMANOVA. We read its abstract, and secondary descriptions.

The warnings are established too. They ride on a second train.

Dinga and colleagues, in 2019, repeated a published recipe for subtypes of depression, on 187 people. The two strongest links between brain measures and symptoms were 0.99 and 0.97 inside the sample. On people held out, they were 0.07 and minus 0.07. The authors did find a best solution of three groups. But they showed that this solution would also appear in a single Gaussian cloud. We read the full text.

Ben-David, von Luxburg and Pál, in 2006: the stability of a grouping, how well it survives a new sample, follows what the algorithm optimises and the symmetries of the data. It is a weak guide to the number of groups. We read the abstract.

Diaconis, Goel and Holmes, in 2008: a map of data that really lie along a line can bend into a horseshoe, when only the near distances carry information. They showed it on the 2005 votes of the United States House. We read the abstract.

Anderson and Walsh, in 2013: tests on tables of distances can take a difference of spread for a difference of position. ANOSIM and the Mantel test are very sensitive to it. PERMANOVA is largely unaffected when the groups have equal sizes. With unequal sizes, none of the three behaved reliably. We read the abstract.

Now the empty outlines.

In hyperscanning, make each pair of people one object. A pair can be described by a matrix, a graph or a distribution, with whichever distance of this series suits it. Then build the table of distances between pairs.

The search made for this episode found no published example of this table, built and then tested. That is a limited search, not a proof.

Then the five gestures. First, the most central pair. It is a real recording, and you can watch it. Second, groups of pairs, compared with groups found in data with no structure. Third, a map of the pairs, with its reference stress. Fourth, the unusual pairs, found from their neighbours. Fifth, a test between two conditions.

Two rules. The unit to shuffle is the pair, never the trial. See Statistics 14.4. And the floor of the table comes from pseudo-pairs. Each person is matched with the partner of another pair, recorded in the same condition, on the same timing. See Hyperscanning 7.5.

And the distance is written down before the result is seen.

None of these outlines has been tested. They are leads.

Chapter 17 in one line. With a table of distances, you can average, form groups, draw, ask the neighbours, and test. None of these gestures sees what the distance ignores. And each one always returns an answer.

One question separates the two sidings. Which unit did you shuffle? And was the distance chosen before the result?
