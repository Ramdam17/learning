PLATE 6.14:09

A zoo of measures

Measures of inter-brain coupling are sorted by four switches: amplitude or phase, linear or nonlinear, symmetric or directed, pair or group. Every measure is blind to something: in simulation, correlation misses a squared relation that mutual information finds, and the phase lag index misses perfect zero-lag locking. Between two heads, shared sources replace volume conduction as the main worry.

TRANSCRIPTEN

It is festival day on the kite hill. A huge poster hangs by the entrance. Every kite model on it is a way to measure whether two brains move together. There are a lot of models. Which one should you fly?

This episode is a map, not a lesson. We sort the kites with four questions. Each question is a switch. Flip it, and the kites sort themselves into two flocks.

First switch. Does the measure read amplitude, or phase? Correlation follows how big the signal is. A phase kite throws the size away, and keeps only the angle. Coherence sits on the border, because it reads both.

Second switch. Linear, or not? A linear measure sees only straight-line relations. Correlation and coherence are linear. Mutual information and transfer entropy also see curved ones. Phase measures are usually counted with the nonlinear family, as in the review by Pereda, Quiroga and Bhattacharya, in Progress in Neurobiology, in 2005.

Third switch. Symmetric, or directed? Correlation, coherence and mutual information treat the pair as one object. Swap Ava and Otto, and the answer does not change. Granger causality, its spectral cousins and transfer entropy ask who leads. Swap the two, and the answer changes.

Fourth switch. A pair, or a group? Most kites fly in pairs. A few need three people or more at once: the O-information, and the order parameter of a whole group.

This sorting is ours. It is a teaching map, and some kites sit on a line between two answers. The chapter flies them one by one, with the same recipe every time: intuition, the calculation in one picture, what the measure is sensitive to, the trap, and what changes between two brains.

Now the important part. Every kite is blind to something. Here are three blind spots, all simulated.

First. Let Otto's signal be the square of Ava's, plus a little noise. The link is perfect, but it is curved. The correlation is minus 0.02. Mutual information finds 0.77 bits, against 0.02 for shuffled data.

Second. Two people tap together, in perfect step. The phase locking value is 0.98. The phase lag index is 0.07. It throws away zero lag on purpose. Now let Ava lead by sixty degrees. The phase lag index jumps to almost one.

Third. Two people with no link at all. Their phase locking value is 0.03. Now play the same ten hertz metronome to both. The value climbs to 0.72, and nobody interacted.

That third blind spot is the one that matters between two brains. Inside one head, the usual worry is volume conduction: one source, seen by two sensors. Between two heads, that worry disappears. But shared sources take its place: the same stimulus, the same rhythm, the same material. So the choice of a measure has to take them into account.

Three reviews are worth keeping on the shelf. Bastos and Schoffelen, in Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience in 2016, explain coherence, phase synchronization and Granger causality, and the pitfalls of reading them, including common input. Pereda and colleagues, in 2005, cover the nonlinear measures. Burgess, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience in 2013, tried the most used measures on simulated signals and on pseudo-pairs. Most of them found connections where none existed.

Let's recap. Four switches sort the measures: amplitude or phase, linear or not, symmetric or directed, pair or group. Every measure is blind to something. Between two brains, shared sources replace volume conduction. So choose the measure knowing what it cannot see.

Next, we fly the first kite: correlation, lags and inter-subject correlation.

PLATE 6.25:52

Correlation, lags and inter-subject correlation

Correlation multiplies two mean-subtracted records and keeps their shape; sliding one record gives the lag at which they line up, as with kites feeling the same gust seconds apart. Inter-subject correlation uses other viewers as a stand-in for the stimulus. Slow signals have few independent samples, so the textbook test gives 65 percent false alarms in simulation, fixed by phase-randomized nulls.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The wind is up on the kite hill. Ava and Otto each fly a kite, from the same hilltop. The same gust lifts both.

When one kite climbs, does the other climb too? At the same moment, or a few seconds later? This is the simplest question we can ask about two signals. The simplest answer is correlation.

Write down the height of each kite, every fraction of a second. Subtract each average. Multiply the two records, sample by sample, and add everything up. Then divide by the size of each record, so the answer sits between minus one and plus one. This is the Pearson correlation.

Here is a simulated pair. The wind is slow, and Otto's kite feels each gust three seconds after Ava's. Correlation at zero lag is only 0.40. The two records look related, but they are out of step.

So slide Otto's record under Ava's, one step at a time, and compute the correlation at every shift. This is the cross-correlation. The curve climbs, peaks, and falls. Here it peaks at 3.4 seconds, with a correlation of 0.81. That peak is the lag: how far one record must move to line up with the other.

A lag is a clue, not a verdict. In a classic study, Stephens, Silbert and Hasson, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2010, scanned a speaker telling a story, then listeners hearing the recording. On average, the listener's brain activity followed the speaker's with a delay. Some regions even ran ahead of the speaker. Coupling that anticipated the speaker went with better understanding.

Now a special case. Let many people watch the same movie, or hear the same story, in a scanner. Take one person. Correlate their record with the average of everyone else. This is inter-subject correlation, introduced for fMRI by Hasson and colleagues, in Science in 2004. Nobody has to say what the stimulus is, because the other people stand in for it. Here, six simulated listeners reach 0.53. Shift each record by a random amount, and it falls to about zero. A close cousin, inter-subject functional correlation, correlates one region in one person with other regions in the others. Simony and colleagues introduced it in Nature Communications in 2016. Nastase and colleagues wrote a tutorial in 2019.

For EEG, the same recipe works on a slower quantity: the amplitude envelope of a rhythm, or its power at each instant. You already met this in the EEG series. Inside one head, the envelopes are orthogonalised first, as in Hipp and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience in 2012. The point is to discount correlations that come only from limited spatial resolution.

What does correlation see? Slow variations that two signals share: trends, gusts, a stimulus. What does it miss? Relations that are not straight lines. A curved link can give a correlation near zero.

Now the trap. Wind makes kites drift slowly, so each height is close to the previous one. Neighbouring samples are not independent. Think of two kites flown by strangers, on different hills. Each wanders. Sometimes both wander up together, by luck.

We simulated four thousand such pairs. Each record has two hundred samples, and each sample is ninety-five percent its predecessor plus noise. There is no link at all. The textbook test calls a correlation significant above 0.14. It declares sixty-five percent of these pairs significant. One pair reaches 0.61, and the test says the probability is below ten to the minus twenty. It is a false alarm.

The reason is simple. Two hundred samples do not carry two hundred pieces of evidence. By Bartlett's formula, the effective sample size is about eighteen, the median over these pairs. The test needs to count wiggles, not samples.

There are two honest fixes. First, shrink the sample size in the test, with Bartlett's formula, as compared by Pyper and Peterman, in the Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences, in 1998. Here it brings the false alarms down to 6.5 percent. For fMRI networks, Afyouni, Smith and Nichols, in NeuroImage in 2019, built a refined estimate of the effective degrees of freedom. Second, skip the formula, and build the null. Randomise the phases of one record, which keeps its slow wandering but destroys any link. This gives 5.3 percent. It is the null strip of this series: no synchrony without its null.

The same trap hits envelopes, and hits them harder. Take two independent ten hertz noises, sixty seconds each, and correlate their envelopes. The naive test calls sixty-seven percent of the pairs significant.

What changes between two brains? Correlation is a natural first measure for fNIRS, for fMRI, and for heart signals. Orthogonalising the envelopes has no reason to exist between two heads, because no source is seen by both. But a shared stimulus is a shared gust, and it correlates two brains that never interacted. So the first suspect is the first source on our compass: the stimulus.

Let's recap. Correlation multiplies two records and keeps the shape. Sliding one record gives the lag. Inter-subject correlation uses the other people as the stimulus. Slow signals fool the test, so count the wiggles, or build the null.

Next, we go from one number to a map in time: wavelet transform coherence.

PLATE 6.36:00

Coherence

Coherence is a correlation computed one frequency at a time, using both amplitude and phase through the averaged cross spectrum. It ignores any constant lag, so it shows linkage but not who leads, and it is biased upward when few segments are averaged. Its imaginary part removes zero-lag volume conduction inside one head, but between two heads it protects nothing and discards true zero-lag synchrony.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Up on the kite hill, Ava's kite and Otto's kite fly side by side. Each tail carries ribbons, one colour for each frequency. Do the two tails wave together? Not as a whole, but ribbon by ribbon. This is coherence.

The idea is simple. Take one ribbon on Ava's kite, and the ribbon of the same colour on Otto's. Do they move together? That is a correlation, computed one frequency at a time. And unlike a plain correlation, it uses both amplitude and phase.

You met this in the EEG series. Cut each signal into short segments. Take the Fourier transform of each segment. At every frequency, multiply Ava's coefficient by the conjugate of Otto's coefficient. Averaged over segments, that gives the cross spectrum. Each term is an arrow. Its length says how strong both signals are at that frequency. Its angle says how far apart they are in phase.

Now normalise. Magnitude squared coherence is the squared length of the average arrow, divided by Ava's power times Otto's power. It runs from zero to one. One means a perfectly stable relation at that frequency. Zero means none.

Here is a pair I simulated. Ava and Otto both see the same ten hertz source, plus noise of their own. I cut it into two hundred and thirty nine segments of two seconds. Coherence at ten hertz is 0.74. Everywhere else the ribbons are independent, and coherence stays near zero: 0.005 between twenty and thirty hertz.

First sensitivity. Coherence does not care how large the delay is, as long as it is constant. Now Otto sees the same source twenty five milliseconds later. A quarter of a cycle at ten hertz. Coherence is 0.72. With no delay it was 0.74. It is the same. A constant lag of any size, including zero, gives the same coherence. So coherence says the two are linked. It does not say who leads.

Second sensitivity. Coherence is built from an average of arrows, so it depends on how many arrows you average. With one segment, the average arrow is just that arrow, and coherence is exactly one, whatever the signals. With independent noise, two segments give 0.50 on average. Ten segments give 0.10. One hundred give 0.01. Roughly one over the number of segments. Bastos and Schoffelen, in their 2016 tutorial review in Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience, note that coherence is positively biased, and the smaller the sample, the larger the bias. So report the number of segments.

Now the imaginary part. Inside one head, a single source reaches two electrodes at the same instant. Through volume conduction, both electrodes see a copy of it, with zero lag. Nolte and colleagues, in 2004, in Clinical Neurophysiology, showed that the coherency of non interacting sources is necessarily real. Coherency is the complex number, before we take its magnitude. So they proposed to keep only its imaginary part: the part of the arrow that points off the real axis. A zero lag copy cannot produce it. The method was designed for volume conduction inside one head, in EEG and MEG.

Look at our two pairs. For the zero lag pair, the average arrow lies flat on the real axis, at about minus one degree. The absolute value of its imaginary part is 0.02. For the lagged pair, the arrow points straight up, at ninety degrees, and the imaginary part is 0.85. At fifty milliseconds, half a cycle, it falls back to 0.01.

Here is the trap. Between two heads, there is no volume conduction to remove. The two scalps are separate, and no conduction path joins Ava's head to Otto's. So the imaginary part protects against nothing here. It only discards every relation at zero lag or at half a cycle. Bastos and Schoffelen state the cost plainly: such approaches come at the expense of not detecting true interactions at near zero phase difference. Between two heads it buys nothing, and it costs something.

What changes between two brains? A zero lag synchrony can be true. Two people tapping to the same beat have rhythms that line up. Or it can come from a common source: the same stimulus, the same room, the same equipment. Burgess, in 2013, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, took people who never met, recorded under the same conditions, and paired them. The widely used measures still found connections, because the EEG really was similar. The full coherence keeps both explanations. The imaginary part removes both. Neither can tell them apart. That is the job of the controls. So choose the measure knowing what you lose.

Coherence is used in the field. Dikker and colleagues, in 2017, in Current Biology, recorded twelve high school students in class over a semester. They measured synchrony with total interdependence, which sums inter-brain coherence across frequencies.

In code, the signal module of SciPy gives magnitude squared coherence. MNE connectivity gives coh and imcoh across epochs.

Let's recap. Coherence is a correlation, frequency by frequency. It is blind to a constant lag, including zero. It depends on the number of segments. The imaginary part removes zero lag, which protects one head from volume conduction, and protects nothing between two heads. Between brains, zero lag may be true, or common. Choose knowing what you lose.

Next, 6.4: wavelet transform coherence.

PLATE 6.45:52

Wavelet transform coherence

Wavelet transform coherence maps when and at which period two slow signals move together, like binoculars whose magnification is the period. A cross wavelet is smoothed in time and period to become a coherence, and in a simulated pair it recovers a shared eight-second wave and its two-second lead. The band is fixed by the design, never by the map, and pseudo-pairs supply the null.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Late afternoon on the kite hill. Ava and Otto each fly a kite, and a slow gust sways them both, about once every eight seconds. But only for part of the afternoon. When does it happen, and at which rhythm?

Coherence, from the previous episode, tells us at which frequency two signals move together. But it gives one answer for the whole recording. A slow brain signal, like fNIRS, is not so steady. Two people can couple for a minute, then drift apart.

So we need to know when, and at which period, at the same time. For that, take a pair of binoculars. The magnification is the period you look at. And what you draw is a map of time against period. This is wavelet transform coherence.

Step one. Turn the knob to a single period. Slide a small wave, a Morlet wavelet, along each record. If you watched the EEG series, you know this move. At every moment it returns two numbers: how strong the rhythm is at that period, and its phase.

Step two, the cross wavelet. Multiply Ava's wavelet by the conjugate of Otto's, moment by moment. The length says how much power they share. The angle is the phase difference between them. This product is the cross wavelet transform.

But a product can be big just because one kite is loud. So step three divides it by the two powers, as in a correlation. Here is the catch. If you do only that, the answer is exactly one, at every moment and every period. In our simulation, the smallest value on the whole map is one, up to rounding.

Smoothing is what turns it into a coherence. Average each term over a short stretch of time, and over neighbouring periods, before dividing. Squared, this is the wavelet transform coherence. It was introduced by Grinsted, Moore and Jevrejeva in 2004, in Nonlinear Processes in Geophysics. Their paper builds on the wavelet guide of Torrence and Compo, in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society, in 1998.

Now sweep the knob from short periods to long ones. Each setting draws one row of the map. Time runs left to right, and period runs upward. Bright yellow means the two records beat together at that moment and that period. Dark means they do not.

This is a simulated pair, with a known answer. Between 100 and 200 seconds, a shared wave of about eight seconds reaches Ava first, and Otto two seconds later. Everywhere else, the two records are independent.

The map finds it. Inside the block, in the band from six to ten and a half seconds, the average coherence is 0.91. Away from the block, the same band gives 0.51.

Near the edges, the wavelet runs out of signal. The shaded zone is the cone of influence. Do not read anything inside it. Long periods lose the most.

Now the arrows. In a bright patch, a small arrow shows the phase difference. Pointing right, the two are in phase. Pointing left, they are opposite. Pointing up, Ava leads. Pointing down, Otto leads. Here, the arrows point up. The average angle in the block is about 84 degrees. A lead of two seconds, at a period of eight, is a quarter of a cycle. That is ninety degrees, so it matches.

What is this measure sensitive to? It is excellent for slow, non stationary signals, which is exactly what fNIRS gives. But it depends strongly on the smoothing. Grinsted and colleagues note that the details of the smoothing have a large effect on the significance level. And it is blind inside the cone of influence.

Notice also that noise does not give zero. Pair each Ava with someone else's Otto, two hundred times, with the same window and the same band. The average coherence is 0.53. The ninety-fifth percentile is 0.73. Our real pair, at 0.91, sits beyond it. Without that null, 0.91 would be just a number.

Now the trap. Once you have seen the map, the bright patch is tempting. Take its band, take its time window, and average. But the pseudo-pairs have bright patches too, by chance. Let each one choose its best band and its best hundred-second window. Then the share of pseudo-pairs above that 0.73 line, which was five percent, rises to eight and a half percent.

So the band is fixed before the analysis, from the experimental design: the length of the blocks, the rhythm of the task.

That is how Cui, Bryant and Reiss did it, in NeuroImage in 2012. Eleven pairs, twenty-two adults, played a cooperation game side by side. Each trial lasted about seven seconds. They analysed periods from 3.2 to 12.8 seconds, a band that includes the trial period, using the wavelet coherence package of Grinsted and colleagues. Coherence in the right superior frontal cortex rose during cooperation, and not during competition.

What changes between two brains? This is the reference measure for fNIRS hyperscanning. But both people see the same screen and the same trial rhythm, so a shared task rhythm can light up the map with no interaction at all. In the source compass, that is route one, the stimulus. The arrows give a first idea of who leads, but they do not prove it. Directed measures come later in this chapter.

Let's recap. Wavelet coherence is a localized correlation, in time and in period. The cross wavelet gives shared power and phase. Smoothing makes it a coherence. The band is fixed by the design, never by the map. And the null comes with it.

Next, we go back to the phase alone, with the phase locking value and its n to m cousin.

PLATE 6.54:53

Phase locking value (PLV), and its n:m cousin

The phase locking value keeps only the angle between two phases at each sample and takes the length of the average unit arrow, from zero to one. It ignores amplitude and who leads, and it never reaches zero for noise, so it needs a surrogate null. Between two heads, a shared stimulus is the first suspect.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Up on the kite hill, two rotor kites spin in the wind. One belongs to Ava, one to Otto. Do they spin in step? This episode builds one of the most used answers to that question in EEG hyperscanning: the phase locking value.

If you watched the EEG series, you know the spinning arrow. A narrow-band signal can be written as an arrow that turns in the complex plane. Its length is the amplitude. Its angle is the phase. The Hilbert transform, or a wavelet, gives us that angle at every sample.

Now take Ava's arrow and Otto's arrow at the same instant. Ignore how long they are. Keep only the angle between them. That difference of phase is a single number, between minus pi and pi. Draw it as a small arrow of length one.

Do this at every sample, and you get a crowd of little arrows. Here is the whole trick. Place them end to end, and look at the average arrow.

If the two kites keep the same angle between them, all the little arrows point the same way. The average arrow is long, close to one. If the angle between them wanders at random, the little arrows point everywhere, and they cancel out. The average arrow shrinks toward zero.

The length of that average arrow is the phase locking value. In symbols: the absolute value of the mean of e to the i times the phase difference. It was introduced for brain signals by Lachaux and colleagues in 1999, in Human Brain Mapping. They averaged over trials, and they already compared their values with surrogate data.

What is the phase locking value sensitive to? First, it ignores amplitude completely. Each arrow has length one, so a loud channel and a quiet channel count the same. That is useful, and it is also a risk. When the signal is weak, the phase is mostly noise, and the measure cannot tell.

Second, it does not care which angle the kites keep. Zero, ninety degrees, or opposite: if the angle is stable, the value is high. So a high value says the two phases are locked. It does not say who leads.

Third, and most important, it depends on how many arrows you average. Here are two independent signals, simulated, with no link at all. With ten arrows, the value is about 0.3 on average. With a hundred, it drops. With a thousand, it gets close to zero, but never reaches it. Roughly, this floor falls with the square root of the number of samples.

And there is a catch. After narrow-band filtering, neighbouring samples are not independent. A one-second window at ten hertz holds only about ten real cycles, whatever the sampling rate. So cycles set the floor, much more than samples do. Two independent signals, filtered in the same narrow band and measured over a short window, can show a clearly positive value.

So a phase locking value, on its own, is not evidence of synchrony. Compare it with a null distribution: pseudo-pairs, or surrogates, computed with the same window and the same filter. We come back to this in chapter seven.

What changes between two brains? Within one head, the classic worry is volume conduction: one source, seen by two electrodes. Between two heads, that worry disappears. But another one takes its place. If both people see the same event, both brains reset their phase at the same moment. The little arrows line up, and the stimulus did it. In the source compass, that is route one. Pseudo-pairs that lived the same timing are the natural check.

One last idea, for the end of the episode. Ava and Otto do not have to oscillate at the same frequency to be locked. Suppose Otto's rhythm runs exactly twice as fast as Ava's. Then two turns of Otto's arrow match one turn of Ava's. We compare two times Ava's phase with Otto's phase, and compute the same average arrow. This is n to m phase locking. It was described for noisy brain data by Tass and colleagues in 1998, in Physical Review Letters.

Here is the trap with n to m locking. A rhythm that is not a perfect sine wave carries harmonics. A ten hertz rhythm with a sharp shape also has energy at twenty hertz. So a one to two lock can be an ordinary one to one lock, seen through a harmonic. Check the one to one case first, and look at the shape of the waves.

Let's recap. The phase locking value keeps only the angle between two phases. It is the length of the average of unit arrows. It ignores amplitude and direction. It is never zero, even for noise, so it always needs its null. Between two brains, the shared stimulus replaces volume conduction as the first suspect.

Next, we throw away the arrows that lie flat on the axis, and meet the phase lag index.

PLATE 6.65:31

PLI and wPLI

The phase lag index counts only whether the phase difference falls above or below the horizon, discarding phases exactly in or out of step; the weighted version weights each vote by its distance from the horizon. Both resist zero-lag common sources. In simulation, one source gives an index of 0.10 at zero lag and 1.00 at 25 milliseconds, so true zero-lag synchrony is lost.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Up on the kite hill, someone has set an old balance scale on a stall. Ava and Otto are about to weigh their synchrony.

We use the same little arrows as in the last episode. At every moment, one arrow shows the angle between Ava's phase and Otto's phase. This time we do not average them. We ask one question about each arrow. Does it point above the horizon, or below?

Above the horizon means Ava is a little ahead. Below means Otto is a little ahead. An arrow lying flat on the horizon, pointing right or pointing left, has no side at all. It falls through a trapdoor, and it is not counted.

That is the intuition. Keep only the synchrony that comes with a delay. Throw away what is exactly in phase, and what is exactly opposite.

Now the calculation. Every arrow above the horizon drops a pebble on the left pan. Every arrow below drops one on the right pan. If the same side keeps winning, the scale tilts. That tilt is the phase lag index.

In symbols: take the sign of the sine of the phase difference, average it over the arrows, and keep the absolute value. Zero is a balanced scale. One is a scale that always tilts the same way. Stam, Nolte and Daffertshofer introduced it in 2007, in Human Brain Mapping, to reduce the effect of common sources in EEG and MEG.

The index has a weakness. An arrow that barely clears the horizon counts as much as an arrow pointing straight up. A touch of noise can push it across, and flip its vote.

The weighted phase lag index fixes this. Each pebble now weighs as much as the arrow is far from the horizon. Arrows close to the horizon weigh almost nothing. In symbols: the absolute value of the mean of the imaginary part of the cross spectrum, divided by the mean of its absolute value. This is the work of Vinck and colleagues, in NeuroImage in 2011.

They also proposed a debiased version. Here is why. With few epochs, even two independent signals give a value above zero, because an absolute value can only add. The debiased estimator removes that excess.

In code, it is one argument of the connectivity function in MNE: the method name. The debiased one estimates the squared weighted index, so it can come out slightly negative.

Let's check the bias. Here are independent signals, simulated, with no link at all. With ten epochs, the phase lag index averages 0.24, and the weighted index 0.34. With two hundred epochs, 0.06 and 0.08. The debiased version stays at zero.

What is the measure sensitive to? It resists common sources. Two electrodes that see the same source see it at the same instant. The arrows lie flat on the horizon, and nothing is counted. That was the whole point, inside one head. But it is fragile for tiny delays, because a little noise flips their side, and the weighted version softens this.

Now the trap. It is tempting to say that the phase lag index cleans synchrony. It does remove the fake zero lag part. It also removes the true zero lag part. Imaginary coherence, in episode 6.3, makes the same bargain.

Between two brains, this matters even more. There is no volume conduction between two heads. So an arrow at zero lag between Ava and Otto is not an artefact of the tissue. Two people tapping together can really be in phase.

Here are two simulated dyads. Each shares one source, plus private noise, over sixty epochs. In the first, Otto follows at zero lag. The phase locking value is 0.97. But the phase lag index is 0.10, with 27 arrows above the horizon and 33 below. The weighted index is 0.04.

In the second dyad, Otto follows twenty five milliseconds later. The phase locking value is 0.92. All sixty arrows sit above the horizon. The phase lag index is 1.00, and so is the weighted index.

Same source, same noise. Only the delay changed, and the verdict flipped. So a low value does not mean there is no interaction. It means there is no interaction with a delay.

In hyperscanning, the weighted index has been used, for example, by Ahn and colleagues in 2018, in Human Brain Mapping. They recorded EEG and MEG during turn taking conversation, and reported significant interbrain phase synchronization. Remember the cost, though. A stimulus that hits both brains at the same instant also falls through the trapdoor, and the measure cannot tell it from true synchrony.

Let's recap. The phase lag index counts which side of the horizon the arrows fall on, and ignores the flat ones. The weighted version weighs each arrow by its distance from the horizon, and its debiased form removes the excess at small sample sizes. Both resist zero lag common sources. Between two brains, they also discard true zero lag synchrony. So read a low value together with the phase locking value.

Next, we meet the circular correlation, in episode 6.7: CCorr and ACCorr.

PLATE 6.76:03

Circular correlation · CCorr and ACCorr

The circular correlation compares each phase with its own mean direction, like two weathervanes with habits. In short windows that mean is unstable, so in one simulated dyad the estimate swings from -0.68 to +0.70 by sliding the window one sample. The adjusted version uses a mean direction chosen for the pair and moves far less, but needs its own null.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the top of the kite hill stand two weathervanes. One is Ava's, one is Otto's. The wind swings them around all afternoon. Do they swing the same way?

This episode is about a correlation for angles. It is called the circular correlation, CCorr for short. And it has a newer cousin, the adjusted circular correlation, ACCorr.

Start with the intuition. Every weathervane has a habit. Over the afternoon it points, on average, somewhere. Call that its mean direction. Now look at each moment. When both sit on the same side of their own habit, they swing together. When one goes left and the other right, they swing against each other.

So we do not compare raw angles. We compare how far each vane sits from its own mean direction.

Here is the calculation, one piece at a time. Take the phase of Ava's signal, and call it alpha. Take the phase of Otto's, and call it beta. Find the circular mean of each, and call them mu and nu. Take the sine of the distance from the mean, for each vane. Multiply the two sines, moment by moment, and add everything up. That is the top of the fraction. The bottom is a normalisation: the square root of the two sums of squared sines. The result sits between minus one and plus one, just like Pearson's correlation. This is the circular correlation coefficient, from the classic circular statistics textbook of Jammalamadaka and SenGupta, in 2001.

Burgess brought it to hyperscanning in 2013, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience. With simulated signals, and pseudo-pairs of people never recorded together, he found that the most common measures reported connections where there were none. The circular correlation was less prone to this. It was not perfect: it still reacts to a lag between the two signals.

Now the catch. Everything rests on those two mean directions. For a rhythm that turns around ten times per second, the phase goes round and round the circle. Over one second, it points almost evenly everywhere, so its mean direction is barely defined. Move the window by one single sample, and the mean can jump a long way.

Here is a simulated dyad, with a medium common source, and a one second window that slides one sample at a time. The mean directions of the two vanes wander all around the dial. And the circular correlation follows them. In this one dyad it swings from -0.68 to +0.70, only by sliding the window.

This is what Zimmermann, Schultz-Nielsen, Dumas and Konvalinka pointed out in 2024, in the journal Imaging Neuroscience. They showed that the conventional circular correlation fluctuates a lot, because the circular mean directions fluctuate. Their fix is the adjusted version.

Here is how it works. Change the top. Instead of the product of sines, take two arrows. The first is the sum of e to the i times alpha minus beta, which measures how steady the phase difference is. The second is the sum of e to the i times alpha plus beta. Take the length of each, subtract the second from the first, and divide by twice the bottom. The bottom keeps the shape of the plain version, but each phase is measured from a mean direction chosen for the pair, the one that makes the link as strong as possible, not from each vane's own habit. No mean direction is needed on top.

Let us slide the window again, on the same dyad. The adjusted version stays between 0.47 and 0.66. The plain version went from -0.68 to +0.70.

We can measure this over three hundred simulated dyads, for each strength of common source. Slide the window by one sample, and see how much the estimate changes. The plain version moves about eleven times more than the adjusted one with no common source. About fifteen times more with a medium one, and about twenty-four times more with a strong one. In their own simulations, Zimmermann and colleagues report the same kind of ratio when they shift the window, between about nine and thirty.

Now the trap. The two versions are not on the same scale. With no common source, the plain version averages about 0.04. The adjusted version averages 0.38. With a medium source, they read 0.11 and 0.51. With a strong one, 0.31 and 0.72. So a value of 0.5 means one thing for one measure, and something else for the other.

That is why each measure needs its own null. We built pseudo-pairs, a vane from one dyad and a vane from another, and took the ninety-fifth percentile. For the plain version it is 0.53. For the adjusted one it is 0.70. With a medium common source, eighteen percent of dyads pass for the plain version, and twenty-three percent for the adjusted one. And one dyad in five gets a different verdict from the two. The choice between them is one of the arbitrary decisions we study in chapter eight. Neither one replaces the null.

What changes between two brains? These are the only phase measures in the chapter that were made for hyperscanning. The series on single brains has no equivalent. And between two heads, the shared stimulus can still make both vanes swing together. That is route one. A pseudo-pair that lived the same timing is the check.

Let's recap. The circular correlation compares each phase to its own mean direction. The mean direction of a short window is unstable. The adjusted version drops each vane's own mean direction, and moves much less. The two live on different scales, so each needs its own null. And they can disagree on the same data.

PLATE 6.86:46

Granger causality, PDC and DTF across brains

Granger causality asks whether adding Ava's past improves the forecast of Otto's signal; partial directed coherence and the directed transfer function split the answer by frequency. Results depend on model order and sampling rate. In simulation, a stimulus reaching Ava first gives a strong one-way value of 0.92 with no coupling, so a direction that ignores the roles of the paradigm is a warning.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Up on the kite hill, a train of kites flies on one long line. Ava's kite leads. Otto's kite follows. Does the leader really pull the follower, or do the two just move together? This episode asks what Granger causality, and its two spectral cousins, can say about direction between two brains.

The idea is simple. Granger proposed it in 1969, in Econometrica. Try to predict the next value of Otto's signal. First use only Otto's own past. Then add Ava's past. If the second forecast is clearly better, Ava's past carries information about Otto's future. We say that Ava Granger-causes Otto.

If you watched the EEG series, you know the recipe. Fit two autoregressive models, one with Otto's past only, one with Ava's past added. Compare the variance of their errors. The Granger measure is the natural logarithm of that ratio. Zero means no help. Do the same the other way round, and you have one number for each direction.

Here is a simulated dyad. Ava's rhythm sits near ten hertz. Otto has his own rhythm near twelve hertz, plus a copy of Ava's signal that arrives thirty-nine milliseconds later. That is a true drive, built in. A model of order ten, chosen by the Akaike criterion, gives 0.11 from Ava to Otto, and 0.001 from Otto to Ava. To know what counts as zero, we shift Ava's record in circles, many times. The ninety-fifth percentile of that null is 0.002. The real value sits far outside, and the reverse direction sits inside.

Now split the measure by frequency. Geweke showed in 1982 that this is possible. Two cousins do the same job. The directed transfer function comes from Kamiński and Blinowska, in 1991. The partial directed coherence comes from Baccalá and Sameshima, in 2001. Both start from one multivariate autoregressive model, with all channels at once. Move its coefficients to the frequency domain, then normalise.

On our dyad, the curve from Ava to Otto is high up to about ten hertz, and the curve the other way stays flat. With two channels, the two measures agree. With many channels they do not: the transfer function also counts indirect paths, and the partial coherence only direct ones.

What is it sensitive to? It is directed, so it suits leader and follower roles. But it depends on the model. With order one, our value is 0.01. With order five, 0.09. With order forty, 0.11. And decimate the same data down to eight hertz, and the two directions come close: 0.09 against 0.07. The delay is then shorter than one sample.

Now the first trap. Suppose Ava and Otto are not coupled at all. But both see the same stimulus, and Ava sees it forty-seven milliseconds before Otto, like a gust that reaches one kite first. Ava's past predicts Otto's future. The measure gives 0.92 from Ava to Otto, and 0.00 the other way. A strong, one-way, entirely spurious result. Add the stimulus to both models, and the value falls to 0.00. That works only if you recorded the stimulus. In the source compass, this is route one again.

The second trap is time. In fNIRS and fMRI, the signal is a slow haemodynamic response. Take a simulated drive with a delay of half a second. At the neural level, the value is 0.51 one way and 0.01 the other. After a common slow response, it drops to 0.03 and 0.02, barely above a null of 0.01. If Otto's response peaks earlier than Ava's, the direction flips: 0.01 against 0.03.

The debate is old. In 2011, Smith and colleagues simulated fMRI networks, and found that lag-based methods performed very poorly. In 2013, Seth, Chorley and Barnett showed that Granger causality is in theory unchanged by haemodynamic convolution, but not by downsampling and noise. In 2015, Seth, Barrett and Barnett still advised caution with fMRI. Filtering is a trap too: Barnett and Seth showed in 2011 that it can change results in practice. This is the state of the debate in 2015, the latest synthesis I verified.

Between brains, Astolfi and colleagues, in 2010, recorded EEG from fourteen pairs of card players. They extended Granger causality across players, and reported links from prefrontal areas of one player to the anterior cingulate of the other. Jiang and colleagues, in 2015, recorded fNIRS in groups of three. Causality ran both ways between leaders and followers, but it was stronger from leaders to followers.

What changes? Both brains enter one model. With sixty-four channels each, that is 128 channels. At order ten, that is 163,840 coefficients. One minute at 128 hertz gives 983,040 numbers: six per coefficient. Far too few. So we reduce first, separating sources with independent component analysis, together with the model. This is called MVARICA, from Gómez-Herrero and colleagues, in 2008.

Then use the roles of the paradigm as a control. If Ava guides, the direction should point from Ava to Otto. If they swap roles, it should swap. In thirty simulated dyads, a true drive gave the right direction in all thirty. With no coupling, and a stimulus reaching Ava first, the measure pointed from Ava to Otto in all thirty, whoever was guiding. It followed the roles in only fifteen: chance. A direction that ignores the roles is a warning.

Let's recap. Granger asks whether A's past improves the forecast of B. PDC and DTF give the answer by frequency. It depends on the model order and the sampling rate. A shared stimulus can fake a direction, and slow signals blur it. Between brains, check that the direction follows the roles.

Transfer entropy

Transfer entropy counts how many bits Ava's past removes from the uncertainty about Otto's next state, like a wind sensor that assumes no shape for the link. For Gaussian signals it matches Granger up to a factor of two, and it finds non-linear links Granger misses. It needs much data and is biased positive even for noise, so it always needs surrogates.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back on the kite hill, the train of kites is still flying. Ava's kite leads, and Otto's kite follows. Granger asked whether Ava's past wind helps predict Otto's next gust, using a straight line. Today we send up a wind sensor that assumes no shape at all for the link between them. It only counts. It is called transfer entropy.

Here is the game. Otto's wind is sorted into four levels, from calm to gusty. Your job is to guess his next level. First, you may only look at Otto's own recent past. You will often be wrong, so some uncertainty is left. We measure it in bits, like yes or no questions you would still have to ask.

Now you may also peek at Ava's wind. If the uncertainty drops, Ava's past carried news about Otto's future. That drop, in bits, is the transfer entropy. In a simulated dyad, with five minutes of wind, the leftover uncertainty is 1.75 bits from Otto's past alone, and 1.65 bits with Ava's past added. The drop is 0.10 bits.

In symbols, the transfer entropy from A to B is the mutual information between A's past and B's future, given B's own past. Thomas Schreiber introduced it in 2000, in Physical Review Letters, to tell a driving element from a responding one. The EEG series covers it in episode 11.3. Here we only need the idea.

How do we measure it without assuming a shape? We count. Sort Ava's present wind into four levels, Otto's present wind into four levels, and Otto's next wind into four levels. That makes sixty-four pigeonholes. At every time step, drop one pebble into the hole where it belongs. Here are four boards, one for each level of Ava's wind. Each board shows Otto's wind now, against Otto's wind next. If Ava's wind makes no difference, the four boards look alike. If it does, they differ. Transfer entropy measures how much they differ.

Now, the link with Granger. When all the variables are Gaussian, the two measures say the same thing: Granger's value is exactly twice the transfer entropy, in natural units. Barnett, Barrett and Seth showed this in 2009, in Physical Review Letters.

Take a simulated dyad where Ava's wind pushes Otto's in a straight line. Granger gives 0.23. Half of that, converted to bits, is 0.17. The counting sensor gives 0.14, a little less, because four bins throw some detail away. From Otto to Ava, both are near zero.

The counting sensor earns its keep when the link is not a straight line. Suppose Otto does not react to the direction of Ava's wind, but to how strong it is. A big gust of either sign pushes him. A line cannot see that: Granger gives 0.00. Transfer entropy gives 0.10 bits from Ava to Otto, and 0.01 the other way.

The price is data. Every pigeonhole needs pebbles. Take two winds that are completely independent. With a hundred samples, the sensor still reports 0.29 bits on average. With three hundred, 0.09. With a thousand, 0.03. With three thousand, 0.01. It is positive every time. Five minutes at ten samples per second is three thousand samples. A thirty-second block is only three hundred. Estimates built from binned counts can stay biased on small samples, even after the usual corrections, as Paninski showed in 2003.

So a positive transfer entropy means nothing on its own. Look at a dyad with no link at all. It scores 0.009 bits. Now shift Ava's recording by a random delay, and measure again, five hundred times. Her rhythm is kept, and her timing against Otto is destroyed. Those shifted copies have a 95th percentile of 0.012, so the unlinked dyad sits inside them. The linear dyad scores 0.137 and the non-linear one 0.104, both far above their own null of 0.013. We build such surrogates properly in episode 7.6.

How short can a dyad be? With the non-linear link, we simulated a hundred dyads and tested each against its own surrogates. At three hundred samples, 96 were detected. At a thousand, all 100. Our simulated link is strong, and real ones are weaker, so these are best cases. With no link at all, 7 dyads in 100 passed, close to the five we expect by chance.

What changes between two brains? Social interactions rarely last long enough for these estimators. So the estimator and its settings must be chosen for a dyad of a few minutes, and reported. Running Granger on the same dyad is a useful comparison. Where they agree, the link is simple. Where transfer entropy sees more, it is not a straight line.

Also, a direction is not a proof of who leads. A stimulus that Ava sees before Otto creates a direction too, and in the source compass that is route one. The roles known from the paradigm, who speaks and who guides, are the control. One example from the literature: Li and colleagues, in NeuroImage in 2025, used a phase version of transfer entropy on EEG from pairs playing an ultimatum game. They reported influence from the proposer to the responder, and back.

Let's recap. Transfer entropy is the number of bits that Ava's past removes from the uncertainty about Otto's future. For Gaussian signals it matches Granger, up to a factor of two, and it can see links that Granger misses. It needs a lot of data, its bias is positive even for noise, and it always needs its surrogates. Between brains, a shared stimulus can fake a direction.

PLATE 6.105:40

Mutual information between brains

Mutual information is the overlap of two overlapping shadows: the bits of uncertainty about one signal that vanish once the other is known. Histogram, nearest-neighbour and Gaussian copula estimators agree on a straight-line link but disagree on a U-shaped one, and the histogram invents information from nothing with few samples. A value therefore needs a null built from time-shifted series.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Up on the kite hill the sun is low, and every kite throws a shadow on the grass. Ava's shadow is pink. Otto's shadow is blue. Where the two shadows overlap, the grass turns yellow. This episode is about that yellow patch: mutual information.

Think of each shadow's area as the uncertainty of one signal, counted in bits. That is its entropy. Ava's entropy is H of A. Otto's is H of B. Together, the two shadows cover a joint area: the joint entropy. The overlap is what you counted twice when you added the two shadows. So the mutual information is H of A, plus H of B, minus the joint entropy.

Read it as a question. How many bits of surprise about Otto disappear once you know Ava? If the two signals are independent, the shadows do not touch, and the answer is zero. The measure is symmetric, and it assumes no particular shape for the relation. The EEG series introduced entropy and mutual information, and the Information series goes further. Here we only need what changes between two brains.

For one special case there is a closed form. When both signals are Gaussian, the mutual information in bits is minus one half times the logarithm, base two, of one minus rho squared, where rho is the correlation. A correlation of 0.6 gives 0.32 bits. A correlation of zero gives zero. In that case, correlation and mutual information tell the same story.

They stop agreeing as soon as the link is not a straight line. Here is a simulated dyad. Otto's kite height follows the square of Ava's, plus a little noise. When her kite is very high or very low, his is high. When hers is in the middle, his is low. The correlation is minus 0.09, almost nothing. Yet the two signals are clearly not independent.

But we never have the true distributions. We only have samples, so we need an estimator. Three are common.

The first is the histogram estimator, also called plug in. Cut each signal into eight bins, count the samples in each cell of the grid, and plug those frequencies into the formula.

The second uses nearest neighbours. Kraskov, Stögbauer and Grassberger described it in Physical Review E in 2004. For each sample, measure the distance to its fourth neighbour, then count how many neighbours fall within that distance in each signal alone. No grid is needed.

The third is the Gaussian copula estimator, by Ince and colleagues, in Human Brain Mapping in 2017. It replaces each value by its rank, turns the ranks into a standard Gaussian, and applies the Gaussian formula. It gives a lower bound on the true value.

Now compare them on the same simulated samples. On a thousand samples of the correlated dyad, whose true value is 0.32 bits, the three estimators give 0.34, 0.37 and 0.36 bits. On the U shaped dyad, they disagree. The histogram gives 0.64 bits. Nearest neighbours give 1.0 bits. The copula gives 0.0 bits. In this simulation the copula estimator sees nothing, because its model of the link has no room for a U shape. Same data, three answers.

Now the trap. Take two signals with no link at all. The true mutual information is zero. With a thousand samples, the histogram estimator gives 0.03 bits on average. With three hundred, 0.12. With a hundred, 0.35. With thirty samples, it gives 1.04 bits, from nothing. The grid has sixty four cells, and with few samples, chance clumps look like structure. The error always pushes upward. The nearest neighbour and copula estimators stay within 0.03 bits of zero.

So a mutual information value alone means nothing. Compare it with a null band. Shift Ava's series in time against Otto's, many times, so that each series keeps its own rhythm, and compute the same estimator each time.

Here is a simulated dyad of 150 samples, with a correlation of only 0.19. The histogram estimator gives 0.67 bits. The shifted pairs give 0.18 on average, and the ninety fifth percentile is 0.23. None of the five hundred shifts reaches the real value. Nearest neighbours give 0.61 against a null mean of 0.01. The copula estimator gives 0.07, against a ninety fifth percentile of 0.04. It barely clears its band. Report the estimator, the number of bins or neighbours, and the null.

What changes between two brains? The same measure works between one brain and the body or the behaviour of the other person: voice, movement, heart rate. That is the door to chapter ten. And a high value does not say where it comes from. A shared stimulus produces mutual information too, so the source compass stays open.

Let's recap. Mutual information is the overlap of two uncertainties. It sees any shape of link, but its value depends on the estimator. With few samples, the histogram invents information. So always show the null, and say which estimator you used.

O-information across brains

With three signals, the O-information separates redundancy, where lanterns blink in unison, from synergy, where information exists only in the whole, as in the exclusive-or lantern. Total correlation minus dual total correlation gives a positive sign for redundancy and a negative one for synergy. A card-game simulation shows that a shared task can fake synergy unless surrogates preserve task structure.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Three kites fly on the hill today. Ava's, Otto's, and Eve's. So far we have measured pairs. This episode asks what a third kite can add, that no pair can see.

You know mutual information from the last episode. It counts what two signals share. With three signals, the Venn picture starts to break. The Information series shows why. Here we keep only what we need.

Give each kite a lantern. Every second it is either on or off. First case: the three lanterns blink in unison. Look at one, and you know the other two. The same information is copied three times. That is redundancy.

Second case. Ava's lantern is a fair coin. Otto's lantern is another fair coin, independent. Eve's lantern is lit exactly when the first two disagree. In logic, that is the exclusive or.

Now look at Ava and Eve alone. It is pure noise. Look at Otto and Eve alone. Also noise. Every pair tells you nothing. But take all three, and the rule is complete. That is synergy: information that exists only in the whole.

Here is a number that tells these two cases apart. It is built from two ingredients. The total correlation is the sum of the single entropies, minus the joint entropy. It says how much the three are tied together. The dual total correlation is the joint entropy, minus what each signal holds that the other two do not know. It says how much of the information is shared among them.

The O-information is the difference. Omega equals total correlation minus dual total correlation. Rosas, Mediano, Gastpar and Jensen introduced it in 2019, in Physical Review E.

Read the sign. If omega is positive, redundancy dominates. If omega is negative, synergy dominates.

Check it on the lanterns. Three copies: total correlation is two bits, dual total correlation is one bit, so omega is plus one. The exclusive or: total correlation is one bit, dual total correlation is two bits, so omega is minus one. In the paper, for binary variables, these two cases are the extremes.

Now a simulated triad of brains. Each signal is cut in two, above or below its median, and entropies are counted directly. First, three signals that share one hidden source, each with its own noise. Omega is 0.3, and the best pair shares 0.4 bits. Redundancy wins.

Second, Ava and Otto are independent. Eve follows the product of the two. The best pair shares essentially nothing. Yet omega is minus 0.35. A pairwise measure would call this triad unconnected. The O-information does not.

What is it sensitive to? It is a balance, not a count. An omega near zero can hide a lot of redundancy and a lot of synergy cancelling each other. It also depends on the estimator, and on how you cut the signals. And the cost explodes. Two heads with sixty-four channels each make 128 channels, and 341,376 triplets. Choose your triplets before you look, or correct heavily.

Now the trap. A card game. Ava sees the first card. Otto sees the second. Eve answers when the cards differ. Nobody talks to anybody. Each brain just follows the task, with a few errors. This is simulated.

Omega is minus 0.29, with 1200 trials. No pair shares anything. Compare with surrogates where each brain's trials are shuffled freely. The surrogates sit at zero, and none of a thousand shuffles goes as low. It looks like synergy between brains.

But the shuffle destroyed the task. Shuffle each brain's trials only among trials with the same pair of cards. The task structure survives. Now the surrogates sit at minus 0.29, and almost half of them go as low as the real value. The synergy was in the task. Not in the interaction.

So the surrogates must keep the structure that the task gives. We come back to surrogates in episode 7.6.

What changes between two brains? A triad can carry information that no pair carries. No pairwise measure will ever see it. But a shared stimulus, route one, can create it as well. Ask where it comes from.

Let's recap. Redundancy repeats. Synergy appears only in the whole. Omega is total correlation minus dual total correlation: positive for redundancy, negative for synergy. Across brains, the task can fake it, so the surrogates must keep the task.

Beyond pairs · Measuring a group

With six brains, the mean of fifteen pairwise values asks whether each pair is steady, while the Kuramoto order parameter asks whether the whole group moves as one. Two antiphase subgroups score high on the first and near zero on the second. Total correlation and hypergraphs add other views, and every measure's floor shifts with group size, so each size needs its own null.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The festival is in full swing, and the sky above the hill is full of kites. Today there are six pilots, not two. Ava and Otto are here, with four friends. Every measure of this chapter took two signals. What do we do with six?

The first answer is the one everyone reaches for. Count the pairs. Six people make fifteen pairs. Measure each pair, for example with the PLV, and average the fifteen numbers. That is the mean pairwise value. Reinero, Dikker and Van Bavel used this logic in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience in 2021. They recorded teams of four with wireless EEG, averaged the pairwise coherence over each team, and found that higher synchrony went with better team performance.

There is a second answer. Do not look at pairs at all. Look at the group as one thing. Give each person an arrow, which is their phase. Add the six arrows, and divide by six. The length of the average arrow is the Kuramoto order parameter, called R. In symbols, R is the absolute value of the mean of e to the i theta. It comes from the Kuramoto model of coupled oscillators, reviewed by Strogatz in Physica D in 2000. If all six arrows point the same way, R is one. If they point everywhere, R is close to zero.

Now we put the two answers side by side, on a simulated group of six. First group: two subgroups of three. Inside each subgroup, the three kites move together. But the two subgroups are in antiphase. When one is up, the other is down. The mean of the fifteen pairs is 0.93. That is very high, because every pair keeps a steady angle, and the PLV does not care which angle. But the order parameter R is only 0.14. The arrows of the two subgroups point in opposite directions, and they cancel. Same group, two verdicts.

Second group, simulated too: six people weakly pulled together, with no subgroups. The mean pairwise value is 0.28. The order parameter is 0.47. Six independent oscillators, with no link at all, give 0.16 and 0.36. So here both measures sit only a little above their floors. Note that the floor of R is higher, because even six random arrows leave a short average arrow.

The two numbers answer two different questions. The mean of the pairs asks: is each pair steady? The order parameter asks: does the whole group move as one?

A third answer is the total correlation, introduced by Watanabe in the IBM Journal of Research and Development in 1960. It asks how much the six signals share, compared with six independent signals. It is the sum of the entropy of each signal, minus the entropy of all six together. For Gaussian signals, we can estimate it from the correlation matrix. On our simulated groups, we get 7.2 bits for the two subgroups, 0.67 bits for the weakly coupled group, and 0.35 bits for six independent oscillators. That last number is not zero. The estimate is biased upward, and we meet the floor again.

All these measures give one number for the whole group. But a group also has a structure. A graph joins two nodes with a link. A hypergraph has links that join three nodes or more at once. Battiston and colleagues reviewed these networks beyond pairs in Physics Reports in 2020.

Picture a tarp stretched between three pegs. Cut the strings between the pairs, and the tarp is still there. A triad can be tied together by something that no pair shows. The O-information, from episode six point eleven, looks for exactly this.

Now the trap. Do not compare groups of different sizes without care. Three people make three pairs. Six people make fifteen. Twelve make sixty-six, and twenty-four make two hundred and seventy-six. The links grow with the square of the group.

The floors move too. Here is independent noise, simulated, in the alpha band, with a one-second window. The mean pairwise value stays at 0.39, whatever the size. But the order parameter falls. It averages 0.53 with three people, 0.37 with six, 0.26 with twelve, and 0.18 with twenty-four. So an R of 0.4 is something in a crowd of twenty-four, and nothing in a group of three.

The count of links suffers as well. Count the pairs above 0.5. Independent noise already gives 0.8 such pairs with three people, 4.4 with six, 19 with twelve, and 79 with twenty-four. A bigger group gives more false links.

So compare like with like. Build a null for each group size, and report the size. Heterogeneity matters too. One person with a different rhythm or a noisier signal can pull down every pair that includes them, and the group value with it.

What changes with many brains? In classrooms, concerts and teams, the group becomes the unit of the study. One class gives one value, not fifteen independent pairs, because the pairs share people. This is the lesson of episode eight point one.

Let's recap. The mean of pairs asks whether each pair is steady. The order parameter asks whether the whole group moves as one. The total correlation asks how much the signals share. A hypergraph keeps links among three or more. And group size changes the floor of every one of them.

And a correlation with a lag, when one brain follows the other, as in Stephens, Silbert and Hasson, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2010. A lag is a clue, not a verdict.

Fourth page: heart and breathing. Our recommendation is a correlation in a sliding window, and episode ten point five gives the details. The literature here is scattered. The systematic review by Palumbo and colleagues, in Personality and Social Psychology Review in 2017, describes it as fragmented. So a simple measure, well reported, is a fair start.

Fifth page: directed questions. Who leads? The entries are Granger causality, the partial directed coherence, the directed transfer function, and transfer entropy. Each comes with reserves. A common source that reaches one person before the other creates a false direction, as Bastos and Schoffelen explain, in Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience in 2016. In fMRI, Smith and colleagues, in NeuroImage in 2011, found that lag based methods did badly in their simulations. So let the design give the control. If the speaker is known, the direction found must follow the speaker.

At the bottom of every page, the club writes the same rule. Choose one main measure before you see the data, and write it down. Report the others as sensitivity analyses, as in episode eight point five. Whatever you choose, the null comes with it, and the compass of sources stays lit.

Let's recap. A question and a signal choose the page. EEG, fNIRS, fMRI, heart and breathing, and directed questions each have their entries. This table is a teaching guide, not a standard. One main measure, chosen first.
