PLATE 7.17:11

The map

The many named tests share one idea: a statistic far out in its curtain of chance. A hardware-store map of twelve drawers sorts them by question (centres, spreads, shapes) and design (one sample, two groups, pairs, many groups). Three questions find the drawer: unit, comparison, pairing. Forty repeat measures from ten patients ring falsely 32.7 percent of the time.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the hardware store. Behind the counter hangs a wall of small drawers, and every drawer holds a tool with a name: the t-test, the Welch test, the Wilcoxon test, the Mann-Whitney test, Levene's test. Students meet them as a zoo. Today we draw the map of the zoo.

Chapter six gave us one idea. A test asks whether a statistic is far out in its curtain of chance. Every drawer holds that same idea. Only the statistic changes, and the way the curtain is built.

Here is the proof, with simulated data. Two groups of 40 people do a speed task. The first averages 399.3 milliseconds, the second 408.4. Their spreads differ: 39.55 milliseconds against 91.14. We ask two questions of these same people. Do the centres differ? Do the spreads differ? Guess before you see: which question will ring?

First, the centres. The statistic is the difference of means: 9.1 milliseconds. NIL builds the curtain by shuffling the group labels 10,000 times. It is 15.6 wide, and our flag sits in the middle of the pile. 5,629 shuffles go at least that far out. The p-value is 0.56.

Now the spreads. The statistic is the ratio of the two spreads: the second group is 2.30 times as spread out. Same recipe, a new curtain. None of the 10,000 shuffles reaches our flag, so p is 0.0001, the smallest value that 10,000 shuffles can give. Same people, two questions, two answers. The question picks the statistic, and the statistic picks the curtain.

Now the map. The rows are the questions: what do we compare? Centres, spreads, or shapes and tails. The columns are the designs: one sample, two independent groups, paired measures where each value has a partner, or more than two groups. That makes twelve drawers.

Each drawer holds two kinds of tool. The parametric one leans on a model of the data, usually a bell curve. The rank one first replaces each value by its place in line. The centres row is the busiest. One sample: the t-test, or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Two groups: Student's or Welch's t-test, or the Mann-Whitney test. Pairs: the paired t-test, or signed ranks on the differences. More than two groups: analysis of variance, or Kruskal-Wallis.

The other rows have their own tools: the F test and Levene's test for spreads, Kolmogorov and Smirnov for shapes. We leave those drawers closed. The next episodes open them: Welch in 7.2, pairs in 7.3, ranks in 7.4, spreads in 7.5, shapes in 7.6, more than two groups in 7.7.

To find a drawer, ask three questions. What is the unit, the thing that counts once: a person, a field, a patient? What do we compare: centres, spreads, or shapes? That picks the row. Are the measures paired: does each value have a partner? That picks the column. Let us try them on six illustrative studies, with simulated data.

One. A botanist checks a seed packet that promises 20 centimetres. She measures 12 seedlings. The unit is the seedling, she compares a centre, and nothing is paired: one sample. The statistic is the mean minus 20: minus 1.62 centimetres. If the packet is right, each gap is as likely to be above zero as below, so we flip the signs 10,000 times. The flag lands in the tail: p is 0.017.

Two. A clinic gives 20 patients a drug and 20 a placebo, and compares a depression score. The unit is the patient, the centres are compared, and the people differ between groups: two independent groups. Drug minus placebo is minus 5.18 points, and shuffling the labels gives p of 0.013.

Three. A sleep lab measures 15 students in an ordinary week, then again in exam week. Guess before you see: which column? The unit is the student, the centres are compared, and every value has a partner, the same student: paired. We take each student's change, and the paired test is one sample of changes. The mean change is minus 0.65 hours, and flipping signs gives p of 0.0015.

Four. A school compares three teaching methods, 15 students each. The unit is the student, the centres are compared, nobody is paired: three groups. One number says how far apart the three means are. It is 0.84, and p is 0.44. The curtain says that this is ordinary.

Five. Does a drug make patients differ more from one another? 25 patients per group. The unit is the patient, but what we compare is the spread, not the centre. The centre question gives p of 0.59. The spreads are 3.63 and 8.33, a ratio of 2.29, and the spread question gives p of 0.0014. The question moved us to another row.

Six. A lab compares reaction times in two groups of 40. The medians are alike, 381 and 370 milliseconds, but the slowest tenth differs: the 90th percentiles are 475.5 and 718.1. A test on the medians gives p of 0.70. A test on the 90th percentile gives 0.039. The unit is the person, we compare tails, and the groups are independent.

One question deserves a second look: the unit. A hospital measures 10 patients, five per arm, four times each. That is 40 numbers, but a patient's visits resemble each other, so there are only ten units. Nothing is going on. Guess before you see: if we feed the 40 numbers to a two-group test, as if they were 40 different people, how often will it ring at the five percent level? Five, fifteen, or thirty percent? In 10,000 simulated studies it rings 32.7 percent of the time. Its curtain is built for 40 people, but chance really spreads about twice as wide. Average each patient's visits first, and compare five against five: 4.8 percent. Ecologists call this error pseudoreplication, after Stuart Hurlbert in 1984. It is why the map starts with the unit.

So the zoo is a map. Every drawer holds a statistic and a curtain. Three questions lead to your drawer: what is the unit, what do we compare, are the measures paired?

Remember: a test asks whether a statistic is far out in its curtain of chance, and the map tells you which statistic and which curtain. Next time: Student and Welch, two means.

PLATE 7.27:14

Comparing two means: Student and Welch

Student's and Welch's t-tests both count how many rulers of noise fit in the gap between two means. Student pools one spread, so with unequal spreads and unequal group sizes its false alarms drift, reaching 18.2 percent. Welch uses each group's own spread and stays near five percent at little cost. Make Welch the default, with no variance test first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hardware store, we open a drawer in the centres row, under two independent groups. Inside lie two tools with two names: Student's t-test and Welch's t-test. Which one do you reach for? Most courses hand you Student's, and mention Welch's late, if at all. Today we see why that is backwards.

Chapter six gave us one idea: a test asks whether a statistic is far out in its curtain of chance. Here the statistic is called t. We build it first, with simulated data, and write the formula afterwards.

Two fertilisers, 15 seedlings each. Fertiliser A gives an average height of 22.81 centimetres, fertiliser B 25.51. The gap between the means is 2.70 centimetres. Is that gap big? It depends on the noise. So we make a ruler out of the noise. Averages wobble from study to study, and the wobble of the gap is its standard error. Here the ruler is 1.16 centimetres long. We lay rulers end to end across the gap: two fit, and a third of another. That count is t: 2.33.

The count has no unit, so it works for any measure. Take an illustrative gap of 10 minutes in hospital waiting times. With a ruler of 2 minutes, five rulers fit. With a ruler of 8 minutes, barely one and a quarter. The same gap is big or small, depending on the ruler.

Is 2.33 rulers far out? NIL builds the curtain by simulation. We draw 10,000 pairs of groups of 15 from one and the same population, so that nothing is going on, and compute the t of each pair. The pile is a bell, with a width of 1.04. Only 5.2 percent of these studies pass 2.05 rulers, the five percent cutoff. Our 2.33 sits beyond it. The blue curve is the one William Gosset worked out in 1908, as a brewer at Guinness in Dublin, publishing under the name Student. Now the formula: t is the gap, divided by its standard error. Gap over ruler.

How long is the ruler? Each group adds its own wobble: its spread, divided by the square root of its size. The ruler is the long side of a right triangle, whose short sides are the two wobbles. Welch, in 1947, builds the triangle from each group's own spread. Student pools: he averages the two variances into one spread, and uses it for both groups.

Take 10 people against 40. Their spreads are 11.32 and 4.77. Welch's short sides are 3.58 and 0.75, so his ruler is 3.66. Student's pooled spread is 6.52. It shortens the first side to 2.06, and stretches the second to 1.03. His ruler is only 2.31, 0.63 of Welch's. A shorter ruler fits more times in the same gap, so Student's t comes out too big. But with equal group sizes, the two rulers are the same length. Here, 20 against 20: 1.77 for both.

So when does it matter? Guess before you see. 10 people against 40, and no true difference. The small group is twice as spread: 8 against 4. How often does Student's test ring at the five percent level? Five, ten, or twenty percent? In 10,000 simulated studies, it rings 18.2 percent of the time. The test assumes the narrow blue curve, but the real pile is wider: 1.52 against 1.02 expected. Welch's test rings 5.0 percent. Its curve is fatter, with a median of 10.2 degrees of freedom, because a small, noisy group is a weak ruler, and Welch counts it as weak.

Now flip it. The small group is the calm one: 4 against 8. Student's test now rings 0.5 percent of the time. It is too timid, and would miss real effects. Welch's test rings 5.2 percent. So Student's false-alarm rate drifts away from five percent in both directions, depending on which group is smaller and which is noisier. Welch's stays.

The same thing in four fields, all simulated. A clinic compares 9 patients with a rare disease to 45 controls, on an anxiety score with spreads 14 and 7. Student's test rings 19.9 percent, Welch's 5.1. Fish from two lakes, 15 against 60, spreads 3 and 6 centimetres: Student 0.5 percent, Welch 4.9. Two classes, 24 against 36 students, spreads 12 and 8: Student 6.8, Welch 4.8. A factory measures 12 screws from one machine and 50 from another, spreads 0.9 and 0.4 millimetres: Student 21.2 percent, Welch 5.2.

Is the safe tool expensive? When the spreads really are equal, the two tests nearly agree. With 20 against 20 they are the same test: for a true gap of 0.8 spreads, power is 68.8 percent for Student and 68.7 for Welch. With 15 against 45, Welch gives up 2.4 points of power. Only at 10 against 40 does it give up 4.3. And with nothing going on, both ring about five percent. That is why Delacre, Lakens and Leys argued in 2017 that Welch's test should be the default.

A common sentence says: Student's test is the main one, and Welch's is the rescue. Read it the other way round. Welch's is the main test. Student's is the special case where the spreads happen to match.

Some courses add a gate: test the variances first. If the variance test rings, use Welch's; if not, Student's. Look at that gate. Same design, 10 against 40, with the small group 1.5 times as spread: 6 against 4. Guess before you see: how often does the variance test notice? Ten, thirty-five, or ninety percent? It rings 34.4 percent of the time. Two studies in three go to Student's test, which rings 12.2 percent by itself. The whole procedure, gate then test, rings 8.9 percent. Welch's alone rings 4.9. At twice as spread, the gate rings 71.2 percent, and the procedure still rings 8.4. The gate misses the cases where the problem is real, and adds errors of its own. Rasch, Kubinger and Moder reached the same verdict in 2011: pre-testing does not pay off.

So here is the routine. Compute the gap. Measure it with a ruler built from each group's own spread. Count the rulers, and read the count in its curtain. No gate, no assumption of equal spreads.

Remember: Welch's test costs almost nothing when the spreads are equal, and protects when they are not, so make it the default, with no variance test first. Next time: paired data, when the same person is measured twice.

PLATE 7.37:06

Paired data

A paired test is a one-sample test on each person's difference, as if a string linked each patient's before and after pegs. Between-person variability drops out, so power depends on how correlated the two measures are: at 0.9 correlation the paired test detects the shift 99.7 percent of the time. Pairing belongs to the design, never to the data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hardware store, we walk along the counter to the third column of the map: paired measures. Here every value has a partner. The same person is measured twice, before and after, or left and right. Last time we built t as a gap counted in rulers. Today's question is what changes when the two measures belong together.

Sixteen patients, simulated. Their systolic blood pressure before a drug, and after eight weeks. Picture two pegboards, before on the left, after on the right, and one string per patient between their two pegs. The average falls from 138.88 to 132.00 millimetres of mercury. But look at the strings. People start from very different places, and the two columns of pegs overlap almost completely.

Now forget the pegs, and keep only the tilt of each string: that patient's after minus before. Sixteen differences. The first patient falls by 12, the fourth rises by 12, the eighth falls by 29. Their mean is minus 6.875, and their spread is 9.084. A paired test is a one-sample test on these sixteen numbers.

We build the curtain by simulation. If the drug did nothing, each patient's change would be as likely to point up as down. So NIL flips a coin for every patient, and flips the sign of the change when it lands tails. We recompute the mean change and drop it as a tile. We do this 10,000 times. The pile is centred on zero, with a width of 2.76. Only 59 of the 10,000 flips go as far out as our minus 6.875, so p is 0.0060.

Now the formula, the same recipe as last time. The gap is the mean change. The ruler is its standard error: the spread of the changes, divided by the square root of 16. That is 9.084 over 4, so 2.271 millimetres. The count is t: minus 6.875 over 2.271, which is minus 3.03. Its curve, with 15 degrees of freedom, gives p of 0.0085, close to the flips.

A common sentence says: the paired test is a different test. It is the one-sample test, applied to differences. The pairing lives in how the data were collected.

What if we ignore the pairing? Treat the same 32 numbers as two groups of strangers. Guess before you see: will p stay below 0.05? The gap is the same, but the ruler is 5.003, more than twice the paired ruler. The count falls to minus 1.37, and p is 0.180. Same data, same drug, and no signal. Why? Between people, one patient sits at 162 and another at 111. A stranger test must carry that spread. The paired test never sees it, because each person is compared with themselves. Between-person variability leaves the equation.

So how much does pairing help? It depends on how alike the two measures are: their correlation. Guess before you see: same 20 people, a true shift of half a spread. Turn the correlation up. Does the paired test detect the shift more often, or less? Here are three simulated groups of 20, on a score with a spread of 15. At correlation zero, the strings crisscross, and the changes have a spread of 17.17. At 0.5 they calm down to 11.12. At 0.9 they run nearly parallel, at 4.81. The paired ruler shrinks from 3.84 to 2.49 to 1.08. The stranger ruler hardly moves.

Now the power, from 10,000 simulated studies each. With no correlation, the paired test detects the shift 32.9 percent of the time, the stranger test 34.6. Pairing gained nothing. At 0.3, the paired test reaches 42.9 percent. At 0.6, 65.7. At 0.9, 99.7 percent, while the stranger test, fed the same data, falls to 16.9. Its ruler stays long, so it is too timid.

Turn it around. How many pairs for 80 percent power? With no correlation, 66, about the 64 per group of two independent groups. At 0.5, 34. At 0.8, 15. At 0.9, just 9. Count values per condition: a pair gives one in each.

The same idea in four other settings, all simulated. Left and right hand: grip strength of 18 climbers, correlation 0.88. As strangers, p is 0.221. Paired, 0.0024. Twins: 25 pairs, one twin tutored in reading, correlation 0.61. As strangers, 0.286. Paired, 0.0995: closer, but not enough. Matched pairs: 30 patients, each matched with a control of the same age and sex, correlation 0.63. As strangers, 0.086. Paired, 0.0089. A crossover: 14 volunteers drink decaf one day and coffee another, correlation 0.80. As strangers, 0.016. Paired, 0.0001.

So pairing is a property of the study design, not of the test. It is decided before the data exist: the same person twice, twins, matched pairs, the two eyes of a patient, the two halves of one leaf. Each person, or each pair, is its own control. In a crossover, we also randomise who drinks coffee first.

Two mistakes remain. The first is ignoring the pairing. With 20 pairs correlated at 0.8 and nothing going on, the stranger test rings 0.02 percent of the time, and it misses real effects. The second is inventing a pairing. Two independent groups of 20, paired by row with arbitrary partners: the paired test rings 5.03 percent, which is fine, but it gains nothing, and its power is 32.2 against 33.8. Now pair them after sorting both groups on the outcome itself. With nothing going on, it rings 60.0 percent. Partners must come from the design, never from the data.

So here is the routine. Ask whether each value has a partner by design. Take each person's difference. Test the differences as one sample, and read t in its curtain.

Remember: pairing is a property of the design, not of the test. Each person is their own control, so between-person variability leaves the equation. Next time: rank tests, when values are replaced by their places in line.

PLATE 7.47:24

Rank tests

Rank tests keep each value's place in line, like a photo finish that discards the clock. Mann-Whitney asks how often a value from one group beats one from the other, so one mistyped value loses its grip. It is not a test of medians in general: with different shapes it rang 67.5 percent at 300 per group. On normal data the cost is small.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hardware store. On the map from 7.1, the centres row holds two rank tools: the Mann-Whitney test for two groups, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for pairs. Wilcoxon proposed both tests in 1945, and Mann and Whitney studied the two-group one in 1947. The trick: forget the values, keep the places in line. A photo finish does exactly that. It keeps who crossed first, second and third, and throws the clock away.

Here are two small groups, illustrative pain scores from zero to ten, five patients each. Group A scored 2, 3, 3, 5 and 6. Group B scored 3, 5, 7, 8 and 9. Line all ten up from lowest to highest and hand out places, one to ten. Ties are the catch. Three patients scored 3, so they share places two, three and four, and each gets the average: 3. Two scored 5, and share places five and six: 5.5 each. Add up each group's places. Group A gets 19.5. Group B gets 35.5. Together that is 55, as it must be.

What does a rank sum say? Draw a grid with every patient of A against every patient of B: twenty-five pairs. In each cell, ask whether A's score is higher. A yes is one point, and a tie is half a point. We count 4.5 points out of 25: 18 percent. That is the Mann-Whitney statistic, and it is the rank sum in disguise: 19.5, minus the 15 that the lowest five places would give, is 4.5. So the test asks about pairs. If I draw one patient from each group, how often does A's score beat B's? Half the time would mean no difference. Of all 252 ways to split the ten places, 28 land as far from half as ours does. The p-value is 0.111.

Now a bigger study, simulated: reaction times, 20 people per group, with a long tail. A is higher than B in 267 of the 400 pairs: 66.8 percent. NIL builds the curtain the way we did in 14.2: shuffle the group labels 10,000 times, and recompute the share each time. The pile is centred on 50 percent, and about 9 points wide. 709 shuffles go at least as far out as our flag, so p is 0.071. Not far enough. The Welch t-test, on the same people, says 0.055.

Now pairs, as in 7.3. Ten patients, pain before and after, simulated. This seed was picked among ten, for readability. The changes are minus 4, plus 1, plus 1, minus 3, minus 1, minus 4, minus 2, plus 1, plus 2, plus 1. Step one: ignore the signs, and rank the sizes. Five changes have size 1, so they share places one to five, and each gets 3. The two 2s get 6.5, the 3 gets 8, and the two 4s get 9.5. Step two: give the signs back. The rises carry 18.5 places, the falls 36.5. Step three: if nothing happened, each change would be as likely to rise as to fall, so we flip signs, as in 7.3. Of all 1,024 sign patterns, 392 are at least as lopsided as ours: p is 0.38.

Two groups of ten, simulated reaction times. The treated group B is faster: its mean is 330 milliseconds against 398 for A, and the Welch p is 0.0002. Now one entry gets an extra digit: B's slowest, 382, is typed as 3,820. B's mean jumps to 674, the gap flips sign, and p becomes 0.45. The rank test saw that value climb from place 13 to place 20, and there it stops. A thousand times bigger would change nothing to the rank test. Its p goes from 0.0001 to 0.0015, and stays.

A rank test changes the question, not only the assumptions. Take two simulated groups. A is a symmetric bell around 100, with spread 10. B is skewed, with a long right tail and the same spread, built so that its median is exactly 100. Is Mann-Whitney fooled? Over the whole populations, A is higher than B in 44.3 percent of pairs, not 50. In one study of 300 per group, the sample medians are 100.3 and 99.9, but A is higher in 44.7 percent of its pairs. Now repeat, 10,000 studies each. With 25 per group, the test rings 11.1 percent of the time. With 100, 28.7. With 300, 67.5. With 1,000, 99.2. With equal shapes, at 300 per group, it rings 5.5 percent of the time, close to the five we promised. The test never looked at the medians. It looked at pairs. Divine and colleagues made this point in 2018: Mann-Whitney is not a test of medians in general. It reads as a shift only when the two distributions have the same shape.

What does it cost to use ranks? On normal data, almost nothing. With 64 per group and a true shift of half a spread, the t-test detects it 80.5 percent of the time, and the rank test 78.8. For very large samples, theory says 95 percent of the t-test's efficiency, three over pi: a different measure, so a different number. On heavy-tailed data, the rank test wins: 59.9 against 41.7. On skewed data: 78.1 against 61.2.

Six quick cases, illustrative and simulated, each saying what the rank test compares. Pain on two wards: how often a patient on A reports more than one on B? 67.3 percent, p is 0.020. Long-tailed reaction times: how often a trial from A is slower? 46.2 percent, p is 0.65. House prices: how often a house in A costs more? 65.1 percent, p is 0.13. Survey answers with many ties: 56.7 percent, p is 0.24. Hospital stays: how often a stay on A is longer? 36.0 percent, p is 0.034. And paired walking times for 12 patients: the rises carry 30.8 percent of the rank total, p is 0.27.

For more than two groups, the rank version is the Kruskal-Wallis test, later in this chapter. On our map, both drawers now hold a rank tool beside the t-test.

Remember: a rank test keeps the places, not the values, so one wild value loses its grip. But it asks a different question: how often does one group beat the other? Next time: comparing spreads.

PLATE 7.57:03

Comparing spreads

A treatment that doubled the spread but not the mean escapes the t-test (p is 0.80). The F ratio of variances misreads heavy tails as differing spread, so Levene replaced variances with distances to the centre, and Brown and Forsythe used the median as the centre. A difference in spread is a result, not only a broken assumption.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hardware store. On the map from 7.1, we have spent three episodes in the centres row. Today we open the widths row. The question is no longer where a group sits, but how spread out it is.

Here is a treatment that helps some people and harms others. It is simulated and illustrative. Forty people get the control, forty get the treatment, and each ends with an improvement score. The control group averages 52.3, the treated group 51.4. Welch's t-test, from 7.2, finds nothing: p is 0.80. Now look at the dots. The control scores huddle around their average, with a spread of 10.9. The treated scores are scattered, with a spread of 19.5.

In truth, the treatment doubled the spread, from 10 to 20. Some patients gained a lot, and others lost. An average cannot show that. A spread can, so we need a test of spreads.

The obvious test divides one variance by the other. That is the F ratio, from 4.4. Its curtain comes from simulation: two samples of 20 from the same normal law, ten thousand times. The ratio lands outside its two cutoffs, 0.40 and 2.53, in 5.2 percent of the studies. Good. Now guess before you see. Give the law slightly heavier tails, a Student law with 10 degrees of freedom, hardly different by eye. There is still no true difference. How often does the F test ring? Five, ten, or twenty percent?

8.9 percent. With 6 degrees of freedom, 13.7. With 5, the law of 4.4, 16.4 percent, the same figure as there. On a skewed law, 16.9. The F ratio reads heavy tails as a difference in spread.

Levene found a way around this in 1960. Stop comparing variances. Compare distances. Take two small groups of six whole numbers. Step one: find each group's centre. Levene used the mean: 50.5 and 38.8. Step two: replace every value by its distance to its own centre. The first group's distances run from 1.5 to 7.5. The second group's run from 4.8 to 20.8. Step three: compare the two piles of distances with the t-test of 7.2. Their averages are 4.17 and 12.83. The gap is minus 8.67, the ruler is 2.53, so t is minus 3.43, and p is 0.0065. A wide group is one whose values lie far from its centre.

Brown and Forsythe, in 1974, changed one thing: the centre is the median. Here that is 50.5 and 40.5. By coincidence, the averages of the distances are the same as before. The ruler is a little longer, 2.64, so t is minus 3.29, and p is 0.0082. Why bother? Because a median is not pulled by one wild value.

Do these tests keep their promise? Same design, equal spreads, 20 per group, ten thousand studies each. On normal data, the F ratio rings 5.2 percent of the time, Levene's test 5.6, and Brown and Forsythe's 4.0. With the 5-degree law, F rings 16.4, Levene's 5.6, and Brown and Forsythe's 4.0. They shrug off heavy tails. A skewed law is another story. With a chi-square law, Levene's test rings 10.2 percent, while Brown and Forsythe's stays near 4. The median-centred test is a little timid at 20 per group, but it is the safer one.

Now power. Back to the treatment: the spread doubled, 40 per group, ten thousand studies. Welch's test, which looks at averages, rings 5.3 percent of the time. The means really are equal, so that is its false-alarm rate. The F ratio rings 99.1 percent of the time, Levene's test 97.5, and Brown and Forsythe's 97.0. Turn the knob. At a ratio of spreads of 1.25, Brown and Forsythe's test finds it 22.9 percent of the time. At 1.5, 60.4. At 2, 97.1. A quarter wider is mostly missed with 40 people per group.

Five more places where this question matters, all illustrative and simulated.

A teaching method: 30 pupils each, spreads 8.4 and 14.4 points, p is 0.0063. A wider spread means the method lifts some pupils and leaves others behind.

A machine: parts from week one and week six, spreads 0.56 and 1.10 millimetres, p is 0.024. The average held, but the precision drifted.

Two labs measuring the same sample: spreads 0.75 and 1.90, p is 0.0053. A wider spread means a less precise lab, which is not the same as a biased one.

Reaction times, rested against tired: spreads 34 and 71 milliseconds, p is 0.0008. Fatigue slows people down, and makes them less consistent.

Income in two regions: spreads 17 and 34 thousand dollars, p is 0.0001. A wider spread means more inequality. Welch's test, on the averages, gives 0.077.

Some courses use a variance test as a gate: if it rings, use Welch's test, otherwise Student's. In 7.2, that gate was Levene's test. With one group 1.5 times as spread, it rang 34.4 percent of the time, and the whole procedure had 8.9 percent false alarms, against 4.9 for Welch's alone. A spread test is not a gatekeeper. Use Welch's test, and look at spreads when spreads are the question.

And that is the point. A difference in spread is a result, not only a broken assumption. Bryk and Raudenbush argued in 1988 that unequal variances deserve substantive attention, because ignoring them can hide real effects. A wider spread says that people do not all respond in the same way.

Two things we left out: comparing whole shapes, in 7.6, and more than two groups, in 7.7.

Remember: a difference in spread is a result, not only a broken assumption.

Compare distances to the centre rather than variances, and prefer the median as the centre.

PLATE 7.67:14

Comparing shapes and tails

Two groups can share a mean and a spread and still differ in shape. Drawing each group as a staircase, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test finds where the curves are furthest apart, but it is weak when only the slowest tail changes. A shift function compares deciles, with bars widened for nine simultaneous readings, and shows where the groups differ.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hardware store. On the map from 7.1, we have compared centres, and then widths. Today the question is wider still: where, along the whole range of values, do two groups differ?

Here are reaction times, simulated and illustrative. Two hundred rested people, with a normal law around 450 milliseconds. Two hundred tired people, built differently: most answer steadily, but one in five has a long lapse. We built the tired law with exactly the same mean and the same spread as the rested one. So in truth, the two laws differ only in shape. In our sample, the averages are 457.7 and 452.4. Welch's test says p is 0.61. The spreads are 107.9 and 103.8, and the Brown and Forsythe test from 7.5 says p is 0.39. Neither test sees anything. Yet the two shapes are not the same.

To compare whole shapes, draw each group as a staircase. Take eight tired people, sorted from fastest to slowest, from 302 up to 680 milliseconds. Walk along the axis. Each time you pass a person, step up by one eighth, that is 12.5 percent. At the end, you have climbed to 100 percent. This is the empirical cumulative curve: at any time, it tells you the share of people who had already answered. With 200 people, each step is only half a percent, and the staircase looks like a smooth curve. A staircase that sits to the right of another one means slower answers.

Now draw both staircases on the same axis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test asks one question: where are the two curves furthest apart, vertically? At 452 milliseconds, the rested curve stands at 52.0 percent, and the tired curve at 64.5. The gap is D, 0.125.

Is that big? Ask chance, as in chapter 14. Pool the 400 values, shuffle the group labels, and measure D again. Ten thousand times. D reached 0.125 or more in 813 of the shuffles, so p is 0.081. The textbook formula, a large-sample law, says 0.088. Two routes, nearly the same answer, and neither falls below 0.05.

So even the shape test shrugs here. We did not expect that, and we keep it. This one sample is on the unlucky side. Draw fresh samples from the same two laws, ten thousand times: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test rings in 66 percent of them, while Welch's test rings in 4.6 percent, its false-alarm rate.

Now the weakness of this test. Take a base law of reaction times, 200 per group, ten thousand studies for each case. First case: every response is 40 milliseconds slower, a shift of the whole curve. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test rings in 99.3 percent of the studies. Second case: only the slowest 30 percent are stretched, by just enough to raise the mean by the same 40 milliseconds. Guess before you see. The same rise in the mean, the same 200 per group. Will the test ring about as often, or half as often, or far less? It rings in only 20.4 percent. Welch's test, which looks at the mean, rings in 73.1. Why? A stretched tail pushes its change far to the right, where the curves climb slowly. The biggest gap is 0.09, against 0.21 for the shift. And only 30 percent of the people moved at all, so D could never be larger than 0.30. The test is most sensitive near the middle, as the NIST handbook says.

So how do we find where two distributions differ? With a shift function, an idea by Doksum in 1974. Take one simulated study from the tail case, 200 per group. Sort each group, and cut it into ten equal parts. That gives nine cut points, the deciles. We estimate each with the Harrell-Davis estimator, a smooth weighted average of the sorted values. Then subtract, decile by decile, the tail group minus the base group. A bar above zero says the tail group is slower at that decile, below zero faster.

How do we draw a bar? Resample each group with replacement, a thousand times, as in chapter 5. Recompute the nine differences each time. Their spread is the standard error. The bar is the difference, plus or minus a multiplier times that error.

Look. The first eight differences run from minus 13 to plus 66 milliseconds, and every bar crosses zero. The ninth decile is 184 milliseconds slower, with a bar from plus 38 to plus 331. Only the slowest responses changed. Welch's test gives p 0.020: it noticed that the mean moved by 37 milliseconds, but not where. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test gives 0.11: nothing.

Why not the usual 1.96 times the error? Because we read nine bars at once. With no real difference, at least one bar leaves zero in 24 percent of studies, nearly one in four. So the multiplier is larger, 2.73 here. We calibrated it by simulation, as Rousselet, Pernet and Wilcox describe: widen the bars until the false-alarm rate, across all nine, falls back to about 5 percent. Correcting many tests in general is the subject of chapter 15.

Now the same tool on our first sample. Every bar crosses zero, so it finds nothing here either. But the pattern is there: the tired group is slower in the lowest decile, faster in the middle by up to 36 milliseconds, and slower again near the top. In fresh samples of these two laws, at least one bar leaves zero in 81 percent of them.

This question, where do they differ, fits many places. Incomes: if only the top grows, eight deciles barely move, and the ninth jumps by 46.7 thousand dollars. Hospital waits: if only the longest change, the ninth decile moves by 18.6 days. Exam scores: if a tutoring programme helps the weakest pupils, the first two deciles gain 5.8 and 5.1 points, and the top gains nothing. A drug that helps only the most severe patients lowers the top decile of the severity score by 2.0 points. Rainfall: if only the extreme days get wetter, the ninth decile gains 7.4 millimetres. All simulated and illustrative. A comparison of means gives one number for each, and misses the story.

Remember: two groups can share a mean and a spread and still differ in shape, so compare the whole curves, and ask where they differ.

PLATE 7.77:09

More than two groups

Analysis of variance compares means by comparing variances: three wires of birds, the spread between the flocks' flags against the spread within each flock, giving F. Testing every pair of groups inflates false alarms, reaching 62.8 percent with ten groups. Kruskal-Wallis is the rank version. A ring says not all equal, never which ones.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the hardware store. On the map from 7.1, one column is left: more than two groups. In the centres row, that drawer holds two tools: the analysis of variance, and its rank version, the Kruskal-Wallis test. The first one compares means. The surprise is how.

Three fertilisers, five plots each, illustrative and simulated. Yield is counted in sacks. Not all equal would mean that at least one fertiliser changes the yield. Picture three wires, one per fertiliser. Each bird is one plot, perched at its yield.

Two spreads matter. The first is between the flocks. Put a flag on each flock's mean: 9.8, 11.6 and 13.4. Plant a pole at the grand mean of all fifteen birds: 11.6. The second spread is inside each flock: how far each bird sits from its own flag.

Square each flag's distance to the pole, and count it once per bird: that adds up to 32.4. Square each bird's distance to its own flag, and add them all: 49.2. Now divide each by its free pieces, as in 4.4. Three flags leave two. Fifteen birds around three flags leave twelve. The between spread is 16.2, the within spread is 4.1, and their ratio is F: 3.95.

This is the F of 4.4, a ratio of two variances. If the fertilisers did nothing, the flags would wobble only as much as the birds do, and F would sit near one. So the analysis of variance compares means by comparing variances. With only two groups, F is exactly the square of Student's t: the same test, in a larger coat.

Is 3.95 far? Ask chance, as in chapter 14. Startle the birds, and let them land again on random wires, keeping their yields. Compute F. Do it ten thousand times. Guess before you look: where does the pile sit? It huddles near one, its average is 1.2, and it has a long right tail. In 506 shuffles, F reaches 3.95. Counting our own arrangement, p is 0.051. Now lay the F curve of 4.4 over the pile. It fits, and its p is 0.048. The formula just rings, the shuffling just misses. Both say we are on the line, and neither is a verdict.

Now turn one knob. Three teaching methods, eight students each, exam scores, illustrative and simulated. Not all equal would mean that at least one method teaches differently. We draw the noise once, centre each group on its own mean, then place the flags by hand, so the spread inside the groups stays exactly the same: 9.6 points. Flags five points apart: F is 2.2, p is 0.14. Ten points apart: F is 8.6, p is 0.0018. Guess before you see. Double the gaps again: does F double, quadruple, or stay put? At twenty points, F is 34.6. Exactly four times, before rounding, because the gap enters squared. The birds did not move inside their flocks. Only the flags did.

Why not simply run a t-test on every pair? Take data where nothing is going on, twenty per group, and test every pair at 5 percent. Three groups make three pairs, and at least one rings in 12.9 percent of studies. Four groups, six pairs: 20.2. Five groups, ten pairs: 29.0. Ten groups, forty-five pairs: 62.8. The analysis of variance asks one question, once, and rings 5.2, 5.2, 4.8 and 4.6 percent of the time. Counting all those false alarms properly is chapter 15.

Now the rank version. Back to the fertilisers. Replace each yield by its place in line, as in 7.4, and let ties share the average place. The flags become mean places: 4.4, 8.5 and 11.1, around a pole at 8. Run the same between and within logic on places. Between: 114.1. Total: 273. Take between over total, multiply by fourteen, the number of birds minus one, and you get H: 5.85. Kruskal and Wallis published it in 1952. Against a chi-square curve with two degrees of freedom, p is 0.054. Shuffling the places gives 0.045. Five per group is small for the curve. And like Mann-Whitney in 7.4, the test compares centres only if the groups share the same shape.

Where do the two tests part ways? Delivery times from three warehouses, ten parcels each, in hours, simulated. Not all equal would mean that one warehouse is faster or slower. Here the means are 33.2, 35.9 and 52.2. The analysis of variance gives F 17.3, p below 0.0001. Kruskal-Wallis gives 0.0008. Now one parcel of the north warehouse is lost for twelve days: its 42.2 becomes 288 hours. The north mean jumps to 57.8, and the within spread explodes. F falls to 0.58, and p rises to 0.57. The ranks saw the parcel climb to last place, and stop. Kruskal-Wallis still says 0.004.

What does a ring mean? Pain relief under three doses, thirty patients each, simulated. Not all equal would mean that the dose matters. The placebo and the low dose are built equal, the high dose higher. The sample means are 1.85, 2.06 and 3.75. F is 11.5, and p is 0.00004. The test says: not all equal. It does not say which. To ask one precise question, such as the high dose against the other two, use a contrast: 9.5. To compare everything with everything, correct for the many tests: chapter 15. And when the spreads differ, Welch gave a version of the analysis of variance that does not pool them, in 1951.

Two more places. Four brands of batteries, twelve each, in hours: not all equal would mean that at least one brand lasts differently. F is 1.28, p is 0.29. Reaction times at three times of day, fifteen each: not all equal would mean that the time of day matters. F is 2.40, p is 0.10. Neither rings. All of it is illustrative.

Remember: the analysis of variance compares means by comparing variances, the spread of the flags against the spread of the birds. A ring says not all equal, never which ones. Next, in 8.1: counts against a model.
