Quality first · Should this channel exist?

Every channel must justify its existence. Starving, blinded and loose channels are three classic bad portraits, and numbers replace eyeballing: the coefficient of variation, the scalp coupling index and peak power, computed in short windows, with thresholds adapted to infants. Criteria are fixed before looking at results, applied to everyone, and every exclusion is reported, since removing a channel is a documented decision.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 4, Cleaning Up. Before we fix anything, we ask a blunt question about every channel — should it exist at all? One bad channel left in can quietly poison everything downstream — your averages, your statistics, your maps.

What does a bad channel look like? Three classic portraits. First, the starving channel — almost no light reaches the detector, and what you see is mostly electronic noise. Second, the blinded channel — so much light that the detector saturates, and the signal clips into a flat, unrealistically high line. Third, the loose channel, hair under the optode or a cap that lifted. The light leaks around instead of going through the head. No heartbeat sudden jumps a signal that wanders. And for comparison, a healthy channel, smooth with the heartbeat ticking away in the background.

Looking at every channel by eye works for a handful. With dozens of channels and dozens of participants, it becomes slow and subjective. So we use numbers.

Metric 1, the coefficient of variation. Take the raw light intensity, divide its standard deviation by its mean, and express it as a percentage. Noisy channels vary a lot relative to their level, so a high value is suspicious. It's simple and fast, but the threshold changes from study to study, and a flat, dead channel can sneak through precisely because it barely varies. So it needs careful tuning.

Metric 2, the scalp coupling index, which we met in the last chapter. Filter the signal around the heart frequencies and correlate the two wavelengths. If the optode touches the scalp, both wavelengths see the same pulse, and the correlation is high. Studies use thresholds around 0.7 to 0.8.

Metric 3, peak power. The correlation alone can be fooled by a tiny signal that happens to be clean. So we also check how strong the cardiac peak stands out in the spectrum. The approach introduced by Luca Pollonini and colleagues combines both. A channel counts as well-coupled only if it has a high scalp coupling index and enough peak power. And instead of one verdict for the whole recording, compute these metrics in short windows. You then get a map, channels against time, and you can see exactly when a channel went bad. Maybe right after the participant scratched their head.

A word about babies. Their hearts beat faster, roughly 1.3 to 3.2 times per second at rest, against about 1 to 1.7 in adults. Use adult filters and adult thresholds on infant data, and you may throw away perfectly good channels. So adapt your frequency band and your thresholds to the population you study.

Notice a pattern. Every threshold is a choice, and that choice is partly arbitrary. Newer methods use machine learning to detect bad channels from many features at once, and they may help. But whatever you use, follow two rules. Decide your criteria before you look at the results, ideally in a pre-registration. And apply exactly the same rules to every participant in every condition. A threshold you tune until your effect appears is not quality control. It's wishful thinking.

And now, the most important idea of this episode. Removing a channel is not a failure. It's a decision, and like every decision in your analysis, it should be visible. The community's best practice guidelines ask authors to say how signal quality was checked and how bad channels were rejected. So report the metric, the threshold, and how many channels and participants you excluded, and in which group. Set a rule in advance for participants who lose too many channels. And never patch a missing channel silently.

So, three portraits of a bad channel, three metrics, windows instead of one verdict, thresholds adapted to the population, chosen in advance, and every exclusion documented. Next time, the Motion Artifact Zoo.

PLATE 4.23:59

The motion artifact zoo

Motion artifacts come in four species: the spike, the baseline shift, the slow drift and the oscillator, each with its habitat such as frowning, head turns or chewing, and baseline shifts and drifts can mimic a response. Accelerometers and threshold-based detection help spot them, and shared channels and same-direction hemoglobin changes distinguish them from brain activity. Good cap fit and cable management prevent many.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the Zoo. Every fNIRS recording gets visitors, and most of them come from movement. Today is a field guide, how to recognize each species and where it lives. How to deal with them comes next time.

First, why does movement show up at all? When the head moves, the optodes can move relative to the scalp. The contact changes, the light path changes, and the detector sees it. Movement can also shift blood around in the skin and scalp, which changes how light is absorbed. fNIRS is more tolerant to movement than MRI or EEG, but it's not immune.

Species number one, the spike. A sudden brief jump, often huge, then straight back down. The optode moved quickly and returned to its place. Habitat, a frown, raised eyebrows under forehead optodes, a cough, a sneeze. Spikes are loud and easy to spot.

Species number two, the baseline shift. The signal jumps to a new level and stays there. The optode didn't come back, it settled into a new position. Habitat, turning the head and pulling on a cable, scratching under the cap, adjusting it. Baseline shifts are dangerous because a step in the middle of a task block can look like a sustained response.

Species number three, the slow drift. No jump, just a gentle slide over many seconds. An optode creeping along the scalp, a cap slowly loosening, a head leaning further and further against a headrest. This is the sneakiest species. It's slow, like the brain response, and it can even follow the rhythm of your task, which makes it hard to detect and hard to remove.

Species number four, the oscillator. A repeating wobble from a repeating movement. Nodding, chewing, talking with the jaw muscles right under the temporal optodes or even the rhythm of walking. If that rhythm comes close to your task frequency, it can blend straight into your results.

A note on the young. Children, and especially infants, move far more than adults, and their recordings are usually much noisier. Expect a bigger zoo.

So how do you spot the animals? By eye at first, but a motion sensor helps enormously. An accelerometer on the head records exactly when the head moved. Put its trace under your fNIRS signal and the culprit's lineup. Video works too.

Algorithms can do the patrol for you. A classic approach in the Homer software slides a short window along each channel, half a second by default. If the signal changes by more than a threshold within that window, either a multiple of its own standard deviation or a fixed amplitude, that moment is marked as an artifact, and a margin around it, one second on each side by default, is masked as well. As always, these thresholds are choices, and they should be reported.

Two clues help you tell an artifact from the brain. Motion often hits several channels at once, especially channels that share the optode that moved. And motion tends to push both wavelengths and both hemoglobins in the same direction, while a real activation draws the mirror image we met in Chapter 2. Keep that clue in mind, one of the correction methods in the next episode is built on it.

And the best artifact is the one that never happens. Fit the cap well. Manage the cables so their weight doesn't pull on the optodes, bundle them and support them behind the participant. Explain and rehearse, relax your forehead, keep your jaw still, don't talk during the blocks. design tasks that need as little movement as possible and plan breaks so people can move when it doesn't matter.

So, Spikes baseline shifts slow drifts and oscillations each with its habitat. Detect them with your eyes, an accelerometer, and well-documented thresholds. And prevent what you can. Next time, fixing motion. Five tools in one rule.

PLATE 4.35:54

Fixing motion · Five tools, one rule

Five repair tools are tried on the same segment: spline interpolation, wavelet filtering, TDDR, principal component analysis and CBSI, each with strengths and weaknesses. Studies show correction usually beats discarding trials, but no method wins everywhere. The one rule is to choose the method before seeing results, apply it to all, reject destroyed trials, and always plot before and after.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we built a field guide to motion artifacts. Today we fix them. Here is one recording with a spike, a baseline shift, and a slow bump right in the middle of the task. You have two options. Throw the damaged trials away or repair the signal. Let's meet five repair tools, try each one on the very same segment, and finish with the one rule that matters more than any of them.

One practical point first. Most of these tools work on optical density before the conversion to hemoglobin. That's where Homer and most pipelines place them because a motion artifact is born in the light path, not in the blood. The exception is CBSI, which needs both hemoglobins.

Tool number one, spline interpolation, proposed by Scholkmann and colleagues in 2010. It only works inside the segments that your detector marked as artifacts, the ones we learned to flag last episode. In each segment, it fits a smooth curve that follows the artifact, subtracts it, and then shifts the pieces so they line up with the signal around them. Splines are excellent on baseline shifts and slow bumps. Their weakness, they're only as good as your detection. Miss an artifact and the spline never sees it.

Tool number 2, Wavelet Filtering from Molavi and Dumont in 2012 The signal is broken down into little waves at many scales, from fast to slow. Most of the coefficients describe normal physiology and follow a tidy distribution. A motion artifact produces a handful of outliers. Set those outliers to zero, rebuild the signal, and the spike is gone. No detection step is needed. the outliers find themselves with a threshold based on the spread of the coefficients. Wavelets shine on spikes. Their weakness, they can also nibble at the response itself and make it smaller.

Tool number three, TDDR, Temporal Derivative Distribution Repair, from Fishburn and colleagues in 2019. Instead of the signal, look at how it changes from one sample to the next. Normal fluctuations are small and follow a bell curve. Motion produces rare, huge jumps far out in the tails. TDDR uses robust regression to give those jumps very little weight, shrinks them, and then adds everything back up. It handles spikes and baseline shifts, and it has no parameters to tune. In simulations, it beat five other methods, and in children aged 7 to 15, it gave stronger, more extensive activation. It's built into MNE in a single line.

Tool number four, principal component analysis. Motion often hits many channels at once, so it lives in the components that channels share. Remove the strongest components, and much of the motion goes with them. The risk, if the brain response is shared too, part of it goes as well. That's why Yücel and colleagues proposed targeted PCA in 2014, which applies the same idea only inside the flagged segments. In one study of young children, it outperformed splines.

Tool number five, CBSI correlation-based signal improvement from Cui, Bray, and Reiss in 2010. It uses the clue from last episode. In real activation, oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin move in opposite directions. Motion pushes them the same way. CBSI rebuilds both signals so that they are perfectly anticorrelated, which removes whatever moved together. It's fully automatic and it cleans spikes and shifts very well. But the assumption is strong. After CBSI, deoxyhemoglobin is just a scaled mirror of oxyhemoglobin. You can no longer study it on its own. And in some conditions, the mirror simply isn't true.

So which one wins? Brigadoi and colleagues compared five methods on real data from a speech task where talking caused slow artifacts at the same time as the brain response. That's the hardest case. Their conclusion, correcting was always better than throwing trials away, and wavelet filtering was the most effective. With infants, Di Lorenzo and colleagues found that combining spline and wavelet worked best and saved nearly all the corrupted trials. But another infant study found a trade-off. Correction kept more trials while often shrinking the response. Rejection kept the true shape at the price of losing many trials. The lesson, there is no universal winner. It depends on your artifacts, your population, and your design.

Which brings us to the rule. Choose your method before you look at your results. Write it in your pre-registration or your analysis plan with its parameters. repairs. Apply the same pipeline to every participant and every condition. What you must never do is try all five, and keep the one that gives you a significant effect. That's a garden of forking paths, and it produces false discoveries.

And sometimes, the right repair is no repair. If a trial is destroyed, reject it. If a channel is mostly motion, go back to episode 4.1 and drop it. Set your rejection criteria in advance too, and report how many trials you lost in each condition, because losing more in one condition is a bias of its own. Finally, always look. Plot the signal before and after correction. If the method did something you can't explain, don't trust it.

So, splines for shifts, wavelets for spikes, TDDR for both with nothing to tune, PCA when motion is shared, and CBSI when you trust the mirror. Correct rather than reject when you can, and choose before you look. Next time, taming the body's own noise. Heartbeat breathing Mayer waves in the scalp.

PLATE 4.45:00

Taming the body's own noise

Heartbeat, breathing, Mayer waves and slow drifts are the body's own noise, and some of it follows the task, especially in oxygenated hemoglobin. Filters remove what is far from the response but not Mayer waves; short channels record scalp noise alone and are regressed out, auxiliary signals add further regressors, and global components are a fallback. Short channels belong in the montage plan.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Motion is the noise you can see. Today it's the noise that comes from inside, the body's own orchestra. Your participants' heart, lungs, and blood vessels never stop playing, and fNIRS hears all of them.

Let's tune in one instrument at a time. The heartbeat around one hertz in adults. Breathing around a third of a hertz. Mayer waves, slow waves in blood pressure, around one-tenth of a hertz. And below that, very slow fluctuations, under about four hundredths of a hertz. Now add the music you actually came for, your task. With 20 seconds of task and 20 seconds of rest, it repeats every 40 seconds at 1 40th of a hertz.

And there's a twist. The orchestra doesn't only play on its own. It can follow your task. Effort and emotion change heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, and blood flow in the skin. On the forehead, one study found that skin blood flow changes could explain most of the signal during a verbal task. Another showed that scalp veins constrict during mental effort. And these processes affect oxygenated hemoglobin more strongly than deoxygenated. That's why we report both.

Tool number one, filtering. A low-pass filter removes what is faster than the response, like the heartbeat and most of breathing. A high-pass filter removes the slowest drifts. Together, they keep a band where the response lives. The rule, the high-pass cutoff must stay well below your task frequency. Cut too high and you remove your own signal. And filters have side effects. They can ring, they distort the edges, and they shift timing if they aren't applied in both directions.

How do people filter in practice? Pinti and colleagues reviewed 110 fNIRS papers published in 2016. Many didn't report key details of their filters, and the methods varied enormously. In their simulations, the best option before a general linear model was a high-order, band-pass filter. Whatever you choose, report the type, the order, and the cutoffs.

But filtering has a blind spot. Mayer waves sit around 0.1 Hz, right where many tasks and responses live. If your blocks last about 10 seconds, your task frequency lands right on top of them, and your statistics can be inflated. You can't filter them away without filtering away your brain. Yücel and colleagues showed that when Mayer waves are as large as the response itself, the estimated response becomes much less accurate. And in a study of over 300 recordings, Luke and colleagues found Mayer waves centered near 0.11 hertz, with some people carrying very large ones.

So we need a different trick. Tool number two, short channels. Remember the banana. A standard channel about 3 cm long sees the scalp and the brain. A short channel around 8 mm to a centimeter in adults barely reaches the brain. It sees mostly the scalp. So the short channel is a recording of the noise on its own. Put it in your model as a regressor and subtract what the two channels share. What remains is closer to the brain.

Today this is considered best practice. Two details matter. First, the scalp isn't uniform. Gagnon and colleagues showed that the benefit drops as the short channel gets further away from the long one. So ideally, every region gets its own short channel close by. Second, short channels also help against Mayer waves. Yücel and Luke both found that short channel correction reduces their impact.

Tool number three, listen to the musicians directly. Record heart rate with a pulse sensor, breathing with a belt, blood pressure, motion with an accelerometer, and even exhaled carbon dioxide. Any of these can become a nuisance regressor. von Lühmann and colleagues went further with temporally embedded canonical correlation analysis. It combines all the auxiliary signals into optimal regressors, allowing for delays, and it improved on short channel regression alone.

No short channels. There are fallbacks. If the noise is global, it shows up in every channel, so you can estimate a common component across channels and remove it. But remember Gagnon's result? Scalp physiology isn't the same everywhere, so a global estimate is only an approximation. And if a strong common component is time-locked to your task, removing it can remove real activation too. Plan short channels when you design your montage. It's much easier than fixing it afterwards.

So, the body plays heartbeat, breathing, Mayer waves, and slow drifts, and some of it follows your task. Filters handle what is far from your response. Short channels and auxiliary signals handle what overlaps with it. Report HbO and HbR and report every setting. Next time, one pipeline in the right order.

PLATE 4.54:25

One pipeline, in the right order

Order is part of the method: check quality on raw intensity, convert to optical density, correct motion, then filter, convert to hemoglobin with the modified Beer–Lambert law, and add systemic regressors. Filtering before motion correction smears spikes into slow bumps. A multi-team comparison agreed at group level but less for individuals, so the pipeline is written as code, documented and frozen in advance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We now have a toolbox, quality checks, motion correction, filters, short channels. Today, we put the tools in order, because in a pipeline, the order is part of the method.

Here is a sensible default. It's not the only valid order, but each step has a reason to be where it is.

Step 1. Look at the raw light intensities and check quality. This is where you compute the scalp coupling index, the coefficient of variation, and you mark the channels that shouldn't exist, as we saw in episode 4.1. These metrics are defined on the raw intensity, so they come first.

Step 2. Convert intensity to optical density. Each sample is compared to the average level on a log scale. Why so early? Because optical density is where light behaves linearly, and it's the space most correction methods were designed for. In one infant study, running the same steps on raw intensities gave smaller, more irregular responses than running them on optical density or concentration.

Step 3. Motion. Detect it then correct it or mark trials for rejection. And do this before filtering. Here's why. A spike is short and tall. Pass it through a low-pass filter and it becomes long and flat. It spreads over neighboring seconds. It may no longer cross your detection threshold. and it now looks a lot more like a slow, brain-like bump. Filtering first hides the artifact without removing it.

Step 4. Filter A band pass or just a high pass, with cutoffs you justify from your task frequency. Or, if your analysis is a general linear model, you can model slow drifts with regressors instead. But don't do both blindly, and never filter the same data twice without noticing.

Step 5. Convert optical density to hemoglobin with the modified Beer-Lambert law, using a differential path length factor that suits your participants' age and wavelengths. Here's a neat detail, this conversion is linear. So linear filtering before or after it gives the same result. That's not true for motion correction, which is non-linear. And CBSI, remember, needs both hemoglobins so it can only come after this step.

Step 6. Systemic Physiology. Short channels and auxiliary signals usually enter as regressors in the general linear model, together with your task. That's where Chapter 5 begins.

A few traps along the way. If you downsample, filter first, or fast signals like the heartbeat fold back into your slow band. Keep the raw data untouched and always work on copies and plot the signal after every step. A pipeline that only shows you the final result is a pipeline that hides its mistakes.

Now, why does all this matter so much? In the FRESH initiative, 38 research teams around the world analyzed the same two fNIRS datasets, each with their own pipeline. Nearly 80% agreed on the group-level results, especially when the hypothesis was well supported by the literature. But agreement was lower for individual participants. And the main sources of disagreement were how teams handled poor quality data, how they modeled the response, and how they ran their statistics. More experienced teams agreed more.

So document everything. Write your pipeline as code, not as a sequence of clicks. Record the software and its version, every parameter, and every channel or trial you excluded with the reason. Share the code with the paper. Store your derivatives in a standard structure, like the NIRS BIDS format from Chapter 3. Good tools help Homer 3 in MATLAB, the NIRS Brain AnalyzIR Toolbox, MNE-NIRS and Cedalion in Python. They all let you save the whole chain as a script.

And for a new study, decide the pipeline before you see the data. Test it on pilot data or on a public dataset then freeze it and pre-register it.

So, quality, optical density, motion, filtering, hemoglobin, then systemic regression. Each step in its place, every setting written down, and the pipeline frozen before the results. That closes chapter 4. Next, chapter 5, from clean signals to answers. We start with the simplest one, averaging blocks.
