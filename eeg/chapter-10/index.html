Volume conduction first

Every electrode records a blurred mixture of sources, so one source seen by two electrodes gives perfect zero-delay correlation without any communication, like two people on a street lit by the same lamp. A shared reference adds more common activity. Because volume conduction is instantaneous, measures that ignore zero delay help, as do source space and Laplacians, but none solves it fully.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter ten: connectivity. Before any measure, we start with the problem that haunts all of them. Volume conduction.

The question of connectivity sounds simple: do two brain regions talk to each other? In EEG, we try to answer it by comparing signals: if two channels rise and fall together, perhaps the regions under them are communicating.

But remember chapter one. The electric field of a single source spreads through the brain, the skull and the scalp, almost instantly. Every electrode records a blurred mixture of many sources.

Picture a city at night, seen from a hill. One very bright window lights up the street below. Two people standing on that street, a few metres apart, both see their faces brighten and dim at exactly the same moment. They are perfectly synchronised. Not because they are talking, but because they are lit by the same lamp.

That is volume conduction. Take one single source, with no partner at all. Place two electrodes near it. Both record the same activity, scaled by how close they are. The two signals are strongly correlated. They are exactly in phase, with zero delay. And yet there is only one source, and no communication.

Now add a second source, truly independent of the first. Every electrode records some of both. Channels that sit between them share both sources. Their correlation reflects how much of each source they pick up, not whether the sources interact.

This is why EEG connectivity between neighbouring electrodes is dominated by volume conduction. Nunez and colleagues showed in the late nineteen-nineties that coherence between scalp electrodes is high at short distances, largely because of this spread.

There is a second trap: the reference. Every channel is measured relative to the same reference electrode, as we saw in chapter three. Any activity at the reference appears, with the same sign, in every channel. That shared part creates correlations between all channels, again without any brain interaction.

So what does volume conduction look like, mathematically? Three properties. It is instantaneous: no delay between the source and the electrodes. It is linear: each electrode is a weighted sum of the sources. And it affects every frequency in the same way.

The first property is the key to the rest of the chapter. A real interaction between two regions takes time: signals travel along axons, through synapses, with delays of several milliseconds or more. Volume conduction has no delay at all. So one family of measures, which we meet in episodes ten point four to ten point seven, ignores everything that happens with exactly zero delay.

The price: true interactions with zero delay exist too, for example when two regions are both driven by a third. Measures that ignore zero delay also ignore those.

Other strategies help as well. Moving to source space, in chapter thirteen, reduces the mixing, though spatial leakage remains. The surface Laplacian, from episode three point thirteen, sharpens the spatial resolution and reduces the spread between nearby channels. And comparing conditions, rather than interpreting raw connectivity values, cancels the parts that stay the same.

But no method makes the problem disappear. When you read an EEG connectivity result, the first question to ask is always: could one source, seen twice, explain this?

Bastos and Schoffelen wrote an excellent tutorial in 2016 on these interpretational pitfalls. It is a good companion to this chapter.

To recap. Connectivity asks whether two regions interact, but every electrode records a mixture of sources. One source seen by two electrodes creates perfect, zero-delay correlation without any communication. The reference adds shared activity to every channel. Volume conduction is instantaneous, linear and frequency-independent. Real interactions usually involve delays, which some measures exploit. And source space, Laplacians and condition contrasts help, but never fully solve it.

PLATE 10.23:46

A map of connectivity measures

Connectivity measures sit on a map defined by three questions: amplitude, phase or both; directed or not; sensitive to zero delay or not. Coherence, the phase locking value and plain envelope correlation count zero-delay relations, while imaginary coherence, the phase lag index family and orthogonalized envelopes ignore them, and Granger-type measures give direction. No measure is best; the question and the data decide.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A map of connectivity measures. Before we meet them one by one, let's see where each measure lives.

There are dozens of connectivity measures, and their names can feel like a maze. But most of them can be placed on a simple map, with a few questions.

Think of a city map at night. Districts are grouped by what they are for. Once you know the districts, every street name makes more sense.

Question one: what part of the signal does the measure use? Amplitude, phase, or both?

Amplitude measures ask whether two signals get louder and quieter together. The amplitude envelope correlation is the main example. Phase measures ask whether two oscillations keep a consistent timing relationship, whatever their size. The phase locking value and the phase lag index are examples. Coherence uses both: amplitude and phase are mixed together.

Question two: is the measure directed? Undirected measures say that two signals are related, but not who drives whom. Directed measures, like Granger causality and its frequency-domain cousins, try to say which signal predicts the other.

Question three: is the measure sensitive to zero delay? As we saw last episode, volume conduction creates relationships with exactly zero delay. Some measures count those relationships fully: coherence, the phase locking value, the plain amplitude envelope correlation. Others are built to ignore them: the imaginary part of coherency, the phase lag index and its weighted version, and the amplitude correlation after orthogonalization.

Put these three questions together, and the map appears.

In the phase district, sensitive to zero delay: the phase locking value. Insensitive: the phase lag index, and the weighted phase lag index.

In the mixed district: coherence, which is sensitive, and imaginary coherence, which is not.

In the amplitude district: the amplitude envelope correlation, and its orthogonalized version, which is not sensitive to zero delay.

And across the river, the directed district: Granger causality, the partial directed coherence and the directed transfer function.

The rest of this chapter walks through these districts in order: coherence, imaginary coherence, then the phase measures, the amplitude measures, and finally the directed ones. After that, we look at the problems all of them share: bias, sample size, and graph analysis.

In Python, the mne-connectivity package computes most of these measures with one function. For epochs, you choose a method by name: coh for coherence, imcoh for imaginary coherence, plv, pli, wpli, and the debiased weighted phase lag index, among others.

One message to keep from this map: there is no best measure. There is a good measure for a question and for the data you have. A question about timing calls for a phase measure. A question about co-activation calls for an amplitude measure. Sensor-space data call for measures that resist volume conduction. And a question about direction needs a directed measure, with all its caveats.

To recap. Connectivity measures can be placed on a map with three questions: amplitude, phase or both; directed or not; sensitive to zero delay or not. Coherence mixes amplitude and phase. Phase measures include the phase locking value and the phase lag index family. Amplitude measures include envelope correlation. Directed measures include Granger causality. And the right measure depends on the question and the data.

Next time: coherence.

PLATE 10.34:02

Coherence

Coherence averages the cross-spectrum arrows (one channel times the conjugate of the other) across epochs and normalizes by the power spectra, giving a value from zero to one; the complex version, coherency, also carries the mean phase difference. It mixes amplitude and phase, is fully sensitive to volume conduction and the reference, and is biased by epoch count.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Coherence. The oldest and most widely used connectivity measure in EEG. It asks: at a given frequency, do two signals keep a consistent relationship across epochs?

Let's build it step by step, with the complex arrows from chapter two.

Step one: take one epoch, and compute the Fourier transform of both channels, X and Y. At ten hertz, each channel gives a complex number: an arrow with an amplitude and a phase.

Step two: multiply X by the complex conjugate of Y. The result is another arrow. Its length is the product of the two amplitudes. Its angle is the phase difference between the two channels. This is the cross-spectrum for that epoch.

Step three: do this for every epoch, and average the arrows, head to tail.

If the phase difference is the same in every epoch, the arrows point the same way, and their average is long. If the phase difference changes randomly from epoch to epoch, the arrows point everywhere, and their average is short.

Step four: normalize. Divide the length of the average arrow by the square root of the product of the two power spectra. This removes the overall size of the signals, and gives a number between zero and one. That number is the coherence. Some authors square it, and call it the magnitude-squared coherence; check which one a paper reports.

Think of two street lamps in the city at night, both flickering. If one always flickers a little after the other, by the same delay, they are coherent at that flicker rate, whatever their brightness.

Before taking the length, the normalized average arrow is called the coherency. It is a complex number: its length is the coherence, and its angle is the average phase difference. Keep that in mind for the next episode.

Now, what is coherence sensitive to?

First, it mixes amplitude and phase. Epochs with large amplitudes contribute longer arrows, and weigh more in the average. So coherence can change because amplitudes change, even if the phase relationship stays the same.

Second, it is fully sensitive to volume conduction. One source seen by two electrodes produces arrows that all point along the real axis, with zero phase difference. They all line up, and coherence is high. Between neighbouring EEG channels, coherence is often high for exactly this reason.

Third, it is biased by the number of epochs. With few epochs, even random arrows do not cancel completely, so coherence is never exactly zero. We look at this in episode ten point ten.

And fourth, the reference. A shared reference adds the same signal to both channels, and with it, zero-lag coherence.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports high alpha coherence between two neighbouring frontal electrodes in one group, and interprets it as stronger communication between the two regions. But alpha power was also higher in that group. Stronger alpha means a stronger common source, seen by both electrodes. The coherence may simply reflect that.

How to use coherence well? Compare conditions with the same number of epochs. Consider the Laplacian or source space to reduce spread. Check whether power differs between conditions. And if volume conduction is a concern, look at the imaginary part, which is the next episode.

In Python, mne-connectivity computes coherence with spectral connectivity epochs, method coh.

To recap. Coherence averages the cross-spectrum arrows across epochs, then normalizes by the power spectra. It runs from zero to one. Its complex version, coherency, also carries the average phase difference. Coherence mixes amplitude and phase, is fully sensitive to volume conduction and to the reference, and is biased by the number of epochs.

Next time: imaginary coherence.

PLATE 10.44:06

Imaginary coherence

Volume conduction puts the cross-spectrum arrow on the real axis, while a delayed interaction pushes it off the axis. Imaginary coherence keeps only that imaginary part of the coherency, and is zero on average for instantaneous mixtures of independent sources. The cost is that it misses true zero-delay interactions, depends on the phase difference, still weighs amplitude, and can be fooled by source leakage.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Imaginary coherence. A simple, clever idea: ignore everything that happens with zero delay.

Last time, we built the coherency: the average cross-spectrum arrow, normalized. It is a complex number, which we can draw in the complex plane from chapter two, with a real axis and an imaginary axis.

Now ask: where does volume conduction put its arrows?

One source seen by two electrodes gives exactly the same signal in both, scaled. The phase difference is zero, or one hundred and eighty degrees if the scaling is negative. In the complex plane, the cross-spectrum arrow lies exactly on the real axis. Every epoch, the same: on the real axis.

A real interaction between two regions, with a delay, gives a phase difference that is neither zero nor one hundred and eighty degrees. Its arrow points off the real axis. It has an imaginary part.

Think of two people talking across a busy street at night. The light from a passing car reaches both faces at the same instant: that is the real axis. Their conversation has a rhythm: one speaks, the other answers a moment later. That delay is what moves the arrow off the axis.

So Guido Nolte and colleagues proposed, in 2004, to keep only the imaginary part of the coherency. This is the imaginary coherence, or ImCoh.

Here is the key property they showed. If the channels are mixtures of independent sources, through instantaneous volume conduction, the imaginary part of their coherency is zero, on average. However strong the mixing. However close the electrodes. So a non-zero imaginary part cannot be explained by volume conduction of independent sources alone. Something with a delay must be going on.

Let's see it with arrows. A simulation with a single common source: all the arrows lie on the real axis, coherence is high, imaginary coherence is zero. Now a simulation with two sources that truly interact with a delay: the arrows lean off the axis, and imaginary coherence becomes clearly non-zero.

Now the price.

First, true interactions with zero delay are ignored too. If two regions oscillate exactly in phase because they are both driven by a third, imaginary coherence will not see them. It is a conservative measure: it misses some real effects in order to avoid false ones.

Second, the value depends on the phase difference. An interaction with a phase difference close to ninety degrees gives a large imaginary part. The same interaction with a phase difference close to zero gives a small one. So imaginary coherence mixes the strength of the interaction with its phase. Two conditions can differ in imaginary coherence because their phase difference changed, not their coupling.

Third, like coherence, it still mixes amplitude with phase: high-amplitude epochs weigh more.

Fourth, it can have either sign. The sign tells you which channel leads, on average, at that frequency, but many analyses report the absolute value.

And finally, in source space, a real interaction between two sources can leak into nearby sources, creating "ghost" interactions that also have an imaginary part. Palva and colleagues described this problem in 2018. We return to it in chapter thirteen.

In Python, mne-connectivity computes it with method imcoh.

To recap. In the complex plane, volume conduction puts the cross-spectrum on the real axis. Imaginary coherence keeps only the off-axis part. Nolte and colleagues showed that it is zero, on average, for instantaneous mixtures of independent sources. It ignores true zero-delay interactions, depends on the phase difference, still weighs by amplitude, and can be fooled by source leakage.

Next time: the phase locking value.

PLATE 10.53:41

PLV · Phase locking value

The phase locking value draws a unit arrow for the phase difference between two channels on each epoch and averages them: the length measures timing consistency, whatever the amplitude. It is fully sensitive to volume conduction and biased by epoch count, with noise near 0.9 over the square root of epochs. Pairwise phase consistency removes the bias, and a corrected imaginary version reduces zero-lag sensitivity.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The phase locking value, or P-L-V. Like coherence, but with amplitude taken out of the picture.

Coherence mixes amplitude and phase: loud epochs count more. Sometimes we only care about timing. Do two oscillations keep the same phase relationship, whatever their size? The phase locking value, proposed by Jean-Philippe Lachaux and colleagues in 1999, answers exactly that.

Here is the computation. At a given frequency, on each epoch, take the phase of channel X and the phase of channel Y. Compute their difference. Draw it as an arrow of length one, pointing in the direction of that phase difference. One unit arrow per epoch.

Average the arrows, head to tail. The P-L-V is the length of that average. One means the phase difference was identical in every epoch. Zero means it was completely random.

If this sounds familiar, it is the same construction as the inter-trial coherence from episode nine point four. There, we measured the consistency of one channel's phase across trials. Here, we measure the consistency of the phase difference between two channels.

Picture two neon signs blinking across a street at night. They do not need to be equally bright. If one always blinks a quarter-cycle after the other, night after night, their phase locking is high.

Because every arrow has length one, the P-L-V ignores amplitude. A weak epoch counts as much as a strong one. This makes it a pure measure of phase consistency.

But two old problems remain.

Problem one: volume conduction. One source seen by two electrodes gives a phase difference of zero in every epoch. All the arrows point the same way, and the P-L-V is one, or close to it. The P-L-V is fully sensitive to zero-delay relationships, just like coherence.

Problem two: the number of epochs. With few epochs, random arrows rarely cancel. The P-L-V of pure noise is about zero point nine divided by the square root of the number of epochs: around zero point three with ten epochs, zero point one with a hundred. So two conditions with different numbers of epochs cannot be compared directly.

There are fixes for each problem.

For the bias, Martin Vinck and colleagues proposed in 2010 the pairwise phase consistency, or P-P-C. Instead of averaging all arrows at once, it compares every pair of epochs, and it is unbiased by the number of epochs: for random phases, its expected value is zero.

For volume conduction, a corrected imaginary P-L-V was proposed by Bruña and colleagues in 2018. It keeps only the imaginary part of the averaged phase-difference arrow, like imaginary coherence does, and corrects for the real part.

And a practical caution: the P-L-V needs a meaningful phase. Where there is little power at a frequency, the phase is mostly noise, and the P-L-V tells you little.

In Python, mne-connectivity offers plv, ppc and ciplv as methods.

To recap. The P-L-V is the length of the average unit arrow of the phase difference across epochs. It ignores amplitude. It is fully sensitive to volume conduction and biased by the number of epochs. The P-P-C removes the bias, and the corrected imaginary P-L-V reduces sensitivity to zero-delay mixing. And phase is only meaningful where there is power.

Next time: the phase lag index.

PLATE 10.63:36

PLI · Phase lag index

The phase lag index keeps only the sign of the phase difference: is one channel ahead or behind, above or below the real axis. The absolute value of the average sign across epochs is the index. Zero-lag mixing contributes nothing, but true zero-lag interactions are ignored too, and small phase differences flip their vote with tiny noise, making it unstable and biased by epoch count.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The phase lag index, or P-L-I. A measure that asks only one question: who is ahead?

The phase locking value counts every consistent phase difference, including zero, which is exactly what volume conduction produces. Cornelis Stam and colleagues proposed in 2007 a radical simplification: forget the size of the phase difference, keep only its sign.

Here is the idea. On each epoch, compute the phase difference between channel X and channel Y, at a given frequency. Is X ahead of Y, or behind? In the complex plane, that is the question: is the phase difference above the real axis, or below it?

Give a plus one if it is above, a minus one if it is below. Average these signs across epochs, and take the absolute value. That is the P-L-I.

If X is consistently ahead, almost all signs are plus one: the P-L-I is close to one. If X is consistently behind: close to one too. If X is ahead in half the epochs and behind in the other half, the signs cancel: the P-L-I is close to zero.

Picture two cars driving through the city at night. You do not measure how far apart they are. You only note, at each crossroads, which one passes first. If it is always the same car, there is a consistent lead.

Now, what happens with volume conduction? One source seen by two electrodes gives a phase difference of exactly zero. Zero is neither above nor below the axis. Those epochs contribute nothing to a consistent lead or lag. So pure zero-delay mixing does not create a P-L-I. That is the point of the measure.

Like the imaginary coherence, the P-L-I also ignores true interactions with zero delay. It is a conservative measure.

But the P-L-I has a weakness of its own: discontinuity.

Imagine a real interaction with a small phase difference, say five degrees. Most epochs land just above the real axis: plus one. But noise scatters the phase difference a little. Whenever noise pushes an epoch just below the axis, its vote flips from plus one to minus one. A tiny change in phase causes the maximum possible change in the vote.

So with small phase differences and some noise, the P-L-I becomes unstable and loses sensitivity. Its value can change a lot with a little extra noise. And when volume conduction adds a large zero-lag component to a small real lag, the phase differences cluster right around the axis, where the votes flip most easily.

The fix is to stop treating all votes equally: let epochs far from the axis count more, and epochs near the axis count less. That is the weighted phase lag index, in the next episode.

The P-L-I is also biased by the number of epochs: with few epochs, random signs rarely cancel perfectly.

In Python, mne-connectivity offers it as method pli.

To recap. The P-L-I keeps only the sign of the phase difference: ahead or behind. It is the absolute value of the average sign across epochs. Zero-lag mixing does not create a P-L-I, but true zero-lag interactions are ignored too. Small phase differences near the axis flip their votes with tiny amounts of noise, which makes the P-L-I unstable. And it is biased by the number of epochs.

Next time: the weighted phase lag index, and its debiased version.

PLATE 10.74:08

wPLI and debiased wPLI

The weighted phase lag index weights each epoch's sign vote by the size of the imaginary part of its cross-spectrum, so epochs near the real axis barely count and noise matters less. Zero-lag mixing still contributes nothing, but amplitude weighting and epoch-count bias return, so a debiased version suits comparisons. In simulation, it alone ignores a false common-source link and detects a true lagged one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The weighted phase lag index, and its debiased version. The fix for the phase lag index's shaky votes.

Last time, the phase lag index gave each epoch one vote: plus one if the phase difference sits above the real axis, minus one if below. Epochs just next to the axis flipped their vote with the slightest noise.

Martin Vinck and colleagues proposed in 2011 a simple refinement: weight each vote by how far the arrow is from the axis. In other words, by the size of the imaginary part of the cross-spectrum.

Here is the computation. On each epoch, compute the cross-spectrum between the two channels, as for coherence. Take its imaginary part: positive above the axis, negative below, and large when the arrow is far from the axis. Average the imaginary parts across epochs. Divide by the average of their absolute values. Take the absolute value of the result. That is the weighted phase lag index, or w-P-L-I.

What does the weighting do? An epoch whose arrow lies almost on the real axis has a tiny imaginary part. It barely counts, so if noise flips it to the other side, almost nothing changes. An epoch whose arrow is far from the axis counts a lot. The measure becomes much more robust to noise and to small amounts of volume conduction.

Picture a city vote where each neighbourhood votes with a weight equal to how sure it is. Neighbourhoods that hesitate, right on the boundary, barely move the result. Neighbourhoods that are clearly on one side carry it.

The w-P-L-I keeps the main virtue of the phase lag index: pure zero-delay mixing, with arrows on the real axis, has zero imaginary part and contributes nothing.

It also has a price: it is no longer purely a phase measure. The imaginary part grows with amplitude, so high-amplitude epochs count more, as in coherence.

And like the others, it is biased by the number of epochs. So Vinck and colleagues also proposed a debiased version, which estimates the squared w-P-L-I in a way whose expected value is not inflated by having few epochs. When comparing conditions or groups with different numbers of epochs, the debiased version is the safer choice.

Now, the moment of truth. Let's run three measures on two simulations.

Simulation one: a single common source, seen by two electrodes, plus independent noise. No interaction at all. The phase locking value is high: it sees the zero-lag relationship. The phase lag index is low. The weighted phase lag index is low too: neither is fooled by the zero-lag relationship.

Simulation two: two sources that truly interact with a delay, plus the same common source on top. The phase locking value is high again, but it was already high without the interaction, so it cannot tell the two simulations apart. The phase lag index rises, but noisily. The weighted phase lag index rises clearly.

Only the w-P-L-I ignores the false interaction and clearly detects the true one, in this illustrative case. That does not make it perfect: it still ignores true zero-lag interactions, and in source space it can still be fooled by leakage. But in sensor space, it is a strong default.

In Python, mne-connectivity offers wpli and wpli2 debiased as methods.

To recap. The w-P-L-I weights each epoch's vote by the size of its imaginary cross-spectrum. Votes near the axis barely count, which makes the measure robust to noise. Zero-lag mixing still contributes nothing. It weighs by amplitude, and it is biased by the number of epochs, so use the debiased version to compare conditions. In simulations with a common source and a true lagged interaction, it ignores the false one and detects the true one most clearly.

Next time: amplitude envelope correlation and orthogonalization.

PLATE 10.83:50

Amplitude envelope correlation and orthogonalization

Amplitude envelope correlation asks whether two regions get louder and quieter together: band-pass filter, take the Hilbert envelope of each signal, and correlate the envelopes, like two buildings filling and emptying their lit windows. Volume conduction inflates it. Orthogonalization keeps only the part of one signal perpendicular to the other, removing instantaneous leakage and true zero-lag interactions alike.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Amplitude envelope correlation, and orthogonalization. A different kind of connectivity: not "do they oscillate together?", but "do they get louder together?".

So far, our measures looked at phase: the timing of each cycle. But oscillations also wax and wane in strength over hundreds of milliseconds or seconds. Two regions may increase and decrease their alpha power together, even if their individual cycles are not phase-locked.

Here is the computation. Band-pass filter both signals, for example in the alpha band. Use the Hilbert transform, from episode three point ten, to get the amplitude envelope of each: a smooth line that follows the size of the oscillation over time. Then compute the correlation between the two envelopes, often after taking their logarithm or squaring them. That is the amplitude envelope correlation, or A-E-C.

Picture two buildings in the city at night. You do not care when each window flickers. You only look at how many windows are lit in each building, over the evening. If both buildings fill up and empty out together, their activity is correlated.

Amplitude and phase connectivity answer different questions. Phase connectivity asks whether two regions oscillate together, cycle by cycle. Amplitude connectivity asks whether they switch on and off together, over longer time scales. A pair of regions can show one without the other.

But there is a problem: volume conduction again. If one source is seen by two electrodes, both envelopes rise and fall together, perfectly. The A-E-C is high, without any interaction.

The fix is called orthogonalization. Joerg Hipp and colleagues, and Matthew Brookes and colleagues, both proposed versions of it in 2012.

Here is the idea, with arrows in the complex plane. At each moment, each signal is a complex arrow. Any part of signal Y that points in exactly the same direction as signal X, or the opposite direction, could come from the same source, seen with zero delay. So we remove that part. We keep only the component of Y that is perpendicular, orthogonal, to X. Then we compute the envelope of what is left, and correlate it with the envelope of X.

Because instantaneous mixing only produces parallel components, removing them removes the zero-lag leakage. Hipp's version does this at every moment, in both directions, and averages the two results.

The price is familiar by now. True interactions with zero delay are removed as well. And orthogonalization removes only the instantaneous, linear part of the leakage: it does not fix everything, especially when the leakage involves more than two sources at once. Brookes and colleagues also proposed multivariate versions that orthogonalize against several signals together.

A-E-C is particularly popular in source-space studies of resting-state networks, where slow envelope fluctuations of alpha and beta reveal large-scale networks that resemble those seen with f-M-R-I.

In Python, mne-connectivity has an envelope correlation function, with an option to orthogonalize pairwise.

To recap. The amplitude envelope correlation correlates the slow envelopes of two band-limited signals. It asks whether regions switch on and off together, a different question from phase synchrony. Volume conduction inflates it. Orthogonalization removes the part of one signal that is parallel to the other, which removes instantaneous leakage, along with true zero-lag interactions.

Next time: Granger causality, P-D-C and D-T-F.

PLATE 10.94:36

Granger causality, PDC and DTF

Granger causality asks whether the past of one signal improves the prediction of another beyond its own past; it measures predictability, not physical causation, as a hidden timer could drive two lights. A multivariate autoregressive model yields frequency-resolved measures: the directed transfer function shows total flow including indirect paths, partial directed coherence only direct links. Mixing can create spurious directions, which time-reversed testing helps expose.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Granger causality, P-D-C and D-T-F. Can we tell who is talking to whom?

All our measures so far were symmetric. The connection from A to B had the same value as the connection from B to A. But many questions are about direction. Does the frontal cortex drive the visual cortex, or the other way around?

The most common answer starts with an idea from economics. In 1969, Clive Granger proposed a simple test. Signal X "Granger-causes" signal Y if the past of X helps predict the future of Y, better than the past of Y alone.

Here is how it works in practice. First, predict Y from its own past, using a linear model. Then predict Y from its own past and the past of X. If adding X makes the prediction errors clearly smaller, X carries information about Y's future. The log ratio of the two error variances is the Granger score.

Picture a detective at night, watching two streetlights. If the second light reliably changes a moment after the first one, the first light helps you predict the second. But that does not prove that one light controls the other. A hidden timer could drive both, with different delays.

This is the key point. Granger causality is about prediction, not about physical causation. That is why careful papers say "Granger-causal", not simply "causal".

Granger's test can also be moved into the frequency domain. John Geweke showed how to split the Granger score by frequency in 1982. Now we can ask whether the influence from X to Y is carried mainly in the alpha band, or in the beta band.

With many channels, we fit a single model to all of them at once: a multivariate autoregressive model, or M-V-A-R. Two popular frequency measures come from this model.

The directed transfer function, or D-T-F, was proposed by Maciej Kaminski and Katarzyna Blinowska in 1991. It measures the total flow from one channel to another, including indirect paths. If A drives B, and B drives C, D-T-F will show a flow from A to C as well.

Partial directed coherence, or P-D-C, was proposed by Luiz Baccala and Koichi Sameshima in 2001. It focuses on direct influences only. In the same chain, P-D-C shows A to B, and B to C, but not A to C.

So the two are complementary. D-T-F answers "where does the influence end up?". P-D-C answers "who talks directly to whom?".

These methods have practical requirements. The model order, the number of past samples used, must be chosen with care, often with an information criterion. The data should be stationary within the window. And you need enough samples to estimate many parameters: the number grows with the square of the number of channels.

And now the big trap: volume conduction. Stefan Haufe and colleagues ran a realistic simulation study in 2013. They found that Granger-type measures, combined with standard significance tests, can detect spurious directed connectivity even with no true interaction. Mixing creates small asymmetries, for example when one channel has a better signal-to-noise ratio than another, and the model reads these as direction.

Their proposed fix is elegant. Reverse the data in time. A true time-lagged interaction should flip direction when time runs backwards. The artifacts of instantaneous mixing do not. So you compare the Granger score on the original data with the score on the time-reversed data, and keep only the difference.

In Python, mne-connectivity includes Granger causality in its spectral connectivity functions, along with a time-reversed version. The phase slope index, from Guido Nolte and colleagues in 2008, is another directed measure, which is robust to instantaneous mixing.

To recap. Granger causality asks whether the past of X improves the prediction of Y. It measures predictability, not physical causation. Geweke's decomposition gives it a frequency profile. D-T-F shows total flow, including indirect paths. P-D-C shows direct links only. And volume conduction can create spurious directions, so use source space, time-reversed testing, or robust measures.

Bias, sample size and surrogates

Connectivity values are rarely zero by chance: independent signals give inflated phase locking with few trials, so unequal trial counts alone can make one condition look more connected. Remedies are equalizing trials and unbiased measures such as pairwise phase consistency. Surrogates (trial shuffling, time shifting, phase randomization) each destroy one property to build a null, and condition contrasts cancel shared biases.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Bias, and surrogate data. Is that connectivity value actually different from nothing?

You computed a connectivity value. Say, a phase locking value of zero point three. Is that a lot? The honest answer is: it depends on what you would get by chance. And chance, for connectivity measures, is rarely zero.

Start with bias. Take two completely independent signals. Compute their phase locking value over only ten trials. The random phase differences will not cancel perfectly, and the value will be well above zero. With a thousand trials, it will be close to zero. So the same measure, on the same kind of data, gives different values just because the number of trials changed.

Picture the city at night. Ten random windows lit up can look like a pattern. A thousand random windows look like noise. Few observations make chance look like structure.

This creates a classic trap. If condition A has eighty trials and condition B has forty, B will show higher connectivity, even if nothing differs in the brain. The first fix is simple: equalize the number of trials across conditions before you compare them. M N E has a function for that, called equalize event counts.

The second fix is to use a measure that is unbiased by design. The pairwise phase consistency, from Martin Vinck and colleagues in 2010, does not depend on the number of trials in expectation. The debiased weighted phase lag index, from episode ten point seven, follows the same logic.

There is a second, sneakier bias: signal strength. Phase is estimated more precisely when the oscillation is strong. If power increases in one condition, phase estimates become cleaner, and phase-based connectivity can rise, even if the true coupling did not change. Suresh Muthukumaraswamy and Krish Singh wrote a cautionary note about this in 2011. So always check power alongside connectivity.

Now, surrogates. A surrogate is a fake version of your data, built to keep some properties and destroy exactly the one you want to test.

Trial shuffling is the simplest. Pair channel A from trial one with channel B from trial seven, and so on. Each signal keeps its own shape, its spectrum and its response to the stimulus. But the trial-by-trial relationship between them is broken. Repeat this many times, and you get a distribution of connectivity values under the null hypothesis.

Time shifting works within continuous data. Shift one signal by a random amount, larger than its autocorrelation, and recompute.

Phase randomization, described by James Theiler and colleagues in 1992, keeps the power spectrum of each signal but scrambles its phases. It is useful to test whether an apparent coupling could arise from the spectra alone.

Each surrogate answers a specific question. Shuffling trials removes the trial-by-trial link but keeps stimulus-locked responses, so when both areas respond to the stimulus, the surrogates themselves look coupled: the test then asks about coupling beyond what the stimulus explains. If instead you want connectivity values free of that shared evoked part, subtract the average evoked response from each trial first, or compare against a baseline period.

Finally, the comparison that matters most is usually not "different from zero", but "different between conditions", or "different from baseline". Comparing condition A with condition B, with equal trial counts, cancels many biases that affect both in the same way. Volume conduction, for example, is similar across conditions, so the difference is more interpretable than the raw value.

To recap. Many connectivity measures are positively biased with few trials. Equalize trial counts, or use unbiased measures like P-P-C and debiased w-P-L-I. Check power, because stronger signals give cleaner phases. Build surrogates that destroy exactly what you are testing. And prefer contrasts between conditions to raw values.

Brain graphs. What happens when you turn a connectivity matrix into a network?

After computing connectivity between every pair of channels or sources, you get a matrix. With sixty-four channels, that is more than two thousand pairs. Hard to look at. Graph theory offers a way to summarize it.

In a graph, each channel or region becomes a node. Each connection becomes an edge, with a weight equal to its connectivity value. Ed Bullmore and Olaf Sporns popularized this approach for brain networks in a widely cited review in 2009.

Picture the city at night, seen from above. Each neighborhood is a node. Each road between them is an edge. Some neighborhoods have many roads: they are hubs. Some groups of neighborhoods are tightly linked to each other: they form clusters.

Here are a few common measures. The degree of a node is its number of connections, or the sum of their weights, called strength. The clustering coefficient asks: are my neighbors also neighbors of each other? The characteristic path length is the average number of steps needed to go from any node to any other.

In 1998, Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz described small-world networks: high clustering, like a regular grid, but short paths, like a random network. Many brain networks have been described as small-world.

Now the cautions, because graph measures are easy to compute and easy to misread.

First, thresholding. Many measures need a binary graph: an edge is either there or not. So people keep only the strongest connections. But the threshold is arbitrary, and the results can change a lot with it. A common practice is to report results across a range of thresholds.

Second, density. Bernadette van Wijk, Cornelis Stam and Andreas Daffertshofer showed in 2010 that graph measures depend strongly on the number of nodes and on the number of edges. If one group simply has stronger connectivity overall, a fixed threshold keeps more edges, and clustering and path length change, even if the organization of the network is the same. Comparing networks with different densities can produce differences that are only about density.

There are several responses. Proportional thresholds keep the same fraction of edges in every subject. Normalizing measures against random networks with the same degrees helps too. Another option is the minimum spanning tree: the backbone that connects every node with the fewest, strongest edges, with no loops. Every tree has the same number of nodes and edges, which makes comparisons fairer.

Third, the graph inherits every problem of the connectivity measure underneath. If volume conduction inflates connections between neighboring electrodes, those electrodes will look like a tightly clustered community. Sensor-space graphs are especially vulnerable, so many researchers prefer source space, with leakage-robust measures.

And finally, a summary number like "small-worldness" tells you little on its own. It is the comparison, between conditions or groups, with careful controls, that carries meaning.

In Python, NetworkX handles general graphs, and bctpy is a port of the Brain Connectivity Toolbox.

To recap. A graph turns a connectivity matrix into nodes and edges. Degree, clustering and path length summarize its structure. Thresholds and density can drive the results, so use proportional thresholds, normalization, or minimum spanning trees. And a graph is only as good as the connectivity measure it was built from.

Choosing a connectivity measure follows a decision tree. TRANSCRIPTEN

Choosing a connectivity measure. A decision tree, to end the chapter.

We have met many measures in this chapter. Coherence, imaginary coherence, P-L-V, P-L-I, w-P-L-I, amplitude envelope correlation, Granger, D-T-F, P-D-C, graphs. How do you choose? Let us walk through a few questions, one at a time.

Question one. What is your question about? Is it about cycles happening together, about power rising and falling together, or about direction? Cycles together points to phase measures. Power together points to envelope correlation. Direction points to Granger-type measures, or the phase slope index.

Question two. Are you in sensor space or source space? In sensor space, every electrode sees a mixture of sources. Measures that accept zero-lag coupling, like coherence and P-L-V, will be inflated between neighbors. So in sensor space, prefer measures that ignore zero-lag interactions: imaginary coherence, w-P-L-I, or orthogonalized envelopes. In source space, leakage is reduced but not removed. The same robust measures are still a good default.

Picture a crossroads in the city at night. Each sign points to a different street, and each street has its own hazards. There is no single right road, only roads that fit your destination.

Question three. Do you expect true zero-lag coupling? Some interactions might be nearly simultaneous. Robust measures will miss them. That is a trade-off you accept knowingly, and should state in your paper.

Question four. How many trials do you have, and are they equal across conditions? With few or unequal trials, use unbiased measures like P-P-C or debiased w-P-L-I, and equalize the trial counts.

Question five. What is your baseline for "nothing"? Choose surrogates that destroy exactly what you test. And prefer contrasts, between conditions or with a baseline, over raw values.

Question six. Did power change too? Stronger oscillations give cleaner phase estimates. Report power alongside connectivity, so readers can judge.

Question seven, for directed measures. Did you check for artifacts of mixing? Use source space, time-reversed testing, or the phase slope index. And remember that Granger means predictability, not physical causation.

Question eight, if you build graphs. Are density and threshold controlled? Use proportional thresholds, normalized measures, or minimum spanning trees.

One more piece of advice. Decide your measure before looking at the results. Trying ten measures and reporting the one that works is a form of p-hacking. Write down your choice, with its reasons, in a pre-registration or at least in your analysis plan.

A good habit is to report one main measure, chosen in advance, and one robustness check with a measure that has different assumptions. If both agree, your conclusion is stronger.

And the tools are all in Python. mne-connectivity for spectral and envelope measures, including Granger. M N E for source estimation. NetworkX and bctpy for graphs.

To recap. Start from your question: phase, amplitude, or direction. Account for volume conduction, especially in sensor space. Match the measure to your trial counts. Build the right null. Check power. And choose before you look.
