PLATE 7.17:17

From an MRI to an electric field

Every field figure in the series comes from a pipeline that turns a structural MRI into labelled tissue, a mesh of small pyramids, one conductivity per tissue and a finite-element calculation. Three dials matter most: how skull and fluid were drawn, which conductivities are used, and where the source sits. A field map is a quantified hypothesis, not a measurement.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every time this series says a field of so many volts per metre inside a head, that number came from somewhere. Either someone measured it, with electrodes inside the head of a patient. Or someone computed it. This episode is about the computing. How do you get from a brain scan to a map of the electric field?

In the architect's office there is a workshop with five benches in a row. A flat picture goes in at one end. A layered scale model comes out at the other, and the model is put to a test. We will walk one head along the five benches. On every bench there is a dial. Keep an eye on the dials.

Bench one: the picture. It is a structural MRI. SPIN, our guest from the fMRI series, brings it in. It is a grid of small boxes, called voxels, and each box has a grey level. The picture tells us what tissue looks like on the scan. It does not tell us what each box is, and it says nothing about electricity. Pipelines usually want a T1-weighted scan. SimNIBS, one of two free tools we will name today, can also take a T2-weighted scan, and recommends it. You will see why in a minute.

Bench two: the tracing table. Every voxel gets a label. Scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, grey matter, white matter, and a few more, such as the eyes, the blood and the muscle. The model-maker cuts one layer of card for each tissue. In SimNIBS this step is called charm. It fits a probabilistic atlas of the head to the scan. In version four point six, from March of this year, the atlas also includes the fat under the skin, to help find the edge of the skull. ROAST, the other free tool, was built on the segmentation of SPM twelve. Its version four point zero, from September twenty twenty-five, can also use a neural network, which does not need an atlas.

Here is the first dial. On a T1 scan, bone and fluid both look dark. On a T2 scan, the fluid lights up. If the border between them is drawn a little wrong, the model gets the wrong bottleneck and the wrong motorway beside it. Episode two point one gave the skull and the fluid their roles. Here is how a model-maker can get them wrong. Saturnino and colleagues wrote in twenty nineteen that errors in these two layers can have a large effect on the field, especially for electrical stimulation. And in twenty eighteen, Nielsen and colleagues checked three automatic skull tools against skulls taken from CT scans of ten people. The tools did not perform equally well.

Bench three: the glue table. Layers of voxels are blocky. The solver wants something smoother. So each layer is filled with tetrahedra: little pyramids, with four corners, glued face to face. Each pyramid belongs to exactly one tissue. ROAST builds this mesh with a tool called iso2mesh.

Bench four: the swatch table. Each tissue gets one number, its conductivity, in siemens per metre. Episode two point one told you what it means. Now notice where it comes from. The tool does not measure it in your participant. It reads it from a table. The standard table of SimNIBS gives, for white matter, zero point one two six. Grey matter, zero point two seven five. Cerebrospinal fluid, one point six five four. Bone, zero point zero one. Scalp, zero point four six five. Those come from a paper by Wagner and colleagues, in two thousand and four.

Now compare. McCann and colleagues gathered fifty-six papers on head conductivity in twenty nineteen. Their weighted averages were about zero point four one for the scalp, zero point zero two for the skull, one point seven one for the fluid, zero point four seven for grey matter, and zero point two two for white matter. The skull and both brain tissues differ by roughly a factor of two between the two tables. The authors themselves attribute the spread to methods and to the people studied. So the second dial is this: which numbers do you write on the swatches? Depending on the study.

Does it matter? Depending on the technique. Saturnino and colleagues tested this with SimNIBS in twenty nineteen. For magnetic stimulation, the field depended mainly on the grey and white matter conductivities. For tDCS, the skull and the scalp mattered too. And the uncertainty was larger for the strength of the field than for its direction. Episode four point five shows what happened when Huang and colleagues tuned these numbers to match recordings inside heads.

Bench five: the calculation. This is the finite-element method. Inside each pyramid, the voltage is assumed to change in a straight line from corner to corner. The solver looks for the voltage at every corner, so that at each corner the current flowing in equals the current flowing out. It knows the conductivity of each pyramid, and the source: the electrodes, or the coil. That is one very large system of linear equations. Then the field is the downhill slope of the voltage. With a coil, the push comes from the changing magnetic field, and the solver adds the charge that piles up at the borders between tissues. ROAST uses a solver called getDP. And the arithmetic is the reliable part. Huang and colleagues compared ROAST with commercial finite-element software in twenty nineteen, and found small differences. The doubt is in what goes in.

The result is a number in every pyramid. To read it, the field is drawn on a surface through the middle of the grey matter: the cortical surface. It is a map, in volts per metre, for a given current or a given coil setting.

One more dial remains. Where does the source sit? The model needs the exact position of each electrode, or of the coil, on this head. Move the source, and the hot spot of the map moves with it. The position is a choice too.

So what have we built? Five benches and three dials that matter most: how the skull and the fluid were drawn, which numbers sit on the swatches, and where the source was placed. Turn any one of them and the map changes. A model map is not a photograph of the field. It is a quantified hypothesis: if this head is made like this, then the field is like that.

Now the classic trap. Reading a coloured map as a measurement. The tools say so themselves: SimNIBS describes itself as a research tool, and says clinical use is not supported. So when you read a paper, ask for the recipe. Which tool and which version. Which segmentation, and who checked it. Which conductivities. Which position. Publish the model like you publish a pipeline.

To sum up. An MRI becomes labelled tissue, then a mesh of pyramids, then one conductivity per tissue. A finite-element solver turns that into a field on the cortex. Every step is a choice, and the result is a hypothesis, not a measurement.

PLATE 7.26:16

No two heads alike

The same electrodes and current produce different fields in different heads, because skull thickness, cerebrospinal fluid and cortical folds differ, like one window with a different light meter behind it in each house. In models of healthy adults the spread is about fifteen to twenty percent of the mean, nearly two to one between extremes. The milliamp is not the dose.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we turned one head into a map of the electric field. Now the question every model-maker asks next. Put the same stimulator on a second head. Do you get the same map?

Back in the architect's office, the table is covered with scale models. Ten of them, ten different heads. They are illustrative: nobody in particular. On each one we place the same electrodes, in the same position, and we run the same current. Then we read the field at the same anatomical target in every head. The maps differ in their details, and above all in the strength at the target.

Think of a standard window. The architect drew one window, and ordered it for ten houses. Behind each window, in the room, there is a light meter. Same window, same sun. And yet the meters disagree, because the houses are not alike. Three things differ.

First, the thickness of the wall. In a head, that is the skull. The thicker it is, the less current gets through. Second, what lies between the wall and the room. Imagine a mirrored corridor that carries light sideways along the wall. In a head, that is the cerebrospinal fluid, or CSF. It conducts current very well and spreads it sideways, so a thicker layer means a weaker field in the cortex behind it. Third, the shape of the room: its bends and its alcoves. In a head, those are the folds of the cortex, and how deep the target sits in one. The light is only an image. What travels in a head is current. But the logic is the same.

So how big is the difference? One study shows it plainly. In twenty twenty, Evans and colleagues took fifty head scans from the Human Connectome Project. With a free tool called ROAST, they put the same montage on every head: one pad behind the left motor cortex, one in front of it, with one milliamp. Then they read the field in the left primary motor cortex.

On average, zero point one eight five volts per metre. But the weakest head got zero point one two five, and the strongest, zero point two four nine. Almost two to one, at the same dose. The standard deviation was zero point zero three three, about eighteen percent of the mean. At two milliamps, everything doubles. The weakest head gets zero point two six, the strongest zero point five two. The gap stays.

Is that one study's quirk? Other studies find the same order of magnitude, depending on the montage and the region. Laakso and colleagues modelled twenty-four men in twenty fifteen. In the hand area, the field varied with a standard deviation of about twenty percent of the mean, and the thickness of the fluid alone explained about half of it. Antonenko and colleagues modelled forty people in twenty twenty-one, twenty younger and twenty older, with six common montages and one milliamp. Field peaks varied between people by up to about a quarter. And anatomy alone, the volumes of head, skull, skin and fluid, accounted for seventy-nine to ninety-four percent of the differences in overall field strength. Mikkonen and colleagues modelled twenty-eight people in twenty eighteen, with square pads over the hand area and the forehead, at one milliamp. The strongest field in the hand area averaged zero point six one volts per metre, and ranged from zero point four seven to zero point eight five.

So the usual picture, depending on the study: a standard deviation of fifteen to twenty percent of the mean, and almost a factor of two between the extreme heads. Remember that this is one dose, one montage, and healthy adults.

What about magnetic stimulation? Studies that report a clean spread are scarcer, so I will only say what I could check. Mikkonen and colleagues also measured the resting motor threshold of those twenty-eight people. Individual averages ran from thirty-one to seventy-one percent of the stimulator output: more than a factor of two. People with a higher threshold tended to have weaker modelled fields in the hand area. The authors attribute this mainly to anatomy, which shapes the electric fields of both techniques. So part of why one person needs more output is what sits between the coil and the cortex. Only part: neurons differ too. And Alawi and colleagues modelled forty-eight people in twenty twenty-three, children, adults and people in their late seventies and eighties, with the same coil setting. The peak field in the cortex was lower in the older group, and was linked to skull thickness.

Now the cautions. First, every one of these numbers is modelled, not measured. Episode seven point one showed how many choices stand behind a map. Episode four point five showed the few measurements we have. Second, these models give every head the same conductivity values, so only the shape of the head differs. Real tissue may differ too, and that is not in these numbers. Third, a field is not a response. The field at the target is the input. What neurons do with it is another question.

Which gives us the classic trap. A methods section says: one milliamp, same montage, same dose. As if the dose were the same. Evans and colleagues drew many groups of fifteen people out of their fifty. The average field in the motor cortex across a group went from zero point one five eight to zero point two one three, between the lowest and the highest group. Two laboratories, the same milliamps, and about a third more field in one than in the other, just from who happened to walk in.

This is where a part of the variability of responses begins. Episode nine point five takes it up. Whether we can fix it, by giving each head the current that reaches the same field, is the question of episode seven point three.

To sum up. The same stimulator, on different heads, gives different fields at the same target. Skull, fluid and folds all play a part. In models of healthy adults, the spread is a standard deviation of fifteen to twenty percent, and almost two to one between extremes, depending on the study. It is a modelled spread, and the milliamp is not the dose.

PLATE 7.37:08

Individualising the dose

Setting the dose by the field reaching the target, rather than by the same current or fraction of motor threshold, is like pumping every tyre to the same pressure instead of the same number of strokes. In models it removes the spread at the target but not elsewhere. It assumes the model is right and that equal field gives equal effect, which tests contest.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the same stimulator gave different fields in different heads. So the milliamp is not the dose. This episode asks the obvious next question. If the field is what reaches the neurons, can we set the dose by the field?

Back in the architect's office, there are two ways to set a dose. The first is the common rule. In electrical stimulation, everyone gets the same current: one milliamp, or two. In magnetic stimulation, everyone gets the same fraction of their own motor threshold, which is the weakest pulse that twitches a hand muscle. A study might say, for example, a hundred and ten percent of threshold. The second is the field rule. The model computes the field in the target of each head. Then the intensity is adjusted, head by head, until every target gets the same field.

Think of pumping up bicycle tyres. The common rule is ten strokes of the pump for every tyre. A thin road tyre and a fat mountain tyre will end up at very different pressures. The field rule is to pump until the gauge reads the same pressure. Each tyre then gets a different number of strokes. This picture has two weak points, and they are the point of the episode. The gauge could be wrong. And the pressure might not be what the rider needs.

First, what is gained. In twenty twenty, Evans and colleagues took the same fifty heads we met last time. They scaled the current of each head so that the field in the left motor cortex reached one common value. In the model, the spread at that target disappeared. But the spread elsewhere did not. In the right motor cortex, and under the electrodes, the variability stayed about the same. You equalise the target, not the whole brain.

In twenty twenty-two, Van Hoornweder and colleagues tested a practical version on three hundred healthy young adults. They called it two S P E D. A first group sets the target field. A second group, of a hundred people, receives the currents that reach it. At one milliamp, the motor cortex field varied by about eighteen percent of the mean with a standard pad montage, and by about thirty-four percent with a ring montage. Reaching the same field needed currents from about half a milliamp to one and a half milliamps for the standard montage. For the ring montage, it was about a third of a milliamp up to two point three. Across the whole cortex, the peak field varied much less than before. In the ring montage, it went from about thirty-three percent to about seven and a half percent. But this is a simulation. The authors say it was not validated in living people. And part of the result is built in: they chose the current to equalise the target.

Now magnetic stimulation. Here the common rule is the motor threshold, measured over the motor cortex, then applied elsewhere. Two things differ between the hand area and the prefrontal cortex. One is distance. In two thousand and seven, Stokes and colleagues found that each extra millimetre between coil and cortex needed about two point eight percent more of the machine's output. The other is the head itself. In twenty twenty-two, Turi and colleagues built sixteen head models and modelled the motor cortex and a prefrontal target. With threshold-based intensity, the field in the cortex still ranged from about ninety-eight to a hundred and seventy-seven volts per metre in the motor cortex, and from about ninety-six to a hundred and seventy-nine in the prefrontal target. With the same settings, the prefrontal field was lower than the motor cortex field. When each model was scaled to the same chosen field, the spread fell to less than one volt per metre. In twenty twenty-four, Numssen and colleagues argued that field-based dosing equalises the stimulation across regions. They also listed what remains open: the uncertainty of the simulation, how to define the target, and whether thresholds are comparable across areas and across protocols. A threshold found with single pulses may not carry over to repeated pulses.

Here is what the field rule assumes. First, that the model is right. Episode seven point one showed how many choices stand behind a map, and episode seven point four asks how far to trust it. If the model is wrong for one head, the field rule moves that head's dose in the wrong direction. Second, that the field at the target is what matters. Does equal field give equal effect? Three studies have asked, with small groups. They disagree.

In twenty twenty-three, Joshi and colleagues tested forty-two healthy adults, with fixed, individualised and sham sessions. Individualising cut the variance of the field at the motor cortex by more than ninety-nine percent. But it did not reduce the variability of the behaviour. In a motor learning task, the field was not linearly related to performance. With alternating current and a memory task, the three conditions did not differ.

In twenty twenty-four, Laakso and colleagues gave twenty-one people five sessions: four currents, from half a milliamp to two, and sham. Every current raised the motor response compared with sham. The field effect was nonlinear, and small compared with other differences between people. The best field also differed from person to person, so it could not be known in advance. They concluded that field-based personalising was unlikely to help.

In twenty twenty-five, Bhattacharjee and colleagues found the opposite, in sixteen adults. Each person had a fixed session at two milliamps, an individualised session, and sham. The individualised dose was scaled to match the current density in the target of a template head. Fifteen of the sixteen improved their reaction time with the individualised dose, against nine with the fixed one. It is a small study, with one montage and one group. I could not find a replication.

For magnetic stimulation, I did not find a study that compared field-based and threshold-based dosing on a clinical outcome. That is a gap, not a result.

Now the classic trap. A methods section says the dose was individualised, as if that settled it. Ask four questions. What was equalised: the field, or the current density? At which target? In which model, with which version? And what was the effect on the outcome, and not only on the field? Even Caulfield and colleagues, in twenty twenty, who computed individual currents in twenty-nine adults, wrote that the dose must now be tested prospectively. They also found that the model-based dose tracked the threshold for a single electrical pulse, but not the magnetic motor threshold.

To sum up. The common rule gives everyone the same input, and the field differs. The field rule gives everyone the same field in one target, and the dose differs. It removes one source of variability. It assumes the model is right and that the field in the target is what matters. Studies disagree on the second point, depending on the study.

PLATE 7.46:42

How far can you trust a model?

A field model is checked like a weather forecast, against gauges: electrodes implanted in epilepsy patients and MRI measurements of current. The pattern of the field matches well, but the amount is uncertain, too high in one study and too low in another. Calibrating to the data is not a test, so a modelled value needs its model, version and a range.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Three episodes ago we built a model of the electric field. Then we saw that it changes from head to head. Then we asked whether we could set the dose with it. Behind all three stands one question. Is the map right?

In the architect's office, the scale model is finished, and nobody has tested it yet. So today we borrow the weather office. A forecast map covers every street in the city. A rain gauge covers one spot. To check a forecast, you put out gauges and compare. And you ask two separate questions. Did the rain fall where the map said? That is the pattern. And did the gauge fill as much as the map said? That is the amount. A forecast can get one right and the other wrong. Our field map is the forecast. The gauges are electrodes inside heads, and the MRI scanner.

First gauge: electrodes inside heads. Only patients with epilepsy have them, implanted for a few days so that surgeons can locate their seizures. In twenty seventeen, Huang and colleagues applied weak current to ten of these patients through two small pads, one at the front and one at the back of the head. They recorded from one thousand three hundred and eighty contacts, on the surface and in depth. Then they built a model of each head and compared.

The pattern was good. For each patient, the modelled and measured fields correlated at about zero point eight one. Pooling all patients, the correlation was zero point eight six at the surface, and zero point eight eight in depth. Even when every head got the same set of conductivities, the correlation stayed at about zero point seven eight. So the forecast knows where it rains. In this dataset, depth was no harder than the surface.

The amount was not as good. With conductivities taken from the literature, the measured field was only about two thirds of the predicted one, give or take a fifth, depending on the patient. The map was too wet. To fix it, the authors tuned the conductivities of skin, skull and brain, patient by patient. The best values differed between patients by up to a factor of ten. Adding finer details, such as the direction of white matter fibres, did not help. That tuning is a calibration. It is not an independent test.

Second gauge: the scanner. Current passing through a head makes a tiny magnetic field, and an MRI sequence can pick up its shift. Jog and colleagues showed this in twenty sixteen. In twenty eighteen, Göksu and colleagues did it in five volunteers, at one milliamp, with fields as weak as one nanotesla. Cables made stray fields, so the team had to correct for them. After that, simulated and measured fields correlated in every volunteer. Current density maps agreed with an R squared of seventy-one percent. But this time the model came out too dry: the simulations underestimated the current by twenty-four percent on average. A newer sequence from twenty twenty-one sharpened the measurement. Some simulations still clearly deviated from it.

Look at the two gauges together. In one study the model overshoots the amount. In the other it undershoots. Different people, different tools, different quantities. So the direction of the error is not a law. What is stable is the lesson: pattern good, amount uncertain, depending on the study.

Now the local detail, the street corner. Puonti and colleagues, in twenty twenty, tested what happens when the scan has artefacts, which the segmentation then draws into the model. Local differences in the simulated field reached thirty percent of the peak field. Drawing fifteen tissues instead of five changed the field locally by up to ten percent of the peak, for some montages. Interfaces between tissues are where the forecast is most fragile.

What about the choice of software? Huang and colleagues compared two free pipelines in twenty nineteen. On the intracranial recordings, they were not significantly different, provided the user knew the tool well. But in twenty twenty-one, Bhalerao and colleagues ran five pipelines on thirty-two volunteers, with one montage. They found no systematic trend: the predicted field depended on the pipeline. For magnetic stimulation, Camera and colleagues compared two solvers in twenty twenty-four. Differences were below two percent in spheres and below twelve percent in a head model. So the arithmetic is the safe part. The segmentation and the settings are not.

And magnetic stimulation itself? Direct measurement is rare. In two thousand and four, Wagner and colleagues recorded in one patient. The current fell with distance from the coil and changed with coil orientation, as the models predict. That is one patient, and a trend. I did not find a larger intracranial comparison for magnetic stimulation. That is a gap, not a result. Most checks are indirect. Numssen, Weise and colleagues relate modelled fields to muscle responses, to find where a muscle is represented. That shows that the model is useful. It does not measure the field.

So how far can you trust a model? For trends, ranking one montage against another, and orders of magnitude, quite far. For the exact value at one spot, much less. Treat a modelled value as a forecast with a range.

Now the classic trap. A paper reports a field of zero point four three volts per metre in the target, to two decimals, with no range, and no version. That is a precision the model has not earned. Report what a forecast office reports: the model, the run, the version. Which tool and which version. Which segmentation, and who checked it. Which conductivities. Where the source sat. Which measure was extracted. That last one matters. Van Hoornweder and colleagues found three hundred and eight different outcome measures in two hundred and two studies. In their models, different measures gave different values from the same head. Guidelines go back to Bikson and Datta, in twenty twelve. A consensus checklist for electrical stimulation, with sixty-six items, was published in twenty twenty-six. Publish the model like you publish a pipeline.

To sum up. Against intracranial recordings and MRI, models predict the pattern well and the amount less well. Local values near tissue borders are the least certain. Calibrating to the data helps, but it is not a test. Report the model, its parameters and its version, and give a range.
