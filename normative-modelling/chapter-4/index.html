A map of normative models

A normative model is a way to draw a curve and its spread, and a family tree of models is read through three questions: what is estimated, how much data is needed, and what happens with sites. Branches estimate a mean and spread, a whole distribution, or only centiles. The tree is not a ranking, since no model is best in general.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw Bayes in one picture. A prior, a crowd of pumpkins, a fan of lines that tightens, and at the end a predictive distribution for one new person. That single object carried both kinds of uncertainty from episode three point six. Today we leave the pumpkin fair for the shipyard, and we step back to look at the whole room.

Picture the drawing floor of a shipyard. Shipbuilders once drew smooth curves with a long flexible strip of wood, held in place by lead weights. The mathematical spline is named after that strip. Each way of bending it is a different promise about the curve.

This chapter is the same. Each model is another way to draw a curve through the data, and another way to say how far a person may stray from it. On the wall hangs a family tree of those models. Today we read the tree, and choose nothing.

Every model of this chapter follows one format. The intuition, the fit in one picture, what the model captures, and the pitfall.

Today, the intuition is the tree itself, and it comes from three questions. What does the model estimate? How much data does it need? And what does it do with sites? Let us take the first question, and look at the branches.

The first branch estimates a mean, and a spread around it. Bayesian linear regression and Gaussian process regression live here. Both give the average at each age, and the width of the cloud around it. In their basic form, both suppose that the cloud is a bell.

They differ in how they bend. Bayesian linear regression bends through the columns we give it. In episode four point three, those columns will be B-splines, small local bumps along the age axis. Gaussian process regression bends through a kernel, a rule that says how much two people of close ages should look alike. It was the model of the 2016 article by Marquand and colleagues, which used it to map how people vary within a clinical cohort.

The second branch estimates the whole distribution. Warped Bayesian regression first stretches the measurement axis, until the data look like a bell. It fits there, and then stretches back. The warp used in the lifespan models of PCNtoolkit is called sinh-arcsinh. GAMLSS goes another way. It chooses a family of distributions, then lets each of its parameters, the position, the scale and the shape, change smoothly with age. It is the framework behind the twenty twenty-two brain charts for the human lifespan, built from more than one hundred thousand people.

The third branch skips the distribution altogether. Quantile regression, from Koenker and Bassett in 1978, fits each centile line on its own. No bell is assumed, so none can be wrong.

Second question. How much data? Gaussian processes are comfortable with a few thousand people, and struggle with very large cohorts. That is why the warped Bayesian regression was proposed. And a quantile line at the two point fifth centile is set by about one person in forty, so it needs a crowd.

Third question. Sites. The simplest answer is a fixed effect. Every site gets its own shift, as one more column in the regression. The other answer is a hierarchy. Each site has its own parameters, drawn from a shared distribution, so a small site borrows strength from the big ones. Hierarchical Bayesian regression does this for the mean and for the spread, without harmonising the data first. In twenty twenty-two, Kia and colleagues showed how to use it to adapt a model to local datasets, even very small ones.

Now, the fit in one picture. Every model of this chapter is fitted to the same simulated data: a made-up cortical index, in arbitrary units, against age, for four hundred and fifty people from three sites. By design, the spread grows with age, and the cloud leans to the right more and more. Site C is small, and only has people from forty-five up. Every branch will meet these traps, on the same centile chart.

So, what does the map capture? It sorts models by what they promise. Some promise a mean and a bell. Some promise a whole distribution that changes with age. Some promise centiles and nothing else. Some promise to share information between sites.

And the pitfall. It is tempting to read the tree as a ranking, with the simple models at the bottom and the best one at the top. It is not one. There is no best model on its own. There are models that assume different things about the shape of the distribution, the size of the data, and the structure of the sites. A promise that does not fit your data gives a very confident wrong curve. Choosing comes later, in episode four point nine, with criteria in hand, and before looking at any patient.

So, to sum up. A normative model is a way to draw a curve and its spread. The tree asks three questions: what is estimated, how much data is needed, and what happens to sites. No branch wins in general.

Bayesian linear regression

Bayesian linear regression keeps a whole fan of plausible lines instead of one, and returns a Gaussian for each person whose variance adds the spread between people to the doubt about the curve. It is fast, and its prior acts like ridge regression. In its basic form it assumes one constant spread and a symmetric bell, which misplaces outer centiles.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we hung a map on the wall. Families of normative models, and what each one assumes. Today we walk to the first stop on that map. The simplest one, and the base of the next two. Bayesian linear regression.

You already know most of it. In episode three point three, we fitted a line through the clouds. In episode three point seven, we gave a prior to a slope, and watched the possible lines tighten. Today we put the two together, and ask the model for a distribution, not just a line.

On the drawing floor of the shipyard, CENTI is holding a long, rigid slat, and bends it over the cloud of dots. These dots are a simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units, against age. It is not real data. It is site A of our simulated set, two hundred and forty people.

Ordinary regression picks one slat, the one closest to the dots. But tilt it a little, and it still fits almost as well. Shift it up a little, the same. Many slats are nearly as good as the best one.

The Bayesian idea is to keep all of them.

Before any data, CENTI holds a whole fan of slats. This is the prior. We said, in effect, that the slope could be almost anything. Here it is wide: up or down by about eight units per decade. That width is our choice, and it is illustrative.

Now the people arrive. Each one pulls the fan toward the dots. Slats that miss fade away. After the two hundred and forty people of site A, the fan is thin. The straight line says that the index falls by about two point four units per decade, give or take about two tenths of a unit. A fan eight units wide became a fan two tenths of a unit wide.

And the fan is not equally thin everywhere. Near the middle of the ages, the slats are packed together. At the two ends, they spread out like a bunch of reeds. A line is pinned down best where the data are.

Three pieces, one at a time.

First, the model, from episode three point three. A person's value equals the columns of X, times the coefficients beta, plus noise. The noise is Gaussian, with one spread for everybody. Call it sigma.

Second, the prior. Each coefficient gets a Gaussian, centred on zero, with a width we choose. This prior is exactly the penalty of ridge regression. The mean of the fan is the ridge solution. If you have used ridge regression, you have met this model, with half of the picture missing.

Third, the posterior. With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise, the posterior over the coefficients is also a Gaussian. We can write it down with a little matrix algebra. No sampler, no waiting. One pass through the data, and the fan is there.

Now the output. For a new person, at a given age, the model gives a Gaussian. Its centre is the middle of the fan. Its variance has two terms. The variance of a new person is sigma squared, plus s squared of x. Sigma squared is the spread between people. S squared of x is the doubt about the curve at that age. You met this sum in episode three point six. It is the posterior predictive distribution, and it carries both kinds of uncertainty, for free.

Let us see it in one picture. This is the centile board. Age along the bottom, the index up the side. The grey dots are the reference people, and they are simulated. For the middle, we use the same cubic curve as in episode three point three. A cubic is still a linear model, because only the coefficients enter in a line.

The median is pink. The outer centiles, two point five and ninety-seven point five, are blue. The pale yellow band is the spread between people. It is about ten units above and below the curve, and it stays that width at every age. The dashed blue band is the doubt about the curve, and MIST sits where it is widest.

At age forty, the blue band is about one unit either way. At age seventy-eight, about three. At age ten, about three too. It opens at the edges, where the data thin out, exactly as it did in episode three point six.

So what does this model capture?

It captures the conditional mean, the curve through the middle. It captures the noise between people, as one number. And it captures the epistemic doubt about the coefficients, age by age.

It is also fast. The matrix to solve is as small as the number of coefficients, not the number of people. That is why Fraza and colleagues, in twenty twenty-one, could take this approach to a large cohort, where Gaussian processes become expensive. We will meet their warped version in episode four point four.

Now the pitfall. Two of them, in fact.

The first is in the model itself. Take away the cubic, and fit a plain straight line. At age ten, the line says the index is about one hundred and seven. In our simulation, the true average at that age is about one hundred and sixteen. The line is nine units too low. And its blue band is only about one unit and a third wide. Thin, and wrong. The blue band measures what the data constrain, not whether the shape is right. Remember the warning of episode three point six.

The second pitfall is in the noise. In its basic form, this model has one sigma for everyone, and a Gaussian shape. Look at the yellow band again. It is ten units wide at every age. But in site A, the people under thirty spread over a band about eight units wide, either side of the curve. The people over fifty-five spread over about eleven. In the simulation, the true spread grows from three to seven units across the ages. The band is too wide on the left, and too narrow on the right.

And the shape. In the simulation, the true skewness runs from about point seven at age eight, to about one point two at age eighty. A Gaussian is symmetric. So the outer centiles, the ones the clinic cares about, are placed wrongly.

So the basic model is a good engine. It needs better bases for the curve, and a way to bend the distribution. Those are the next two episodes.

So, to sum up. Bayesian linear regression keeps a whole fan of lines, not one. It returns a Gaussian for each person, with the spread between people, and the doubt about the curve. It is fast, but its basic form assumes one spread and a symmetric bell.

Next time, B-splines. How to bend the line, without letting it run wild.

PLATE 4.35:36

B-splines: bending the line

A polynomial is one global curve that wobbles at the edges, where reference data are thinnest, so a B-spline replaces it with a row of small local bumps, each with one weight, and the curve is their weighted sum. It is still regression with different columns. The pitfalls are the number and placement of knots, and unchecked extrapolation beyond the last one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we gave a straight line a memory of its own uncertainty. Bayesian linear regression draws a whole bundle of possible lines, and the bundle opens up where data are rare. But it was still a straight line. Today we teach that line to bend.

Welcome to the drafting floor of the shipyard. Before computers, hull curves were drawn here at full size. A draughtsman took a long, thin, flexible strip of wood. He pinned it down at a few points with heavy lead weights. Between the weights, the strip relaxed into the smoothest curve it could find. That strip was called a spline.

Mathematicians borrowed the name in the nineteen-forties, for smooth curves glued together from small pieces. This episode is about the most useful way of writing them down. The B-spline.

Start with the problem. Our simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units, is not a straight line of age. In the simulation it falls fast in childhood, from about one hundred and twenty-two at age eight to a plateau near one hundred. Then it declines slowly. A line cannot follow that. We met the obvious fix in episode three point three. Add columns to the regression: age, age squared, age cubed.

But a polynomial is one global curve. Pull it down at one end, and the other end moves too. Add more powers to follow a bump, and it starts to wobble near the edges of the age range. A mathematician named Carl Runge described that wobble in nineteen hundred and one. It is the very place where our reference data are thinnest.

The spline floor has a better idea. Let the curve be local. Pin the strip at a few places, and let each pin act only on its neighbourhood.

So the weights become knots. A knot is an age at which one piece of the curve hands over to the next. The knots cut the age axis into intervals.

On each interval, we do not write age, age squared, age cubed. We lay down a row of small, smooth bumps. Each bump is a basis function. It rises from zero, peaks, and falls back to zero. A cubic bump is nonzero over only four neighbouring intervals, and exactly zero everywhere else. Neighbouring bumps overlap, like roof tiles.

Here is the fit in one picture. Under the chart, draw every bump, each in its own colour. Each bump gets one weight, a single number. Stretch the bump by its weight. Then add all the stretched bumps together. The sum is the curve, drawn above, in pink.

Look at what the weights do, in this illustrative fit. The first bump is tall, because the index starts high. The next few shrink, because the index falls. The bumps in the middle are nearly equal, because the plateau is flat. Each weight controls the curve in its own neighbourhood, and nowhere else.

And this is still regression. The bumps are just columns. For each person, we evaluate every bump at their age, and the evaluations form a row of the design matrix. In episode three point three, we saw that a curve in age is still linear in the coefficients. Here it is again. The method has not changed. We changed the columns, and nothing else.

That is why the Bayesian linear regression of the last episode takes B-splines without any change. It keeps its prior, its predictive distribution, its two kinds of uncertainty. It simply fits a curve, instead of a line.

The warped Bayesian linear regression of the next episode uses the same trick. In the paper that introduced it, Fraza and colleagues expanded age into a cubic B-spline basis with five evenly spaced knots. They chose it over polynomials, which, they wrote, can induce global curvature. They also noted that a B-spline basis can be seen as a low-rank approximation of a Gaussian process, a model we will meet in episode four point five. It scales to very large datasets.

So what does a B-spline capture? A smooth, nonlinear trajectory of the average, without the wobble of high powers. Each part of the curve answers to the data near it. A rapid rise in childhood does not drag the curve down in old age.

Now the pitfall. Where do the knots go, and how many?

Too few knots, and the strip is too stiff. It slides straight through the early rise and misses the childhood peak. Too many, and the strip is pinned at every point. It follows the noise, and wiggles through the data.

Place the knots evenly, and they ignore where the data are. Place them at quantiles of age, and they follow the people, and leave the edges with few knots. Neither is a free choice. It is a decision, to be justified, and checked on data the model has not seen. We come back to that in chapter seven.

There is a second, quieter trap, at the ends. In our simulated reference, only four people are younger than fourteen. Only thirteen are seventy-four or older. The curve there is held up by very few points. And beyond the last knot, the curve does not stop. It keeps going, in a direction decided by its last pieces, with no data to say whether that is right. The model will draw a confident line out there. We will come back to this extrapolation in chapter eleven.

So, to sum up. A B-spline replaces a global polynomial with local bumps, each with one weight, and the curve is their sum. It bends where the data bend, as long as you choose the knots with care.

Warped Bayesian linear regression

Warped Bayesian linear regression changes the ruler instead of the model. The measurement axis is stretched, like a rubber sheet, until the noise is Gaussian, then the regression is fitted there and released back, which produces asymmetric centiles with honest z-scores, using a sinh-arcsinh warp. Its warp is learned only from reference data and is fixed across ages.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we taught the line to bend. With B-splines, the centre of the cloud can follow any smooth trajectory across the years. But look at the bands around it. They are still symmetric, the same distance above and below. Today we fix the shape of the bell itself.

We are back in the drafting room of the shipyard. This is the simulated cortical index again, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. This is site A, with two hundred and forty people, and a smooth curve through the middle of them.

Now look at the people around sixty years old. Some sit far above the curve. Almost nobody sits equally far below it. The cloud has a long upper tail.

In this simulation we know the truth, because we wrote the recipe. At age sixty, the 2.5th centile sits 6.8 units below the median. The 97.5th centile sits 15.9 units above it. More than twice as far, on the heavy side.

A Bayesian linear regression, as we built it two episodes ago, cannot draw that. Its noise is a Gaussian bell, and a Gaussian bell is symmetric. At sixty, its two outer lines would sit about 11.5 units either side of the mean. Roughly 84 below, and 107 above. Too low on both sides, and the top one is three units short of the truth.

So here is the idea of this episode. Do not change the model. Change the ruler.

Picture the centile chart drawn on a sheet of rubber. We grab the sheet by the measurement axis, and we stretch it. The long upper tail is pulled in. The short lower tail is pushed out. We keep stretching until the cloud around the curve looks like a symmetric Gaussian bell, at every age.

In that stretched space, the model is the one you already know. A Bayesian linear regression with a Gaussian bell. It fits quickly, and it gives us a centile line for every centile we like.

Then we let the rubber go. The sheet springs back, and every line springs back with it. A symmetric band becomes an asymmetric one, with a short lower side and a long upper side. The centiles have taken the shape of the data.

Let us write it down. Call the measurement y. The warp is a function, phi. In warped space, phi of y is modelled as a straight-line regression plus Gaussian noise. To go back to the original units, we apply the inverse of phi. We stretch, we fit, we release.

The warp has to follow three rules. It must be monotonic, so that order is kept: a larger measurement stays larger. It must be invertible, because we need to come back. And it must be differentiable, because the likelihood in the original units needs the slope of the warp. Fraza and colleagues set these rules in the paper that introduced this model, in NeuroImage, in 2021.

Which warp? They tried several: a shift and a scale, a Box-Cox warp, and a sinh-arcsinh warp. They report that the sinh-arcsinh warp gave the best fit among those they evaluated.

So here is the warp, written in Plex Mono on the screen. The hyperbolic sine of b times the inverse hyperbolic sine of y, minus a. Two numbers, a and b. Two knobs. One bends the bell toward one side. The other makes the tails heavier, or lighter.

This family of curves comes from Jones and Pewsey, in Biometrika, in 2009. In their parametrisation, the two knobs are called epsilon, for skewness, and delta, for tail weight. We simulated our data with exactly this family. Please remember, that is the simulation's recipe, not a discovery about brains.

The fit is in one picture. On the left, the cloud in the original units, with an asymmetric swarm. On the right, the same people after the warp, with a symmetric swarm and a plain straight band. An arrow goes from left to right, and the grey dots slide to their new positions. Fit on the right. Release back to the left.

How are the knobs set? Not by hand. The two warp numbers are learned together with the regression, by maximising the likelihood of the data, in the sense of empirical Bayes. The warped likelihood is the usual one, minus the logarithm of the slope of the warp. The authors report that the cost stays roughly the same as the classic model.

That cost is the point. Their data were about ten thousand UK Biobank participants, aged forty to eighty. They report that the method scales to population-sized datasets, and that it has a clear computational advantage over Gaussian process regression for whole-brain analyses. We will meet that regression next time.

Now, what does this model capture?

First, asymmetry. The upper centiles can sit far from the median, and the lower ones close to it.

Second, the weight of the tails. A bell with heavier tails puts more people far out, and the warp can reproduce that.

Third, and this is quiet but important, honest z-scores. Remember episode two point two, where a naive z-score of plus two and minus two gave very different rarities on a skewed distribution. Here, the z-score is computed in the warped space, where the bell is symmetric. It reads the same on both sides. Episode three point five explained why that matters. And the authors report that the benefit is mostly in the outer centiles.

Now, the pitfall. There are two.

The first. The warp is learned from the reference data, and from nowhere else. In a region with few people, the model has nothing local to say about the tails. Look at the simulation. Below fourteen years old, only four people. Above seventy-four, only thirteen. There, the outer lines are not measured. They are the shape of the tails learned elsewhere, carried along. Read them with the blue mist of uncertainty in mind.

The second. In the original formulation, the warp numbers are single numbers, the same at every age. But in our simulation, the skewness grows with age. It is 0.71 at eight years, and 1.22 at eighty. One fixed shape cannot be exact at every age. We will meet a family of models, GAMLSS, that lets the shape change with age.

And one more word of caution, from the paper itself. Non-Gaussian models can overfit. For some measures, the authors found that a plain B-spline model did as well, or better. Flexibility is not free.

So, to sum up. A warped Bayesian linear regression stretches the measurement axis until the noise is Gaussian, fits there, and releases. It keeps the speed of the linear model, and it draws asymmetric centiles. But the warp is only as good as the data it was learned from.

PLATE 4.56:50

Gaussian process regression

Gaussian process regression chooses no curve shape: a kernel says how alike two nearby ages should be, and the data select the curve, with a doubt that grows where people are missing. That doubt reflects where the data lie, not how much people differ. It also assumes constant noise and costs grow with the cube of the number of people.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we warped the measurement axis, so that a skewed cloud of people became a Gaussian one. The curve underneath it was still a shape we had chosen: a line, or a spline. Today we stop choosing the shape. Gaussian process regression.

Picture a long, flexible slat on the drawing floor. Now imagine it is not one piece of wood. It is made of a thousand tiny springs, and each spring holds hands with its neighbours. Pull one spring, and the ones next to it follow. The ones far away barely notice.

That is the whole idea. We do not say, the curve is a parabola. We say something much weaker. Two people of nearby ages should have nearby values. Two people of very different ages need not.

The rule that says how nearby is nearby is called a kernel. Draw it as the strength of the tie between two ages. Ages one year apart, tied tightly. Ages thirty years apart, hardly tied at all. The most common one is the squared exponential, a smooth bell-shaped tie. It has a knob, the length-scale. A short length-scale, and the springs only hold their closest neighbours: the curve wiggles. A long length-scale, and the springs hold hands over many years: the curve is smooth and slow.

Here is another way to hear it. To draw the curve at one age, the model looks around. It takes the people nearby, and mixes their values into a weighted average. The kernel decides the weights. People close in age count a lot. People far away count almost nothing. So the curve at forty is shaped mostly by people in their late thirties and early forties.

That makes the length-scale a real choice. Too short, and the curve chases every single person, noise included. Too long, and it flattens real features. In our simulated data, the index drops fast in childhood, then declines slowly for decades. One length-scale has to serve both. Keep an eye on that compromise when you read a fit.

Formally, a Gaussian process is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which have a joint Gaussian distribution. In plain words, before seeing any data, the kernel describes a whole family of possible curves. Then the data arrive. Every curve that passes far from the people is thrown out, and what is left is a bundle of curves that agree with the data.

Now the fit, in one picture. This is our simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units, against age. These are the people of site A, simulated. The pale yellow ribbon is the spread between people, the aleatoric part. Around the average curve, the Gaussian process draws a second band, dotted and blue. That is the epistemic part, what the model does not know because it has not seen enough people.

Look at where the blue band lives. Where people are plentiful, it is thin. Here that covers most of life: from about sixteen to seventy-four years old, the band keeps almost the same width. Even between fifty-four and sixty-two, where site A has only thirteen people, it is barely wider. The springs are pinned by many points, and a few neighbours on each side are enough. It opens at the two ends. At the young end, site A has only three people under fourteen, the youngest at nine. At twelve, the band is already twice as wide as in the middle, and at eight, four times. At the old end, the last person is seventy-seven. Past seventy-five, the band widens, and beyond the last person, it keeps opening. The band does not need to be told to do this. It comes out of the maths. Far from the data, the springs have nothing to hold, and the curve drifts back toward its prior, with the doubt growing.

Notice that the two bands answer two different questions. The yellow ribbon asks, how much do people of this age differ from one another? The blue band asks, how sure are we of where the middle is? A person's deviation needs both. But the two must stay apart, and we will see why in a moment.

Compare this with a polynomial. A polynomial can shoot off confidently past the last point. A Gaussian process says, here I am guessing, and here is how much.

So what does it capture? Smooth, flexible trajectories, without choosing a polynomial degree or placing knots. And an epistemic uncertainty that behaves well: small where we have data, large where we do not. It is the model of the founding paper. In 2016, Marquand and colleagues used a Gaussian process to predict a brain measure from a clinical trait, with a confidence for every person. Then they read each person's distance from that prediction as a deviation, with that confidence in the denominator.

In the Information series, you will meet the same split between what the world scatters, and what we have not yet learned. This model draws the second one very honestly.

Now the pitfall. Or rather, three of them.

The first is about the blue band. In the standard form, with a kernel like the squared exponential, that band is computed from the ages of the people, and from the kernel. It does not depend on the measured values at all. Rasmussen and Williams, in their book on Gaussian processes, say so plainly. This is the point made by Xu and colleagues in 2021. The variance the model gives you tells you where the data are dense. It does not tell you how much people differ from each other at that age. If you divide a deviation by that variance, you are dividing by a map of where your data happen to lie.

The second is the noise. In its basic form, the model assumes one fixed amount of Gaussian noise at every age. But look at our simulated people. Their true spread more than doubles between eight and eighty years old, and the cloud leans to one side. A constant, symmetric noise cannot follow that. So the yellow ribbon is flat when it should widen. The warping of the last episode, or a model with its own variance curve, is what repairs this.

The third is cost. A Gaussian process has to hold every person in relation to every other person. The basic algorithm scales with the cube of the number of people. With four hundred and fifty people, no problem. Double the people, and the work is eight times bigger. Tens of thousands of people, and the basic form is simply too heavy. Approximations exist for large datasets, but they are a different model, with their own compromises.

So, to sum up. A Gaussian process does not choose a curve. It says how alike neighbours should be, and lets the data pick, with a doubt that grows where data are missing. But that doubt is about where the people are, not about how much they differ, and the cost grows fast.

PLATE 4.67:30

Hierarchical Bayesian regression

Reference data come from several sites with different scanners. Ignoring sites pools everyone and separate charts leave small sites shaky, so hierarchical Bayesian regression gives each site its own curve drawn from a shared distribution, so a small site borrows strength and can be calibrated to a new site. It is slow, depends on its prior, and needs convergence checks.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a Gaussian process let the data decide how a curve bends, by saying how alike two nearby ages must be. But there was a quiet assumption. Every person in that reference was measured the same way. Real reference samples are not. They come from many sites, each with its own scanner. Today, the model that was built for exactly that. Hierarchical Bayesian regression.

Welcome back to the drafting floor of the shipyard. This time, there is not one workshop but several. One workshop per shipyard, each drawing hulls on its own floor.

Our simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units, comes from three sites. Site A has two hundred and forty people. Site B has one hundred and fifty. And site C is small and new. It has sixty people, all of them older than about forty-five.

In the simulation, site B reads about three units higher than site A. Site C reads about four units lower. And their spreads differ too. B is a little more spread out, and C a little less.

That is a site effect. Nobody is healthier or sicker here. The machines are just different. Now, what do we do about it?

There are two obvious answers, and both are wrong in an instructive way.

The first answer is to ignore the sites. Pour everyone onto one chart, and fit one curve. This is complete pooling. It is fast. But a person from site C now looks abnormal for being scanned at C.

The second answer is to give every site its own chart. Fit three separate curves. This is no pooling. It is honest about differences. But site C has only sixty people. Its curve will be shaky, and below forty-five it has no data at all.

The hierarchical model sits between them. It is called partial pooling.

Here is the idea, in one sentence. Every site has its own curve. But the sites are not strangers. Each site's curve is drawn from a shared distribution, as if the shipyards had all been given the same master template, and each had bent it a little.

Let us write it down, one line at a time.

Each site has its own intercept. That is the height of its curve.

The intercept of site s is drawn from a normal distribution. Its mean is shared by all the sites. Its spread, called tau, says how much the sites are allowed to differ.

The slope and the noise level of each site are handled the same way. Each gets its own value, tied to a shared distribution.

And here is the beautiful part. Tau is not chosen by hand. It is learned from the data.

If the sites turn out to be alike, tau becomes small, and every site is pulled toward the common curve. That is close to complete pooling. If the sites turn out to be very different, tau becomes large, and each site is left almost alone. That is close to no pooling. The data choose where to sit between the two.

Now, the fit in one picture. Three curves on the same centile board. One for each site, in its own colour. And a fourth, thicker one, the common curve in the middle.

The big sites, A and B, stay close to what their own data say. The small site, C, is pulled toward the common curve. We call that shrinkage. Where its data are scarce, it borrows from its neighbours. This picture is illustrative. It shows the idea, not a fitted result.

And the band around site C is wider. It has fewer people, so the model knows less about it. That is the epistemic band, the blue dotted one, carried by MIST. It is wide for the small site, and it stays honest.

That was the principle. Now, a new site walks in.

Part two. A new site, with very few people.

Suppose a hospital has just bought a scanner. It has scanned a handful of healthy volunteers. It cannot build a norm of its own. But the hierarchy has already learned what a typical site looks like.

So we do not start from nothing. In the study that introduced this approach to the clinic, Kia and colleagues did the following. They first fitted the model on a large reference. Then they took what the model had learned about the typical site, and used it as the starting belief, the prior, for the new one. The new site's few people then update that belief into a posterior.

In the Information series, that move, from prior to posterior, is a gain of information. Here it is a gain in knowledge about one scanner.

What did they find? The study used more than thirty-seven thousand scans, from seventy-nine scanners, in the published paper.Hierarchical Bayesian regression, and a plain fixed-effect model, did best at the multi-site fit. Both beat naive pooling, ComBat harmonisation, and no pooling. And when adapting to new clinical sites, the model beat fitting each site alone. Even at some sites with almost no training scans, the normative ranges stayed reasonable.

So, what does it capture?

First, site effects in the mean. Site B sits three units above A, and a model like this can find it.

Second, site effects in the variance. A site whose measurements are more spread out gets its own noise level.

Third, it does all this without touching the data. There is no harmonisation step beforehand. The site is part of the model, not a correction applied before it. We come back to this in episode five point four.

And the fourth thing is the uncertainty. When a score is computed for someone from a small site, the width of the band is still there, and the score keeps that doubt.

Now the pitfalls. There are three.

The first is cost. This model is fitted by sampling, not by a formula. A sampler draws thousands of possible curves from the posterior, and that is slower than the closed form of Bayesian linear regression.

The second is the prior. With few people at a site, the prior is doing a lot of the work. Change it, and the small site's curve moves. It must be chosen with care, and reported. Remember episode three point seven. A prior is a decision, not a detail.

The third is convergence. A sampler can look as if it has finished, and still be lost. So we run several chains, from different starting points, and check that they agree. The usual check is a number called R hat. A current recommendation, from Vehtari and colleagues in twenty twenty-one, is at least four chains, an R hat below one point oh one, and an effective sample size above four hundred. A sampler that did not converge gives a norm that looks like a norm, and is not one. Report the check.

There is a fourth, quieter pitfall, and it belongs to site C. Below forty-five, site C has no people. Its curve there is borrowed entirely from the other sites. The model will draw it with confidence, and the blue band should open wide to say so.

Two more limits from the same study. The basic version assumes a Gaussian bell, so a skewed measure can give poorly calibrated centiles. And each brain measure is modelled on its own, so the site effect is handled one measure at a time.

So, to sum up. Hierarchical Bayesian regression gives each site its own curve, tied to a shared distribution, so that a small site borrows strength from the large ones. Check the sampler, and state your prior.

Next time, we let the whole shape of the distribution change with age. Episode four point seven, GAMLSS.

GAMLSS

GAMLSS chooses a family of distributions and lets every parameter, location, scale and shape, follow its own smooth curve of age. The simulated cloud moves, widens and leans, and the model captures all three at once, as in the lifespan brain charts. Its pitfalls are the many choices of family, flexibility and convergence, each needing checks.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we gave every site its own curve, tied to a shared master, so that a small site could borrow strength from the big ones. But inside each site, one thing stayed fixed. The shape of the bell. Today we let the bell itself change, age after age. The name of the idea is GAMLSS.

We are back on the drafting floor of the shipyard. This is the simulated cortical index, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. These are the people of site A, from eight to eighty years old.

Look at the cloud, and ask what changes with age. Three things. The middle of the cloud falls. The spread grows wider. And the upper tail stretches away from the rest.

We know the truth here, because we wrote the recipe. At eight years old, the middle ninety-five percent of people spans about twelve units. At eighty, it spans about twenty-seven. The standard deviation climbs from 3.0 to 7.0. The skewness climbs from 0.71 to 1.22.

So the cloud is not one bell moving up and down. It is a bell that moves, widens, and leans.

Here is the idea. Picture three long, flexible slats lying on the same floor. The first slat sets the position of the bell. The second sets its width. The third sets its lean. Each slat is held by its own lead weights, so each one bends with age by itself, and none of them drags the others along.

That is GAMLSS. The letters stand for generalized additive models for location, scale and shape. Rigby and Stasinopoulos introduced it in 2005, in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series C.

The recipe has two steps. First, choose a family of distributions. Second, let every parameter of that family follow its own smooth curve of the covariates.

Let us write it on the board. The measurement y follows a distribution with parameters mu, sigma, nu and tau. Mu is the location. Sigma is the scale. Nu and tau carry the shape, roughly the skewness and the tail weight. Each parameter, passed through a link function, equals its own smooth function of age.

The word additive means that each curve can be built by adding simple pieces. A parametric curve, or a smooth nonparametric one, such as a spline. The authors allow both. And the word smooth means the curve may bend, but it may not jump.

A link function is a small safety net. A scale must stay positive, so many models fit the logarithm of sigma, and then transform back.

You have already met a small version of this. In episode two point two, the growth charts used the LMS method. Three curves: the median, the spread, and the skewness. Cole and Green published it in 1992, and it turns out to be a three-parameter GAMLSS. The framework simply lets you choose any family, and any number of curves.

Now the fit, in one picture.

At the bottom, the parameter curves, one small panel each. Mu falls with age. Sigma rises. The skewness rises. The tail weight is a flat line. At the top, the centile chart that these curves produce. Hand the parameters to the family at each age, and it hands back every centile you like.

In our simulation, these are the true curves, the ones the recipe wrote. They are not a fit. A fit would try to recover them from the points. The tail weight of the recipe, delta, is a constant, 0.9. We chose it. Please remember, that is the recipe, not a discovery about brains.

Look at what the chart does. At eight, the 2.5th centile sits 4.5 units under the median, and the 97.5th sits 7.3 above. At eighty, 7.3 below, and 19.3 above. The band widens, and it leans, and nobody drew it by hand.

That was the first half. Take a breath.

Part two. What does this model capture, and where does it bite?

It captures a whole distribution that changes shape with age. Not just a moving mean. Heteroscedasticity, the spread that changes with age, from episode three point four. Skewness, from three point five. And, if the family has the parameter, the weight of the tails. All of it is estimated in a single fit, and every centile is read from the same fitted distribution. That is why the lines cannot cross, as long as the distribution is a proper one.

Here is the proof that it scales to the real world. In 2022, Bethlehem and colleagues published brain charts for the human lifespan, in Nature. They gathered 123,984 scans, from 101,457 people, in more than one hundred studies. The ages run from 115 days after conception, to one hundred years.

Their framework is GAMLSS. They used a generalized gamma family, with three parameters. For the location and for the scale, age enters through fractional polynomials, which are a handful of powers of age, picked from a short list. And each study gets its own random intercept, in the location and in the scale.

Notice what they left flat. In their final models, the shape parameter has no age term at all. Even this huge chart lets only two curves move. That was a choice, and it is a good reminder. You decide what is allowed to vary.

For neuroimaging, the case was made earlier, in a 2021 preprint by Dinga and colleagues. They compared Gaussian and non-Gaussian models on 150 regions of cortical thickness in UK Biobank. They report that the non-Gaussian model fit better in a vast majority of regions. They give an example of a person with a Gaussian z-score of 2.8, who would score 2.2 under the non-Gaussian model. The tail changes what the number means.

They also report that letting the site act on both the mean and the scale improved predictions in new people. That connects straight back to the last episode.

In the Information series, a rare event carries more surprise. A heavy tail decides how surprised we should be by an extreme score.

Now, the pitfall. GAMLSS gives you many choices.

First, the family. Gaussian, generalized gamma, sinh-arcsinh, and many more. Second, how flexible each curve is. Third, which parameters get a random effect. Each choice changes the tails, and the tails are exactly where we want to read patients.

The family should be justified by diagnostics, not by habit. We will build those diagnostics in chapter seven, episode one.

Second, flexibility is not free. Every extra curve is one more thing to estimate. Dinga and colleagues warn that this approach is not suited to small datasets, because of the risk of overfitting. And in a region with few people, say below fourteen years old in our simulation, where we drew only four people, the curves for spread and shape have almost nothing local to learn from. Their outer lines are carried in from elsewhere. Read them with the blue mist of uncertainty in mind.

Third, the fit can fail quietly. The software finds the curves by iterating. In the R package, if it stops before converging, it prints a warning. Read that warning. A fit that has not converged is not a norm.

So, to sum up. GAMLSS chooses a family of distributions, and lets every parameter follow its own smooth curve of age. It captures a whole distribution that changes shape, but it asks for many choices, and each one must be checked.

Quantile regression

Quantile regression fits each centile on its own by tilting the cost of errors, with no family or bell assumed, so any shape is captured. The price is data, since tail centiles rest on few people. Centile lines can cross where people are rare, and the output is centiles rather than a z-score.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we let every part of the bell follow its own curve of age. Position, width, and lean. But we still had to name a family of distributions first. Today we drop the family altogether, and estimate each centile on its own. The name of the idea is quantile regression.

We are back on the drafting floor of the shipyard. This is the simulated cortical index again, in arbitrary units. It is not real data. These are the two hundred and forty people of site A, from eight to eighty years old.

Here is the intuition. Think of what a regression line does. Ordinary regression finds the line that makes the squared gaps as small as possible, and that line follows the mean. There is a sister recipe. Make the absolute gaps as small as possible, and the line follows the median. Equal numbers of people fall above it and below it.

Now tilt the ruler. Suppose a person below the line costs nineteen times as much as a person above it. The line cannot stay in the middle any more. It has to slide down, until only five percent of the people are left below it. That line is the fifth centile. Tilt the other way, and you get the ninety-fifth.

So each centile is its own little optimisation problem. In the quantile regression literature, the tilted cost is called a tilted absolute value function, and with a straight line, the problem can be solved very efficiently by linear programming. Koenker and Bassett introduced the method in 1978. Nowhere did we say the word Gaussian. We never wrote down the shape of the bell.

This is where the plan of the chapter gets simple. One slat per centile. Each slat is fitted alone, and none of them knows the others exist.

Now the fit, in one picture. First the median slat. It is a cubic curve of age, fitted to the two hundred and forty people of site A. Then the fifth centile, on its own. Then the ninety-fifth.

We can check the slats. The fifth centile slat leaves nine people of site A below it, and four exactly on it. The median leaves one hundred and seventeen below and one hundred and nineteen above. The ninety-fifth leaves eleven people above it, and four on it. This is only a check on the people we fitted, but it is what the recipe promises.

So what does it capture? Any shape of bell. Skewed, heavy-tailed, or both. The upper slat is free to sit far from the median, and the lower slat is free to sit close. There is no warp to choose and no family to name. Quantile regression has also been applied to growth charts. In 2006, a team that included Koenker himself compared it with a penalised likelihood method, on the data used for modern Finnish reference charts.

Now the pitfalls. There are three.

The first is data. A centile out in the tail is a statement about a few people. The two and a half percent line, at a given age, rests on very few of them. A model with a shape can borrow strength from the middle of the bell. This model borrows from no one. It needs more people, and the further out you go, the more.

The second is crossing. Each slat is fitted alone, so nothing forces the fifth to stay under the ninety-fifth, or the 2.5th under the fifth. We measured it. In this fit, the 2.5th and fifth lines swap places from about age seventy-four. The ninety-fifth and the 97.5th swap from about the same age. And at eighty, even the median and the seventy-fifth lines swap. Only nine of the two hundred and forty people at site A are older than seventy-four. A centile cannot be above a higher centile. When two lines cross, the model has contradicted itself, in exactly the place where the people are rare. There are repairs. One is to sort the crossed values back into order, a rearrangement proposed by Chernozhukov and colleagues in 2010. Another is to fit all the slats together, under a rule that forbids crossing. A repair makes the picture tidy. It does not add the missing people.

The third is the score. Quantile regression gives you centiles, not a z-score. A person lies between two of your slats, and you read a rank by interpolating. Beyond the outermost slat, you only know that the person is above it, or below it. Then, to speak in z-scores, you convert the centile with the Gaussian table of episode two point two. Minus 1.96 and plus 1.96 are the 2.5th and the 97.5th centiles. That conversion is honest as a label. It does not mean the data were Gaussian.

In the Information series, the surprise of an event is how unlikely it is. A rank far out in the tail is a very surprising rank, and it is the one this model knows least about.

So, to sum up. Quantile regression fits each centile on its own, assuming no shape for the bell. It captures any shape, but it needs many people, its lines can cross where people are rare, and it gives a centile, not a z-score.

Choosing a model

There is no best normative model, only a bet that can be defended and checked. Seven fits on the same simulated data agree in the middle of the age range and disagree at the edges and in the outer centiles. A grid of questions covers sample size, sites, shape, transfer, computing and software, and the choice is made before seeing patients.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we fitted one line for each centile, and assumed nothing about the shape of the bell. That was the seventh model of this chapter. Today we put them side by side, and ask the question that decides an analysis. Which one do I use?

We are back in the drafting room of the shipyard. On the wall hang seven plates, one per model. Bayesian linear regression. B-splines. Warped Bayesian linear regression. Gaussian process regression. Hierarchical Bayesian regression. GAMLSS. And quantile regression. Every plate shows the same people. This is the simulated cortical index again, in arbitrary units, with three sites and four hundred and fifty people. It is not real data.

Here is the intuition, and it is a little unfashionable. There is no best model. Each model is a bet. Bayesian linear regression bets on a Gaussian bell. A Gaussian process bets that people of close ages look alike. A hierarchical model bets that sites differ, but are related. GAMLSS bets that you can name a family of distributions. Quantile regression bets nothing on the shape, and pays for it in data. Choosing a model means choosing the bet you can defend for your data. And a bet can be checked. That is the whole job of this episode: to say what each bet costs, and how to check it before it matters.

So here is the fit in one picture. These seven fits are real ones, made on the simulated data with the published software: PCNtoolkit and scikit-learn in Python, and the gamlss package in R. Look along the seven plates. In the middle of the age range, from twenty-five to sixty-five, the median and the twenty-fifth and seventy-fifth centiles of the seven fits sit within about two units of each other. The outer centiles are already further apart, about six units. Now look at the edges, below fourteen and above seventy-four. There the lines fan apart, even the median, by more than six units, and the outer centiles by more than eleven. On the quantile regression plate, the top lines even cross after seventy-eight, because each centile is fitted on its own, and nothing keeps them in order. That is the pattern: the models agree in the middle, and disagree where the data are few.

We can see why without fitting anything, because in this simulation we know the truth. At age sixty, on site A, the true distribution is skewed. Take a Gaussian bell with exactly the right mean and exactly the right spread. It still misses the true median by about one point three units. It misses the 2.5th centile by about three point five units, and the 97.5th by about three. The shape of the bell matters most in the tails. And the tails are exactly where we read a patient. A person at the 97.5th centile is the one we call unusual. If the line sits three units too low, that person is called unusual too early.

The literature points the same way. In 2024, the CentileBrain team benchmarked eight algorithms on healthy people, thirty-seven thousand four hundred and seven of them, from eighty-seven datasets. Four of the eight gave comparable errors on the usual measures, which score the prediction of each person's value. The authors of the warped model say it in their own paper. Explained variance and the mean standardised log loss are not necessarily sensitive to the benefit of warping, which mostly affects the outer centiles. A model can win on the usual score, and still be wrong where you will read it.

Now, what does the choice capture? A grid of six questions.

One. How many people? With a few hundred, Gaussian process regression is a good fit, and it is the model of the founding paper, by Marquand and colleagues, in 2016. But for the exact version, the cost grows roughly with the cube of the number of people. With tens of thousands, the warped Bayesian linear regression was built for that case. Its authors tested it on about ten thousand UK Biobank participants.

Two. How many sites? With one scanner, you can skip this. With many, put the site inside the model. Hierarchical Bayesian regression does it without harmonising the data first, as Kia and colleagues showed in 2022.

Three. What shape is the bell? If it is nearly Gaussian, with a constant spread, a plain model is enough. If it is skewed, you need a warp, or GAMLSS, the framework that built the brain charts across the lifespan, in the Nature paper of Bethlehem and colleagues, in 2022. The hierarchical model also began with a Gaussian likelihood, and de Boer and colleagues gave it a sinh-arcsinh one in 2024. Quantile regression needs no shape at all, if you can afford the data.

Four. Must the model travel? If your controls come from one site and your patients from another, the model needs to be recalibrated with a handful of local people. The hierarchical model was designed for it. Its authors showed that a reference model can be reused on small local datasets.

Five. How much computing? The linear models are fast. Hierarchical models are sampled, and sampling is slow. In the 2024 paper, a Gaussian version took more than two hours per fold. The most flexible version took more than twenty-seven.

Six. What software do you have? As of November 2026, the documentation of the Python toolkit PCNtoolkit describes two models: Bayesian linear regression, and hierarchical Bayesian regression. GAMLSS lives in R, in the gamlss package. A model you cannot run reliably is not an option.

Now the pitfall. It has two parts.

The first part is the choice itself. If you pick the model after looking at the patients, the one that makes them look most different, then the norm has been tuned to the answer. The choice is made before. It is made on people the model has not seen, and it is judged on the outer centiles, not only on the middle. We come back to this in chapter seven. In the Information series, a score of this kind is called surprise. How unlikely did the model find each new person? A model with overconfident tails is surprised too often.

The second part is that flexibility is not free. The warped model's own authors write that non-Gaussian models can overfit. Remember our simulation, with only four people younger than fourteen, and thirteen aged seventy-four or older. A flexible model there does not learn the tail. It learns noise. And Gaussian process regression has its own trap. Xu and colleagues showed, in 2021, that its predictive variance comes from the kernel and from where the data sit, not from how much people really differ. It should not be the spread of a norm on its own.

So, to sum up. There is no best model, only a bet you can defend. Compare them on the same data, look at the tails, and choose before you see the patients.
