What can go wrong, technique by technique

Side effects of non-invasive stimulation in supervised research are mostly mild, and serious ones are rare, known and watched for, but each number depends on who counted. TMS carries a seizure risk of about eight per hundred thousand sessions, tDCS mostly skin effects, ultrasound heat and mechanical effects, and implanted electrodes surgical risks such as hemorrhage and infection.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Educational content, not medical advice. This chapter reports published rules and published numbers for supervised research. It gives no advice for personal use.

Welcome to the municipal pool. Before anyone gets in the water, the lifeguard checks the board by the edge. Today the board lists what can go wrong, technique by technique. Every line on it answers the same four questions. What happens? How often? Who counted? And when?

Here is the point to keep. In supervised research, most side effects of the non-invasive techniques are mild and short. The serious ones are rare, they are known, and they are watched for. But rare is not zero, and every number depends on who was counted and how.

Start with magnetic stimulation. The most serious risk is a seizure. In 2019, Lerner, Wassermann and Tamir surveyed active laboratories and clinics. A hundred and seventy-four groups reported about three hundred and eighteen thousand sessions, between 2012 and 2016. They counted twenty-four seizures. That is about eight per hundred thousand sessions.

That average hides two very different situations. When the stimulation followed the published guidelines and the person had no known risk factor, the survey found four seizures. That is fewer than one in sixty thousand sessions. When the person had a brain lesion, epilepsy, or a medication known to lower the seizure threshold, the 2021 safety update by Rossi and colleagues calculated about thirty-three per hundred thousand sessions from the same survey. Both figures come from a voluntary survey, so the authors call them approximate.

Two details matter. Over sixty-two percent of those seizures happened at the very first exposure, and three quarters within the first three. And a faint can look like a seizure. Syncope comes from low blood flow to the brain, often with stress, pain or dehydration. The guideline asks operators to tell the two apart. It gives no rate for syncope, so neither do we.

Milder effects are far more common. In a study of one hundred and thirteen healthy volunteers, Maizey and colleagues found mild effects, such as headache or nausea, after about five percent of sessions. Part of that may be coincidence rather than the coil.

Then there is the noise. The click of the coil is loud. Measured peaks reach between about one hundred and twenty-eight and one hundred and thirty-nine decibels, depending on the study and the way the sound is weighted. That is near the limit for impulse noise. With well-fitted earplugs, the studies reviewed in 2021 reported no change in hearing. One person had a small permanent shift in one ear after an earplug slipped out during stimulation. This is why hearing protection is in the guideline.

Now electrical stimulation through the scalp. The commonest effects are on the skin: itching, tingling, burning. In a review of two hundred and nine trials, published in 2011, Brunoni and colleagues pooled the studies that reported them. Itching was reported by thirty-nine percent of active groups and thirty-three percent of sham groups, a difference that was not significant. Tingling, twenty-two against eighteen percent. Headache, fifteen against sixteen. The sham group feels these things too, which is why expectation matters.

Skin burns are rare. The 2017 guidelines by Antal and colleagues name them as the main moderate event, and they trace them to poor contact between the electrode and the skin. The same guidelines report no serious adverse event in over eighteen thousand sessions. The 2016 review by Bikson and colleagues counts over thirty-three thousand sessions in about a thousand people, again with none. The count depends on the review and the date, and both say that absence of a report is not proof of absence. The 2017 guidelines also list eleven documented cases of mania or hypomania in patients with depression, with a cause that is hard to prove.

Focused ultrasound brings two physical risks. The first is heat, and it builds up where the energy is absorbed, especially in bone and skin. The second is a mechanical effect. A sound wave pulls on tissue with a negative pressure, and at high levels it can form tiny bubbles. The mechanical index grows with that pressure and falls with the frequency.

In 2025, the ITRUSST consortium published a consensus on levels of non-significant risk. The mechanical index should not exceed one point nine. For heating, meeting any one of three kinds of level is enough. A temperature level: a rise of at most two degrees, or a peak of at most thirty-nine degrees. A thermal dose, in equivalent minutes at forty-three degrees: at most two in brain, sixteen in bone and twenty-one in skin. Or a value of the thermal index. The authors write that these are not safety limits as such, and that more data are needed. Human data are still thin. A 2019 review by Pasquinelli and colleagues kept thirty-three studies, in humans and animals. Thirty of them found no adverse effect, even when a regulatory index was exceeded. Two reported tiny hemorrhages after long or intense stimulation above the safety limits.

Last, implanted electrodes. Here the main risks come from surgery. A 2023 meta-analysis by Rasiah and colleagues pooled twenty-one thousand patients with Parkinson's disease, followed on average for twenty-six months. Hemorrhage, two point four percent. Infection, four point two. Revision surgery, four point nine. Seizure, one point nine. The hemorrhage risk rose with the number of microelectrode recording tracks. A 2018 review by Engel and colleagues gives intracranial complications between three point four and three point eight percent, depending on how the studies are counted. Their own centre saw two small hemorrhages in a hundred and twenty-three patients, both with transient deficits. In a randomised trial, Weaver and colleagues reported a serious adverse event in forty-nine of a hundred and twenty-one implanted patients within six months. Thirty-nine serious events were related to the surgery, and one patient died of a cerebral hemorrhage.

After surgery, there are the effects of the stimulation itself. Tingling, speech changes, mood changes, eye movements. They depend on which contact is active and on the amplitude. In a 2017 review, Dayal and colleagues report, from one small testing study, that about one patient in four had speech problems at some setting. The review describes sensory effects as fading within seconds at a steady setting, and mood effects as responding to a lower amplitude or another contact. Effects from the motor pathway tend to persist, so the setting has to change. That is what programming is for.

Back at the pool. The board shows a pattern. Frequent effects are mild. Serious effects are rare, with sources, dates and populations attached. Who may enter the water at all is another question, and the next episode takes it up. The published guideline texts themselves get their own episode after that.

Contraindications and screening

Screening is part of the protocol and is written down: each answer sends a person to stop, ask or note. Where a condition falls depends on the technique and on the edition of the text. TMS, electrical stimulation, ultrasound and implants each have their own list, and a yes usually opens a risk and benefit discussion rather than a ban.

Educational content, not medical advice. This chapter reports published rules for supervised research. It gives no advice for personal use.

Back at the municipal pool. Last time, the board listed what can go wrong. Today the lifeguard holds a clipboard, and the question comes before the water. Who should not go on this ride at all?

Think of the signs at a funfair. Every ride posts its own list. A visitor can pass one ride and be stopped at the next. Stimulation works the same way. The list depends on the technique. It also depends on which text you read, and when it was written.

Here is the point to keep. Screening is not a formality. It is part of the protocol, and it is written down. The questions, the answers, who read them, and who decided. The tES form even leaves a line for a signature.

Each answer lands in one of three places. Stop: an absolute contraindication. Ask: a relative one, where a named person weighs risk against benefit. Or note: a precaution, recorded and watched. The texts do not always sort a condition into the same place.

Start with magnetic stimulation. In 2009, Rossi, Hallett, Rossini and Pascual-Leone published the consensus guidelines. They named one absolute contraindication: metal hardware in close contact with the coil. Examples were cochlear implants, implanted pulse generators and medication pumps. The reason is physical. The pulse induces currents in metal, and an implanted device can malfunction.

The same text listed conditions of increased or uncertain risk. A personal history of epilepsy. A brain lesion. Drugs that lower the seizure threshold. Sleep deprivation and alcoholism. Implanted brain electrodes. Pregnancy. Severe or recent heart disease.

Its screening form asked about epilepsy, fainting, head injury, hearing, pregnancy, metal in the skull, cochlear implants, implanted stimulators, pacemakers and medications. A yes was not a ban. The principal investigator, or the treating physician, had to weigh risk and benefit. In 2011, the authors published a revised version of the form.

Then the field moved. In 2021, an update led by Rossi, with Antal, Bestmann, Bikson and colleagues, left the 2009 screening advice in place. But it changed several positions. On drugs, it said the earlier caution was no longer supported by the data on seizures. It still asks teams to document drugs, sleep deprivation, infection and alcohol. And if several transient factors add up, it says the session could be postponed.

On pregnancy, it found no study designed to test safety. It reviewed twelve reports, fifty patients in total, and cautiously concluded that figure-of-eight coil stimulation is minimal risk. It adds that safety data for the H-coil are not yet available.

On a history of epilepsy, and on ferromagnetic metal implants inside the skull, it says they are typically exclusionary in basic research, but not absolutely contraindicated. On pacemakers, a figure-of-eight coil is considered safe if it is not fired within ten centimetres of the device. For cochlear implants, it says to avoid TMS unless a further safety evaluation shows it is safe. Deep brain stimulators and cortical electrodes need a justified reason, a pre-specified protocol and ethics oversight.

Now transcranial electrical stimulation. Antal and colleagues published their guidelines in 2017. Their form has ten questions. Metal or electronic implants in the skull. Devices elsewhere in the body. Head or spinal surgery. Head injury. Skin problems. Epilepsy. Fainting. Pregnancy. Medications. Earlier stimulation. Skin is on this list because the current enters through it. And again, a yes is not an absolute contraindication. It starts a risk and benefit discussion.

The metal question is not the TMS question. Bikson and colleagues reviewed tDCS safety in 2016. They note that excluding people with head implants is common, as a precaution. But they found no report of injury, and models predict no extra risk from a head implant. A concern about pacemakers is higher when electrodes sit off the head, but it remains hypothetical. On pregnancy, Antal reports only two case reports, and notes that fields fade quickly with distance.

Now ultrasound. The consensus of the ITRUSST group, published in Brain Stimulation in 2025, sets levels for nonsignificant biophysical risk. But it assumes people without contraindications, with normal thermoregulation, without vascular vulnerabilities, and without ultrasound contrast agents. It does not list the contraindications. It leaves them to the ethics committee, and says later reports will address them. For this technique, the sign is still being written.

Last, implanted systems. Take deep brain stimulation. In 2001, Nutt and colleagues described a man with implanted electrodes who received diathermy, a heating treatment, on his upper jaw. He was left with permanent lesions in the brainstem and deep brain. The authors suspected that the electrodes had heated. A 2021 review by Rahimpour and colleagues adds a case of heating during an MRI of the spine. That review notes that diathermy is a common contraindication for people with metal implants. It is also why makers publish MRI conditions for each system.

So here is the classic trap. A clean form is not a guarantee, and a yes is not always a ban. The form is a tool for a decision. And the decision depends on the technique, the text, and its date. That is why a protocol names the version it follows.

To sum up. Screening is part of the protocol, and it is documented. Absolute, relative or precaution depends on the technique and the text. TMS, electrical stimulation, ultrasound and implants each have their own sign. Next: the published safety criteria, the consensus texts themselves, on the shelf.

Published safety criteria

Each technique has its own safety rulebook, printed in editions: magnetic stimulation in 1998, 2009 and 2021, electrical stimulation in 2017 and 2026, ultrasound in 2024 and 2025 and still being written, while implants follow device labelling. These are expert consensus, not licences, and editions differ in details such as maximum duration. A protocol should name the text it follows and its year.

Back at the municipal pool. The first board listed what can go wrong. The clipboard asked who should not go in. Today the lifeguard opens the rulebook itself. Who wrote it? When? And what does it actually set?

Think of a board game. Every game has its own rulebook, printed in editions. A new edition can replace pages, or only add an appendix. Before a match, the referee writes one line on the score card: rules followed, edition and year. Published safety criteria work the same way. One rulebook for each technique. And each protocol names the edition it follows.

One thing first. A guideline is not a regulatory status. It does not license a device, and it does not approve a use. These texts are expert consensus. They are what an ethics board reads.

Start with magnetic stimulation. The first edition is Wassermann, 1998, in Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology. It came from a workshop in June 1996. By then, seven seizures from repetitive stimulation were known. The text set limits on the stimulation parameters. It also covered monitoring, the expertise of the team, the management of a seizure, and contraindications.

The second edition came in 2009. Rossi, Hallett, Rossini and Pascual-Leone wrote it, after a conference in Siena in March 2008. Manufacturers and regulators attended. Its best known page is a table. It gives the longest safe single train, by frequency and by intensity, for a figure-of-eight coil. At twenty hertz and a hundred and ten percent of motor threshold, the table says one point six seconds. At ten hertz, five seconds was simply the longest duration tested. The same text says that the data on theta burst were not yet enough to claim or deny a hazard.

The third edition came in 2021. Rossi and thirty-six colleagues wrote it, after a new workshop in Siena in October 2018. The work ran until April 2020. Notice what it did not do. It left the 2009 pages on screening and on conventional protocols in force, and covered only new issues. New devices and pulse patterns, including theta burst. The duties of device makers. A summary of the minimum training for operators. And a note on the ethics of neuroenhancement. The authors also write that they could not rate levels of evidence, because virtually no study was designed to assess safety. As of November 2026, no newer edition was found.

So a TMS protocol reads two texts together. The newer one says which pages of the older one still stand. Screening forms live in these texts too, and the last episode covered them.

Now electrical stimulation. In 2016, Bikson and colleagues published an evidence review of tDCS safety. Over thirty-three thousand sessions, and over a thousand people with repeated sessions. No serious adverse effect. It defined conventional tDCS as up to four milliamps, up to forty minutes, and up to seven point two coulombs.

In 2017, Antal and colleagues wrote the consensus guidelines, after a conference in Göttingen in September 2016. They used a modified Delphi process. They defined low-intensity stimulation as below four milliamps, up to sixty minutes a day, with electrodes from one to a hundred square centimetres, at frequencies up to ten kilohertz. They also added structured interviews, and sections on ethics, regulation and reporting. Look at the two definitions. One says forty minutes. The other says sixty. The version matters.

In April 2026, an update appeared, in the same journal. Sixty-two authors, endorsed by the European Society for Brain Stimulation and by the IFCN. It reviews data from 2017 to 2025. It reports more than three hundred thousand sessions without a serious event related to the stimulation. It keeps four milliamps and sixty minutes a day as the range where safety is well established. And it widens the scope: temporal interference, home-based use, training.

Now ultrasound, the youngest of the three. The texts come from one group, the ITRUSST consortium, in Brain Stimulation. In 2024, a reporting guide: a checklist of six groups of parameters. The transducer and its drive system. The drive settings. The free-field acoustic parameters. The pulse timing. Estimates of exposure in the brain. And the intensity.

In 2025, the biophysical safety consensus. Eighteen authors, received in May, accepted in October. It sets levels of non-significant risk, for low-intensity stimulation at roughly two hundred to eight hundred kilohertz. For the mechanical effects, the mechanical index must not exceed one point nine. For heating, one of three conditions is enough. A temperature rise of at most two degrees, or a peak of at most thirty-nine. A thermal dose of at most two, sixteen or twenty-one equivalent minutes, in brain, bone and skin. Or a limit on the exposure time, set by the thermal index.

The authors write that these are not safety limits. They write that the text will be updated. And they leave the physiological effects to a future report.

The figure one point nine is also the ceiling that the US regulator sets for diagnostic imaging machines. Its guidance, issued in 2019 and revised in February 2023, adds intensity ceilings, such as seven hundred and twenty milliwatts per square centimetre. Those are rules for imaging devices. They are not a consensus for stimulation.

Last, implanted systems. As of November 2026, no consensus text of this kind was found. The labelling of each system governs, including its conditions for MRI.

Here is the moment to keep. These texts evolve. Magnetic stimulation: 1998, 2009, 2021. Electrical stimulation: 2017, then 2026. Ultrasound: 2024 and 2025, still being written. A protocol that says only "we followed the safety guidelines" says little. One that names the text and its year can be checked by an ethics board.

The classic trap is to cite from memory, or to cite the oldest text found first. Each edition says which of its pages still stand. Read the edition, then write its year.

To sum up. One rulebook for each technique. Each new edition replaces pages or adds an appendix. A consensus is expert opinion, not a licence. And a protocol names the edition it follows.

Consumer devices, enhancement and ethics

The label on a brain-stimulation product decides which rulebook applies, and the European Union, the United States and Canada each open a different door. A large pool of studies found no reliable enhancement in healthy people, though other analyses are mixed, and ethical questions stay open on enhancement, consent and personality after an implant. Research adds screening, sham and ethics review.

Back at the municipal pool. Near the entrance, a stall has opened. Its sign says: brain boosters. Headbands, caps, a small box with a dial. The promise is sharper thinking and a better memory. No brand here, and no shopping guidance. Three questions instead. Which rules cover a stall like this? What do the studies say? And what is left that science cannot answer for us?

Picture an inspector's window beside the stall. In every country, the inspector reads the same thing first: the label. What does the box claim to do? And what could it do to the body? The answer decides which rulebook applies.

Start with the European Union. The Medical Devices Regulation of 2017 has a list called Annex sixteen. It names products with no medical purpose that are regulated as if they had one. Item six is equipment for brain stimulation: electrical currents, or magnetic fields, that penetrate the skull to modify neuronal activity.

The common specifications for that item were adopted on the first of December 2022, and apply from the twenty-second of June 2023. They cover tDCS, tACS, tRNS and TMS, and leave out invasive devices. A maker must analyse psychological risks, cognitive trade-offs, and the effects of repeated use. Unless there is specific evidence of safe use, people with a history of epilepsy, people under eighteen, and pregnant women are excluded by default. The label must say which non-medical effect can be expected.

A companion regulation, of the same date, puts this equipment in class three, the highest class, so a notified body must assess it. Its reasoning: such changes can last, and unintended effects may be difficult to reverse. Products already on the market before June 2023 have transition periods, which run to the end of 2028 or 2029 as amended in 2023. As of November 2026, no later change to these rules was found.

Now the United States. Here the claim is what counts. The F-D-A reissued its general wellness guidance on the sixth of January 2026, replacing the 2019 version. For low-risk products that promise only general wellness, the agency does not intend to examine whether they are devices at all. Mental acuity and learning capacity are on its list of wellness claims.

But the guidance applies a risk test first, and gives an example of a product that fails it: a neurostimulation product that claims to improve memory, because of the risks of electrical stimulation. It also says that falling under this policy does not show a product to be safe or effective.

As of November 2026, a search of the agency's public clearance and De Novo records, by device name, found no transcranial direct current stimulator. The brain stimulators it has authorised carry medical claims. A cranial stimulator for insomnia or anxiety is class two. In December 2025, one was approved for adults with major depression, as a class three device.

Canada. The Food and Drugs Act defines a device by what it is made, sold or represented for. One listed purpose is restoring, modifying or correcting the functioning of any part of the body. As of November 2026, no rule specific to non-medical brain stimulators was found. Searched by name, the Health Canada licence database returns tDCS licences in class two, which are medical device licences.

Three countries, three different doors. And in each of them, the label opens the door.

Now the three questions. They are posed here, not answered.

The first: cognitive enhancement in healthy people. The 2021 magnetic stimulation guideline already had a section on the ethics of neuroenhancement. In 2013, the Nuffield Council on Bioethics named non-medical applications as one of its five areas of policy. If a small gain existed, who would have access to it? Who would feel pressure to take it? What would be traded for it?

The second: consent. Who can give it? A person in distress, hoping for relief. A teenager. Someone whose school or workplace expects results. The European specifications exclude under eighteens by default. Episode 11.5 takes up children and adolescents.

The third: patients with an implant. Schüpbach and colleagues followed twenty-nine people with Parkinson disease, before and eighteen to twenty-four months after subthalamic stimulation. Movement improved markedly. Social adjustment did not. Marital conflicts occurred in seventeen of twenty-four couples.

A 2014 review by Castrioto and colleagues explains part of it. Stimulating this nucleus can release a brake on behaviour, and lowering drug doses can unveil apathy, anxiety or depression. Adjusting stimulation and drugs together helps. And in 2018, Gilbert and colleagues screened more than fifteen hundred articles on whether this kind of stimulation changes personality and identity. They found few primary studies, and wrote that the debate rests on limited evidence. The concern remains.

So, when a person feels changed, who decides whether it is a side effect or a new self? And what should consent cover before the operation?

Here is the moment to keep. First, what the evidence says about enhancement. In 2015, Horvath, Forte and Carter pooled single-session tDCS studies in healthy adults. In fifty-nine analyses, none showed a significant effect, including working memory and language production. Episode 13.3 followed this literature. Other analyses were more mixed, so the verdict depends on the analysis. It does not say that stimulation never works. It says that this pool found no reliable gain in healthy people.

Second, why self-stimulation outside research is not a cheaper version of research. In 2016, Wurzman and colleagues wrote an open letter to do-it-yourself users in the Annals of Neurology. The current spreads through the head, and effects can reach connected networks. What a person does during stimulation may change the result. Gaining one ability may cost another. Small changes in current or duration can change the effect. Responses vary between people. And healthy people have less to gain. Two authors disclosed ties to a device maker.

The same year, Wexler studied this community. Its members build and share knowledge of their own, and also borrow from the journals, with the electrode placement system of scientists. They cope with the limits of self-experimentation as best they can.

Now compare. Research does not have a different current. It has a frame: screening before the first session, a sham condition, a protocol written beforehand, an ethics board, and a record of every adverse event. Take the frame away, and what remains is the current.

The classic trap is to read a label as a test. A wellness label, or a regulatory class, is a regulatory status. It is not a result. The F-D-A text says so itself.

To sum up. The label decides which rulebook applies, and the answer differs by country. Three ethical questions stay open: enhancement, consent, and the person after an implant. A large pool found no reliable enhancement in healthy people, and other analyses are mixed. And the frame is what research adds to the current.

Stimulating children and adolescents

The same stimulator setting gives a different field in a child's head, with thinner skulls producing higher modelled fields, so a child is not a small adult. The developing brain is the very thing being modulated. Safety data from supervised paediatric use are mostly reassuring but thinner, authorisations are few and carry age limits, and minors need parental permission, their own assent and follow-up.

Back at the municipal pool. Beside the big pool there is a small one, for children. It has its own depth marks. The red line painted for adults would be wrong here. Today's question: is a child just a small adult? And what do the published texts say?

Pour the same bucket of water into both pools. In the small pool, the level climbs higher. Stimulation has the same trap. The same setting on the stimulator does not give the same field in two different heads. The bucket is schematic. The next numbers are not.

Two heads, in profile. A child and an adult. The same dose card: transcranial direct current stimulation of the motor cortex, two pads of five by five centimetres, one milliamp.

In 2018, Ciechanski and colleagues built a model of each person's head from an MRI scan. Fifty-one people. Nineteen children, aged six to twelve. Sixteen adolescents. Sixteen adults. For the montage with the anode over the right motor cortex, the mean field at the motor cortex was zero point one nine volts per metre in children. Zero point one five in adolescents. Zero point one three in adults. Children differed from both other groups. Adolescents and adults did not differ significantly.

Part of the reason is the skull. Under the motor cortex, it measured five point two millimetres in children, and seven point two in adults. The authors also point to the fluid space around the brain, and to changes in gray and white matter.

Five years earlier, Kessler and colleagues had modelled two children, aged eight and twelve, and three adults. Skull thickness was two point eight and three point one millimetres in the children, and four point three to four point eight in the adults. On average, children received higher peak fields for the same current. But the ranges overlapped with adults who have smaller heads. And the two children had different peaks, although their heads were similar in size. The authors wrote that the field cannot be predicted by skull thickness alone.

For magnetic stimulation, a 2023 modelling study by Alawi and colleagues compared children aged four to twelve, adults, and elderly people, with a figure-of-eight coil over the prefrontal cortex. The peak field fell as age rose, and children showed the widest spread over the cortex.

Everything on these maps is modelled. None of it is measured in the brain. It is a calculation with assumptions, for these heads, and these montages.

Now the second half of the picture. The developing brain is still wiring itself. That plasticity is exactly what stimulation tries to modulate. In 2012, Cohen Kadosh and colleagues asked what it would mean to use direct current stimulation to enhance learning in children, with typical and atypical development. In 2021, the magnetic stimulation guidelines add a sober reason for care. A serious adverse event could affect a developing nervous system, and result in lifelong impairments.

So what do the safety data say? In 2015, Krishnan and colleagues reviewed forty-eight studies, with more than five hundred children and adolescents aged two and a half to seventeen. After magnetic stimulation, headache was the most common event, at eleven point five percent. After transcranial current stimulation, tingling led, also at eleven point five percent. Serious events were relatively few, and the authors asked for longer follow-up.

In 2020, Zewdie and colleagues reported one centre, three hundred and eighty-four children, and about three and a half million stimulations. No serious adverse event, and no seizure. The authors judged standard paradigms minimal risk. The 2021 guideline counts more than a hundred studies since 2009, and about two thousand children under eighteen. For single and paired magnetic pulses, with suitable hearing protection, it concludes safe from age two. Below two, the hearing data are not available.

And the 2026 update of the electrical stimulation guidelines reports that adverse events in children look comparable to those in other groups.

Look at what this says, and what it does not. It says that within supervised paradigms, the events reported were mostly mild. It does not say that a dose is validated for each age. A 2016 review by Palm and colleagues reports two statements side by side. Models suggest that half the adult current gives similar peak fields in a child. And some studies report standard adult parameters used safely. Neither statement is a dose. And the same review finds a lack of data for most psychiatric disorders of childhood and adolescence.

Now the regulators. Each stamp is a status, not a recommendation, as of November 2026.

Magnetic stimulation. In the United States, two clearances carry an indication for adolescents. K two three one nine two six, in March 2024, and K two five one three nine one, in November 2025. Both: adjunct treatment of major depressive disorder in patients aged fifteen to twenty-one. In Canada, the licence database lists magnetic stimulation systems, in class three. It shows no indication and no age limit, so an adolescent indication could not be established. For Europe, the CE records were not searched for this episode.

Deep brain stimulation. In the United States, a humanitarian device exemption, H zero two zero zero zero seven, approved in April 2003. Chronic, intractable primary dystonia, in patients seven years of age or older. That route requires safety and probable benefit, not proof of effectiveness. In Canada, deep brain stimulators hold class four licences, with no age limit shown.

Vagus nerve stimulation. This stimulates a nerve in the neck, not the brain itself. Approved in the United States in 1997. A supplement in June 2017 set the age at four and older, as adjunctive therapy for partial onset seizures that resist antiepileptic drugs. In Canada, a class four licence is active, with no indication shown.

One more: external trigeminal nerve stimulation, with electrodes on the forehead. Also not brain stimulation in the strict sense. The F-D-A granted a De Novo authorisation in April 2019, for paediatric A-D-H-D, as monotherapy, in children aged seven to twelve. In Canada, the licence record ends in July 2019. No active licence was found.

No other paediatric authorisation was found as of November 2026, in the searches made for this episode.

Now the ethics that belong to minors. In the United States, the American Academy of Pediatrics describes two things, in its 2016 policy. Parental permission, and the child's assent. As children mature, their views count more, in clinical practice and in research. The 2021 magnetic stimulation guideline says that children cannot give informed consent. It adds that research without direct benefit should come only after safety has been studied in adults. And it notes that local law, or the labelling of a device, may limit research with minors.

Four questions, then, before a protocol. Is the benefit expected for this child? Did the parents give permission? Did the child give assent, in words fitted to age? And who follows the child after the last session? Episode 11.4 noted that the European consumer rules exclude under eighteens by default. And episode 7.2 showed that adult heads already differ. In children, the models add one more source of difference.

The classic trap is to treat a child as a small adult. A simple rule that shrinks the current with head size does not follow from these models. The field depends on the head, and on the montage.

To sum up. The same setting gives a different field in a child. The developing brain is the very thing being modulated. Safety data are reassuring but thinner. Authorisations are few, with age limits. And the child needs permission, assent, and follow-up.
