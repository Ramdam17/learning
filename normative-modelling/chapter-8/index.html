Brain charts for the human lifespan

Brain charts for the human lifespan pool about 124,000 MRI scans from over one hundred studies into one reference, built with GAMLSS so that location, scale and shape vary with age and each study gets its own offset. The charts give milestones, such as peaks of grey and white matter, and comparable centiles. The authors list limits: sampling bias, thin ages, no single-scan diagnosis.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ran a fair contest between models. The same lot of weights for everyone, the same splits, the same measures, and a simple baseline to beat. Today we visit a model that no single lab could have built. Welcome to the container port, and to its biggest ship.

A growth chart for height starts at birth and stops in childhood. For the brain, nothing like it existed, from before birth to old age. In 2022, a large international team published one in Nature. The title is simple. Brain charts for the human lifespan.

Look at the cargo. The team pooled one hundred twenty-three thousand, nine hundred eighty-four MRI scans, from more than one hundred primary studies. Those scans belong to one hundred one thousand, four hundred fifty-seven people. The youngest are one hundred fifteen days after conception. The oldest are one hundred years old.

Each study is a container, and each container has its manifest. Its own scanners, its own age range, its own protocol. This is the site problem of chapter five, multiplied by more than one hundred studies.

So how do you build one norm from all of that? With GAMLSS. The letters stand for generalized additive models for location, scale and shape. Location is where the middle of the distribution sits. Scale is how wide it is. Shape is how skewed it is, and how heavy the tails are. In GAMLSS, all three can depend on age. For the brain volumes here, the middle and the width do. The authors chose this framework because the World Health Organization recommends it for growth charts. It is the chapter four idea, pushed to the limit.

For the brain volumes, they used a family of distributions called the generalized gamma, which can be skewed. Age enters through flexible curves called fractional polynomials. And they fitted separate charts for females and for males, as paediatric charts do.

Every study also gets its own offset. Remember our simulated example, where site B reads about three units higher than site A. Here the model absorbs that kind of shift, study by study, as a random effect. The version of the processing software is a term in the model too. In the fMRI series, you saw why that matters.

Now read the charts. Grey matter volume rises quickly from mid-gestation, and peaks at five point nine years old. After that, it falls almost in a straight line. That peak comes two to three years later than earlier reports, which relied on smaller samples and narrower age ranges.

White matter takes much longer. It peaks at twenty-eight point seven years, and declines faster after fifty. Subcortical grey matter sits in between, with a peak at fourteen point four years, in adolescence.

Two global measures peak early. Mean cortical thickness peaks at one point seven years. Total surface area peaks near eleven years.

The charts also give the speed of change. Grey matter grows fastest around five months of age. White matter grows fastest around two point four years. The curve tells you where a brain is, and the speed tells you how quickly it is moving.

The ventricles tell another story. They grow until about two years old, stay on a plateau until about thirty, then rise slowly. In the sixth decade, the rise becomes exponential.

Notice the width of the bands too. The spread between people is not constant. It changes with age, and the model estimates it explicitly. This is why a model of the mean alone would not be enough.

The authors also write that the brain reaches about ten percent of its maximum size before seventeen weeks after conception, and about eighty percent by age three. Most of the growth happens early.

Let us place one person on this chart. An illustrative person, not a real one. Say a volume sits on the fifteenth centile for her age and sex. It means that about fifteen in one hundred people in the reference have a smaller volume. It does not mean that anything is wrong. We saw this in the chapter on deviations. A centile on a lifespan chart is read exactly like a centile on a growth chart, and it asks the same care.

Now, the key moment. What do these charts give us?

First, milestones. The authors report peaks that had not been reported before, such as an early period when grey matter takes over from white matter as the dominant tissue, in the first month after birth. A reference this wide can show where a trajectory turns.

Second, centiles that can be compared across studies. A new study is aligned to the reference by estimating its own offsets, with maximum likelihood. The authors found that more than one hundred scans were enough for stable estimates. This is the transfer of episode seven point four, at a larger scale.

Third, the centile scores are stable. When the same person is scanned again, the score hardly moves. They are also more heritable than the raw volumes, by almost twelve percentage points on average. And they relate to birth. Greater gestational age and higher birth weight went with higher centile scores, even in adults.

Fourth, disorders sit on one common scale. Alzheimer's disease showed the biggest difference from controls. Schizophrenia ranked third, behind Alzheimer's and mild cognitive impairment. The differences were largest in late adulthood, and in adolescence. Those are results for groups, not for one person.

The team also released the charts as an open online tool, called BrainChart. Its goal is to benchmark any current or future sample of MRI data against this reference.

Now the limits, in the authors' own words.

Even this large dataset was biased towards European and North American populations, and towards European ancestry groups within them. Some ages were thin. Foetal, newborn, and mid-adulthood, between thirty and forty. Only about one scan in ten came from a longitudinal study, so the curves come from people scanned once. And the model cannot fix every flaw of the original studies, such as how patients were recruited, or how a diagnosis was made.

The authors also write that the analogy with paediatric growth charts does not mean that brain charts are ready to diagnose an individual patient. They expect that considerable further research is needed. And their results do not yet support centile scores from a single patient's scan. That was their position in 2022. Before trusting any chart, read what its authors say about it.

So, to sum up. More than one hundred studies, one chart, and a clear list of limits. Brain charts give us milestones and comparable centiles, and they tell us who is missing.

The big cohorts

Cohorts arrive like containers with a manifest: ages, country, recruitment, modality. UK Biobank starts at forty and had about 5.5 percent participation, ABCD starts at nine or ten, and between twenty-one and thirty-five these cohorts offer almost nobody. Size says how many people were measured, not who they are, so a norm built on a narrow crowd bends the image.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we looked at the biggest cargo in the port: brain charts for the whole human lifespan, built from more than one hundred studies. Today we walk down the quay, and open the manifests of some of the cohorts that feed charts like these. Who is on board, how old they are, where they come from, and how they got here.

First container. UK Biobank.

About half a million people, aged forty to sixty-nine when they joined. They were recruited between two thousand six and two thousand ten, through twenty-two assessment centres in England, Scotland and Wales. Brain imaging came later. It began in twenty fourteen, in dedicated imaging centres, and the project announced in July twenty twenty-five that it had reached one hundred thousand people scanned.

Now read the small print of the manifest. About nine point two million people were invited, and about five point five percent took part. Compared with those who did not, they were older, more often women, and lived in less deprived areas. Fry and colleagues, in twenty seventeen, call this healthy volunteer selection bias. Volunteers are not a random draw.

Then came the scans. In the first batch of nearly fifty thousand people scanned, the average age was sixty-four. Among those who came to be scanned, three percent were current smokers, against eleven percent of those who did not come. And three percent reported a non-white ethnic background, against six percent.

The authors of the imaging paper say it plainly. The cohort is not representative of the UK population. But its size still allows valid comparisons between measures. Keep that sentence in mind. A cohort can be valid for comparing people inside it, and still be a poor mirror of a whole population. A norm is a mirror held up to one person. If it was built from a narrow crowd, it bends the image.

Second container. ABCD, the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study.

Nearly twelve thousand children, all aged nine or ten when they enter. Twenty-one sites across the United States. The children were found through schools, sampled by probability, to look like the country's children. The sites were not drawn at random, but together their catchment areas cover more than twenty percent of American children of that age. Each child is followed for about ten years, with brain imaging every other year.

Twenty-one sites means twenty-one scanners, each with its own habits. In the fMRI series, we saw that a scanner leaves a fingerprint on the data. Here, every site brings its own. Chapter five is about that.

Third container, with two boxes. The Human Connectome Project, in its development and aging versions. The development project planned to enrol more than thirteen hundred healthy participants, aged five to twenty-one. The aging project planned more than twelve hundred, aged thirty-six to one hundred and over. Those are plans, from the protocol paper of twenty eighteen. Notice the gap between twenty-one and thirty-six.

Now put the three manifests side by side. ABCD starts at nine or ten years. UK Biobank starts at forty. Between twenty-one and thirty-five, these three cohorts offer almost nobody. Each cohort covers a slice of life, and a slice of society.

So here is the key moment. The size of a cohort tells you how many people were measured. It does not tell you who they are. A reference made of forty thousand people can still be thin at some ages, in some countries, in some social groups. The authors of the brain charts say the same about their own data. MRI studies carry known biases, compared with the diversity of the world's population. The number on the container is not the manifest.

You can see this in our own data. This is the simulated dataset of this series, labelled simulated. It has four hundred and fifty people, aged eight to eighty. That sounds like a lot. But only four of them are younger than fourteen. Thirteen are older than seventy-four. And only twenty-six are between fifty-four and sixty-two.

The same happens with real data. The transfer set that we use for real examples comes from the braincharts project. Its test file holds four hundred and thirty-six people, from six open datasets, aged from five point four to seventy-three. Yet only twenty-eight of them are forty-five or older, and all twenty-eight come from one of the six datasets.

Where data are thin, a centile line is a guess. That is what the blue mist, in our charts, is there to show. We will come back to who is missing, and why it matters, in chapter eleven.

So, to sum up. A cohort comes with a manifest: ages, country, recruitment, modality. Read it before you read the size. Big does not mean representative.

Pretrained models you can reuse

Fitted normative models can be borrowed, such as the braincharts models and CentileBrain, each with its own licence. Running one means matching column names, checking that columns hold values, and learning the new site from adaptation people kept apart from test people, since overlap made some children look too ordinary. A reused model brings its software, atlas and population along.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we opened the manifests of the big cohorts. Who they scanned, where, and at what age. Today a container comes off the ship that holds something different. Not people. Models that are already fitted.

Here is the idea. Building a norm takes a large reference sample, a long fitting, and a lot of care. Someone has already done that work, and shared the result. So you can unload it on your own computer, and use it.

Let us look at the best known one. In two thousand twenty-two, Saige Rutherford and colleagues published a paper in a journal called eLife, with the title "Charting brain growth and aging at high spatial precision". They fitted normative models on fifty-eight thousand, eight hundred and thirty-six people, scanned at eighty-two sites. Their ages run from two to one hundred years.

The models describe cortical thickness and the volume of subcortical structures, region by region. The cortex is cut with the Destrieux atlas. The images were processed with FreeSurfer, version six.

The model itself is one we know by now. A Bayesian linear regression, with a B-spline of age. A sinh-arcsinh warp bends the scale, so that skewed data can still be read as a bell. Age, sex and site are the covariates. Site is treated as a fixed effect.

And now the part that matters most for you. The authors shared all of it. The fitted models and the code to transfer them to new sites are on GitHub. The models are also on Zenodo, with a version number and an open licence. And a notebook lets you run the whole thing in the cloud, without installing anything.

The same group went one step further. A web portal, called PCNportal, lets you upload a table, and get deviation scores back, without writing code. As described in its two thousand twenty-three paper, it offers models for volume, cortical thickness, surface area, and resting-state connectivity. It runs on a remote server, and according to that paper, your files are deleted after thirty days.

The repository also keeps the notebook for surface area, and one for connectivity between networks. In the fMRI series, a structural image is segmented into numbers. Here, a table of such numbers is exactly what the model expects as input.

Braincharts is not the only shared model. In two thousand twenty-two, Richard Bethlehem and colleagues published brain charts in Nature, built from a hundred and one thousand people, from before birth to one hundred years. Their fitted models are on GitHub, under a licence that forbids commercial use and modified versions. In two thousand twenty-four, the CentileBrain project, from the ENIGMA consortium, shared models fitted on thirty-seven thousand healthy people aged three to ninety, through a web portal, for research use. And the PCNtoolkit group keeps adding models, now for white matter too, with no licence written next to the files. So before you pick one, read its licence, the way you would read its label. For the rest of this episode, we stay with braincharts.

Now let us run it, on data we can show. This is the transfer set that the authors ship with the models. It comes from OpenNeuro. Six datasets, and one table of regional thicknesses. The paper counts five hundred and forty-six people. The files hold five hundred and forty-six rows: a hundred and ten for adaptation, and four hundred and thirty-six for testing. But rows are not people. In one dataset of young children, twenty-eight children appear twice. Once per visit, with two ages about a year apart, and exactly the same brain measures. So the table holds five hundred and eighteen different people. We cite it as the braincharts transfer data. And this time, we load the model of the newer tutorial, and run it with PCNtoolkit, version one point four.

First step. Your table must look like the model's table. Same column names. Same covariates. Age and sex. And a site name. Here the files hid a trap. For ten of the hundred and fifty regions, the name the model expects points to a column of zeros. The real values sit under a slightly different spelling. Matching names is not enough. Check that the column holds values.

Second step. If your site was not in the training set, the model has never met your scanner. So it must learn your site. The tutorial calls this learning the site effect. You give it a set of adaptation people, and it estimates an offset and a spread for your site.

Look at the adaptation file. Each of the six sites gives between sixteen and twenty-three people. The tutorial only asks for two people of each sex in a site, just enough to estimate a variance. And then it adds a warning. You want many more than that, to get a reliable estimate.

The authors add one more rule. Adaptation must be done on a held-out calibration set. Not on the people you will test. Yet in their own files, nine of the children who appear twice are in both: once for adaptation, once for testing. We measured what that does. With the files as shipped, these nine children look more ordinary than the other children of their site. Four percent of their scores fall beyond plus or minus one point nine six, against thirteen percent for the others. Take them out of the adaptation, and run again. Now they look like everyone else, about twenty-one percent against twenty-three. The model had partly learned the very children it then scored. The price is that the site now adapts on eight children only, and all its scores spread out.

Third step. Only then do you score the test people, and read their z-scores. Here is one region, the left superior frontal gyrus, for the women of one site, aged twenty-one to sixty-nine. The lines are the centiles of the adapted model, and the dots are real people. This woman, at twenty-two, sits at a z-score of minus two point seven. Over all the test people and all the regions, twelve percent of the scores fall beyond plus or minus one point nine six, where a perfect norm would give five, as we saw in episode six point two.

So that is what we see. A container of ready models, a table that must match, and a site learned from other people than the ones you test.

Now the key moment. Reusing a pretrained model saves you from building a norm from scratch. You do not need to recruit thousands of controls. But the model comes with its history, and you inherit it.

Look at what it was built on. FreeSurfer version six. A given atlas. Ages two to one hundred, but with a caveat. The authors write that FreeSurfer was not adjusted for the very young ages, from two to seven years. In our transfer set, one site has children from about five to eight years old. So the youngest end is where to be careful.

The authors also say that the cohorts are likely drawn mostly from Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic societies. And the UK Biobank alone contributes fifty-nine percent of the full sample. Last episode told us what a big cohort can hide.

And one small trap. The tutorial we read loads a model trained on about fifty-seven thousand people. The paper reports fifty-eight thousand, eight hundred and thirty-six. A newer tutorial names a model of sixty-seven thousand people, from eighty-nine sites. Read the numbers of the file you actually use, not the title of the paper.

So here is the rule. If you want to use a pretrained model, reproduce its processing chain exactly, and adapt it to your site, with enough people, kept apart from your test. We come back to adaptation in episode seven point four.

Things change fast in this field. As of November two thousand twenty-six, the current release of PCNtoolkit is version one point four. The tutorial we read belongs to the zero point thirty-five documentation, and asks for version zero point twenty-seven or later. The newer tutorial suggests twenty to a hundred healthy controls per new site. Check the versions before you start.

To sum up. A pretrained model is a norm you can borrow. It saves months of work, and it brings its software, its atlas, and its population with it. Next time, we read the label on the container. Episode eight point four. How to read a pretrained model's label.

PLATE 8.44:29

Reading a pretrained model's label

A pretrained model carries a label with seven lines: reference population, sites, preprocessing, covariates, model, metrics and valid range, plus a licence. Preprocessing matters because another FreeSurfer version or atlas yields numbers that look alike but measure a different quantity, and the valid range warns, here, about ages two to seven. The label is read before any code is run.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we unloaded a container of pretrained models onto our computer. Models that were already fitted, ready to be reused. Today we do not run any code. We read the label on the container.

Every model comes with a small card, in the same few lines. Reference population. Sites. Preprocessing. Covariates. Model. Metrics. Valid range. Our magnifying glass will read it line by line, for the large cortical thickness model of the last episode. The facts come from its paper, Rutherford and colleagues, published in eLife in 2022.

First line, the reference population. Fifty-eight thousand eight hundred thirty-six people, from eighty-two sites, aged two to one hundred years. That is the size and the span of the people who defined the norm. Our series says it often. A norm is never better than the population that built it. So a good label also says where those people live, and how they were recruited. When the card does not say, the line is left empty, and an empty line is a question to ask.

Second line, the sites. The model has learned the quirks of those eighty-two scanners. Yours is not one of them. We will come back to that.

Third line, preprocessing. This is the line people skip. The thickness values in this model were measured with FreeSurfer, version six point zero. The brain was cut into regions with the Destrieux atlas. Quality control used the Euler number, a summary of how tangled the reconstructed surface is. Earlier work found that it follows manual quality ratings closely. The segmentation chain of the fMRI series is exactly this kind of chain, and here its version number is written on the box.

Why does the version matter? A study from 2012 ran the same thirty scans through three versions of FreeSurfer. Between version five and the two older versions, cortical thickness changed by almost three percent on average, and volumes by almost nine percent. Same brains. Only the software version differed.

Fourth line, the covariates. Age, sex and site. Age enters through a cubic B-spline. Nothing else. If you want to correct for something this model never saw, the label tells you that you cannot.

Fifth line, the model. A Bayesian linear regression, with a warp of the sinh-arcsinh family that lets the bell be skewed and heavy-tailed. Same family as episode four point four.

Sixth line, the metrics. Explained variance, mean squared log-loss, and the skewness and excess kurtosis of the deviation scores. If the card gives you only the explained variance, ask where the others are.

Last line, the valid range, and this is the one that bites. The authors write that FreeSurfer was not adjusted for the very young ages, two to seven years. So the label must carry a warning for that range.

The transfer file that we use in this series shows why. It comes from six OpenNeuro sites. Its column names are Destrieux-type regional thicknesses, so the atlas matches. But its ages run from five point four to seventy-three years, and only one of the six sites goes beyond forty-four. Past that age, a centile is a thin guess.

And there is a licence line. The code of the repository carries the GNU General Public Licence, version three. Before reuse, check the licence of the models and of the data too. This is as of November 2026.

Now the key moment. A model built with one software version and one atlas does not apply to measures made with another version, or another atlas. The numbers look the same. They are not the same quantity. In the language of the Information series, you would compare your person to the wrong reference distribution, and nothing would warn you.

So here is the habit. Before the first line of code, read the label. Compare each line with your own data. Population, sites, software and version, atlas, covariates, metrics, range. Half of the misuses show up right there.

To sum up. A pretrained model is only as reusable as its label is complete, and the label is read before the code. Next time, we leave structural MRI and step into the airport. Functional MRI and connectivity.
