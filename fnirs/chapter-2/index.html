From light to concentration · Beer–Lambert, modified

A glass of grenadine shows the Beer–Lambert law: absorbance grows with concentration and path. Adding milk models scattering, so the path is multiplied by a differential path length factor and only changes from a baseline are tracked. Two wavelengths give two equations and two unknowns, yielding changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin, whose exact amplitudes depend on assumptions and the partial volume effect.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we followed photons through the head, and a lucky few made it to the detector. Now, the real question, how do you turn how much light came back into how much oxygen is in the cortex?

Start with something simpler, a glass of grenadine. Shine a light through it. The more syrup, the less light gets out. Double the concentration, and the absorbance doubles. Double the width of the glass, same thing. That's the Beer–Lambert law, absorbance equals how strongly the molecule absorbs times its concentration times the distance the light travels. Absorbance is logarithmic. One unit means a tenth of the light gets through, two units a hundredth.

Now pour in some milk. Light no longer goes straight through. It bounces around so it travels much further than the width of the glass. And some of it scatters away and never reaches the detector at all. Your head is the milky version.

So we patch the law in two ways. First, the distance, we take the source detector separation and multiply it by the differential path length factor, about 6 for an adult head. That factor isn't a universal constant, it changes with wavelength and with age. Published formulas like one from Scholkmann and Wolf in 2013 adjust it from babies to older adults. Second, the light loss to scattering. We don't know how much it is, but we assume it stays constant. So instead of absolute values, we only look at changes. Compare each moment to a quiet baseline, and the unknown losses cancel out.

One problem left. Hemoglobin comes in two flavors, oxygenated and deoxygenated. Both absorb near-infrared light, but not equally. Below about 800 nanometers, deoxyhemoglobin absorbs more. Above it, oxyhemoglobin takes over. So we shine two colors, one on each side. Which two? Pairs far apart work best. One wavelength below about 720, combined with one around 830, separates the two hemoglobins more reliably than two neighboring colors. That's a classic result from Gary Strangman and colleagues in 2003. Two wavelengths, two measurements, two unknowns. Solve that little system, and out come two curves, the change in oxyhemoglobin and the change in deoxyhemoglobin. Add them up and you get total hemoglobin, a rough proxy for blood volume. Some systems use more wavelengths, even a whole spectrum, to track other molecules too, like cytochrome C oxidase, an enzyme inside our cells.

In analysis software, all of this is one small step in the pipeline, raw intensity to optical density to concentration. Keep two things in mind. These are changes, not absolute concentrations, a few micromolar up or down from baseline, to get a feel for the numbers. If only oxyhemoglobin changed, 1% less light at 850 nanometers over an 18 centimeter path would mean a rise of about a fifth of a micromolar. And their size depends on assumptions, the path length factor, and the fact that only part of the light's journey actually crosses the cortex. Since we spread the change over the whole path, the real change in the brain is larger than the number we compute. That's called the partial volume effect. Shapes and timing are robust. Exact amplitudes less so.

So light in light out a corrected law in two colors. That's how photons become hemoglobin. Next time, why those two curves move in opposite directions.

PLATE 2.22:58

HbO and HbR · Two sides of one story

When a brain region works, nearby vessels widen and deliver far more oxygenated blood than the neurons consume, like watering the whole garden for one thirsty flower. Oxygenated hemoglobin rises and deoxygenated hemoglobin falls, a mirror pattern that signals cortical activation. Both curves rising together, or deoxygenated hemoglobin staying flat, points to blood pressure or skin rather than neurons.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, light became two curves, oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin. When a brain region gets busy, they do something curious. One goes up. The other goes down. Why?

Zoom in on the cortex. Neurons talk with electrical signals, and talking costs energy. A lot of it, the brain is about 2% of your body weight, but it uses roughly 20% of your oxygen. To make that energy, they burn glucose with oxygen, delivered by blood flowing through tiny capillaries. Red blood cells carry hemoglobin. Loaded with oxygen, it's oxyhemoglobin. Once it has dropped its oxygen off, it's deoxyhemoglobin.

When neurons fire more, they use a little more oxygen. So you might expect deoxyhemoglobin to pile up. But the brain overreacts. Nearby arterioles widen and fresh, oxygen-rich blood rushes in. far more than the neurons actually need. How much more? In a classic study, Peter Fox and Marcus Raichle found blood flow rising by about 30%, while oxygen use rose by only about 5%. It's like watering the whole garden for one thirsty flower. This link between neural activity and blood flow is called neurovascular coupling. Why the overreaction? It's still debated. Flow seems to be driven by signals from active neurons, relayed by support cells like astrocytes, rather than by an oxygen shortage.

The flood washes the deoxygenated blood away. So, in the active region, oxyhemoglobin rises and deoxyhemoglobin falls. Mirror images Some studies even report a brief initial dip, a small rise in deoxyhemoglobin in the first second or two before the flood arrives. It's small and controversial. There's some total hemoglobin rises too because the vessels now hold more blood.

That mirror is the signature of cortical activation. Usually the oxycurve is bigger and easier to see. The deoxycurve is smaller, but it's often less contaminated by blood flow in the skin. And deoxyhemoglobin is the very same molecule behind the BOLD signal in functional MRI.

And the coupling itself isn't fixed. It changes with age. In newborns, responses can look different, sometimes even inverted, and it shifts again in older adults. Disease medication, even caffeine, can alter it too.

So keep an eye on the mirror. If both curves rise together, or if the deoxy curve doesn't move at all, be suspicious. That pattern often comes from blood pressure or from the skin rather than from neurons. We'll come back to that.

So neurons fire, blood rushes in more than needed, oxy goes up and deoxy goes down. Next time, how slow is that response and why does it matter?

PLATE 2.32:42

The hemodynamic response · Slow on purpose

The blood response lags neurons: it starts after a second or two, peaks around 5 to 7 seconds and returns to baseline after 15 to 20 seconds. Responses to close stimuli overlap and roughly add up, which lets a model predict the signal as shifted copies of one response. This shapes experiment design, with long rests, varied intervals and slow sampling being enough.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Neurons react in milliseconds. The blood response we measure takes its time. Let's watch it unfold.

Give the brain a short stimulus — 1 or 2 seconds. Nothing happens at first. After a second or two, oxyhemoglobin starts to rise. It peaks around 5 to 7 seconds after the stimulus, then slowly comes back down, often dipping slightly below baseline before settling 15 to 20 seconds later.

That shape is called the hemodynamic response function. Deoxyhemoglobin draws the mirror image, smaller and often a little later.

Why so slow? Because it's plumbing. Signals have to reach the arterioles upstream, their walls have to relax, and the extra blood has to travel down to the capillaries. That takes seconds, not milliseconds.

And the shape isn't identical everywhere. Its timing can shift by a second or two between brain regions, between people, and with age. In infants, the response is often slower and broader. So a single textbook curve is a useful starting point, not a law of nature.

Now make the stimulus longer, a 20-second block of finger tapping. The responses pile up and merge into a plateau that lasts as long as the block, with the same slow start and slow end.

Two short stimuli close together. Their responses overlap and roughly add up. That's both a nuisance and a gift. A nuisance because you can't simply cut the signal into neat pieces. A gift because if responses add up we can model them, predict the signal as a sum of shifted copies of one response. That idea is the heart of the general linear model coming in Chapter 5. Just remember that the adding up is approximate. When stimuli come very close together, the combined response is often a bit smaller than the simple sum.

What does this mean for your experiment? For blocks, a common recipe is 15 to 30 seconds of task, then at least as much rest, so the signal can come back down. For short events, space them several seconds apart and vary the intervals, so the model can tease overlapping responses apart. Watch out for habituation too. Repeat the same stimulus many times and responses can shrink. And since the response is so slow, about 10 samples per second is plenty for the brain part of the signal. Never expect a sharp, instant blip. In fNIRS, everything is slow on purpose.

So, a lag, a peak at 5 to 7 seconds, a slow return in responses that add up. Next time, everything else hiding in your signal.

PLATE 2.43:03

Everything else in your signal

A raw signal carries the heartbeat, breathing, Mayer waves, slow drifts, motion spikes and skin blood flow, with the brain response as a small slow bump. Mayer waves fall near the frequency of typical task rhythms, so filtering cannot separate them. Systemic signals appear in every channel and push both hemoglobins the same way, so recording auxiliary signals and avoiding that band help.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here's a raw fNIRS signal. Somewhere inside, there's the brain response we want. But it's sharing the line with a lot of other guests.

First, the heart. Every beat sends a pulse of blood through the scalp and the brain, a fast ripple around once per second in adults and closer to twice per second in infants. It's actually good news, if you can see the heartbeat your optodes are in good contact.

Then, breathing, a slower wave every 3-5 seconds as blood pressure and volume rise and fall with each breath. Breathing matters for another reason too. When people talk, sigh, or hold their breath, the carbon dioxide in their blood changes, and carbon dioxide widens or narrows blood vessels everywhere in the brain. If breathing changes with your task, it can fake an activation.

Then come Mayer waves, slow oscillations of blood pressure around once every 10 seconds, driven by the nervous system that regulates our vessels. They come and go, and at times they can be bigger than the brain response itself. And beneath everything, very slow drifts over minutes. The instrument warming up, optodes settling into the hair, the body's own slow changes.

Add sudden spikes and jumps when the participant moves, a frown, a head turn, chewing, talking. And changes in blood flow in the skin, which have nothing to do with the brain. The tricky part, skin flow can follow the task too. When a task is stressful or exciting, heart rate and blood pressure rise with it, and so does blood flow in the scalp. Kirilina and colleagues showed this clearly in 2012, in the skin of the forehead.

And the brain response. A small, slow bump hiding in the middle. Look at the spectrum, and you see the problem. The heart and breathing sit at higher frequencies, easy to filter out. But Mayer waves sit right where the task response lies. A block design with 20 seconds of task and 20 seconds of rest repeats every 40 seconds. That's about 0.025 Hz, not far below the Mayer peak. A filter can't separate things that share the same frequencies.

Two clues help you spot these intruders. Systemic signals tend to show up in every channel at once, while brain activity is local. And they often push oxy- and deoxyhemoglobin in the same direction, breaking the mirror we met in episode 2.2.

That's why cleaning fNIRS takes more than a filter, where you can keep your task rhythm away from the Mayer band. In record, whatever can help later, short channels for the scalp, a breathing belt, a pulse or blood pressure sensor, an accelerometer for movement. And remember, one person's noise is another person's research topic. Heart rate, breathing, and blood pressure carry rich information of their own.

So, heart, breath, Mayer waves, drifts, motions, skin, and a little bit of brain. Next time, how to look at all this in time, frequency, and space.

PLATE 2.53:07

Where to look · Time, frequency, space

One recording can be read in time, frequency and space. Time shows when responses start and peak, the spectrum reveals heartbeat, breathing, Mayer waves and task rhythm, and time-frequency maps show rhythms changing, while channel maps and projection onto the cortex show where. Compared with EEG and functional MRI, the method trades speed and sharpness for portability, silence and tolerance to movement.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You now know what's hiding in an fNIRS signal. But a signal can be looked at in different ways, and each view answers a different question. Let's take one recording and turn it around.

First Time. The classic view, concentration against time for one channel with the task blocks shaded behind. It answers the question when, when the response starts when it peaks and when it ends. It's where you spot motion spikes, check that the heartbeat is there, and see whether the curves follow the task. Line up the trials on each stimulus and average them and you get the cleanest time view of all, the block average coming in Chapter 5.

Second Frequency Take the very same signal and ask, which rhythms does it contain? The power spectrum spreads the signal along a frequency axis. The heartbeat becomes a peak near 1 Hz, breathing near a quarter of a Hz, Mayer waves near 0.1, and your task shows up at its own repetition rate. It's the view for choosing filters and for checking signal quality.

Between the two, there's a hybrid, time-frequency maps, often built with wavelets, showing which rhythms are present at each moment. They're handy when rhythms come and go, like Mayer waves. and they're the main tool for measuring synchrony between two brains in Chapter 7.

Third space. Each channel sits at the midpoint between its source and detector. Color each one by the size of its response and you get a channel map, a top-down view of where things happen. Smooth between the channels and you get a pretty topographic map. But careful, between channels there is no data, only interpolation.

To go further, we project onto the brain itself. Using the optode positions, digitized on each head, or taken from a standard template, together with a model of how light travels through the head, each channel is linked to the patch of cortex it is most sensitive to. Now results can be named with brain regions and compared across studies or with MRI.

So where does fNIRS sit among brain imaging methods? Plot temporal resolution against spatial resolution. EEG is fast, down to milliseconds, but blurry in space, centimeters. Functional MRI is sharp in space, a few millimeters, but slow, because it depends on the same blood response as fNIRS. And you must lie still inside a loud magnet. fNIRS sits in between. Its timing is set by that slow blood response. Its spatial resolution is around a centimeter or two, and it only sees the outer cortex. But it's portable, quiet, and tolerant to movement. It works with babies, in classrooms, and on two people talking face-to-face.

So, time tells you when, frequency tells you which rhythms, space tells you where, and the cortex tells you which region. fNIRS isn't the fastest nor the sharpest, but it's the most at home in real life. That wraps up Chapter 2. Next chapter before you record. First stop designing your montage.
