PLATE 9.17:21

A line through a cloud

Least squares picks the line like a rod hung on springs, settling where the total of squared stretches is smallest. For 24 seedlings the slope is 2.32 centimetres per week, with a typical miss of 2.57. The slope is an estimate with its own fan of possible lines; four times the seedlings halves its width, and doubling the scatter doubles it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the vegetable garden. Seedlings grow taller with every week, but not every seedling by the same amount.

Today we put a line through a cloud of points: how it is chosen, what it says, and how far to trust it.

Here is a simulated study: 24 seedlings, each measured once. Every dot is one seedling. Across is the number of weeks since planting, and up is the height in centimetres. The cloud rises, but it is fuzzy. We want one straight line through it.

Here is a way to choose. Lay a rod across the cloud, and hang each dot on the rod with a spring. Every spring pulls the rod toward its dot. A spring stretched twice as far stores four times the energy, so a long stretch costs a lot, and a short one hardly anything.

Let go of the rod. It swings, wobbles, and settles. Where it rests, the springs are as relaxed as they can be. That resting rod is the line of least squares. Its total of squared stretches is 145.7 square centimetres.

Try any other line, and the springs are more stretched. Tilt the rod steeper, and the total is 228.2. Tilt it flatter, and it is 228.2 again. Lift it by 3 centimetres, and it is 361.7. The rod found the smallest total.

Adrien-Marie Legendre published this idea in 1805, in a book on the orbits of comets. He called it the method of least squares. Gauss later claimed he had used it since 1795.

Three numbers describe our line. The slope is 2.32 centimetres per week. The intercept is 2.17 centimetres, where the line meets the vertical axis, at week zero. The typical miss is 2.57 centimetres: that is the spread of the dots around the line, and its name is the residual standard deviation.

Read the slope with care. It does not say that every seedling grows 2.32 centimetres each week. Look at two seedlings about one week apart, 3.9 weeks and 5.0 weeks. The older one is 4.15 centimetres shorter. The slope compares seedlings one week apart on average, and each one misses the line by about 2.6 centimetres.

One thing always holds: the line passes through the point of the two averages, 4.66 weeks and 12.96 centimetres. The formula for the slope comes after the springs: the sum of the products of the gaps to the averages, divided by the sum of the squared gaps of the weeks.

Lines through clouds are everywhere, so let us read four more, each in its own units. All are simulated.

Children first: height against age, 30 children from 4 to 12 years. The slope is 6.3 centimetres per year. The intercept is 78.1 centimetres. The typical miss is 5.8 centimetres.

Study hours against an exam score, 35 students. Each extra hour goes with 3.8 more points. The intercept, 46.2 points, is the score at zero hours. The typical miss is 7.3 points.

An ice cream shop: cones sold against the temperature in degrees Celsius, over 30 days. The slope is 11 cones per degree. The intercept is minus 90 cones, which is nonsense, because nobody sells minus 90 cones. The line holds only where we have data, between 12 and 32 degrees. The typical miss is 20 cones.

A laboratory calibration: 12 standard solutions of known concentration. The slope is 0.083 absorbance units for each milligram per litre. The intercept, 0.014, is the reading of a blank. The typical miss is 0.008. In a laboratory, a small miss is the whole point.

Four worlds, the same three numbers. The slope is in units of y per unit of x.

But a line from 24 seedlings is one study. Would another study give the same line? Let the machine find out. The whole garden has 5,000 seedlings, and its own line has a slope of 2.02.

SCOOP draws 24 seedlings, fits a line, and puts them back. Our study found a slope of 2.32. The next draws give 2.14, then 1.51, then 2.08. Every study has its own line.

After 100 studies, the lines make a fan. It is thin near the average week, and wide at the two ends, because every line tilts around the same middle.

After 10,000 studies, the slopes pile up in a hill. Its centre is 2.02, the truth of the garden. Its width, the standard deviation of the slopes, is 0.23.

That is the sampling distribution of chapter one, for a slope instead of a mean. The standard deviation of the slopes is the standard error of the slope. The fan is its interval bar.

We cannot rerun a garden, but one study is enough to estimate that width: the typical miss, divided by the square root of the sum of squared gaps of the weeks. For our study, 2.57 over the square root of 101.9. That is 0.25. Averaged over the 10,000 studies, the formula gives 0.23, just as the simulation measured.

So a 95 percent interval for the slope is the slope, plus or minus 2.07 standard errors. For our study, it runs from 1.79 to 2.84. Over the 10,000 studies, such intervals contained the garden's slope in 95.3 percent.

Now two knobs. Guess before you see: with four times as many seedlings, how much narrower is the fan?

Take 6 seedlings, then 24, then 96. The width of the slopes goes from 0.57, to 0.23, to 0.11. Four times the seedlings, half the width. The typical miss stays put, near 2.5. More seedlings do not make seedlings less variable. They make the line more certain.

The second knob is the scatter. Three gardens, where seedlings stray from their line by 1.25, 2.5 and 5 centimetres. The slopes spread by 0.12, 0.23 and 0.46. Double the scatter, and the fan doubles.

So a slope reported without an interval is one line of the fan, shown as if it were all of them.

One word before we finish. Why is a line through a cloud called a regression? Francis Galton used that word in 1886. He had first seen the effect in the seeds of sweet peas, and then confirmed it on the heights of parents and children. The word belongs to that story, not to the line, and episode 17.4 tells it.

Remember: least squares picks the line with the smallest total of squared misses, and its slope is an estimate with a fan of its own. Next time: one number for how tightly a cloud hugs its line, the correlation.

PLATE 9.27:24

Correlation

Correlation is the slope when both variables are measured in standard deviations, so it ignores units; for the seedlings it is 0.89. Its square is the share of variance in common. It describes straight lines only, and is fooled by curves, a single isolated point, cut ranges and mixed groups. On twenty plants, its interval is very wide.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the vegetable garden. Last time a line went through a cloud of seedlings, and its slope came with units: 2.32 centimetres per week.

Change the units, and the slope changes. Measure the height in metres, and the slope is 0.023 metres per week. Count in days, and it is 0.33 centimetres per day.

Today we want one number for how tightly a cloud hugs its line, a number that does not care about units. It is called the correlation.

Here is the trick. Take our 24 seedlings. For each one, ask how many standard deviations it sits above or below the average age, and above or below the average height. The weeks have a standard deviation of 2.10, and the heights of 5.49 centimetres. Every dot becomes a pair of gaps, counted in standard deviations. The units are gone, and the cloud keeps its shape.

Now fit the line on these standardised axes. Its slope is 0.89. That number is the correlation, written r. The correlation is the slope when both variables are measured in standard deviations. In metres or centimetres, weeks or days, it is always 0.89.

To see r, stretch an elastic loop around a standardised cloud. When r is zero, the loop is a round hoop: knowing the weeks tells you nothing about the height. As r grows, the loop is squeezed into a thin oval along the diagonal. At 1 it would be a line. Tilt it the other way, and r is negative.

Here are five clouds of 60 points, with r equal to 0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, and minus 0.6. The sign says which way the cloud leans. The size says how tight it is. Francis Galton sketched the idea in the 1880s, and Karl Pearson gave it the formula we use, in the 1890s.

Square r, and it has a second meaning. The heights of our seedlings vary: their squared gaps to the average add up to 692.0 square centimetres. Around the line, the squared misses add up to only 145.7. So the line removed 79 percent of the variation. And 0.89 squared is 0.79. The square of r is the share of variance in common: the part of the height that the weeks account for along a straight line.

Read the five clouds again. At r equal to 0.3, the share is only 9 percent. At 0.6, it is 36 percent. At 0.9, it is 81 percent. Guess before you see: if r is 0.5, what share of the variance is in common? A half, a quarter, or a tenth?

A quarter. Half of r is only a quarter of the variance.

Now let us read r in five more fields. All are simulated.

Study hours against exam score, 35 students: r is 0.65, so 42 percent of the variance in scores goes with hours.

An ice cream shop, cones sold against the temperature, over 30 days: r is 0.94, and 87 percent is in common.

A clinic: age against systolic blood pressure, 40 adults. r is 0.58, and 34 percent is in common. Age matters, and still most of the spread belongs to other things.

Sleep: hours slept against reaction time, 30 volunteers. r is minus 0.67. More sleep goes with faster reactions, so the sign is negative. And 44 percent is in common, because the square forgets the sign.

A laboratory calibration, 12 standard solutions: r is 0.9997, and 99.9 percent is in common.

But r measures one thing: how well a straight line describes the cloud. Here are four clouds it summarises badly.

First, a curve. An enzyme works best at a middle temperature, and badly when it is cold or hot. The link is strong, yet r is 0.03. A curve is not a line.

Second, one isolated point. Twenty patients. Nineteen show no link at all: r is exactly zero. Add one extreme patient, and r jumps to 0.56. One point made the correlation. We built this cloud on purpose: the nineteen are set to zero.

Third, a truncated range. A test and the first-year mark: among 400 applicants, r is 0.70. A university admits only the top quarter on the test. Among the 100 admitted, r falls to 0.25. Cut the range, and r shrinks, though the link has not changed.

Fourth, two mixed groups. Two clinics, dose against response. Within each clinic, more dose goes with a lower response: r is minus 0.73 in one and minus 0.22 in the other. Mix the two clinics, and r is plus 0.82. Draw the cloud before you trust the number.

Now the strong moment: how stable is r? A second garden, of 5,000 plants: hours of sun against tomatoes per plant. In the whole garden, r is 0.42, so r squared is 0.17.

SCOOP draws 20 plants and computes r. The first draws give 0.14, then 0.51, then 0.52. Guess before you see: after 10,000 draws of 20, where do the middle 95 percent of the r values fall?

From minus 0.03 to 0.74. In 3.5 percent of the samples, r is even negative, and in 16 percent it is above 0.6. The same garden, the same truth, and a sample of 20 can show no link at all, or a strong one.

So here is one study of 20 plants. Its r is 0.25. We cannot see the garden's 0.42, so we report r with an interval. It uses Fisher's z transformation, and it runs from minus 0.21 to 0.63. It contains zero, and it contains 0.6. Over 10,000 studies, such intervals caught the garden's r in 94.4 percent.

A correlation without its interval looks surer than it is.

Now turn the knob of the sample size: 10, 20, 80, and 320 plants. The spread of r goes from 0.29, to 0.20, to 0.09, to 0.05. At 320 plants, the middle 95 percent of r lies between 0.33 and 0.50. With only 10 plants, r is negative 11 percent of the time.

Remember: r is the slope of the standardised cloud, and its square is the share of variance in common. It describes a straight line only, it is fooled by curves, outliers, cut ranges and mixed groups, and on a small sample it needs its interval. Next time: code a group as zero or one, and the t-test turns out to be a regression.

PLATE 9.37:20

The t-test is a regression

A t-test is a regression: code the group as 0 or 1, and the slope is the difference of means, with Student's t, identical in 10,000 simulated studies. Paired tests are the intercept of the differences, and one-sample tests an intercept after shifting the axis. Welch needs a different spread per group, and rank tests are only approximations.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the vegetable garden. Until now, the horizontal axis held a number: weeks, or hours of sun. Today it holds a label. This plant had compost, that one did not. A line cannot pass through labels, you might think. It can, and when it does, a whole family of tests folds into one.

Here are 30 tomato plants, simulated. Fifteen grew in plain soil, and fifteen in compost. The yield per plant is on the vertical axis. Now we give each group a code: plain soil is 0, compost is 1. The label has become a number, so the plants can stand on an axis.

Fit the line, exactly as in 9.1: the least squares line through the cloud. With only two columns, it has little choice. It passes through the average of each column. At 0, it sits at 1.69 kilograms, the average of plain soil. That height is the intercept, a. At 1, it sits at 2.53, the average of compost. The climb is 0.84 kilograms. That is the slope, b, and it is the difference of the two means.

Nothing lives between 0 and 1. The stretch of line between the columns is only a ruler. Guess before you see: what if we had coded the groups 1 and 2 instead? The slope stays 0.84, and its test does not change. Only the intercept moves, to 0.85. The two numbers we pick do not matter. The step between them does.

Like any slope in 9.1, this one has a standard error, and its t is the slope divided by that standard error. It is 4.64, with 28 degrees of freedom, and p is 0.00007. Now run Student's t-test on the same 30 numbers. The difference is 0.84. The t is 4.64, and p is 0.00007. The same numbers, to every digit.

Was it luck? Simulate 10,000 studies of 15 plants against 15. In each one, compute t twice: once from the slope of the line, once from Student's formula. Guess before you see: in all 10,000 studies, how big is the largest gap between the two? About nine quadrillionths, which is the rounding of the computer. The two are the same number. Student's t-test is a regression slope on a group coded 0 or 1.

One warning. This is Student's test, which pools a single spread for both groups. Welch's test is not the plain regression. Take a new comparison: eight noisy measurements against 32 steady ones, with the same true average. The spreads are 3.63 and 0.91. The regression says t is minus 2.37, and p is 0.023. Welch says t is minus 1.27, and p is 0.24. In 10,000 studies where nothing is going on, the plain regression rings the five percent alarm 25.6 percent of the time. Welch rings it 5.0 percent. Welch needs errors that have a different spread in each group, which is a more advanced regression. The plain line assumes one spread.

Second example: paired data. Twelve runners, and their 5 kilometre times in minutes, before and after a training block. A paired test is a test on the differences, one number per runner. The first three changes are minus 3.8, plus 0.1, and minus 2.9. Now the model has no x at all. It is just y equals a. The intercept is the average difference: minus 1.57 minutes. Is it zero? Its standard error is 0.45, so t is minus 3.49, with 11 degrees of freedom, and p is 0.0051. The paired t-test gives exactly this. The paired test is the intercept of the differences.

Third example: one sample against a value. A roaster promises 250 grams per bag. We weigh 14 bags, and they average 246.1. Subtract 250 from every weight, and the question is the same: is the intercept zero? The intercept is minus 3.9 grams. Its standard error is 1.11, t is minus 3.52, and p is 0.0038. The one-sample t-test gives the same. A one-sample test is an intercept, after shifting the axis.

Fourth example: a 0 or 1 predictor beside a continuous one. Forty commuters, simulated, and their commute in minutes. First, against distance. The slope is 1.94 minutes per kilometre: one more kilometre adds 1.94 minutes. Now the same minutes against a flag for taking the tram. For the 26 commuters without it, the intercept is 36.0 minutes. The slope is minus 14.3. A slope is always the change in y for one more unit of x. With a code of 0 or 1, one unit is the whole step from one group to the other. So tram riders are 14.3 minutes faster, with t equal to minus 3.48, and p of 0.0013. One equation, two kinds of x.

Fifth example: ranks. Waiting times in two clinics, 14 patients each. Waiting times are skewed, with a few very long ones. The Mann-Whitney test replaces each time by its rank, from 1 for the shortest to 28 for the longest. So do the regression on the ranks instead of the minutes. Mann-Whitney gives p equal to 0.113. The regression on ranks gives 0.109. Student on the raw minutes gives 0.123. Over 10,000 simulated studies of this design, the two rank methods agree on the five percent verdict in 98.0 percent. This is an approximation, not an identity, and it is better with more patients. It is a point made by Jonas Lindeløv, in an online guide last updated in 2019.

Now fold the map of chapter seven. Its twelve drawers sit in four columns. Take the first row, the centres. One sample: an intercept. Two groups: a slope on a code of 0 and 1. Pairs: the intercept of the differences. More than two groups: several columns of 0 and 1, which is analysis of variance, in 9.5. The rank tools fold the same way, on ranks, as approximations. The other rows ask other questions, and we leave them.

So here is the strong moment. One model: y equals a plus b times x, plus noise. Learn it once, and the classical tests are its settings. Several predictors come in 9.4, groups and interactions in 9.5, and checking the errors in 9.6.

Remember: a t-test is a regression. Code the group as 0 or 1, and the slope is the difference of means, with Student's t. Pairs and one-sample tests are intercepts. Welch needs a different spread for each group, and rank tests are approximations. Next time: several predictors, and what it means to hold the others constant.

PLATE 9.47:25

Several predictors

With two predictors, each slope means the effect with the others held constant. Sun's slope falls from 1.82 to 1.06 once water joins the model, because sunny plants also get more water. Predictors that travel together make slopes trade places while predictions stay good. Whether to control for a variable depends on the causal story, which the numbers cannot tell.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the vegetable garden. A seedling never grows because of one thing only: sun matters, and so does water. Until now, the horizontal axis held one predictor. Today it holds two, and every slope changes its meaning.

Here is a simulated study of 60 plants. Each dot is one plant: hours of sun per day across, growth in centimetres per month up. The line of 9.1 has a slope of 1.82 centimetres per hour of sun.

Water, in litres per week, has a slope of 1.13 centimetres per litre. But sunny plants also get more water: the correlation between the two predictors is 0.57. Which one is doing the work?

Put both in one model. Growth equals a, plus b one times sun, plus b two times water, plus noise. The sun slope falls from 1.82 to 1.06. The water slope is 0.78.

Why did the sun slope shrink? Plants with more sun also have more water: 0.97 litres more for each extra hour. Each litre is worth 0.78, and 0.97 times 0.78 is 0.76. The line with sun alone gave sun the credit for water's work. And 1.82 minus 1.06 is exactly that 0.76.

So a slope in a model asks: how much more does a plant grow with one more hour of sun, when its water is held constant?

What does held constant look like? First way: cut the plants into four thin slices of water. The sun slope inside the four slices is 0.84, 0.60, 2.05 and 1.52. Those are noisy, 15 plants a slice. Their average, 1.25, is near 1.06 but not equal: slices only approximate.

Second way, exact. For each plant, remove what water explains. Keep the miss from the line of growth against water. Do the same for sun: keep the miss from the line of sun against water. Plot the two sets of misses against each other.

Fit a line through that cloud. Its slope is 1.0618, and so is the sun slope of the model, to every digit shown. This is the Frisch, Waugh, Lovell theorem, from papers of 1933 and 1963. Each slope in a model is the slope of what remains of its predictor, once the others are removed.

Now the whole model at once. With two predictors, it is a tilted plane. Flattened onto the page, it becomes parallel lines, one for each level of water. Take 7, 10 and 13 litres. All three lines have the sun slope, 1.06. Each level up shifts the line by 2.34 centimetres, which is three litres at 0.78 each.

The same reading in other fields, all simulated.

House price in thousands of dollars, against size and number of rooms, for 80 houses. Alone, a room is worth 55.3 thousand. With size in the model, 10.3. The size slope barely moves, from 2.64 to 2.33 per square metre.

Reading score against age and shoe size, for 80 children. Alone, each shoe size goes with 2.28 more points. With age in the model, it is minus 1.03, within two standard errors of zero.

Ice cream sales and drownings, against temperature, over 60 weeks. Each extra hundred cones goes with 0.82 more drownings a week. With temperature in the model, it is 0.23, and its interval includes zero.

Salary against experience and age, for 40 people, almost twins: the correlation is 0.95. Alone, the slopes are 1.85 and 1.76 thousand a year. Together, they are 1.56 and 0.31. The model cannot tell them apart.

Let us measure that. In the garden, the true slopes are 1.0 for sun and 0.8 for water. Draw 40 plants, fit both slopes, and drop the pair as one dot.

First garden: sun and water barely related, correlation 0.2. After 10,000 samples, the dots make a small round cloud around the truth. The sun slope spreads by 0.26.

Second garden: almost twins, correlation 0.95. Same truth. Guess before you see: how much wider is the spread of the sun slope?

It is 3.1 times wider: 0.80 instead of 0.26. The cloud is a long slanted strip, and the correlation between the two estimates is minus 0.95. Three samples give 0.12 and 1.28, then 1.26 and 0.76, then 0.32 and 1.34: the slopes trade places.

The average 95 percent interval for the sun slope is 1.02 wide in the first garden, and 3.19 in the second. In 10.2 percent of the samples, the sun slope even comes out negative.

But the predictions stay good. A new plant that follows the pattern, with much sun and much water, is predicted with an error of 0.71 centimetres in the twin garden, against 0.80 in the first. A plant that breaks the pattern, with much sun and little water, is off by 3.19, against 0.92.

Now the strong moment. Controlling for a variable means adding it to the model. Whether that helps depends on the story behind the data, and the numbers do not contain the story.

Take the same two predictors and one outcome, in three simulated worlds of 1,000 people. The true effect of the first predictor is 0.50 in each.

World one: the second predictor causes both the first one and the outcome. The slope alone is 1.04, too big. With the second predictor in the model, it is 0.48. Adding it is right.

World two: the first predictor acts on the second, which acts on the outcome. The slope alone is 0.54, near the truth. With the second predictor in the model, it is 0.09. Adding it erased the effect we asked about.

World three: the second predictor is an effect of both. The slope alone is 0.51. Adding it gives minus 0.25, an effect nobody put there.

Same variables, same model, three answers: a regression cannot say which variables to put in. That is 19.3, where these pictures become graphs. You met the same choice in 19.2, with pooled and split tables.

Remember: each slope is the effect of its predictor with the others held constant. Predictors that travel together make the slopes trade places, yet predictions stay good. Controlling for a variable depends on the causal story, not on the model. Next time: ANOVA, interactions and contrasts.

PLATE 9.57:27

ANOVA, interactions and contrasts

A factor with three levels becomes two columns of zeros and ones, and the F test compares a model with and without them. An interaction is a difference of differences, tested as one extra column. A contrast is a question written as weights summing to zero. Saying it works in women but not men needs a test of the difference.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the vegetable garden. In 9.3, a group coded 0 or 1 became a slope. Today there are three groups, then two factors at once, and finally a question written as weights.

Three fertilisers: bone meal, kelp, and a new blend, Nova. Five tomato plants each, fifteen in all, simulated, with the yield in kilograms. A line cannot follow a name, so we turn the names into numbers. Two columns are enough. The first says kelp: one or zero. The second says Nova: one or zero. A bone meal plant has zeros in both.

Plant by plant, the rows fill in. The table you get is called the design matrix. Now fit the least squares line of 9.1, with two columns instead of one. The intercept is 1.92: the average of bone meal, the group with all zeros. The kelp slope is 0.30, and 1.92 plus 0.30 is 2.22, the kelp average. The Nova slope is 0.66, and 1.92 plus 0.66 is 2.58, the Nova average. Each slope is a difference from bone meal.

Do the fertilisers differ at all? Compare two models. The first gives every plant the same average, 2.24. Its squared misses add up to 4.916. The second gives each fertiliser its own average. The misses shrink, to 3.824.

So the factor removed 1.092. Divide that by its two columns: 0.546. Divide what is left by the twelve free pieces, fifteen plants minus three averages: 0.319. Their ratio is F: 1.71, and p is 0.22. This is the F of 7.7, the spread of the flags against the spread of the birds, reached another way. Guess before you see: over 10,000 simulated studies, how far apart are the two ways? About five quadrillionths of their size. The same number. An analysis of variance compares two regressions: with the factor, and without it.

Now two factors at once. Fertiliser, old or new, and watering, low or high. Ten plants in each of four cells, simulated. Plot the four averages as two lines, one per fertiliser. Old goes from 1.47 to 2.67 kilograms with more water. New goes from 2.69 to 4.01. New beats old by 1.22 kilograms with little water, and by 1.34 with much water. The lines are nearly parallel: the fertiliser works the same way at both waterings.

When the lines fan out or cross, it does not. That is an interaction, and it is a difference of differences: 1.34 minus 1.22 is 0.12. Its standard error is 0.50, so t is 0.24, and p is 0.81. No sign of an interaction here. In the design matrix, it is one more column: the product of the two, which holds a one only for new fertiliser with much water. Its coefficient is 0.12, the same difference of differences.

Here is one that fans out. An advertising campaign in two cities, a hundred shoppers per arm, simulated. Sales rise by 5.8 percent in the first city, and move by minus 0.9 in the second. The difference is 6.7, and its p is 0.006.

Back to the three fertilisers. An F says not all equal, never which. To ask one precise question, write it as weights on the three averages. Bone meal against kelp: plus one, minus one, zero. The estimate is minus 0.30 kilograms, t is minus 0.84, and p is 0.42. The new blend against the two old ones together: minus a half, minus a half, plus one. The estimate is 0.51 kilograms, which is 2.58 minus 2.07, the average of the old two. The t is 1.65, and p is 0.12. That is smaller than the 0.22 of the global F, because the question is sharper, but neither rings with five plants a fertiliser. Weights of minus one, minus one, plus two give twice the estimate, and the same t and p. The weights add up to zero, because a contrast compares, and does not ask how big everything is. Write your contrasts before you see the data. If you try many, correct them: that is chapter 15.

Now the strong moment. A drug is tested against a placebo on 20 women and 20 men. In truth, it helps both by the same 6 points. Simulated. In this study, women gain 9.6 points, with p of 0.0012. Men gain 4.7, with p of 0.23. The report says: it works in women, and not in men. But is the gap between 9.6 and 4.7 itself clear? Test it. The difference is 4.9, and its p is 0.30. Nothing here says the sexes differ.

Repeat the study 10,000 times, always with the same true effect. Guess before you see: how often does exactly one sex ring? In 5,036 studies, or 50.4 percent. Women alone: 2,584. Men alone: 2,452. Both: 2,024. Neither: 2,940. Alone, the women's test rings 46 percent of the time, and the men's 45. And the interaction test, on the very same studies, rings 5.2 percent, as it should.

This is a frequent mistake. Nieuwenhuis, Forstmann and Wagenmakers reviewed 513 neuroscience articles in 2011, in five journals. In 157 of them, two effects were compared. Seventy-eight did it correctly, with a test of the difference. Seventy-nine concluded that effects differ because one was significant and the other was not.

Three more cases, simulated. A new teaching method in two schools: plus 4.4 and plus 1.0 points, a difference of 3.4, with p of 0.33. Brain stimulation against sham: controls respond by 2.1 microvolts, patients by 1.4, a difference of 0.7, with p of 0.60. Memory training in young and older adults: 4.6 points and 0.0, a difference of 4.6, with p of 0.044. In the advertising and memory studies, the difference is itself significant, so the claim is backed.

Remember: a factor with three levels becomes two columns of zeros and ones, and the F test compares the model with and without them. An interaction is a difference of differences, tested as one more column. A contrast is a question written as weights that add up to zero. To say two groups differ, test the difference, never two separate p values. Next, in 9.6: diagnostics, where the model confesses.

PLATE 9.67:13

Diagnostics

Model assumptions are about the residuals, the misses around the line: linearity, constant variance, normality and independence. Unbending the line reveals curves, funnels and runs, and not every violation costs the same: dependent misses ring 50.9 percent instead of five. One mansion holds a whole line; leverage and Cook's distance find it, and both fits should be shown.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the vegetable garden. In 9.5, the F test, the interaction and the contrasts all quietly assumed that the model was right. Today we check. Every assumption is about what the line leaves behind: for each plant, the miss between the dot and the line. Those misses are the residuals.

Four things are assumed, and all four concern the residuals. Linearity: on average, the misses are zero all along the line. Constant variance: the misses are as large at one end as at the other. Normality: the misses follow a bell. Independence: one miss tells you nothing about the next. Each has its picture. The misses against the fitted values show the first two. The Q-Q plot of 4.5 shows the third. The misses in time order show the fourth.

First, a healthy one. Thirty garden beds, simulated: fertiliser in grams across, growth in centimetres up. The line rises 1.63 centimetres for each gram, and the typical miss is 2.91. Now unbend the line. Slide every dot straight up or down by the height of the line at that spot, until the line lies flat. Across is now the fitted value, the height the line gives. Up is the miss. What is left is a shapeless band: no curve, no fan, no pattern. That is what a healthy model looks like. Its Q-Q plot hugs the diagonal. And the promise? Draw ten thousand such studies, thirty beds each, and count how often the 95 percent interval for the slope contains the truth. It does in 94.8 percent. The promise holds.

Now break the assumptions, one at a time. A dose-response, simulated: the response levels off as the dose grows. As a cloud with its line, it looks acceptable, and the line explains 81 percent of the variation. Unbend the line. Guess before you see: where will the misses be positive, at the ends or in the middle? The cloud confesses: a hill. The misses are negative at both ends and positive in the middle. What does it cost? Over ten thousand studies, the 95 percent interval for the average response at the middle dose contains the truth in only 2.8 percent of them. A fix, in one line: a curved model, or a transformation. That is chapter 10.

Second, income against age, simulated: the older, the more spread out. As a cloud, the line seems fine. Unbend it, and a funnel opens: small misses on the left, big ones on the right. The interval for the slope still catches the truth in 93.7 percent of ten thousand studies. That is a little under the promise, so for the slope, the damage is small. The damage is in the predictions. The 95 percent interval for one new 55-year-old covers them in only 84 percent of the studies. For a new 25-year-old, it covers them in 100 percent. The interval has nearly the same width everywhere: too wide for the young, too narrow for the old. A fix: the unequal-variance idea of 7.5, or robust standard errors.

Third, reaction times against hours awake, simulated. Most trials are quick, and a few are very slow. The misses against the fitted values are lopsided, but the picture to read is the Q-Q plot. The right end shoots away from the diagonal, and the left end falls short: the banana of 4.5. Guess before you see: does this hurt the interval for the slope? No. In 95.0 percent of ten thousand studies, it catches the truth. With thirty points, skewed misses barely matter for the slope. They matter for predictions. A new reaction time lands above the 95 percent prediction interval in 5.9 percent of the studies, and below it in 0.0 percent, where each side was promised 2.5. A fix: the robust and rank methods of chapters 2 and 7.

Fourth, daily temperatures over a month, simulated, with no trend at all. The weather has memory: a warm day follows a warm day. Yet the line finds a trend, minus 0.10 degrees a day, with a p of 0.0005. Unbend it, and read the misses in order. They come in long runs, above the line, then below. Neighbouring misses correlate at 0.57. The thirty days are not thirty fresh observations. Over ten thousand months with no trend at all, the test rings in 50.9 percent of them, not in 5. A fix: chapters 11 and 13.

Put the five promises side by side. A healthy model: 94.8 percent. The curve: 2.8. The funnel: 93.7. Skewed misses: 95.0. Dependent misses: 49.1. Not every broken assumption costs the same, and you cannot know which one you have without looking.

The residuals can hide one more thing. Twenty houses, simulated: nineteen ordinary ones, and a mansion. With all twenty, the line climbs 2.55 thousand dollars per square metre, and p is below 0.0001. Now look at the mansion's own miss: 1.26 typical misses, smaller than the 2.06 of an ordinary house. The mansion pulled the line toward itself, so its residual is modest. The residuals alone will not find it. Two other numbers will.

Leverage is how far a house's size lies from the others. It depends only on the sizes, never on the prices. An ordinary house has a leverage between 0.05 and 0.08. The mansion has 0.86. Across the twenty houses, leverage averages 0.10, so the mansion alone is more than eight times that. Cook's distance is how much the line moves when the house is removed. Cook introduced it in 1977. For the mansion it is 32.8. For the largest of the others, 0.13. Remove the mansion, and the line flattens: the slope is minus 0.21, with a p of 0.72. One house holds the whole line.

So what do we do? We do not delete it silently. We report both fits, side by side. With the mansion, 2.55, and p below 0.0001. Without it, minus 0.21, and p of 0.72. Then we ask what the mansion is. A typing error? A different kind of house? The model confesses in its residuals, but a point with a high leverage can muffle its own confession. So we also ask for leverage and for Cook's distance. Whatever we decide, the reader gets to see the data, both fits, and the reason.

Remember: the assumptions are about the residuals. Plot them against the fitted values and in a Q-Q plot, check the leverage and Cook's distance, and when one point holds the line, show the fit with and without it. Next, in 10.1: the chapter changes, and the outcome becomes yes or no.
