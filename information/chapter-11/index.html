Four ways to fill an adjacency matrix

An adjacency matrix is a road map, and four ways of filling it differ in what they show: thresholded correlation, mutual information, transfer entropy, and windowed local transfer entropy. On a simulated switch between two pathways, static maps show both links at once, but the windowed version shows they alternate. A static matrix can display a network that never existed at any moment.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter 11, Time Resolved Information Networks. The previous chapters gave us measures between pairs of signals, correlation, mutual information, transfer entropy, and their local versions. Put one measure for every pair into a table and you get a network. This chapter is about what happens when that network changes over time and how to summarise and segment it. We start with the table itself.

Here is the intuition. We are planning a road trip between four towns and we want a map of the roads. There are four ways to draw it. A simple map marks a road or no road between two towns. A better map draws each road with its width.

A more useful map adds one-way arrows because traffic does not flow the same way in both directions. And the best map is live. It shows the traffic right now and changes through the day. Each map is an adjacency matrix. A square table, one row and one column per town, with the road from row to column in each cell.

Here are the four ways with brain signals as towns.

First, a thresholded correlation. Compute the correlation between every pair of signals and put a 1 where it exceeds a threshold, a 0 elsewhere. The matrix is binary, symmetric, and static, one map for the whole recording.

Second, the values themselves, correlation or mutual information from chapter 4. Now the matrix is real valued but still symmetric and static. The road has a width but no direction and no time.

Third, transfer entropy from chapter 8. Each cell holds the information that the past of the row signal adds to the prediction of the column signal. The matrix is real valued and asymmetric. The road from one town to another can differ from the road back. Still static.

Fourth, local or specific transfer entropy from episode 8.9. Instead of one value per pair for the whole recording, we get one value per pair at every moment, which we can average in short sliding windows. The matrix is real-valued, asymmetric, and time-dependent. The map becomes a film.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated four signals, 4,000 samples each. Every signal has its own memory of 0.5. During the first half of the recording, signal 1 drives signal 2. During the second half, signal 1 stops and signal 3 drives signal 4 instead. Values are in bits with Gaussian estimators.

The thresholded correlation at 0.1 gives two links, between 1 and 2 and between 3 and 4. Both present both symmetric for the whole recording.

The mutual information gives the widths 0.02 bits between 1 and 2, 0.03 between 3 and 4 and almost nothing elsewhere.

Transfer entropy adds the arrows 0.07 bits from 1 to 2 and 0.07 from 3 to 4. Nothing in the reverse directions. Now we know who drives whom, but the static map still says that both roads were open all the time.

The fourth map, local transfer entropy averaged in windows of 400 samples, tells the real story. From 1 to 2, it sits around 0.15 to 0.2 bits in the first half, then drops to slightly negative values, around minus 0.08 in the second half. From 3 to 4, the opposite, slightly negative, then around 0.2. The two roads were never open at the same time.

Look at the film frame by frame. At the start, one bright arrow from 1 to 2 and nothing else. Around the middle of the recording, during the windows that straddle the switch, both arrows are dimmed. Each window mixes coupled and uncoupled samples. Then one bright arrow from 3 to 4. A single static frame would show both arrows dimmed, for the whole recording which describes no actual moment.

That is the strong point of today. With a time-dependent measure, the matrix becomes a film. A static matrix averages different epochs into one picture and can show a network that never existed at any single moment. Once the matrix is a film, we need to summarise it at every instant. That is what the next episodes do.

Notice the negative values. Local transfer entropy can be negative at a given moment, when the source's past is misleading about the target, as we saw in episode 8.9. Averaged in a window where there is no coupling, it hovers around zero, sometimes below. Some summaries in the next episode assume that every entry of the matrix is non-negative. That assumption will matter.

What does the choice of matrix depend on?

First, the question, symmetric measures describe shared dependence. Asymmetric measures describe predictive direction. Time-dependent measures describe change.

Second, the threshold for binary matrices. A different threshold gives a different network and with it different summaries. Here, the two correlations were 0.16 and 0.20. A threshold of 0.25 would have kept neither link and the binary map would have been empty.

Third, the window for time-dependent matrices. The windows also set how sharp a transition can look. A switch can never appear faster than one window length. Short windows follow fast changes but are noisy. Long windows are smooth but blur transitions. Our windows of 400 samples are a choice and the film depends on it.

And fourth, the estimator which brings in every sensitivity of chapters 5 and 8, bias, embedding, lag, volume, conduction.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study computes one transfer entropy matrix over a whole recording, finds links from region 1 to 2 and from 3 to 4, and concludes that both pathways operate together during the task. But if the pathways alternate, the static matrix shows both and no moment of the recording looks like it.

The fix, when the question involves change, use a time-resolved matrix, report the window, and check whether links that appear together in the static matrix are actually present at the same moments.

The notebook for this episode builds the four matrices for the four simulated signals, and plays the local transfer entropy film window by window.

Let's recap.

An adjacency matrix can be binary or real, symmetric or asymmetric, static or time-dependent. Thresholded correlation, correlation or mutual information values, transfer entropy, local transfer entropy.

On our four signals, the static map showed two pathways at once. The film showed them alternating, around 0.2 bits each when active.

Local values can be negative, which matters for the summaries to come.

Once the matrix is a film, it must be summarised at every instant.

Summarizing a directed network I · Strength and spectrum

Four gauges compress a transfer entropy matrix into a few numbers: total strength, Frobenius norm, spectral radius with its eigenvector, and spectral gap. The Perron Frobenius theorem makes the spectrum trustworthy only for non-negative, irreducible matrices. In the example, one weak entry estimated at minus 0.05 turns the leading eigenvalue negative and gives two regions negative centrality, while strength and norm barely move.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Summarising a directed network Part 1 — Strength and spectrum.

In Episode 11.1, the adjacency matrix became a film, one matrix of transfer entropies for every window of time. Nobody can watch 16 numbers or 4,000 change at once. We need a few numbers per frame that say how the network is doing. Today, summaries based on strength and on the spectrum of the matrix and one theorem that makes the spectrum trustworthy, under conditions that transfer entropy estimates do not always meet.

Here is the intuition. At a check-up, the doctor does not examine every cell of your body. She reads a few dials, your pulse, your blood pressure, your temperature. Each dial compresses the whole body into one number, and each dial is only meaningful if the body works the way the dial assumes. A blood pressure cuff that is too loose still shows a number. It just means nothing.

First gauge total strength. Add up every entry of the matrix, the total amount of directed information flowing in the network. Per node, the sum of a row is what that node sends, and the sum of a column is what it receives.

Second gauge the Frobenius norm. Square every entry, add them up, and take the square root. Like total strength, but it weights strong links more than weak ones, and it ignores signs. A negative entry adds to the norm as much as a positive one.

Third gauge the spectral radius. Compute the eigenvalues of the matrix and take the largest in absolute value. For a matrix of influences, it describes how strongly influence can circulate and amplify around the network, through loops of every length. The eigenvector that goes with it gives each node a score, a kind of centrality, how much it takes part in that dominant pattern of circulation.

A word of caution about that reading. In a linear dynamical system, the spectral radius of the coupling matrix has a precise meaning. Below one, perturbations die out, above one, they grow. A transfer entropy matrix is not a coupling matrix. Its entries are amounts of information in bits, not coefficients of a dynamical rule. Its spectral radius summarises the pattern of information links, but a value above or below one says nothing about stability.

Fourth gauge, the spectral gap, the difference between the largest and the second largest eigenvalue in absolute value. A large gap means one pattern dominates clearly, a small gap means two or more patterns compete, and the leading one can change with small perturbations.

Now the theorem that makes the third and fourth gauges trustworthy. The Perron Frobenius theorem says that for a matrix with no negative entries, the spectral radius is itself an eigenvalue, real and non-negative, and it has an eigenvector with no negative entries. That is what lets us read the eigenvector as a set of scores. If, in addition, the matrix is irreducible, meaning that every node can reach every other node through some chain of links, then the leading eigenvalue is strictly positive and simple, and its eigenvector is strictly positive. Every node gets a positive score.

Transfer entropy is non-negative in theory, so a transfer entropy matrix should enjoy all of this. In practice, it depends on the estimator.

Now the calculation in one picture.

Take a network of four regions with a matrix of transfer entropies in bits. A strong loop runs from region 1 to 2, 2 to 3, 3 to 4, and 4 back to 1, with values between 0.15 and 0.22. Weaker links between 0.01 and 0.03 connect the rest. Every entry is non-negative and every region can reach every other. The matrix is irreducible.

The gauge is total strength 0.90 bits, Frobenius norm 0.38. The leading eigenvalue is 0.22, real and positive, as Perron Frobenius promises. The next largest is minus 0.18, so the spectral gap is 0.04. And the leading eigenvector gives all four regions positive scores, 1, 0.90, 0.96, and 0.80.

As a side check, cut every link that leaves region 4. The matrix is still non-negative but no longer irreducible. Nothing comes back from region 4. The leading eigenvalue drops to 0.12, still real and positive, but region 4's score is now exactly 0. Non-negativity guarantees a meaningful eigenvector. Irreducibility guarantees that everyone counts.

Now the strong point of today. Go back to the full matrix and change a single weak entry. The transfer entropy from region 3 to region 1 was 0.03 bits. Suppose a k-nearest neighbour estimator, which can return negative values as we saw in episode 8.9, estimates it at minus 0.05 instead. One entry out of 12, a difference of eight hundredths of a bit.

Total strength barely moves, 0.82. The Frobenius norm barely moves either, 0.38 because it ignores the sign. But the spectrum changes character. The eigenvalue largest in absolute value is now minus 0.20, negative, with a positive eigenvalue of 0.20 just behind it. The spectral gap collapses to 0.001, and the leading eigenvector has mixed signs, 0.95 minus 0.95, 1 minus 0.66. Two regions now have negative centrality, which has no meaning at all.

A single negative transfer entropy from a k nearest neighbour estimator and the guarantees of Perron Frobenius fall.

What does each gauge depend on?

Total strength depends on every entry and so on estimation bias. A positive bias on every cell adds up across the whole matrix and grows with the number of nodes.

The Frobenius norm hides signs so it cannot reveal negative estimates.

The spectral radius and the eigenvector depend on the Perron Frobenius conditions, non-negative entries, and irreducibility for strictly positive scores. When either fails, the leading eigenvalue may be negative or complex, and the eigenvector may have mixed signs or complex entries.

The spectral gap depends on how many strong loops compete. Near ties like our minus 0.20 and plus 0.20 make the leading pattern unstable from one window to the next.

And all of them depend on the window, the estimator, and the threshold choices of episode 11.1.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study computes eigenvector centrality of transfer entropy networks in sliding windows and reports that a region's centrality flips sign at certain moments, interpreting the flips as the region switching from driver to suppressor. But the matrices were estimated with a k-nearest neighbour estimator, some entries were slightly negative, and the flips came from the loss of the Perron Frobenius guarantees.

The fix, before any spectral summary, check the signs of the entries. Either clip negative estimates to zero and say so, or use a bias-corrected estimate with a significance test, setting non-significant links to zero. Check irreducibility and report the spectral gap with the spectral radius so that readers can see when the leading pattern is unstable. Clipping is itself a choice with a cost. Setting every negative estimate to zero biases the matrix upward because the positive noise on the other entries stay. A significance test against surrogates with non-significant links set to zero treats positive and negative noise alike. Whatever you choose, apply it the same way in every window and every group.

The notebook for this episode builds the 4-region matrix, computes all 4 gauges, cuts the links from region 4, flips 1 weak entry to a negative value, and shows the eigenvector before and after.

Let's recap.

4 gauges, total strength, Frobenius norm, spectral radius, and spectral gap.

Perron Frobenius? For a non-negative matrix, the spectral radius is a real eigenvalue with a non-negative eigenvector. If the matrix is also irreducible, the eigenvector is strictly positive.

Our four-region network, leading eigenvalue 0.22, scores all positive.

One weak entry estimated at minus 0.05 instead of 0.03. The leading eigenvalue became negative, the gap collapsed to 0.001, and two regions got negative scores.

The Frobenius norm did not notice because it ignores signs, and total strength moved only a little. Only the spectrum revealed the problem by breaking. A transfer entropy matrix is not a dynamical coupling matrix, so its spectral radius says nothing about stability. Check signs before spectra.

Summarizing a directed network II · Entropy, asymmetry, efficiency

Five more gauges summarise a directed network: spectral entropy, asymmetry index, net flow per node, global efficiency, and mutual information between in and out strength. Two rules protect them. Centring the matrix before the singular value decomposition removes a dimension and creates negative entries that mean nothing, and the zero of a transfer entropy means zero predictive dependence, so it must not be shifted.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Summarising a directed network Part II — Entropy, asymmetry, efficiency.

In Episode 11.2, four gauges summarised a matrix of transfer entropies by its strength and its spectrum, and one negative estimate broke the spectrum. Today we add gauges of a different kind. How evenly the matrix spreads its structure, how one-sided it is, which nodes send more than they receive, how easily information can travel across the network, and whether senders are also receivers. Along the way, two rules about transfer entropy matrices that are easy to forget.

Here is the intuition. At the end of each day of the road trip, we write a log. How varied were the routes, one main road or many roads used equally? How many roads were one way? Which towns did we leave more often than we arrived in? How long, on average, from any town to any other, taking the fastest route? Each question is a gauge, and each one reads the same map differently.

First gauge spectral entropy. Take the singular value decomposition of the matrix. The singular values measure how much of the matrix lies along each independent pattern. Normalise them so that they sum to one and compute their Shannon entropy. If one pattern dominates, the entropy is low. If all patterns are equally strong, it is maximal the log of the number of nodes.

A related idea from Salvatore Morgera in 1985 is the covariance complexity, an information theoretic measure of how spread out the eigenvalues of a covariance matrix are. It applies to covariance matrices. Spectral entropy applies the same spirit to any matrix through its singular values.

Second gauge, the asymmetry index. Compare the matrix with its transpose. One common choice divides the size of the difference, the matrix minus its transpose by the size of the sum. Zero for a perfectly symmetric matrix, one for a matrix where every link goes only one way.

Third gauge net flow per node. For each node, what it sends, the sum of its row, minus what it receives, the sum of its column. Positive values mark net senders, negative values net receivers. Across the whole network, net flows always add up to zero.

Fourth gauge global efficiency. Turn each link into a distance, for example one over its strength, so that strong links are short roads. Find the shortest path between every ordered pair of nodes with Dijkstra's algorithm and average the inverses of those distances. High efficiency means information can reach anywhere quickly, possibly through intermediate nodes.

Fifth gauge, the mutual information between in degree and out degree, or in strength and out strength across nodes. Do the nodes that send a lot also receive a lot? Mutual information answers that, whatever the shape of the relation.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We use the four-region matrix of episode 11.2, with its loop from 1 to 2, 3, 4, and back to 1. Values in bits.

Spectral entropy, the singular values are 0.24, 0.19, 0.18, and 0.14. Their entropy is 1.98 bits, close to the maximum of 2, no single pattern dominates.

Asymmetry index, 0.83, strongly one-sided as expected for a loop. The symmetrised matrix would give zero.

Net flow, regions 1 and 3 each send 0.05 bits more than they receive. Region 2 receives 0.02 more than it sends, and region 4 0.08 more. The four values add up to zero.

Net flow is a balance sheet. It says who sends more than it receives, not through which links, nor to whom. Two networks with very different wiring can share the same net flows.

Global efficiency, 0.11. The shortest route from region 1 to region 3 does not use the direct link of 0.02 bits, a road 50 units long. It goes through region 2, 5 units plus 5.6. Efficiency looks at the network's best routes, not at individual links.

And the number depends on how strengths become distances. With minus the base 2 logarithm of each strength instead of its inverse, the same network has an efficiency of 0.26. Neither is wrong, they are different gauges, and only values computed the same way can be compared.

And for the mutual information between in and out strength, four nodes are too few, so we simulated networks of 60 nodes. When hubs both send and receive a lot, the in and out strengths have a correlation of 0.77 and a mutual information of 0.54 bits. When nodes specialise, strong senders being weak receivers, the correlation is minus 0.64 and the mutual information 0.66 bits. Mutual information sees both kinds of dependence equally. In a random network, it is 0.06 bits, the same as its value after shuffling the nodes, 0.05. That is the estimation floor for 60 nodes and 3 bins.

Now the strong point of today in two rules.

First rule, do not centre the matrix before the singular value decomposition. This is not a principal component analysis. In principal component analysis, we subtract the mean of each column because we study variation around the mean. Here the matrix itself is the object. If we subtract the column means anyway our four-region matrix loses one dimension entirely. Its smallest singular value becomes exactly zero, and its spectral entropy drops from 1.98 to 1.57 bits. The centred matrix also contains negative entries, which no longer mean anything as amounts of information.

Second rule, the zero of a transfer entropy has a meaning. It means zero predictive dependence, the source's past adds nothing to the prediction of the target. It is not an arbitrary baseline that can be shifted. Centring, standardising each row or adding a constant destroys that meaning, and with it the interpretation of every gauge that relies on it, from net flow to efficiency.

What do these gauges depend on?

Spectral entropy depends on the number of nodes through its maximum, so compare it in normalised form across networks of different sizes.

The asymmetry index depends on noise. Estimation noise is not symmetric, so even a truly symmetric system produces an asymmetry above zero in finite data. We simulated a symmetric four-region ring with memory 0.5 and coupling 0.2 20 times with 2,000 samples each. The estimated asymmetry index averaged 0.09 and ranged from about 0.03 to 0.16. Below that level, asymmetry cannot be told apart from noise.

Net flow depends on estimation bias. If one region's signal is noisier, its outgoing transfer entropies may be biased differently from its incoming ones.

Efficiency depends on the conversion from strengths to distances, 1 over the strength or minus the logarithm and on what happens to 0 or negative entries. A 0 becomes an infinite distance and a negative 1 has no distance at all.

And the in and out mutual information depends on the number of nodes and the binning, with a floor that must be estimated by shuffling.

And in a time-resolved analysis, every gauge inherits the window of episode 11.1. A gauge that jumps between windows may reflect a change in the network, or just a noisy estimate. Plotting it against the same gauge computed on surrogate data tells the two apart.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study runs a principal component analysis on a set of transfer entropy matrices, centring them first, and reports the spectral entropy of the components as a measure of network complexity. The centring removed a dimension and created negative values. The reported entropy describes deviations from the average network, not the networks themselves.

The fix, decompose the matrices as they are without centring, keep zeros as zeros, and if negative estimates appear, handle them explicitly as in episode 11.2.

The notebook for this episode computes the five gauges on the four-region matrix, compares the spectral entropy with and without centring, runs Dijkstra's algorithm on the distance matrix, and simulates the 60-node networks for the in-and-out mutual information.

Let's recap.

Five more gauges, spectral entropy of singular values, asymmetry index, net flow per node, global efficiency, and mutual information between in and out strength.

On the four-region loop, spectral entropy 1.98 bits, asymmetry 0.83, net senders 1 and 3, efficiency 0.11.

Do not centre before the singular value decomposition. It removed a dimension and dropped the entropy to 1.57.

The zero of a transfer entropy means zero predictive dependence. Keep it!

Recurrence plots

A recurrence plot marks the pairs of times at which a system comes back near an earlier state, like dots on a grid built from a road-trip diary, and it rests on one choice: how close counts. A sine wave and white noise differ at a small radius, but as the radius grows everything recurs and even noise draws diagonals. No universal threshold exists.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Recurrence plots.

In episodes 11.2 and 11.3, we summarised a network at each moment. Now we need tools to see when the dynamics change. Today's tool comes from dynamical systems rather than information theory, the recurrence plot. It is a picture of when a system comes back to where it has been and it rests entirely on one choice, how close counts as coming back.

Here is the intuition. Keep a diary of a long road trip, noting your position every hour. Then draw a square grid with the hours along both sides. Put a dot at row i and column j whenever your position at hour i was close to your position at hour j. If you drove in a loop, the dots form diagonal lines. Once you are back on the loop, you stay close to your earlier path for a while. If you wandered at random, the dots are scattered. If you move to a new region halfway through, the grid splits into blocks. All of that depends on what you call close, within a kilometre or within a hundred.

Here is the recipe. First, reconstruct the state of the system at each time by embedding, as in Chapter 7, a vector of m values of the signal spaced by a delay. Then compute the distance between every pair of state vectors. Then decide which pairs are recurrent. There are two ways.

The first is the original one by Jean-Pierre Eckmann, Sylvie Oliffson Kamphorst, and David Ruelle in 1987. For each time, mark its k nearest neighbours in state space, a fixed number of neighbours. The density of dots is then the same everywhere by construction, and the plot need not be symmetric. j can be among the nearest neighbours of i without i being among those of j.

The second, now the most common, uses a fixed radius, epsilon. A pair is recurrent when its distance is below epsilon. The plot is symmetric, and the density of dots, called the recurrence rate, depends on how the trajectory fills the space.

From the plot, recurrence quantification analysis extracts numbers. The simplest are the recurrence rate, the fraction of dots, and determinism, the fraction of dots that lie on diagonal lines of at least two points. Determinism is high when the system revisits its past in sequences, as a deterministic system would, and low when recurrences are isolated, as for noise.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We compare two signals of 400 samples, both standardised. A sine wave with a period of 25 samples plus a little noise and pure white noise. We embed both in three dimensions with a delay of six samples and use the maximum norm.

With a radius of 0.3 standard deviations, the sine gives a recurrence rate of about 1% and a determinism of 0.12. The noise gives 0.4% and 0.01. With a radius of 0.8, the sine gives 12.5% and 0.67, the noise 7.5% and 0.13. With a radius of 1.2, the sine gives 26% and 0.83, and now even the noise has a determinism of 0.37. As the radius grows, everything becomes recurrent, and even noise starts to draw diagonal lines.

Look at the two pictures at a radius of 0.3. The sine draws long parallel diagonals, spaced by 25 samples. Every time the wave comes back, it follows its own earlier path. The noise draws a thin, scattered dust of isolated dots, with no lines and no spacing. The eye sees the difference immediately. The numbers depend on the radius.

The fixed neighbour version behaves differently. With 10 nearest neighbours for every time point, the sine's recurrence rate is 2.6%, and it would be the same for any signal of this length. The density is fixed by construction. What changes is which points are neighbours, and the plot is no longer symmetric. In our sine, more than 2,000 pairs are recurrent in one direction but not the other. Fixing the number of neighbours removes the density as a quantity to compare, and keeps only the structure.

So which radius? This is the strong point of today: there is no universal threshold.

Several criteria have been proposed, each tied to a purpose. Lorenzo Matassini, Holger Kantz, and colleagues in 2002 optimised the neighbourhood size for noise reduction, linking it to the noise level of the signal. Stefan Schinkel, Olaf Dimigen, and Norbert Marwan in 2008 proposed to choose the threshold that maximises the power to detect a signal. Hauke Kraemer, Reik Donner, Jobst Heitzig and Norbert Marwan showed in 2018 that a threshold set as a fixed fraction of the attractor's size makes the results depend on the embedding dimension, and recommended a fixed recurrence rate instead, which is a fixed quantile of the distances. And a review of pitfalls by Marwan states plainly that a general, systematic answer to threshold selection is still missing. The right threshold depends on the aim of the analysis.

We tried the Schinkel criterion on our own detection problem. 40 segments of 200 samples containing a sine wave buried in noise of equal amplitude, and 40 segments of pure noise. For each radius, how well does determinism separate the two groups, measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, where 0.5 is chance? With a radius of 0.2 or 0.4, 0.5, no better than chance. At 0.6, 0.77. At one standard deviation, the best value, 0.85. At two, it falls back to 0.62. For this problem, the best radius is about one standard deviation. For another problem, it would be different.

What does a recurrence plot depend on?

First, the threshold and how it is defined. A fixed radius, a fixed number of neighbours, or a fixed recurrence rate.

Second, the embedding, dimension, and delay change every distance, as Kraemer and colleagues showed.

Third, the norm, maximum, or Euclidean distance give different neighbourhoods for the same radius.

Fourth, noise. Noise blurs every distance. An older rule of thumb asks for a radius of several times the noise standard deviation to recover noise-free structures.

And fifth, normalisation. A radius in standard deviation units changes meaning when an artefact inflates the standard deviation.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study compares determinism between two groups using one fixed radius, chosen because it is common in the literature. But the groups differ in signal amplitude and noise level, so the same radius corresponds to different recurrence rates in each group. The determinism difference may reflect the threshold, not the dynamics.

The fix state the threshold rule and its purpose, consider a fixed recurrence rate so that groups are compared at equal density, and show that the conclusion holds over a range of thresholds, not at a single value.

The notebook for this episode builds recurrence plots by fixed radius and by fixed neighbours, computes recurrence rate and determinism across radii for the sine and the noise, and reproduces the detection analysis that picks the best radius for one particular problem.

Let's recap.

A recurrence plot marks every pair of times whose states are close, by k nearest neighbours as in the original definition, or by a fixed radius.

As the radius grows, everything becomes recurrent. At 1.2 standard deviations, even white noise reached a determinism of 0.37.

There is no universal threshold. Criteria depend on the aim, noise reduction, signal detection robustness across embeddings.

For our detection problem, the best radius was one standard deviation, with an area under the curve of 0.85.

PLATE 11.58:21

Quadrant scans · Detecting transitions

The quadrant scan cuts a recurrence plot at a given time and measures the fraction of recurrences staying on their own side, which peaks at a transition. The original version needs data after the cut, so it is acausal. A causal version uses only the past but detects the switch about half a window late, and shorter windows detect sooner with a noisier background.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Quadrant scans — Detecting transitions.

In episode 11.4, the recurrence plot showed when a system comes back near its past. If the system switches from one regime to another, its recurrence plot splits into blocks. The states before the switch recur among themselves, the states after recur among themselves, and few recurrences cross between the two. Today we turn that picture into a number that peaks at transitions — the quadrant scan.

Here is the intuition. On the road trip, pick any hour and draw a cross through the diary grid at that hour, a vertical and a horizontal line. The grid is now cut into four quadrants. Two of them compare the trip before that hour with itself and after that hour with itself. The other two compare before with after. If you picked an ordinary hour in the middle of a long stretch of similar driving, the places you visit before and after look alike, and plenty of dots fall in the crossing quadrants. But if you picked the exact hour when you left the coast and drove into the mountains, almost nothing after looks like anything before. The dots stay on their own side. The fraction of dots that stay on their own side is highest at the moment of the change.

Here is the recipe. For each time t, split the recurrence plot, or a window of it around t, into four quadrants with a row and a column at t. Count the recurrences in the two quadrants that compare before with before and after with after. Divide by the recurrences in all four quadrants. That ratio is the quadrant scan Q of t. Points of a regular regime which recur across t keep Q moderate. At a transition, recurrences stop crossing and Q reaches a local maximum.

The method was analysed in detail and extended with a weighting scheme by Ayham Zaitouny, David Walker, and Michael Small in 2019. They showed that the quadrant scan can detect both changes of state and changes of dynamics, and applied it to temporal and non-temporal data, including multivariate and large datasets.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 600 samples of a signal that switches regime at sample 300. A slow oscillation with a period of 25 samples becomes a fast one with a period of 9 samples plus a little noise. We embedded it in three dimensions with a delay of 4 samples, built the recurrence plot with a recurrence rate of 10%, and computed the quadrant scan in windows of 100 samples on each side of t.

Far from the switch, Q sits around 0.5. Half of the recurrences in the window cross the cut, half stay on their side. At the switch, Q rises to 0.93, with its peak at state number 296. Each embedded state spans 8 samples, so the peak lands within a few samples of the true transition.

Now look at what that computation used. To evaluate Q at time t, it counted recurrences up to 100 samples after t. It looked into the future. The original quadrant scan is acausal. It can locate a transition precisely, but only once the data after the transition are available.

That is the strong point of today. The original version is acausal and the causal variant has a price.

To make it causal, compute Q at time t using only the past. A window covering the 200 samples before T, cut in the middle, at t minus 100. The cut now asks, was there a transition 100 samples ago? On our signal, the causal scan peaks at sample 396. The transition is found at the right place around 300, but only when we are already 100 samples past it. The delay equals the half window. A shorter window would detect sooner, but with fewer recurrences on each side and a noisier, less reliable peak.

We check the trade-off on the same signal. With half windows of 50 samples, the causal scan detects the switch at sample 346, with 25 samples at 321. Each time, the delay is about the half window, but the background gets noisier as the window shrinks. Away from the switch, Q fluctuates with a standard deviation of about 0.01 for half windows of 50 or 100 samples, and nearly three times more 0.03 for 25. On this clean simulated switch, the peak still stands out clearly. On real EEG with smaller differences between regimes, those background fluctuations would produce false alarms, and the shortest windows would cry wolf.

So the choice is explicit. Offline, to segment a recording into regimes, use the acausal scan and enjoy its precision. Online, to react to a transition as it happens, for example in neurofeedback or seizure warning, use the causal scan and accept a delay of half a window or more.

What does a quadrant scan depend on?

First, everything the recurrence plot depends on from episode 11.4, embedding norm and above all the threshold. We fix the recurrence rate at 10%. A different rate changes the contrast between regimes.

Second, the window. A window that is too short has too few recurrences to compare. A window that is too long can contain several transitions, which then blur into one.

Third, the kind of transition. A change of state where the system moves to a different region of state space separates the quadrants strongly. A subtle change of dynamics within the same region separates them less and gives a smaller peak.

Fourth, slow drifts. A gradual change of level without any change in dynamics also stops recurrences from crossing and can produce peaks. The quadrant scan detects that something changed, not what changed.

And fifth, the definition of a peak, a threshold on Q, a comparison with surrogate data, or the largest local maximum. Each choice changes how many transitions are reported.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study runs the original quadrant scan on EEG recorded during a task, finds peaks just before stimulus onset, and concludes that the brain changes state in anticipation of the stimulus. But the scan at a given time uses data up to half a window later. A transition caused by the stimulus itself can produce a peak before the stimulus, because the window already contains the post-stimulus data.

The fix say whether the scan is acausal or causal. For claims about timing, especially anticipation, use the causal version or shift the acausal peaks by the half window to bound when the information was available, and compare peak rates with surrogate data that keep the spectrum but remove transitions.

Transition hierarchies.

In episode 11.5, the quadrant scan found a transition in a signal that switched once. Real signals rarely switch once. They change on many timescales at the same time. A slow change of state within it faster episodes within those brief events. Today we look at what happens when the same signal is analysed at several scales. Transitions appear, merge, or disappear. This is an open research question and we present it as one, not as an established method.

Here is the intuition. Look back on a long road trip. At the scale of the whole trip there was one big transition, the day you left the coast for the mountains. At the scale of a day there were more, from motorway to village roads from driving to hiking. At the scale of an hour many more, every town every pause. None of these lists is wrong. Each one answers the question at its own scale. And the lists do not always nest neatly. A change that matters at one scale can vanish at another, and two changes at a fine scale can merge into one at a coarser scale.

Here is how we explore it. A recurrence plot depends on how states are reconstructed. The embedding dimension and delay together set the length of signal that each state covers its embedding window. The quadrant scan adds its own scale, the half window on each side of the cut. We vary both and record where the scan finds peaks.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 1200 samples with a two-level structure. In the first half, the signal alternates every 150 samples between a slow oscillation with a period of 20 samples and a slower one with a period of 32. In the second half, it alternates at the same rhythm between faster oscillations with periods of 8 and 12 samples. So there is one big transition at sample 600 and three smaller ones in each half.

First, we vary the quadrant scan window with a short embedding covering seven samples. With half windows of 30 samples, the scan finds peaks at about 595, 750, 900, and 1050, the big transition and the three small ones of the fast half. With half windows of 75 samples the same four. With half windows of 250, only one peak remains at about 595. The small transitions have merged into the background of the big one.

But notice what was never found, the three small transitions of the slow half. With an embedding window of only seven samples, an oscillation of period 20 and one of period 32 look almost the same, a smooth curve and the states overlap.

Second, we vary the embedding. With four dimensions and a delay of six samples, each state now covers 19 samples. Same quadrant window of 30. Now the scan finds the slow half's transitions at about 140, 285, and 440, and the fast halves at about 740, 890 and 1040. But the big transition at 600 is gone. With this embedding, the boundary between the halves no longer stands out against the transitions within them.

Put the results side by side as a map. Scale on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis, a mark wherever the scan finds a peak. With the short embedding and short windows, four marks, one at 600, three in the fast half. As the scan window widens, the three fast half marks fade and only 600 survives. With the longer embedding, six marks, three in each half, and the mark at 600 is missing. No single row of the map tells the whole story, and no row is a simple subset of another. The map itself is the result.

Why would the big transition disappear? A likely reason, with a 19 sample embedding, the states of the slow half and the fast half differ, but so do the states within each half by similar amounts. The quadrant scan measures contrast relative to the neighbourhood of the cut. When every cut looks like a boundary, none stands out.

That is the strong point of today. The set of transitions you find depends on the scale at which you look, both the embedding window and the scan window, and the dependence is not a simple nesting. Change the scale and transitions appear, merge, or disappear. Whether there is a single correct hierarchy of transitions in a brain signal and how to recover it is an open research question. We present it as such.

What would a principled answer need? It would need to track each transition across a continuum of scales rather than three or four chosen values, as is done for features in scale space methods of image analysis. It would need a null model at every scale to decide which peaks are more than fluctuations. It would need a way to relate the scales of the analysis to the time scales of the underlying processes, which, as episode 10.3 showed for multiscale entropy, is not automatic. And it would need validation on systems where the true hierarchy is known, like our simulation, and then on brain recordings with independent markers such as sleep stages or task events.

What does a transition hierarchy depend on?

First, the embedding window, which decides which differences between states are visible at all.

Second, the scan window, which decides which transitions are resolved and which merge.

Third, the threshold of the recurrence plot and the rule for calling a peak.

And fourth, the kind of transitions present. Changes of state stand out at most scales, subtle changes of dynamics only at some.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study analyses an EEG recording with one embedding and one window, finds four transitions, and reports them as the transitions of the recording. Another study with a different embedding finds six at different times. Neither is wrong, both are partial, each tied to its scale.

The fix report transitions together with the scales that produced them. When possible, show how they move across a range of scales as a map of transitions against scale, and avoid statements about the transitions of a signal without saying at what scale.
