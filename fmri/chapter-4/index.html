Look at your data first

Preprocessing begins by looking at the data, because a number can hide a disaster. MRIQC computes quality metrics and visual reports, a tSNR map shows where the signal is stable, and the carpet plot, with one thread per voxel, reveals head jumps and breathing waves. Two participants with the same average motion can look perfect in a table yet differ sharply on the carpet.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 4, Preprocessing. Our new world is a laundry. Before anything goes into the machine, a good launderer looks at every garment. A stain here, a hole there, a pen forgotten in a pocket. Data deserve the same care. Today, the first rule of preprocessing. Look at your data first.

Why look when software does everything? Because a number can hide a disaster. A summary table may say that a run is fine, while a few seconds of it are ruined. And once an artifact enters the pipeline, it spreads quietly into every later step.

The first helper is MRIQC, introduced by Oscar Esteban and colleagues in 2017. It reads a BIDS dataset, and for every scan, it computes image quality metrics. They need no perfect reference image. For functional runs, they include head motion, signal stability, ghosting, and sudden jumps in intensity. MRIQC also draws a visual report for each scan and a group report where unusual runs stand out from the crowd.

Could a machine simply decide for us? The MRIQC team trained a classifier to accept or exclude anatomical scans. On new sites, its accuracy was about 76%. Useful but not enough to replace your eyes.

The second view is the Temporal Signal-to-Noise Map, or tSNR. For each voxel, divide its mean over time by its standard deviation over time. A stable voxel has a high tSNR. Edges of the brain, regions near the sinuses, and runs with motion show low values. The tSNR map shows you where your signal is reliable, and where it simply is not there.

The third view is the carpet plot, described by Jonathan Power in 2017. Picture a woven carpet. Each thread is one voxel. Time runs from left to right. Threads are grouped by tissue, gray matter, white matter, and fluid. Above the carpet, we draw traces like head motion and breathing. Power called this plot the single most informative summary of a scan he knew how to make.

Now learn to read the patterns. A fine, even grain all over is thermal noise normal. A sharp vertical band crossing every thread at once is a head movement. A slow, dark wave across gray matter, often after a deep breath, is respiration. And a band that appears only in the edges of the brain points to motion or to something outside the brain.

Here is the moment that makes the point. Two participants with the same small average head motion. On the summary table, both look perfect. On the carpet, the first is calm and even. The second has three sharp jumps and a long, dark band after a big sigh. The average hid everything. Looking at the carpet takes 10 seconds and it changes your decision.

A few habits for the laundry. Look at every run, not just a few. Check the first volumes, the mean image, the tSNR map, and the carpet. Write your exclusion criteria before you look at any results and apply them the same way to everyone. Rate scans with a written protocol and keep notes. And remember that bad data cannot be washed clean later. They can only be found early.

To sum up, numbers can hide disasters. MRIQC computes quality metrics and visual reports. The tSNR map shows where your signal is stable. The carpet plot shows motion, breathing, and noise at a glance. Decide your criteria before seeing results. Next time, motion correction.

PLATE 4.23:47

Motion correction

The head is rigid, so six numbers, three translations and three rotations, describe each image. Software realigns every volume to a reference and yields six motion curves, from which framewise displacement summarizes movement per image. Realignment is not enough, because motion also alters the signal itself through spin history, so the parameters enter the model as nuisance regressors and bad volumes can be scrubbed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In our laundry, there is a folding table with an outline printed on it. Each shirt that comes out of the dryer lands a little crooked. Before stacking, we slide it and turn it until it fits the outline. Then the whole pile lines up. A functional run is a pile of brain images, and each one lands a little crooked too. Today, motion correction.

Even a willing participant moves. They breathe, they swallow, they slowly sink into the cushion, they nod when a run starts or ends. A few tenths of a millimeter are enough to change which tissue sits inside a voxel at the edge of the brain.

The good news is that the head is rigid. It moves, but it does not change shape. So each image can be described by six numbers. Three translations, left and right, back and front, up and down. And three rotations. A nod, a tilt towards the shoulder, and a shake as if saying no.

First we choose a reference image. It can be the first volume, the middle one, the mean of the run, or the single band reference from chapter 3. Then for every other volume the software tries small shifts and turns until the image matches the reference as closely as possible. This is what MCFLIRT does in FSL, the realign step in SPM and 3dvolreg in AFNI.

The result is six curves over time, one per parameter. Always look at them. A slow slide often means the head is sinking into the cushion. A sudden spike means a quick movement, a cough, a startle, a sneeze.

To summarize motion in one number per image, Jonathan Power and colleagues proposed the framewise displacement in 2012. For each volume, take how much each of the six parameters changed since the previous volume. Rotations are turned into millimeters as the distance traveled on a sphere of 50 millimeters, about the size of a head. Then add the six absolute values. Their first threshold was half a millimeter. Many studies now use a stricter 0.2 millimeters. And remember that the same value means more movement per second with a short TR.

Now the warning of this episode. Realigning is not enough. We put each shirt back on the outline but the creases stay. When the head moves, the signal itself is changed. Spins that were excited at the old position arrive with a different history and give a different signal for several images. Karl Friston and colleagues described this spin history effect in 1996. Voxels at the edges mix different tissues, and the distortions of the image change with the position of the head in the field.

So motion leaves traces that realignment cannot remove. That is why the motion parameters themselves go into the analysis as nuisance regressors. Often an expanded set of 24 proposed by Friston and colleagues with the current position, the previous position, and their squares. And volumes with large framewise displacement can be flagged and removed, a step called scrubbing, which we will meet again in the denoising episode.

One more detail, each resampling of an image blurs it a little. Modern pipelines like fMRIPrep combine realignment, distortion correction and normalization into a single resampling step, to blur only once.

To sum up, the head is rigid so six parameters describe each image. We realign every volume to a reference, plot the six curves and compute the framewise displacement. But realignment does not erase the traces of motion. Spin history, edges, and distortions remain, so motion parameters go into the model and bad volumes can be scrubbed. Next time slice timing.

PLATE 4.33:52

Slice timing

A volume is not a snapshot: its slices are acquired one after another across the repetition time, in an order stored in the sidecar. Slice timing correction interpolates each slice to a reference time, which matters most for long TRs and fast designs. A temporal derivative in the model can absorb small delays instead, and motion complicates the order of steps.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In our laundry, a row of washing machines runs the same program, but they were not started together. The first one started at the top of the minute, the next one a moment later, and so on down the row. Look through the windows, and every drum is at a different point in its cycle. A brain volume works the same way. Today, slice timing.

A volume looks like a snapshot, but it is not. With classic echo-planar imaging, the brain is acquired slice by slice, one after the other during the TR. With a TR of 2 seconds, the last slice can be recorded almost 2 seconds after the first. Same volume, same label in the file, but different moments in time.

The order varies. Ascending from bottom to top. Descending from top to bottom. Or interleaved all the odd slices first, then all the even ones. Each scanner protocol has its own order, and the exact time of every slice is stored in the slice timing field of the JSON sidecar.

Why does it matter? Imagine the same brief response in two voxels, one in the first slice and one in the last. When we line up their time series volume by volume, the second one looks shifted by almost a full TR. Our model assumes that every voxel was sampled at the same moment. For late slices, the predicted response and the real one no longer match, and the fit gets worse. This hurts most with short events, rapid designs, and comparisons of timing between regions.

Slice timing correction fixes this by shifting time series instead of washing machines. First, we choose a reference time, often the middle of the TR. Then, for each slice, we interpolate its time series as if every sample had been taken at the reference time. FSL uses a windowed sinc interpolation. SPM shifts the phase of each frequency in the Fourier domain. Both estimate values between the real samples.

So when is it useful? It used to be said that with short TRs, below 1 second slice timing correction was unnecessary. Multiband sequences also acquire several slices at once, which shrinks the spread of times. But David Parker and Qolamreza Razlighi in 2019 still found a benefit at sub-second TRs and for connectivity analyses. The gain is smaller but not zero.

There is another route. Instead of shifting the data, adapt the model. Adding the temporal derivative of the response function lets each voxel's response move a little earlier or later, typically by up to about a second. Some packages also let you sample the model at the time of a reference slice.

And there is a complication. Motion. When the head moves, a piece of tissue can drift from one slice to another, and therefore from one acquisition time to another. That is why the best order between slice timing and motion correction depends on how much participants move, and on the slice order, as Sladky and colleagues showed in 2011.

A few practical points. Make sure the slice times are in your sidecar files and that they match the real acquisition, including multiband groups. Decide before analyzing whether you correct the data or model the delays and report it. Do not do both by accident.

To sum up, a volume is not a snapshot. Slices are acquired at different times within the TR in an order saved in the sidecar. Slice timing correction interpolates every slice to a reference time. It matters most with long TRs and fast designs, but helps even with short TRs. A temporal derivative can absorb small delays. And motion complicates everything. Next time unwarping distortions.

PLATE 4.44:01

Unwarping distortions

Air next to tissue bends the magnetic field, and because echo-planar imaging is slow along the phase direction, a field offset is mistaken for position and the image stretches or squeezes. A field map or a pair of opposite phase-encoding images lets each voxel be moved back, like blocking a wool jumper. Signal dropout is a hole that only acquisition can prevent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A wool jumper comes out of the machine, and something is wrong. One side is stretched, the other is squeezed, all along the same direction. The fix is called blocking. You pin the jumper on a board with a grid, and using your measurements you pull each part back where it belongs. But if a moth has eaten a hole, no amount of pinning will close it. Today, unwarping distortions.

Where does the stretching come from? Air and tissue react differently to a magnetic field. Near the sinuses and the ear canals, air sits right next to brain, and the field there is no longer uniform. Near these cavities, the field offset can exceed 100 Hz.

Echo-planar imaging is very fast in one direction and slow in the other, the phase encoding direction. Along that slow direction, a field offset is mistaken for a position. Each voxel is pushed by an amount equal to the field offset, in hertz, times the total readout time in seconds. For example, 100 hertz with a readout of 50 milliseconds moves the signal by 5 voxels. That example is illustrative, but the order of magnitude is real.

Where neighboring voxels are pushed together, the signal piles up and looks too bright. Where they are pulled apart, it thins out and looks too dark. Reverse the phase encoding direction, and the image is pushed the other way. Let us call this mischief warp.

To undo warp, we must measure the field, with the scans from chapter 3. A field map from two gradient echoes, an approach described by Peter Jezzard and Robert Balaban in 1995. Or a pair of images with opposite phase encoding, one pushed forward and one pushed back. From such a pair, TOPUP by Jesper Andersson, Stefan Skare, and John Ashburner in 2003 estimates the displacement that explains both.

Then comes the blocking. Every voxel is moved back along the phase encoding direction by its estimated displacement. And intensities are corrected too. Where signal had piled up, it is spread out again. Where it had thinned out, it is gathered back.

How do you know it worked? Draw the boundary between gray and white matter, taken from the sharp anatomical image, on top of the functional image. Before correction, the front of the brain spills over the lines. After, it should follow them. Tools like fMRIPrep show exactly this view in their reports. Look at it for every participant.

Now the moth hole. In some places, the field changes so fast within a single voxel, especially across the slice, that the spins inside it fall out of step and cancel each other. The signal is not moved. It is gone before it is even recorded. This dropout hits the orbitofrontal cortex and the inferior temporal lobes near the ear canals.

Unwarping cannot bring back a signal that was never measured. Only the acquisition can help. Thinner slices, a shorter echo time, tilting the slices or extra gradients called Z-shims. Nikolaus Weiskopf and colleagues showed in 2006 that the best slice tilt can recover much of the sensitivity in these regions. If your hypothesis lives near the sinuses, check the coverage in your pilot before the real study.

And in the analysis, be honest about the holes. A region without signal cannot show an effect. Its absence on your map is not evidence that it was silent. Group masks often lose these areas, so say so in your report.

To sum up, air and tissue bend the field. EPI turns field offsets into displacements along the phase encoding direction. Measure the field with a field map or an opposite phase encoding pair and unwarp each voxel. Check the result against the anatomy. But dropout is a hole, not a stretch, and only the acquisition can prevent it. Next time, coregistration and normalization.

PLATE 4.54:17

Coregistration and normalization

Two jobs align brains: coregistration fits the functional image to the same person's anatomy with a rigid transform guided by the boundary between white and gray matter, and normalization then warps the anatomy into a standard template, first linearly and then non-linearly, in a single resampling. Large structures line up but fine folds do not, so one coordinate is never exactly one area.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the end of a laundry day, two jobs remain. First, every garment must find its owner. Then, for the shop window, every shirt goes onto the same standard mannequin, whatever the size of the person who wears it. Brain images need both jobs too. Today, coregistration and normalization.

The first job is coregistration. We align a person's functional images with that same person's anatomical image. Same head, same session, so the shape does not change. A rigid transform is enough, six parameters again. The difficulty is that the two images look nothing alike. The functional image is blurry and low in contrast. The anatomy is sharp with bright white matter and darker gray matter.

A clever trick solves this. From the anatomy, we know exactly where the boundary between white and gray matter lies. Boundary-based registration proposed by Douglas Greve and Bruce Fischl in 2009 slides the functional image until its intensity changes most sharply right across that boundary, all over the brain. The functional image is blurry but it still has that edge. fMRIPrep uses this approach by default.

The second job is normalization. Now we bring each person's anatomy into a standard space such as MNI-152, our mannequin atlas. It starts with a linear step, 12 parameters that fixes overall position, size, and proportions. A small head is scaled up, a long head is shortened. Then comes a non-linear warp with a huge number of local adjustments that pushes and pulls each region to match the template.

Many algorithms exist. ANTs with its SyN method, FNIRT in FSL, DARTEL in SPM. In 2009, Arno Klein and colleagues compared 14 non-linear methods in more than 45,000 registrations between manually labeled brains. ART, SyN, IRTK, and DARTEL gave the best results.

In practice, the steps are chained, functional to anatomy, anatomy to template. Their transforms are combined, and as we saw with motion, the functional data are resampled only once, straight into the standard space.

Now the honest part of this episode. Take two brains that were normalized perfectly into the same template. Look at the big picture first. The overall shape, the ventricles, the major lobes, all line up well. Then look closer. The fine folds do not. Each person has their own pattern of sulci, and some folds even exist in one brain and not in the other.

And brain areas are not defined by folds. They are defined by their microscopic architecture, and their borders shift from person to person. So the same coordinate is never exactly the same area. Probabilistic atlases measure this. The Jülich atlas built from 10 post-mortem brains tells you, at each coordinate, the probability of being in a given area. Often it is a probability, not a certainty.

There are ways to do better. Surface-based methods align the folding patterns themselves on the inflated or spherical surface we met in Chapter 2. The Human Connectome Project went further, aligning surfaces using several kinds of maps at once, which made it possible to draw a map of 180 areas per hemisphere, published by Matthew Glasser and colleagues in 2016.

As always check, draw the outline of the template on top of each normalized brain and the anatomy's boundaries on top of the functional image. The edges should match, fMRIPrep reports show both views. A failed registration is easy to see and terrible to miss.

To sum up, coregistration aligns functional images to the same person's anatomy with a rigid transform best guided by the gray and white matter boundary. Normalization warps the anatomy into a template, first linearly, then non-linearly. The transforms are combined and applied once. And remember, the same coordinate is never exactly the same area. Next time smoothing.

PLATE 4.63:39

Smoothing

Spatial smoothing replaces each voxel by a Gaussian-weighted average of its neighbors, with width given by the FWHM. It lowers noise, helps activations overlap across people and satisfies assumptions of some statistics, but small effects merge or vanish and signal bleeds across tissues. The right kernel depends on the question, decoding usually wants little, and it is chosen before seeing results.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last stop in the laundry, the ironing board. A warm iron removes all the little creases. But press a huge iron too hard on a fine embroidery, and the small stitches flatten and the colors bleed into each other. Ironing is a choice of size and pressure. Today, smoothing.

Spatial smoothing replaces each voxel by a weighted average of itself and its neighbors. The weights follow a bell shape in three dimensions, a Gaussian kernel. Its width is given by the full width at half maximum or FWHM. That is the distance between the two points where the bell falls to half its height. In practice, kernels of 4 to 8 mm are common, and 8 mm is often the default in software.

Why blur data we worked so hard to sharpen? There are three reasons.

First, noise. Thermal noise is random from one voxel to the next. When we average neighbors, the noise partly cancels, while a real activation that spreads over several voxels survives. This idea is called the matched filter. It works best when the kernel is about the size of the effect you are looking for.

Second people. As we saw in the last episode, even after normalization, anatomy does not line up perfectly between brains. A little blur makes activations from different people overlap more, which helps group analyses.

Third, statistics. Some correction methods based on random field theory assume that the data are smooth enough. A common rule of thumb is a kernel at least twice the voxel size.

Now the price. Look at this map with one large activation and two small ones close together. With no smoothing, all three are there but noisy. At 4mm, the noise calms down and all three survive. At 8mm, the two small spots merge into one. At 12, they almost disappear, swallowed by the bell. Smoothing also bleeds signal across the banks of a sulcus, into white matter and fluid, and it can move a peak.

So the right kernel depends on the question. For a classic group analysis of large regions, a moderate kernel helps. For fine maps, small structures, or a single person, smooth little or not at all.

And decoding. Multivariate pattern analysis, which we meet in Chapter 7 look at fine patterns across voxels. So they are often run on unsmoothed or lightly smoothed data. But the story is not simple. Hans Op de Beeck reported in 2010 that decoding of orientation survived even 8 millimeters of smoothing, while other studies found losses for the finest patterns. Here too, think about the scale of the information you expect.

There is also another way to iron. Instead of blurring in the volume, smooth along the cortical surface, as we saw in Chapter 2. Neighbors along the ribbon are averaged, but the two banks of a sulcus stay apart.

One last rule. Choose your kernel before you look at results, write it down, and report it. Trying several kernels and keeping the one that gives the prettiest map is a fork in the garden of forking paths, which we will explore in the last chapter.

To sum up, smoothing averages each voxel with its neighbors, with a Gaussian kernel defined by its FWHM. It reduces noise, helps overlap across people, and satisfies some statistics. But it shrinks small effects, merges neighbors, and bleeds across tissues. Match the kernel to your question, consider the surface, and decide before you look. Next time denoising.

PLATE 4.74:21

Denoising · Confounds, CompCor, ICA

Denoising is a stain-removal kit of nuisance regressors: motion parameters, white-matter and fluid signals, CompCor, ICA-AROMA, censoring of bad volumes, and the debated global signal regression. A comparison of strategies showed clear trade-offs between removing motion effects and distance-dependent artifacts. Every regressor costs a degree of freedom, so the strategy is chosen before looking at results and what is removed is reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Some stains survive the wash. So every good laundry keeps a stain removal kit. One product for grease, another for wine, another for ink. But every product also wears away a little of the fabric. Use too many and the shirt gets thin. Today, denoising. The idea is simple. We measure or estimate the signals we do not want, and we put them in the model as nuisance regressors. Whatever the data share with them is removed. Our kit has several products.

First, motion parameters. The six realignment curves from episode 4.2 often expanded to 24 with their previous values and their squares.

Second, signals from tissues where we do not expect the neural signal of interest. The average signal in white matter and in the fluid around the brain. Their fluctuations come from breathing, heartbeat, the scanner, and motion. So we remove them from every voxel.

Third, CompCor, introduced by Yashar Behzadi and colleagues in 2007. Instead of one average per tissue, it finds the main patterns of variation inside white matter and fluid, with a principal component analysis and keeps the strongest few. The anatomical version is called aCompCor.

Fourth, ICA-AROMA, by Raymond Pruim and colleagues in 2015. The data are split into independent components. Each component is then judged by a few simple features. Does it follow the head motion curves? Is it mostly fast fluctuations? Does it sit on the edge of the brain or in the fluid? Components that look like motion are removed.

Fifth, censoring also called scrubbing. Volumes with a large frame-wise displacement or a sudden jump in intensity are flagged. They are either removed or each one gets its own regressor.

And sixth, a product that divides the profession. Global signal regression removes the average signal of the whole brain. It cleans widespread noise very well, but it can also remove real signal and change the pattern of correlations between regions.

So which kit should you use? In 2017, Rastko Ciric and colleagues compared 14 common strategies in 393 young people. They used three benchmarks. How much connectivity still depends on motion after cleaning. Whether short connections are still inflated compared to long ones, a distance-dependent artifact. And how many degrees of freedom each strategy uses up.

The results showed clear trade-offs. Strategies with global signal regression best reduced the link between connectivity and motion, but left the distance-dependent artifact. Censoring reduced both but cost more degrees of freedom. And the weakest strategies could not even recover the modular structure of brain networks.

Now the fabric. Every regressor you add removes one degree of freedom. Every censored volume removes data. With 200 volumes and 50 or more nuisance regressors, not much is left to estimate what you care about. And if one participant loses many more volumes than another, the groups are no longer comparable. Count what you remove and report it.

There is also a subtler trap. If head motion is correlated with your task, for example, people move whenever they press a button, then motion regressors can absorb part of your real effect. Check how your nuisance regressors correlate with your design before you trust the result.

Practical tools make this easier. fMRIPrep outputs a table with all these confounds for every run, and nilearn offers ready-made strategies to load them. Choose your strategy before you see your results, apply it to everyone in the same way, and write it in your pre-registration. To sum up, denoising removes signals you do not want with nuisance regressors: motion parameters, tissue signals, CompCor, ICA-AROMA, censoring, and carefully the global signal. Strategies are not equal and they trade cleanliness for degrees of freedom. Count what you remove, watch for regressors that overlap your task and decide before you look. PLATE 4.84:04

One pipeline, in the right order

A preprocessing pipeline runs an anatomical lane and a functional lane in the right order, combines all transforms into a single resampling, and leaves smoothing and denoising to the analysis, delivering confounds as a table. fMRIPrep packages this as a glass box with reports. Software versions change results, so versions are frozen in containers and reported, while SPM, FSL, AFNI and others are alternatives.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Our laundry has a conveyor belt. Sorting, inspecting, washing, drying, folding, ironing, packing. Every station matters, and so does the order. Nobody irons a shirt before washing it. To close chapter 4, let us put all our steps on one belt. Today, one pipeline in the right order.

The belt has two lanes. On the anatomical lane, the sharp T1 image is corrected for uneven brightness, separated from the skull, and divided into gray matter, white matter, and fluid. Optionally, FreeSurfer reconstructs the cortical surfaces. Then the anatomy is registered to a template, such as MNI-152.

On the functional lane, a clean reference image is built for each run. Head motion is estimated against it. Slice timing can be corrected. The distortion field is estimated from the field map or the opposite phase encoding pair. The run is aligned to the anatomy with boundary-based registration. And all the confound signals are computed, motion, tissue signals, CompCor, frame-wise displacement.

Then comes the clever part. All the transforms, motion, distortion, coregistration, and normalization are combined and applied in a single step. The data are resampled only once into each space you ask for, volume, or surface.

Smoothing and denoising usually come later in the analysis itself because they depend on your question. That is why the confounds are delivered as a table, not removed for you.

This belt has a name, fMRIPrep, published by Oscar Esteban and colleagues in Nature Methods in 2019. It is analysis agnostic. It combines tools from FSL, ANTs, FreeSurfer, and AFNI, choosing one for each step. It follows a glass box philosophy. Every participant gets an HTML report with views at each quality checkpoint. And the report includes a ready-made methods paragraph with every tool and its citation for your paper.

Now the warning of this episode. Software is not timeless. In 2012, Ed Gronenschild and colleagues processed the same 30 anatomical scans with three versions of FreeSurfer, on different computers and operating systems. Between the newest version and the older ones, brain volumes differed by about 9% on average, and cortical thickness by about 3%. Changing the workstation or the operating system gave differences about half as large. Those are the sizes of many effects that studies report.

So freeze your belt. Use one version for every participant in a study and do not upgrade halfway through. Software containers like Docker or Apptainer package the whole environment, the tool, its libraries, and the operating system so that the same analysis runs the same way years later. And always report the exact version you used.

fMRIPrep is not the only belt. SPM, born in London, works in MATLAB. FSL comes from Oxford, AFNI from the National Institutes of Health in the United States. FreeSurfer specializes in surfaces, ANTs, and registration. The Human Connectome Project has its own minimal preprocessing pipelines, built for its data. And in Python, nilearn picks up where preprocessing ends. Each has its defaults and its habits. Pick one, understand what it does, and report it fully.

To sum up, preprocessing is a chain with an order. An anatomical lane, a functional lane, and transforms combined into a single resampling. Smoothing and denoising are left to the analysis. fMRIPrep packages this as a glass box with reports and a methods text. Versions matter, so freeze them in a container and report them. That closes chapter 4. Next time we open Chapter 5, Task Analysis with the General Linear Model built by hand.
