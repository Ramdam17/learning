Layers

Flights between airports need layers: one per airline, each node copied in every layer, copies joined by couplings with a switching cost. In the European air network of 37 airlines, Istanbul and London Luton are two flights apart on the flat graph, but no airline serves both, so the trip needs a change. Flattening hides that price.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, every graph in this series had one kind of dot and one kind of line. This chapter breaks that rule, and it begins in a pastry shop.

On the wall hangs a flight planner. You want to fly from one airport to another. Look at it the way we have learned to look: each airport is a dot, and each flight is a line.

A graph was hiding on the wall.

But our graph is missing something. Every flight has a colour, and each colour is an airline. Changing from one airline to another can cost time and money, and a plain graph cannot say that.

So dots and lines are not enough. We need layers, like the layers of a mille-feuille.

Start small, with people. Here are five of them, an illustrative example: Ana, Ben, Cleo, Dan and Eve. They are linked in three ways. Some are friends, some work together, and some are family.

Put everything in one graph, and the three kinds of link look the same. Instead, give each kind of link its own layer. Friendship is the pink layer, work is the blue one, and family is the yellow one.

Each layer holds all five people. A person is copied once in every layer. The copies of one person are joined by a short link that says: this is the same person, in another layer.

Crossing that link has a price. We call it the switching cost.

Let a walker go from Ana to Dan. First she follows a friendship, from Ana to Ben. Then she steps down at Ben, and pays the switching cost. Last, she follows a family link, from Ben to Dan. Two links, one crossing. A trip is a path through the layers.

In 2014, Kivelä and colleagues wrote a review that gives these multilayer networks a common framework and a shared vocabulary.

Remember the table from one point five. The row is where a link starts, and the column is where it ends. A layered network has a table too.

It has fifteen rows and fifteen columns: five people, times three layers. The diagonal holds one block for each layer, and each block is that layer's own table. Off the diagonal sit the couplings, one mark for each person and each pair of layers.

Count the filled cells. Sixteen belong to links inside a layer, two for each link. Thirty belong to couplings.

Now flatten the network. Add the three diagonal blocks on top of each other, and throw the rest away. What is left is an ordinary graph. The layers are gone.

Now the real thing. In 2013, Cardillo and colleagues described the European air network as thirty-seven layers, one for each airline. The data are flights between European airports, from EUROCONTROL. The dataset lists four hundred fifty airports, and four hundred seventeen of them have at least one flight. Altogether the layers hold three thousand five hundred eighty-eight links.

Its table has sixteen thousand six hundred fifty rows, and as many columns: four hundred fifty airports, times thirty-seven airlines. Almost all of it is empty. Only two thousand thirty-four of those rows are an airport that an airline really serves.

Flatten it, and you get one graph. Two airports are linked if at least one airline flies between them. That makes two thousand nine hundred fifty-three links. This flat graph is connected: any two of the four hundred seventeen airports have a route.

But ask a traveller who stays on one airline. Only thirty-five percent of the pairs have such a trip.

Take Istanbul, code LTBA, and London Luton, code EGGW. On the flat graph, they are two flights apart. There are twenty different ways to do it, through twenty different airports. That looks very well connected.

Now put the layers back. On every one of those twenty routes, the two flights belong to different airlines. Fifteen airlines serve Istanbul. Six serve Luton. Not one airline serves both.

So the trip needs a change, and a change has a price. Here is the price we will use. It is a modelling choice, not a measurement. A flight costs one. Changing airline at an airport costs two, as much as two flights.

The cheapest trip is still two flights, plus one change. Its cost is four, not two. Try a price of one or three, and the cost becomes three or five. The pair still needs a change.

This pair is not an exception. Count the pairs of airports that are two flights apart on the flat graph: twenty-nine thousand five hundred sixty. For seven thousand eight hundred five of them, twenty-six percent, no airline flies the whole trip.

At our price, sixty-six percent of all pairs need at least one change. Three thousand four hundred eight need two. Ten pairs need three.

A shortest path, a centrality, a community: any measure from chapters six to eight, computed on the flat graph, cannot see airlines. For this traveller's shortest path, we have just seen what that costs. Keeping the layers keeps the price of passing from one to the other.

To sum up. One layer for each kind of link, and each node copied in every layer. Couplings join the copies, and they carry a switching cost. The table becomes a table of blocks: layers on the diagonal, couplings off it. Flattened, the price disappears.

Next, a question. Two people are recorded at the same time, each with a brain full of regions. Is that one network, or two?

PLATE 15.24:49

Two brains, one network

Two brains recorded at once form two layers, with links between layers joining different regions, and the table has four blocks. A link between brains is a resemblance between signals, never a cable. In a simulated pair, a beat both people hear creates dark cells, but pseudo-pairs show which single link belongs to this pair alone.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. Two people are recorded at the same time. Is that one network, or two?

Here is a simulated example. Person A and person B each wear a cap, and each cap follows six regions of one brain. Twelve regions in all. Nothing here is a real recording.

Inside one brain, we know what to do. Measure how much two regions keep step, and draw a link when it is strong. The measure is the phase locking value. It runs from zero to one, and it says how steadily two rhythms keep the same offset.

That gives one graph for person A, and one for person B. Two layers.

Now compare the two people. Take a region of A and a region of B, and compute the same number. Draw a link when it is strong. These links run between the layers.

They are not the links of the last episode. There, a link joined two copies of one node. Here, it joins two different regions, and it carries a measure, not a price.

Write it as a table. Twelve rows, twelve columns, a hundred forty-four cells. It falls into four blocks of thirty-six. Person A sits at the top left, and person B at the bottom right. Between the brains are the two blocks off the diagonal. This measure has no direction, so those two blocks are mirror images. The Hyperscanning series draws this same table, with the same four blocks.

People have done this with real pairs. In 2010, Dumas and colleagues recorded the EEG of nine pairs of people who imitated each other's hand movements. Each head wore thirty-two electrodes. They computed the phase locking value between the electrodes of one head and those of the other.

When the partners moved in synchrony, the authors found an inter-brain network. It linked the right centro-parietal regions of the two partners, in the alpha mu band.

Now be careful. What is a link between two brains? There is no fibre between two skulls. No cable, no nerve. The link is a number computed from two signals, and it says that the two signals resemble each other.

Anything that makes two signals resemble each other makes a link. A shared beat does it. Two people who hear the same beat, and never speak, will show it.

Our simulated pair hears one beat every second, like everyone in this simulation. In four regions of each brain, the beat sets off a ten hertz burst. Look at the table between the brains. Those sixteen cells are dark, with a mean of zero point three seven. The other cells are near zero point one, except one that we keep for later. A network between the brains.

But did the two people make it? To find out, borrow the reference networks of chapter ten, with a twist. Take person A, and pair that person with a person B from another pair. They never met, but they heard the same beat. This is a pseudo-pair.

We have twenty simulated pairs, so we can make three hundred eighty pseudo-pairs: twenty times nineteen.

The pseudo-pairs show the same dark cells. In them, the sixteen cells have a mean of zero point three nine. The beat made those cells, not the people.

First, test the whole block. Our pair averages zero point two three over its thirty-six cells. Sixty-one percent of the pseudo-pairs fall below that. It sits in the middle of the cloud, so as a whole the block says nothing about this pair.

Now go cell by cell, and give each cell its own reference: the same cell in the three hundred eighty pseudo-pairs. A cell is flagged when it beats ninety-nine of every hundred pseudo-pairs. With thirty-six cells, luck would flag about a third of one.

One cell is flagged, between region three of A and region six of B. Its value is zero point five zero. The highest pseudo-pair value for that cell is zero point three zero. None reaches it.

This link is not a cable either. But it is something this pair has and the pseudo-pairs do not. Here we built it on purpose: those two regions share a rhythm that belongs to this pair alone. So we know the answer.

How many pseudo-pairs to draw, how to build them for each design, and what they do not control: the Hyperscanning series gives that test its own episode, seven point five.

To sum up. Two brains make two layers, and the links between them join different regions. The table has four blocks. A link between brains is a resemblance between signals, never a cable. A beat that both people hear can create one, and pseudo-pairs show which links belong to the pair.

Next, a question about time. A speaks to B at two o'clock. B speaks to C at one. Can the news from A reach C?

PLATE 15.35:12

Networks in time

A graph that merges all moments can claim a path where the clock forbids it. Time-respecting paths follow contacts in order, and windows cut a recording into a sequence of graphs. On 59,835 private messages, 99.4 percent of user pairs connect in the merged graph, but only 77 percent can be reached with the clock. Reaching is not symmetric.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question about time. A speaks to B at two o'clock. B speaks to C at one. Can the news from A reach C?

Draw it as a graph, the way we always have. A is linked to B. B is linked to C. So there is a path from A to C, and the answer looks like yes.

Now look at the clock. At two o'clock, A tells B the news. But B spoke to C at one o'clock, an hour earlier. That conversation is over. B has no later talk with C, so the news stops at B. C never hears it.

The graph hid this. It squashed all the moments into one. A link that exists at one moment looked like a link at every moment.

A path that respects the clock is called a time-respecting path. It follows contacts, and each contact comes strictly after the one before. We say strictly: two contacts in the same second cannot be chained. Holme and Saramäki reviewed temporal networks in twenty twelve, and time-respecting paths are in that review.

Change the story a little. Suppose B spoke to C at three o'clock instead. Then the news goes from A to B at two, and from B to C at three. That path respects the clock, and C hears the news.

There is a second way to put time into a network. Cut a recording into windows, and build one graph in each window. You get a sequence of networks, like the frames of a film. Studies of dynamic functional connectivity in fMRI often use windows, and Hutchison and colleagues reviewed the field in twenty thirteen.

Here is a simulated recording: three regions, three hundred time points, seed one five three. Over the whole recording, no pair passes our cut-off of zero point five. The strongest correlations are zero point four six, between regions one and two, and zero point four two, between regions two and three.

Now use windows of sixty points, sliding by thirty. In the first four windows, regions one and two are linked. In the last five, regions two and three are. The link moved, and the whole-recording average hid it. Window length and cut-off are both our choices.

Next, a real recording. It comes from an online community at the University of California, Irvine, studied in two thousand nine: one thousand eight hundred ninety-nine users, and fifty-nine thousand eight hundred thirty-five private messages over about one hundred ninety-four days.

Treat each message as a contact between two people at one moment, and ignore who sent it. A link here is a pair of users who exchanged at least one message.

Cut those days into twenty-eight weekly windows. Each week holds six hundred seventy-six links on average. The busiest week has eleven thousand two hundred ninety-four messages, and the quietest has fifty-seven. Merge all the weeks, and you get thirteen thousand eight hundred thirty-eight links. Over three in four of those links appear in a single week.

Now respect the clock on that real data. Start a piece of news at one user, and let it travel only along messages that come later. Do this for every user in turn.

Count ordered pairs of users: first one, then the other. In the merged graph, ninety-nine point four percent of them are connected. With the clock, only seventy-seven percent can be reached. Part of that gap is the edge of the recording: a message sent on the last day has no time left to travel.

Look at the curve. For the median user, the news has reached thirty-one percent of the others after thirty days, seventy-eight percent after a hundred days, and eighty-six percent at the end. The merged graph promised ninety-nine point seven. It depends on who starts: the sender of the very first message reaches ninety-eight percent.

Now go back to A and C. A cannot reach C, but C can reach A. C speaks to B at one o'clock, and then B speaks to A. There is no arrow anywhere here. Contacts are symmetric. Time alone gave this link a direction.

In a graph without time, a path from A to C is also a path from C to A. With time, it is not.

Reaching is not even passed along. A reaches B, and B reaches C, yet A does not reach C.

In the real recording, the same thing happens at scale. Take every pair of users where at least one can reach the other: one point eight million pairs. For forty-three percent of them, the news flows one way only. Part of this is the edge of the recording, but A and C show that it needs no edge at all.

This episode has a neighbour in the Information series, chapter eleven, Time-resolved information networks. It treats information networks that change over time.

To sum up. A contact has a time. Time-respecting paths follow the clock. Windows cut a recording into a sequence of graphs. And reaching is not symmetric: C can reach A while A cannot reach C.

PLATE 15.44:54

Communities across layers and time

To follow communities across time windows, matching groups afterwards leaves noise and ambiguity. Multislice modularity builds one network where each region links to its own copy in the next window, with a weight omega setting how freely groups change. Flexibility counts a region's changes. In a simulated recording, matching leaves 38.8 changes where the truth is nine, and omega of 0.5 leaves six.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. In each window we can find the communities of chapter eight. But how do we know that a group in one window is the same group in the next?

Picture a dance with twelve dancers and ten rounds. In each round, the dancers form circles. After every round, a caller writes down who stood with whom. Now the caller wants to follow the circles from one round to the next.

Here is the same problem in a simulated recording: twelve regions, ten windows, seed one five four. Each window is a weighted network. Three modules of four regions are built in. Regions four, eight and twelve are made to change module, nine changes in all, out of one hundred eight possible steps. These are our choices, not measurements.

Find the communities of chapter eight in each window on its own. Each run names its groups by size: the biggest is group zero, then one, then two. A name carries no memory. The same circle of dancers can be group zero in one window and group one in the next. Over a hundred runs, an average of sixty-eight of the one hundred eight steps change name. Most of those changes are only names.

Matching afterwards is the obvious fix. Pair each group with the group of the previous window that it overlaps most. This helps, but two problems stay. When a group splits into two equal halves, the halves tie, and nothing says which one inherits the name. And each window is noisy and is settled on its own, so small wobbles look like moves. After matching, an average of thirty-eight point eight changes remain. The truth is nine. Twenty-seven point five of them happen in the nine regions that never moved.

Mucha and colleagues proposed a different route in twenty ten. Do not detect first and match later. Build one network from all the windows at once. Each region appears once per window. Inside a window, the links are that window's links. And each region is also linked to its own copy in the next window, with a weight called omega.

Then look for communities in that single network, with the modularity of chapter eight. Each copy that stays in its group earns a bonus of omega. Now a group in one window and a group in the next share a name, because they come out of one optimization.

Omega is a dial. At zero, nothing links the windows, and we are back to independent windows. As omega grows, staying put pays more. Set it very high, and nobody can move.

Here omega is point five, on the same scale as the link weights. Averaged over a hundred runs, six changes remain. Five point four of them are in regions four, eight and twelve. The other nine regions carry only point six. At omega five, nobody moves. Even the three regions that really did move are folded into a neighbouring group.

Across the dial: thirty-seven point six changes at omega point oh five, nineteen point six at point two, six at point five, point eight at one, and none from two upward.

Slices do not have to be windows. They can be the layers of fifteen point one: friends, work, family. The same trick links each person to their copy in the next layer, and the groups it finds run across layers.

Now the measure. The flexibility of a region is the number of times it changes group, divided by the number of steps. At omega point five, region eight scores zero point two seven, region four zero point one seven, and region twelve zero point one six. The nine others stay at zero point oh three or less. The three flexible regions come out on top, below their built-in values, because the coupling smooths short visits.

This number cannot be defined on a simple graph, because a simple graph has no next window.

Bassett and colleagues used it in twenty eleven. Eighteen people learned a motor sequence in the scanner, over three sessions. Each session was cut into twenty-five windows. Flexibility in the first session predicted learning in the second. In the second session, flexibility predicted learning in the third. Within the same session, it did not.

One caution, the same as in chapter eight. These numbers depend on the settings. Another omega gives another flexibility. So report a range of omega, and the resolution too, and average over many runs, because the optimization is stochastic.

To sum up. Matching groups afterwards leaves noise and ambiguity. Multislice modularity links each region to its own copy, and finds all the groups at once. Omega sets how freely groups may change. And flexibility counts the changes.

Next, a question about size. So far, every link joins two things. What if one interaction joins three?

PLATE 15.55:27

More than pairs

A triangle drawn as a plain graph cannot tell one article by three authors from three articles by two. A hypergraph stores groups of any size. In the Enron emails, 3,317 of 9,895 triangles are open, one in three. A simulated contagion also behaves differently when groups act as groups, sustaining itself from a half-sick start.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Three people wrote one article together. How do we draw it?

The usual way is one dot per author, and one line between every two. Three dots, three lines: a triangle.

Now take three other articles. Ana and Ben wrote the first. Ben and Cleo wrote the second. Ana and Cleo wrote the third. Each article has two authors.

Draw it the same way, and you get the same triangle. The picture cannot tell one article by three from three articles by two. Yet these are not the same story.

How many stories hide behind that triangle? Allow the three pairs and the one trio, and try every combination. Nine different collections of articles leave exactly this picture.

A hypergraph fixes this. Its links are groups, and a group may hold any number of people: two, three, or eighteen. The French mathematician Claude Berge gave these objects their textbook in nineteen seventy, in his book on graphs and hypergraphs. In twenty twenty, Battiston and colleagues wrote a review of the whole field, in Physics Reports. We follow it here.

We will draw a group as a cookie cutter. It stamps one shape around all its members at once. A pair is a small cutter. An article by three is a larger one. The dots are the same, but now the picture says which groups exist.

You already know a way to store this. In episode one point four, we put people on one side and events on the other: a bipartite graph. A hypergraph is the same object. Each group becomes a node of its own, linked to its members. Nothing is lost.

Now look at the triangle again. If the three people appear together in one group, we fill it. It says: these three interact together. If we only see the three pairs, we leave it empty. It says: these three interact two by two. Benson and colleagues called them closed and open triangles, in twenty eighteen.

Let us count them in real data. This is the Enron email collection, cut down by Benson and colleagues to a core of one hundred and forty-three addresses. Each message is a group: the sender, and all the recipients. There are ten thousand eight hundred and eighty-three messages.

Draw them as a plain graph, with one line for every two people who ever shared a message. You get one thousand eight hundred lines, and nine thousand eight hundred and ninety-five triangles.

Six thousand five hundred and seventy-eight of them are closed: the three people were together in one message. Three thousand three hundred and seventeen are open. Each pair shared some message, but the three were never together.

That is thirty-three point five percent: one triangle in three. And the plain graph draws them exactly alike.

So a simple graph cannot tell three conversations of two from one conversation of three. Does it matter? For dynamics, it can.

Episode twelve point four gave a clue. A habit needed two stamps, and clustering put two stamps on one card. Now the group itself carries the rule.

Here is a model by Iacopini and colleagues, from twenty nineteen. People catch something, recover, and can catch it again. Every sick friend passes it on with a small chance per step: that is the pair rule. And there is a second rule, for filled triangles. If two members of a triangle are sick, they pass it to the third with an extra chance: that is the group rule.

We simulated it, so everything you see next is simulated. Two thousand people, about twenty links and six triangles each, with seed one fifty-five. Each sick person recovers with a chance of five percent per step. That recovery rate is our choice.

First, the pair rule alone. We set the strength at zero point seven five, a little below the point where pairs alone can keep the contagion alive. Begin with one percent sick, and it dies out. Begin with half the people sick, and it dies out too.

Now switch on the group rule. The links do not change. Start with one percent sick, and it dies out. Start with half sick, and it settles near forty-two percent.

Slowly raise the strength, and the picture is the same. The pair rule alone needs a strength of about one. With the group rule, a half-sick start sustains itself from zero point seven, and a one percent start from zero point eight five. Two stable outcomes exist at once, and which one you reach depends on how many are sick at the start. A critical mass.

Iacopini and colleagues found the same shape, and suggested it may help explain why social change so often needs a critical mass. Ours is one random network and one choice of settings. It is a toy, not a measurement of any real crowd.

Filled and empty triangles differ in one more way. An empty triangle leaves a hole. Counting such holes in data is the topology of data. It has its own series, Geometry of Data, and we leave it there.

To sum up. A graph draws pairs. A hypergraph draws groups. A filled triangle says three together, an empty one says three in pairs, and in the Enron emails one triangle in three is empty. And a contagion can behave differently when groups act as groups.
