PLATE 11.17:13

What counts as one observation?

Fifty neurons in each of ten animals do not make n equal to five hundred. The animal was randomised, so n is ten. Counting neurons made a drug that does nothing look significant, and the naive test rang 54.6 percent of the time. Averaging each animal's neurons restores the promised 4.8 percent. Always count the units that were randomised or sampled.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the apartment building. Chapter eleven is about data that live on floors: neurons inside animals, pupils inside classrooms, trials inside people. Today's question looks too small to matter, and it decides everything. A lab records fifty neurons in each of ten animals. Five animals get a drug, and five get saline. How big is n? Five hundred, or ten? Guess before you see.

Here is such a study, simulated. Each row is an animal, and each dot is one neuron's firing rate, in spikes per second. Saline animals are blue, drug animals are pink. Now the secret: in this simulation, the drug does nothing at all. But animals differ. One has a lively brain area, another a quiet one, and the neurons of one animal resemble each other. So each row is a cloud at its own height.

The software sees a table of 500 rows, and it reports n equal to 500. It compares 250 neurons against 250. The saline group averages 33.33, the drug group 29.91. The t-test says t is 4.18, with 498 degrees of freedom, and p is 0.00003. Significant, in a study where nothing happens. I picked this study because it rang.

Why does it ring? Build the curtain of chance, as in 6.2. Repeat the study 10,000 times in this same world, where the drug does nothing and animals differ, and record the gap between the groups each time. The pile is the true curtain, and it is wide: its spread is 2.90. The t-test drew another curtain, the blue curve, which assumes 500 independent neurons. Its spread is only 0.88. Our flag, minus 3.43, is far out on the blue curve, but well inside the pile. The honest p is 0.237, not 0.00003. The test thought it had five hundred opinions. It had ten.

Now count. The rule promises that, when nothing is going on, about five studies in a hundred ring. Guess before you see: will the naive test ring ten percent, twenty-five, or fifty? It rings 54.6 percent of the time. In 6.2, the p-values spread evenly. Here they pile up near zero, and more than half fall below 0.05.

Where did the count go wrong? Ask a question: what was randomised? Nobody flipped a coin for each neuron. The coin was flipped for each animal, and its neurons came along inside. A neuron cannot be given the drug alone. So the units are animals. The rule is this: the unit of inference is the unit that was randomised, or sampled, when nothing is randomised and we chose these animals to speak about animals.

So count honestly. Average the fifty neurons of each animal, and keep one number per animal: ten numbers, five against five. Each cloud collapses into its average. The t-test now has 8 degrees of freedom, t is 1.68, and p is 0.131: no signal, which is the truth. On the 10,000 null studies, this rule rings 4.8 percent of the time. The promise of five percent holds again.

Two knobs control the damage. The first is the number of neurons per animal. Guess before you see: if we record more neurons, does the false-alarm rate fall, stay, or rise? With one neuron per animal, the test is honest: 4.9 percent. Add neurons, and the rate climbs: 15.0 percent with five per animal, 24.9 with ten, 54.6 with fifty, 67.6 with a hundred. More neurons made the test surer of itself, and no better informed about animals.

The second knob is how much of the variation sits between animals. If animals did not differ at all, neurons would really be independent, and the rate stays near five percent. With only five percent of the variation between animals, fifty neurons per animal already give 29.5 percent. With half of it between animals, 70.9 percent. The flat line at the bottom is the analysis with one number per animal: about five percent, everywhere.

The trap is not about neurons. It appears wherever things sit inside other things. For each study, ask the same question: what was randomised or sampled? Here are six, the first one ours, simulated with an assumed similarity inside each unit.

Two, pupils. A teaching method is given to whole classrooms: four against four, with 25 pupils each. The method went to classrooms, so n is 8, not 200. Naive: 36.7 percent. One number per class: 4.9. Three, trials. Sixteen volunteers are split into two groups, and each does forty reaction-time trials. The people were randomised. So n is 16, not 640. Naive: 64.2 percent. Per person: 5.0. Four, leaves. A fertiliser is sprayed on whole plots: three against three, with thirty leaves measured in each. The spray went to plots. So n is 6, not 180. Naive: 54.9 percent. Per plot: 5.1. Five, blood. Twenty patients, half on a new drug, six blood samples each. The patient took the drug, not the vial. So n is 20, not 120. Naive: 34.1 percent. Per patient: 5.5. Six, cells. Three culture dishes get a compound, three do not, and a microscope counts two hundred cells in each. The compound went into the dish. So n is 6, not 1,200. Naive: 66.9 percent. Per dish: 5.1.

Sometimes the small thing is the unit. If the question is about one animal, which of its neurons respond to a sound, then neurons are the units, and n is fifty. But the answer holds for that animal alone. The question sets the level.

Back in the building, this mistake has a name. In 1984, the ecologist Stuart Hurlbert called it pseudoreplication, and found it in 48 percent of the field experiments that used statistical tests. In neuroscience, it has been counted: in 2010, Stanley Lazic read one issue of Nature Neuroscience: among 17 papers with statistics, 2 clearly had it, and 5 more were suspected. In 2014, Aarts and colleagues examined 314 articles in five journals: 53 percent had nested data, and ignoring it can push false alarms up to 80 percent.

Remember: n is the number of independent units at the level where the question lives. Ask what was randomised or sampled, and count that. Next time: the levels of a study, and what varies on each floor.

PLATE 11.26:56

The levels of a study

Studies have floors, like an apartment building: trials sit inside people. Quadrupling the trials only polishes each person's average, so the spread of the group mean falls from 10.1 to 9.2 and hits a floor. Quadrupling the people halves it to 5.0. A question about people lives on the people floor, so only more people help.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the apartment building. Last time we counted the units: ten animals, not five hundred neurons. Today we climb the floors. Every floor has its own spread, and the spread tells you what more data can fix.

Here is a reaction-time study, simulated. Twenty people each do sixteen trials, and we average all 320 trials into one number for the group. Each person sits on a tray, and each dot on the tray is one trial, in milliseconds. Two things vary.

First, people differ. Some are quick and some are slow, so the trays sit at different places. In this simulation, that spread between people is 40 milliseconds. Second, one person is not the same on every trial. The dots scatter around their own average. That spread within a person is 80 milliseconds.

Two floors, and a spread on each. Squared, these are the variances: 1,600 between people, and 6,400 within a person. Only one fifth of the variation sits between people. The question we ask is about people in general, so it lives on the people floor. Keep that in mind. This study's group average is 412.2 milliseconds.

That number is one study. Repeat the study 10,000 times, and the group averages make a pile. It is centred on 420, and its spread is 10.1 milliseconds. That spread is the uncertainty of our group average.

Now a decision. We can afford four times more data. Guess before you see. Option one: every person does four times more trials, 64 instead of 16. Option two: we recruit four times more people, 80 instead of 20. Either way, that is 1,280 trials. Which shrinks the spread more: the trials, the people, or both about the same?

Four times more trials: the spread goes from 10.1 to 9.2. A fall of 9 percent. Four times more people: from 10.1 to 5.0. Half. Same number of trials, very different result.

Turn the knob slowly. With 20 people, add trials. The spread falls fast at first, then it flattens. It stops near 9, and it never goes below a floor: 8.9 milliseconds. Now add people instead. There is no floor. The spread keeps falling: 7.1 with 40 people, 5.0 with 80, 2.5 with 320. Each dot is 10,000 simulated studies.

Now the formula, after the simulation. The variance of the group average is the variance between people, divided by the number of people, plus the variance within a person, divided by people times trials. For our study: 1,600 over 20 is 80, and 6,400 over 320 is 20. The total is 100, so the spread is 10. The simulation gave 10.1, the formula gives 10.0, and the other dots agree as well. Look at where the trials sit: only in the second term. Add as many as you like, and the first term stays.

Here is a misreading to avoid: "we have 1,280 trials, so n is 1,280." No. Those trials only polish the average of each person we already have. How far we can speak about other people depends on how many people we have.

Statisticians call this inflation the design effect, a name from Leslie Kish, in 1965. It says how many times larger the variance is than if every trial were independent. Here it is 4. Trials of one person resemble each other, so our 320 trials are worth only 80 independent ones.

Suppose we have 320 trials in all. How should we split them? Guess first: many people with few trials each, or few people with many trials each? Five people with 64 trials each: a spread of 18.2. Twenty people with sixteen: 10.1. Eighty people with four: 6.4. Three hundred twenty people with one trial each: 5.0. More people is better. But a new person costs more than a new trial, so the split is a question of budget.

There are two honest ways to analyse such a study. Route one: summarise each person by one number, their average, and test those numbers, as in 11.1. Route two: model every floor at once, with a mixed model, which we build in 11.4. Route one is simple, and as 11.1 showed, it keeps the test honest. But it forgets something. It forgets how many trials each person did, and how much one trial varies.

See what that costs. Imagine a phone game where some people play once and others play hundreds of times. Twenty players: the lightest has one trial, the heaviest has 300. A person with one trial has a very noisy average, and one number per person gives it the same say as the person with 300. Weigh each person by what their number is worth, and the spread falls from 13.4 to 11.5. Same data, same people. To match it, the plain average would need about a third more people. Here the two spreads were given. In practice a model estimates them from the data.

The same floors are everywhere. Count them, then ask where the question lives.

One: trials in people in labs. A question about people lives on the people floor. A question about whether labs differ lives on the lab floor, and needs many labs. Three labs with a thousand trials each are still three labs.

Two: pupils in classes in schools. A reading programme is given to whole schools. The question lives on the school floor. Hundreds of pupils, but only a handful of schools. Counting pupils would be counting rows, as in 11.1.

Three: measurements in leaves in trees. A question about a tree species lives on the tree floor. More measurements per leaf polish the leaf, not the forest.

Four: blood pressure readings in patients in clinics. A drug given patient by patient is a question for the patient floor. A new appointment system for a whole clinic is a question for the clinic floor. And some patients have two readings, while others, with a home monitor, have hundreds.

Five: sessions in mice in litters. Pups of one litter resemble each other. If whole litters get the treatment, the litter is the unit. If every litter has treated and untreated pups, the comparison lives on the mouse floor, inside the litters.

Remember: more trials shrink the noise inside each person, and only that. To speak about other people, you need more people. Next time: repeated measures, when each person does every condition.

PLATE 11.37:21

Repeated measures

When every person does every dose of caffeine, treating the sixty numbers as different people buries the dose under differences between people: p is 0.67. Removing the people piece, as the paired test does, gives p of 0.019. Two weak points remain: sphericity, repaired by the Greenhouse and Geisser correction, and empty cells that drop whole patients.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the apartment building. Last time we learned that more trials only polish each person, and that to speak about other people we need more people. Today, every person does every condition. That changes the analysis, and it brings two weak points.

Here is a study, simulated. Twenty people drink coffee with three amounts of caffeine, none, one cup and two cups, on three different days. We measure each person's reaction time, in milliseconds. Each person is a thin line through three results. Quick people are quick on every day, and slow people are slow on every day. People differ by about 40 milliseconds. The dose changes things by only a few. The three averages are 437.6, 431.0 and 424.0.

First analysis, a mistake. Pretend these are sixty different people, twenty in each group, and run the one-way analysis of variance from 9.5. All the variation goes into one pile, and the dose is lost inside it. The F ratio is 0.41, and p is 0.67. Nothing to see.

Second analysis, the same sixty numbers. Now we remember that they come in rows, one row per person. Cut the total variation into three pieces: between people, between doses, and what is left. The people piece is 92.5 percent of everything. The doses are 1.4 percent. What is left, 6.1 percent, is the noise that remains when each person is compared with themselves. Throw the people piece away, and divide the dose piece by what is left, each per degree of freedom. The F ratio is 4.38, and p is 0.019. Same numbers, opposite conclusion.

A misreading to avoid: "you removed the people, so you cheated." No. The people piece was never part of the comparison, because every person did every dose. Removing it only stops it from drowning the comparison. The price is paid in degrees of freedom: 38, which is 19 people times 2 comparisons, instead of 57.

Guess before you see. Repeat the study 10,000 times. In how many studies will each analysis find the dose: a few, half, or most? Treating the numbers as different people finds it in 0.6 percent of the studies. Repeated measures finds it in 83.8 percent. The first analysis divides by differences between people, but those differences were never in the comparison, because every person is in every group.

Now the link with 7.3. Keep only two doses: none, and two cups. The paired t from 7.3 is 3.104. Run repeated measures on the same two columns, and F is 9.63. Square the t, before rounding, and you also get 9.63. It is the same test. Repeated measures is the paired test, with more than two conditions. With only two conditions there is a single set of differences, so sphericity cannot fail.

Now the first weak point. With three conditions there are three pairs of columns: none against one cup, one cup against two cups, none against two cups. For each person, take the three differences. The analysis assumes that the three sets of differences wobble equally. This is called sphericity. Here are the spreads of the three sets: 22.2, 19.6 and 19.5 milliseconds. The exact value is 21.2. As equal as twenty people allow.

When does it break? Take four sessions of a training programme, with no real change, simulated. A person who is quick in session one is probably quick in session two, less so in session four. Neighbouring sessions are more alike, so the differences wobble unequally: 29.3 between sessions one and two, and 49.5 between sessions one and four. The exact values grow with the gap: 25.3, 33.9 and 39.5.

What does that cost? Simulate 10,000 studies where nothing happens, twenty people, four sessions. If neighbours are unrelated, the plain test gives a false alarm in 4.6 percent of studies. As neighbours grow more alike, false alarms rise: 5.6 percent, 6.8, then 7.0.

The repair is the Greenhouse and Geisser correction, from 1959. It measures the unevenness in one number, epsilon. Epsilon is 1 when the differences are perfectly even, and falls toward one third with four sessions. Multiply both degrees of freedom by epsilon, and the test turns cautious: 3.8, 4.3, 4.6 and 4.6 percent. In our caffeine study, epsilon is 0.97, and p moves from 0.019 to 0.021. Mauchly's test, from 1940, checks the assumption directly. With neighbours this alike, it flags the problem in 67.4 percent of the studies.

Now the second weak point: empty cells. Pain scores of twenty patients at three visits after surgery, simulated. The scores run from 0 to 10, and the true averages fall from 6.0 to 5.4 to 4.8. Ten percent of the scores are missing at random, because a patient skipped a visit. Here, six cells are empty. Repeated measures needs a full row, so those six patients are dropped entirely, and twelve scores we did measure go in the bin.

Guess the average. Each patient has three chances to lose a score, so the chance of keeping a full row is 0.9 times 0.9 times 0.9, which is 73 percent. About a quarter of the patients go. Over 10,000 simulated studies, 5.4 patients out of 20 are dropped on average, and 10.2 measured scores are thrown away. Power falls from 78.3 percent to 62.3. The interval for the first visit minus the last visit is 21 percent wider. Notice what hurts. The test itself did nothing wrong. We fed it fewer people, and it paid for each one we lost. A method that keeps the other scores of those patients exists, and it is where we go next.

The same shape is everywhere. Memory recall under three noise levels, the same students in each. Heart rate lying, sitting and standing, the same volunteers. Plant height over three weeks, the same plants, where neighbouring weeks are alike, so watch sphericity. Three different classrooms is not repeated measures, because nobody is measured twice. Two other repairs exist, a multivariate analysis called MANOVA, and robust standard errors for clustered data. We only name them here.

Remember: compare each person with themselves, but check that the differences wobble equally, and count the empty cells. These two fragile points are the reasons to move on. Next time: mixed models, with random intercepts and random slopes.

PLATE 11.47:08

Mixed models: random intercepts, random slopes

A mixed model gives each person their own line, tied to a shared average. Like kites on elastic strings, the people with the fewest trials are pulled most toward the average. A person with two trials moves from a slope of 23.6 to 13.2. This bias-for-variance trade cuts typical error, and false alarms drop from 9.7 to 5.2 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the apartment building. Last time we met two weak points: differences that must wobble equally, and an empty cell that throws out a whole person. Today, the model that repairs both. It is called a mixed model, and its idea is simple: one line per person.

Here is a study, simulated. Eighteen people do a reaction-time task over ten days of restricted sleep. Not everyone comes every day. Some have ten trials, and two people have only two. In all, 111 trials. Each person starts at their own level, and slows at their own rate. We set the starting levels to average 250 milliseconds, spread by 25. And the slowing to average 10 milliseconds per day, spread by 6.

First drawing: one line for everyone. This is complete pooling, the plain regression of 9.1. It ignores who is who. The slope is 9.5 milliseconds per day.

Second drawing: one line per person, sharing nothing. This is no pooling. Each line uses only its own person's trials. Look at the people with two trials. A line through two points always fits perfectly, and it is mostly noise. The person seen on days 3 and 8 gets a slope of 23.6. Their true slope, known only because this is a simulation, is 12.9.

Third drawing: the mixed model. It says the people are not unrelated. The intercepts and slopes of the 18 lines are drawn from a population, with an average and a spread. Laird and Ware wrote the standard version in 1982. The average line is the fixed effect. How each person departs from it is the random effect. A misreading to avoid: random does not mean unpredictable. It means we treat these 18 people as a sample of a larger population.

Fitted, the fixed slope is 8.8 milliseconds per day. The spread of slopes is 4.7. The spread of starting levels is 25.6. Every person has a line again, but the lines are tamed. The person with two trials goes from 23.6 to 13.2.

Picture each slope as a kite. No pooling cuts the string, and the kites go wherever the noise blows them. Complete pooling ties every kite to the post, the average slope. Partial pooling uses an elastic string: the fewer the trials, the stronger the pull. Here are the 18 people, sorted by trials. The person with two trials moves a long way. The people with ten trials barely move.

Two numbers may look contradictory. The 18 separate slopes spread by 6.5, and the model says 4.7. The separate slopes include noise, and the model tries to take it out. In this sample the 18 people really spread by 4.9, a little under the 6 we set.

Is the pull worth it? Guess before you see. Repeat the study 10,000 times, with new people each time, and measure how far each person's slope falls from the truth. The typical miss, in milliseconds per day: no pooling 4.4, complete pooling 6.0, partial pooling 3.2. Partial pooling beats no pooling in 91 percent of the studies.

It depends on the trials. For people with two or three trials: 6.5 without pooling, 4.1 with it. For four to six: 4.3 against 3.3. For seven to ten: 3.0 against 2.6. The pull is 61 percent of the way for the first group, 45 for the second, and 30 for the third. Few trials, big gain. Many trials, the methods agree.

Shrinkage is not a flaw. It is the trade of 5.1. Take one person with two trials, whose true slope is 19, above average. Repeat 10,000 times. No pooling is right on average, 19.0, but it shakes by 7.0. Partial pooling averages 15.9, a bias of 3.1 too low, but it shakes by only 2.9. The typical miss falls from 7.0 to 4.3. A little bias buys a lot of variance. For a person at the average slope, there is no bias at all, and the miss falls from 7.0 to 2.5.

Now two kinds of random effect. Random intercepts only: people start at different levels, but every line has the same slope, so the lines are parallel. Random intercepts and random slopes: the lines fan out. On our data, parallel lines cannot see that people slow at different rates, so they blame the starting levels, 36.6 instead of 25.6, and the noise, 28.4 instead of 24.7. What this costs, and how to choose, is next time.

Now the average effect, with its interval. Ignoring people, the naive regression gives 9.5, with an interval from 6.4 to 12.6. The mixed model gives 8.8, from 5.8 to 11.8. Two different averages? They weight people differently: the plain line lets people with many trials count more. In this one study the intervals look alike. The difference shows over many studies.

Guess before you see. Simulate 10,000 studies where the true average slope is zero. How often does each method raise a false alarm? The naive regression: 9.7 percent. The mixed model: 5.2. The naive standard error is 1.59, but its estimates really spread by 1.87: the error bar is 15 percent too short. For the mixed model it is 1.67 against 1.70.

The same structure is everywhere. Children's reading scores by school year, one line per school: eight points more per year on average, and schools differ by 1.8. Plant height by week across greenhouses: 2.7 centimetres per week. Patients' blood pressure across visits: minus 2.7 per visit, patients differing by 1.6. Students' marks across tests, one line per class. Neurons firing across trials within animals: the rate falls by 0.6 spikes per second per trial. Each is simulated, and illustrative. In each one, the fixed effect is the average line, and the random effect is how each unit departs from it.

Here is what makes it strong. One fit gives the average effect and how much people differ, together: 8.8 milliseconds per day on average, and 4.7 between people. Choosing the random effects honestly, crossed effects, and fits that fail to converge wait for 11.5. The same structure with priors is for 16.6.

Remember: a mixed model gives every person a line, ties the lines to a shared average, and pulls the least-measured people the most. That pull is the bias and variance trade at work. Next time: specifying a mixed model honestly.

PLATE 11.57:05

Specifying a mixed model honestly

Which random effects to keep is a statement about what you sampled. If the effect varies between people and the model has only random intercepts, 1,200 trials count as 1,200 opinions: false alarms reach 28.2 percent. Words behave like people, so cross them too. Asking for too much makes the fit land on a boundary.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the apartment building. Last time, every person got a line. Today, the question we left open: which random effects does the model keep? The answer is not a setting in the software. It is a statement about what you sampled.

Here is a study, simulated. 20 people do a reaction-time task in two conditions, with 30 trials in each. On average, the condition changes nothing. But it changes each person differently: it slows one person down, and speeds another up. The effect varies between people. In the words of 11.4, each person has their own slope.

Fit two models to this design. The first lets people differ in their starting level only: random intercepts. The second also lets the effect differ from person to person: random intercepts and random slopes.

Now repeat the study 10,000 times, always with a true average effect of zero, and count the false alarms. A false alarm is a p-value under 0.05.

Guess before you see. When the effect varies by 40 milliseconds between people, how often does the simple model cry wolf?

If nothing varies, the simple model is fine: 5.2 percent. The full model is a little cautious: 2.9. With a spread of 10 milliseconds, the simple model raises 7.0 percent. At 20, 11.6. At 40, 28.2 percent, more than five times what it promised. The full model stays at 3.6, 4.5 and 4.9.

Why? Look at one study, with a spread of 40. These are the 20 people's own effects, one dot each. They spread by 42, so their average has an error bar of 9.5. The simple model claims only 5.9.

Over all 10,000 studies, the simple model claims 5.9 on average, but its estimates really spread by 10.7. The full model claims 10.5. The ratio of the two claims is 1.79. The 9.5 belongs to this one study, and the 10.5 is the average over 10,000.

The simple model counts 1,200 trials as 1,200 opinions about the effect. They come from 20 people. It is the pseudo-replication of 11.1, hiding inside a mixed model.

This is the warning of Barr, Levy, Scheepers and Tily, in 2013. They simulated it, and advised keeping the random effects the design calls for: keep it maximal.

But asking for too much has a price. New simulation: a two-by-two design, three trials per cell, and 8 people. The maximal model gives each of four terms its own spread, plus every correlation between them: ten numbers to estimate. In truth, only two of the four terms exist.

The fit lands on a boundary in 99.5 percent of the studies: some combination of the terms is given no spread at all. With 16 people, 98.5 percent. With 32, 96.7.

What does the software do? I checked one widely used Python library, on 100 of the eight-person studies. It printed a warning in 97 of them, and reported that it had not converged in 45. It still returns numbers, so the warning is easy to ignore.

And the power? The true effect is 40 milliseconds. With 8 people, the maximal model finds it in 23.6 percent of the studies, and the model that matches the truth in 24.6. With 16 people, 59.2 against 60.5. With 32, 91.4 against 91.7. The loss is about one point.

Bates, Kliegl, Vasishth and Baayen, in 2015, argue that a failure to converge usually means the model is too complex for the data. Matuschek and colleagues, in 2017, argue that the maximal model can lose power. In our design the loss is small. And we only knew which two terms were real because we wrote the simulation. In a real study, you do not.

Now the other thing you can sample. 30 people each read 20 words, 10 frequent and 10 rare. Simulated, with no average effect of frequency. But words differ: some are read fast, whatever their frequency. People and words are crossed: every person reads every word. Clark named the mistake in 1973: the language-as-fixed-effect fallacy. It treats the words as if they were the whole language, when they are a sample, like the people.

Give the model a random intercept for people only, and watch the false alarms. When words differ by 20 milliseconds, 18.7 percent. At 40, 38.2. At 60, 50.9. Cross people and words, and the rate returns to 5.1, 4.5 and 5.2. At a spread of 40, the people-only model claims an error bar of 8.8, and the real spread is 19.5. It counts 600 readings. For the effect of frequency, what really repeats is 20 words.

The same question comes back in other studies. These are illustrative. Pupils in classes in schools: each level gets its own random intercept. Patients treated by doctors in hospitals: three levels again. Trials within neurons within animals, the building of 11.1. A tasting panel rating several wines: judges and wines are crossed, like people and words. And dyads in hyperscanning: the pair is the unit, which the hyperscanning series takes up in full.

A word about priors. With only 5 groups, the spread between groups is hard to estimate. Simulated: 5 groups of 8 observations, with a true spread of 15. The usual fit says exactly zero in 24.2 percent of the studies. A Bayesian model, whose prior keeps the spread away from zero, never fell below 4.5, and its typical miss is 8.1 against 10.0. But it overshoots more often: above double the truth in 6.9 percent of the studies, against 3.7. The same structure with priors is for 16.6.

Here is the strong moment. The random effects are a declaration. For everything you sampled, people, words, classes, ask: do I want to speak about more than these? Then it gets a random intercept. For every effect you test, ask: could it differ between those units? Then it gets a random slope. It is a statement about the design, not an option of the software.

Remember: the random effects say what you sampled. Leave out a slope the design calls for, and the error bar is too short. Ask for more than the data can hold, and the fit lands on a boundary. Next time: deleting rows.
