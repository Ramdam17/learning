The logic of a null hypothesis

A null hypothesis is a precise world where nothing happens, borrowed like a ruler to measure surprise. In the tea room, a woman picks four of eight cups; pure guessing gets a perfect pick one time in seventy, but three right happens nearly one time in four. The same count of chance works for quizzes and creams.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a tea room, and a claim. A woman says she can taste whether the milk or the tea went into a cup first. Would you believe her?

Nobody needs a course to doubt it. But a course can tell us how to check. This is the idea behind every test in this chapter, and we will build it three times: with cups, with a quiz, and in a clinic.

Ronald Fisher described this very experiment in his 1935 book, The Design of Experiments. Eight identical cups stand in a row. In four of them the milk went in first. In the other four, the tea did. The cups are shuffled, so only the host knows the order.

She must now pick the four cups that she believes had the milk first. Suppose she picks all four correctly. Is that proof that she can taste the difference?

Before you answer, try a different question. What would happen if she could not taste anything at all? Then she would be guessing. And that is a world we can describe exactly.

She chooses four cups out of eight. Count the ways to do that. There are seventy. Seventy different sets of four, and only one of them is the right set. If she is only guessing, every set is equally likely. So the chance of a perfect pick is one in seventy, about 1.4 percent.

Read that number with care. It does not say how likely she is to have a gift. It says only how often pure guessing would be this lucky.

In that world, a perfect pick would be very rare. That is the whole argument, and it has three steps. First, suppose that only chance is at work. Second, ask how surprising the result would be. Third, decide.

Now suppose she picks three of the four correctly. Guess before you see: is that still surprising?

Look at the wall again. Sixteen sets share exactly three cups with the right one. One set shares all four. So a pure guesser gets three or more right in seventeen sets out of seventy. That is 24.3 percent, nearly one time in four. Picture seventy guessers in the tea room: about seventeen of them would walk out with three or four right. A guesser would do this well all the time. Three out of four convinces nobody. Only the perfect pick would make us doubt the guessing world.

Now turn one knob: the number of cups. With four cups, two of each kind, there are only six sets. A perfect pick happens by luck one time in six, so it proves nothing. With six cups there are twenty sets. With eight cups, seventy. With ten cups, five of each kind, two hundred and fifty-two. The same perfect score gets harder and harder to fake, and the host chose that number before anyone tasted anything. The design of an experiment decides how much evidence a result can give.

Here is a trap. Her claim is that she can taste the difference. So why not build a world where that claim is true, and test it? Because that world has no single shape. Can she taste it a little? Nine times out of ten? Always? Each answer is a different world, with different chances for every result. Nothing can be calculated until we pick one, and we would have to guess it.

The world of pure guessing has exactly one shape. So that is the world we build, and we call it the null hypothesis. Null means no effect, nothing special going on. It is a precise model of chance.

We can also check the count by simulation. Here are ten thousand simulated guessers, each picking four cups at random. Guess before you see: how many pick perfectly? Of them, 155 did. Most guessers, 5,076 of them, got exactly two right. Only 162 got none, and 155 got all four. Counting predicts about 143. Simulation and counting agree, up to the luck of one run. Notice the order of things. We described the guessing world before the data arrived, so we cannot bend it to fit the result afterwards. Simulation will matter more when counting by hand is impossible.

Now the same logic in a classroom. A student answers ten true or false questions, and gets nine right. Did she study, or did she get lucky? Her teacher cannot see inside her head, but can ask what a guesser would do. Build the guessing world: every answer is a coin flip. Guess before you see: how many right does a guesser usually get? Five. Exactly five happens in 252 of the 1,024 sequences, 24.6 percent, and the counts fall away on both sides. Ten flips land in 1,024 different ways. Eleven of them give nine or ten right. That is 1.1 percent, so luck is a poor explanation.

Now suppose she got seven right. A hundred and seventy-six sequences give seven or more. That is 17.2 percent, about one guesser in six. So nine out of ten is hard to blame on luck, and seven out of ten is not. Turn the knob again: eighteen right out of twenty is the same share, but only 211 of more than a million sequences reach it. That is 0.02 percent. More questions, more evidence, for the same score.

And now a clinic. Twelve patients each try two creams, in random order, and say which they prefer. This example is illustrative. Ten prefer the new cream. In the null world, no cream is better, so each patient flips a coin. Twelve flips can land in 4,096 ways, and 79 of them give ten or more for the new cream. That is 1.9 percent. Suppose only seven had preferred it. Then 1,586 sequences do as well, which is 38.7 percent: a row of coins does that all the time. Same three steps: a world of chance, a count of surprise, a decision.

The decision has two outcomes. If the result would be rare in the guessing world, we doubt that world. If it would be common, we have no reason to doubt it. A shrug does not say that she cannot taste. It says only that this test gave us no reason to say that she can. And where to draw the line between rare and common is a choice, which this chapter returns to.

One more thing. We do not believe the guessing world. We borrow it, like a ruler, to measure how far our data stand from it. If we reject it, we have not proved her gift. We have only found that guessing explains the result poorly.

In this video, we never tested the claim we care about. We built a world where nothing happens, and asked whether the data feel at home there.

Remember: we do not test our claim directly. We build a world where nothing is going on, and ask whether our data are comfortable in it.

Next time, we turn that surprise into a number: the p-value.

The p-value: what it is

A p-value is the share of the curtain of chance, the histogram built in the world where tea does nothing, that lies at our result or beyond. Simulating ten thousand studies gives 313 as extreme as the tea difference, so p is 0.031. When the null is true, p-values spread evenly between zero and one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the tea room. Last time, a perfect pick by pure guessing had a chance of one in seventy: 0.014. Three cups right, or more, had a chance of seventeen in seventy: 0.243. Each of those numbers is a p-value: how often pure chance would do at least that well. But a measurement cannot be counted like cups. So today we build the world of chance by simulation, draw back its curtain, and read the number from it.

Here is a study. Forty people, drawn from the simulated pot of chapter one. Twenty drink a cup of tea, twenty drink a glass of water, and then everyone does a reaction time task. The tea group averages 291 milliseconds. The water group averages 396. The difference, tea minus water, is minus 105 milliseconds. But in chapter one, we saw how much the mean of twenty people moves by luck alone. So, is minus 105 a lot?

To answer, we need a world to compare with. NIL, the null hypothesis, describes that world precisely: tea does nothing. Each person would have the same reaction time with tea or with water. In that world, the two groups are just two ladles from the same pot, and their difference is pure luck.

We can simulate that world. Draw forty people, split them in two, compute the difference. Again, and again: ten thousand studies where tea does nothing. Their differences pile up into a histogram. It is centred on zero, with a standard deviation of about 48 milliseconds. This is the curtain of chance: the histogram of our statistic when the null hypothesis is true. Its title always says how it was built. This one was built by simulation.

Now NIL draws the curtain, and we plant a pink flag at our result: minus 105. Far out, on the thin edge of the pile. How far out? Guess before you see. Out of ten thousand studies, how many land at least as far from zero as ours: about thirty, about three hundred, or about three thousand? Now we count them, on either side, because tea could have helped or hurt. There are 313 of them, out of ten thousand. A share of 0.031. That shaded tail is the p-value.

Here is the definition. The p-value is the probability, in the world of the null hypothesis, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. The American Statistical Association wrote it this way in its 2016 statement, with this very example: a difference between the means of two groups.

Each piece of that sentence matters. In the world of the null hypothesis: the whole histogram comes from NIL's world, not from ours. At least as extreme: we take the whole tail, not just our exact value, because any exact value is rare. The one observed: one flag, from one study.

Here is a misreading that this protects us from: the chance of exactly our result. The bar at our flag, ten milliseconds wide, and its mirror hold 154 studies: a share of 0.015. A slice one millisecond wide holds only 17: 0.0017. Every exact value looks astonishing, so that question measures nothing. The tail asks the useful question: how often does chance reach our flag, or go beyond it?

Same recipe, a different world. A teacher tries a new revision method with twenty-five students, and twenty-five others do not. Same exam, scored out of one hundred. This example is illustrative. The new group averages 67.7, the other group 61.8: a difference of 5.9 points. In NIL's world, the method does nothing. Ten thousand simulated classes give a new curtain, with a standard deviation of about four points. Our flag lands on the slope of the pile, not on its edge. There are 1,393 studies out of ten thousand at least that far out: p is 0.139, about one in seven. Quite ordinary in NIL's world.

One more, to see the other end. Twenty men and twenty women, and their heights in centimetres; again, illustrative. The men average 174.8, the women 161.8: a difference of 13. In NIL's world, sex makes no difference to height. Ten thousand simulated studies: the largest gap was 11.9. Our flag sits beyond the whole pile, so the tail holds zero studies. Is the p-value zero? No. It is below one in ten thousand, which is as far as this simulation can see.

In real life, nobody can simulate the pot, because nobody has it. So the curtain is built another way. If we knew how spread out the pot is, a formula would draw it directly. Here is that curve, laid over our simulation. They match. The formula gives a p-value of 0.030, the simulation 0.031. For the heights, the formula says 0.00002. In practice that spread must be guessed from the data too, and chapter 7 is a map of those formulas. Chapter 14 builds the curtain a third way, by shuffling the data we have.

Now a strange experiment. Run ten thousand studies in the world where tea does nothing, and compute a p-value for each one. Guess before you see: where do they land, between zero and one? Piled near one, bunched around one half, or spread evenly? Here they come. The histogram is flat. A p-value of 0.9 is as common as 0.5, or 0.03. When the null hypothesis is true, every value is equally likely. So 5.2 percent of these studies land below 0.05. That is what the five percent means: in a world where nothing is going on, about one study in twenty still crosses the line.

Now give tea a real effect. Every tea drinker answers 100 milliseconds faster. That effect is invented, and large on purpose. Ten thousand new studies. Now the p-values pile up near zero: 54 percent of them land below 0.05.

Now turn the knob down. At 60 milliseconds, 23 percent of the studies land below 0.05. At 30 milliseconds, 9 percent. At zero, the flat floor again. The weaker the effect, the flatter the floor. How often a real effect gets caught is called power, and it has its own episode.

So here is what a p-value is. It is a statement about the data, given the hypothesis. If nothing were going on, results at least as extreme as ours would turn up about three times in a hundred. It is not the reverse. It does not say that there is a three percent chance that tea does nothing. That reverse question, how probable a hypothesis is given the data, needs another ingredient, a prior. Chapter 16 is built on it.

To remember: the p-value is the share of the curtain of chance that lies at our flag or beyond it. A small one means our result would be surprising in NIL's world. Next time: the p-value, what it is not. The common misreadings, one by one.

PLATE 6.36:56

The p-value: what it is not

Five misreadings of the p-value break under simulation. It is not the chance that the null is true or that results were luck, because that depends on how many ideas were real. It is not effect size, absence of significance is not absence of effect, and significant versus not significant does not mean two results differ.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the tea room. Last time, the tea study gave a p-value of 0.031. In NIL's world, results at least that far out turn up about three times in a hundred. That sentence is short, and it is easy to bend. Today we meet five ways to bend it. Each one sounds natural, and each one breaks under a simulation.

One: three percent is the chance that tea does nothing. Two: three percent is the chance that our result was luck. Three: a small p-value means a big effect. Four: a p-value above 0.05 means there is no effect. Five: significant here and not significant there means the two differ. In 2016 the American Statistical Association published six principles against such readings. Greenland and colleagues listed twenty-five misinterpretations in all. We take five, and we start with the third.

Is a small p-value a big effect? Take blood pressure, with invented numbers. Pressure varies between patients with a standard deviation of 15 millimetres of mercury. First study: five patients per arm, and the drug lowers pressure by 9. That is a lot. We build NIL's curtain for five per arm, from ten thousand null studies. It is wide, about 9.5. Our flag sits inside the pile. There are 3,489 studies in 10,000 at least that far out, so p is 0.349.

Second study: 5,000 patients per arm, and the drug lowers pressure by 1. Nobody would feel that. Guess before you see: which study has the smaller p-value? The curtain for 5,000 per arm is only 0.3 wide. Our flag falls far outside it. Only 8 studies in 10,000 go that far, so p is 0.0008. The big effect got the big p-value, and the tiny effect got the tiny one. A p-value mixes two things: how large the effect is, and how precisely the study could measure it. To judge size, read the effect itself, in its own units.

Now the fourth reading: a p-value above 0.05 means no effect. Here is a new gel for pain, scored from zero to ten. Suppose it really works: it lowers the score by 0.8 points more than a placebo. Patients differ, with a standard deviation of 2 points, and each study has 20 patients per arm. We run 100 studies, and each is a dot at its estimated difference. Guess before you see: how many cross the line, with p below 0.05? Only 23. The other 77 are not significant, for a gel that works.

Look where those dots fall. Most sit on the good side of zero, and they scatter from minus 0.8 to 2.8. A study that misses the line has not measured zero. It has measured too coarsely to tell. Over 10,000 studies, 76 percent land above 0.05, though the effect is real. So a p-value above 0.05 says only that this study could not tell. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The fifth reading is about comparing two results. Hospital A tests the gel and gets p equal to 0.029. Significant. Hospital B gets 0.14. Not significant. So the gel works in A and not in B? A found 1.37 points, and B found 0.92. The gap between them is 0.45, small next to the noise. A test of the gap gives p equal to 0.617.

Now make it systematic. Take 10,000 pairs of hospitals where the gel works exactly the same in both. In 3,701 pairs, exactly one hospital crosses the line. And in only 443 of those do the two hospitals differ significantly: 12 percent. Significant and not significant can come from one and the same world. Gelman and Stern said it in 2006: the difference between significant and not significant is not itself significant. Real papers make this mistake. Nieuwenhuis and colleagues went through 513 neuroscience articles. In 157 of them the comparison came up, and in 79 it was done the wrong way.

Now the first two readings. Is 0.03 the chance that NIL is true, or the chance of luck? To see why a p-value cannot say so, we need to know how many ideas were true to begin with. A lab tests 1,000 ideas, each in a trial with 20 patients per arm. HUNCH, the prior, believes that one idea in ten is real. So 100 are real, and 900 do nothing. Each idea rolls to the gate, and only p below 0.05 gets through.

Look at what comes through. Guess before you see: among the results that pass, how many do nothing? Of the 900 ideas that do nothing, 50 pass: NIL's five percent, give or take. Of the 100 real ideas, 68 pass. So 118 pass, and 50 of them, 42 percent, are luck. Now look only at those that just made it, with p between 0.01 and 0.05. There are 67, and 40 of them do nothing: 60 percent. A p-value near 0.03 was not a three percent chance of anything.

Turn HUNCH's knob. If half the ideas are real, 392 pass, and 21 do nothing: 5 percent. If only one idea in a hundred is real, 56 pass, and 51 do nothing: 91 percent. Go back to one in ten, but make the effect weaker, so that only 19 of the 100 real ideas get through. Now 60 pass, and 41 do nothing: 68 percent. Same p-value, same gate, four different answers. David Colquhoun made this kind of count in 2014.

The share of passing results that are real depends on two things the p-value does not contain: how many ideas were real, and how often a real one gets through. To answer the question people really ask, we need that prior. Chapter 16 is built on it.

So a p-value answers one question: how surprising are these data, if NIL's world were true? It is not the chance that NIL is true. It is not the chance of luck. It is not the size of an effect, and it does not show that an effect is absent. It cannot even tell two studies apart.

Remember: a p-value is about the data, given the null world. For the effect, look at the difference itself. For the rest, look at what was believed before. Next time: two kinds of error, and the smoke detector.

PLATE 6.46:56

Two kinds of error

A smoke detector shows two errors: a false alarm in a quiet night (alpha) and a missed fire (beta). Moving the threshold trades one for the other, and costs decide where to stand, from a home to a final drug trial. Alpha is a promise about a rule fixed before the data, so choosing it afterwards breaks it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the tea room. Last time we saw what a p-value is not. Today we ask what to do with one. A study ends with a decision: reject NIL's world, or keep it. To decide, we need a rule, and the usual rule is short. If p is below 0.05, reject. Otherwise, keep. Like any rule, it can go wrong, and today we count how. Look up at the ceiling. A smoke detector will help us.

A smoke detector measures the smoke level in a room, and it rings when the level passes a threshold. The units here are invented. On a quiet night there is no fire, and the level wanders around 30, because of toast, steam and dust. This is NIL's world. On a fire night, the level wanders around 55. The two worlds overlap. Some quiet nights are smoky, and some fires start slowly.

We simulate a thousand quiet nights and a thousand fire nights, and we set the threshold at 46.5. Guess before you see: how many of the thousand quiet nights will ring, and how many of the thousand fires will stay silent?

Look at the quiet nights first. 54 of them ring. That is a false alarm: the detector rings, and nothing is burning. We call it an error of the first kind, and its rate is alpha. Here alpha is 5.4 percent, close to the 5 percent we aimed for. Now the fire nights. 190 of them stay silent. That is a missed fire, an error of the second kind, and its rate is beta: 19 percent. The other 810 fires are caught.

Put the four cases in a table. The rows say what the night really was. The columns say what the detector did. Quiet and silent: correct. Fire and ringing: correct. Quiet and ringing: a false alarm. Fire and silent: a missed fire. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson formalised this pair of errors in 1933, and the names stuck. Their recipe was: fix alpha first, then, among the rules with that alpha, choose the one that misses the fewest fires.

Now translate the table into a study. A quiet night means the tea does nothing, so NIL is true. A fire night means tea really changes reaction times. Ringing means p below 0.05, so we reject NIL. A false alarm is announcing an effect that is not there. A missed fire is shrugging at an effect that is real. Take a teacher who tests a new method on her class. A false alarm: she adopts a method that changes nothing. A missed fire: she drops a method that works.

Read alpha with care. A common sentence says: at the five percent level, we are wrong five percent of the time. That is not quite right. Alpha counts rings among quiet nights only. On fire nights, the wrong rate is beta, and here it is 19 percent. And alpha is not the share of rings that are false. That share also depends on how many nights had fires, and the factory of last time showed it can be much larger.

Now turn the dial. Push the threshold to the ceiling, and the detector never rings: no false alarms, and every fire missed. Push it to the floor, and it rings every night: no fire is missed, and every quiet night is a false alarm. The real settings lie between. Slide the threshold up from the usual one, so the detector is harder to please. The false alarms thin out. But watch the fire nights: more and more of them slip under the line. Guess before you see: at a strict setting, will we miss a few fires, a fifth, or half? Look at 55.8. Only 7 quiet nights ring. But 516 fires stay silent, more than half. Now slide the threshold down to 38.5. Now 196 quiet nights ring, nearly one in five, and only 37 fires are missed.

Guess before you see: is there a setting where both errors are zero? No. The two worlds overlap, so every setting buys less of one error by paying with the other. The curve on the right shows every setting at once. You can only slide along it.

Which setting is right? It depends on what each error costs. In a home, a false alarm costs a minute of annoyance, and a missed fire can cost a life. So we accept many false alarms, and set the threshold low. An airport scanner is the same: a false alarm opens one harmless bag, and a missed threat is far worse, so it rings easily. A lab screening hundreds of compounds for a pilot is in the same position. A false alarm costs one follow-up experiment, and a missed compound may be a lost cure. But take the final trial before a drug is approved. A false alarm there means patients swallow something that does nothing. That lab wants a strict setting. Same rule, three costs, three different alphas.

So where does 0.05 come from? In 1925, Ronald Fisher wrote that a p-value of 0.05, one chance in 20, is a convenient point at which to call a deviation significant. Convenient, not sacred. A p-value of 0.049 and one of 0.051 are nearly the same evidence. A rule draws a line, and a line has to be somewhere. In 2018, Benjamin and 71 colleagues proposed a new default of 0.005. In our detector, that is the strict setting: few false alarms, many missed fires. Lakens and 87 colleagues answered the same year. Do not swap one default for another, they said. Justify your alpha, study by study.

One last point matters most. Alpha is a promise about a rule, kept over many nights. Here are 10,000 quiet nights, with the threshold fixed at 46.5 before the first night. Guess before you see: where will the share of false alarms settle? The share of false alarms wobbles at first, then settles near 5 percent: 489 of 10,000.

Now change the rule. After seeing each reading, the analyst may say, I meant the 0.10 level all along. Same quiet nights, and now 1,000 ring: 10 percent. The promise of 5 percent is gone, because the data chose the rule. This is why the level must be set before the data are seen. Episode 6.6 shows more ways it goes wrong.

So there are two kinds of error. A false alarm, alpha, in a world where nothing happens. A missed fire, beta, in a world where something does. Moving the threshold trades one for the other, the costs decide where to stand, and the rule is fixed before the nights begin.

Remember: alpha is the false-alarm rate of a rule fixed in advance, and beta is the rate of missed effects. Lowering one raises the other. Next time: power, and how to hear the fire.

PLATE 6.57:18

Power

Power is the share of real effects a study detects, like a radio finding a station in static. Four knobs set it: effect size, number of people, noise and alpha. Halving the effect costs four times the people. Low power also exaggerates what it finds: significant studies of a 30 millisecond effect average 117, and some point the wrong way.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the tea room. Last time, the smoke detector caught 810 of the 1,000 fires, and missed 190. That caught share has a name: power. Power is the share of real effects that a study will detect. Today we learn how to build a study that can hear the fire. We also learn what a study that cannot hear it does to the results we read.

Meet a radio. Somewhere on the dial there is a station, and around it there is static. The station is the effect. The static is chance. A study is one listen, and it must decide: station, or only static?

Take our tea study. Twenty people drink tea, twenty drink water, and we compute tea minus water. Say tea really speeds people up by 100 milliseconds. We run the study ten thousand times in each world. Where tea does nothing, the difference wobbles around zero, with a spread of 48 milliseconds. That is the static. Where tea works, it wobbles around minus 100. That is the station.

The squelch knob sets the cutoff. A study says station only if its difference lands past 95 milliseconds, on either side. With that setting, 517 of the 10,000 static studies cross the line: 5.2 percent. That is alpha. Guess before you see: how many of the 10,000 studies with a real effect will cross it? 5,353 do: 53.5 percent. The normal curve gives 54.2. The needle on the radio reads the power: this study hears a real effect of 100 milliseconds little more than half the time.

Four knobs turn that needle. The first is the strength of the station, the effect. At 60 milliseconds, the needle falls to 24.3 percent. At 30, to 9.2 percent. At zero there is no station, and the needle sits at 5.2 percent: that is just alpha, the false alarms of last time.

The second knob is the antenna, the number of people. With 80 per group instead of 20, both hills become half as wide, and the needle jumps to 98.3 percent. The third is the static itself. A sloppier stopwatch adds noise, the spread grows by half, the hills smear into each other, and the needle drops to 27.6 percent. The fourth is the squelch. Move alpha from 0.05 to 0.005, and the cutoff moves out to 136 milliseconds, and the needle drops to 22.4 percent. This is the price of strictness from last time, seen from the other side: fewer false alarms, less power.

Guess before you see. The station is half as strong: 50 milliseconds. How many people per group bring the needle back to where it was? Twice as many, or four times? With 80 per group, four times as many, the needle reads 54.0 percent. Half the effect costs four times the people.

Now plot the power against the number of people, for effects of 100, 50 and 25 milliseconds. Every curve climbs, then flattens. A usual target is 80 percent. Jacob Cohen proposed it in 1988, because he judged a false alarm four times as serious as a miss: 5 percent against 20. Reaching it takes 37 people per group for 100 milliseconds, 148 for 50, and 589 for 25. Each time the effect is halved, the people are quadrupled. Simulated studies agree: about 80 percent each time.

The same recipe works in a different world. A trial compares a new treatment with a placebo, on a depression score. This example is illustrative. The score has a spread of 10 points, and a drop of 5 points would matter. A rule of thumb, from Robert Lehr in 1992, says: people per group is about sixteen times the spread squared, over the effect squared. Sixteen times a hundred, over twenty-five, is 64. Simulated trials of 64 per group catch the drop 81.2 percent of the time.

A common misreading says: the power of a study is the chance that its result is true. It is not. Power belongs to the design, and we compute it before any data, for an effect we assume. If tea does nothing, the needle reads only alpha, whatever the study finds. So we plan for the smallest effect that would matter to us, and we ask what the design can hear.

One more world, with no reaction times at all. A coin lands heads 60 percent of the time, and we flip it 100 times to ask whether it is fair. The test rejects fairness at 61 heads or more, or 39 or fewer. A fair coin gets there 3.5 percent of the time, a little under 5 because heads come in whole numbers. The biased coin gets there 46.2 percent of the time. To reach 80 percent, we need 199 flips. These counts are exact, not simulated.

Now the part that matters most. What happens when power is low? Let the real effect be just 30 milliseconds, with 20 people per group. Of 10,000 studies, 1,022 are significant. Each dot is a study, and the significant ones are pink. Guess before you see: the truth is 30. What do the pink studies say on average: about 30, about 60, or about 120? Their average is 117 milliseconds, 3.9 times the truth. Only studies that overshoot can cross the line, so the ones that cross are exaggerated. Worse, 58 of those 1,022 point the wrong way: tea slower, not faster. That is 5.7 percent.

Now give the study 400 people per group. Power is 79 percent. 7,902 studies are significant, and their average is 34 milliseconds: 1.13 times the truth, and none with the wrong sign. Across study sizes, the more power, the smaller the exaggeration. Andrew Gelman and John Carlin called these the errors of sign and magnitude, in 2014. Low power does not only miss effects. It guarantees that the effects it finds are too big.

Is this a rare problem? In 1962, Cohen reviewed a year of a psychology journal. For a medium-sized effect, the average power was 48 percent. In 2013, Katherine Button and colleagues gathered neuroscience meta-analyses, and found a median power of 21 percent. And when such a study finds nothing, the silence says little. The needle was low, so a missing station is not proven.

So power is the share of real effects a study hears. Four knobs set it: the size of the effect, the number of people, the noise, and alpha. We plan it before the study, because low power misses effects and exaggerates the ones it finds.

Remember: power is the chance of detecting a real effect, and low power also exaggerates what it finds. Next time: what happens when we peek.

PLATE 6.67:25

Peeking, tails and stopping rules

A p-value is valid only for the analysis you would have run whatever the data said. Four habits move the curtain of chance without saying so: peeking with repeated tests, stacking analysis freedoms, choosing the tail after seeing the sign, and choosing the analysis after the plot. With no effect at all, peeking every five people gives false alarms 24.9 percent of the time.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the tea room. Today we ask what happens when the analyst peeks. A p-value comes with a promise, and the promise holds only if the plan was fixed before the data arrived. We will break that plan four ways, and count the damage each time. In every simulation today, tea does exactly what water does. So every significant result is a false alarm.

First, peeking. We plan 100 people per group, but we are impatient. After every 5 people per group, we run the test. If p is below 0.05, we stop and announce an effect. Here is one simulated study. The line is its p-value, as people arrive. At 40 per group it dips under 0.05, to 0.021. We would have stopped there. Had we kept going, it would have climbed back to 0.228 at 100. I picked it as the first of 10,000 studies that crosses and then returns.

Guess before you see. With no effect at all, and a test every 5 people, how often does a study stop with p below 0.05? Five percent, ten percent, or about a quarter? Of 10,000 simulated studies, 2,494 stop with a significant result. That is 24.9 percent: one study in four, with nothing going on.

Now turn the knob called number of looks. One look, at the planned 100: 4.7 percent, close to the promised five. Two looks: 7.8. Five looks: 14.1. Ten: 19.4. Twenty: 24.9. And a look after every single person: 37.7 percent.

Peeking has many faces, all illustrative. A lab tests 20 people per group, sees p equal to 0.07, and adds ten more. The decision taken after looking: whether to continue. The honest version: fix the sample size beforehand, as in 6.5. A website team checks an A/B test every morning, and ships the winner the first day p drops below 0.05. The honest version: fix the duration in advance, or use a method built for continuous monitoring.

And a clinical trial may look at its data several times, legitimately. The number of looks and the threshold at each are written down before the first patient. Take five looks, at 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 per group. Testing each at 0.05 gives 14.1 percent. Testing once, at 100, gives 4.7. In 1977, Stuart Pocock proposed the same stricter threshold at every look: here, p below 0.0158. That gives 4.7 percent. In 1979, Peter O'Brien and Thomas Fleming proposed a threshold that starts extremely strict and relaxes: 0.000005 at the first look, then 0.0013, 0.0085, 0.023, and 0.041 at the last. That gives 4.9 percent. The promise is kept, because the rule was fixed before the first look.

Second, the stack. Peeking is one freedom among many. Start with a plain study: 20 per group, one outcome. 5.0 percent. Now allow two outcome measures, and their average, and report whichever works: 9.8 percent. Add ten more people per group if the first test fails: 14.6. Add the choice to adjust for gender, or for its interaction with the group: 29.5. Add an outlier rule, with or without the people beyond two standard deviations: 42.1.

In 2011, Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn ran this kind of simulation. Their first three freedoms alone give 9.5, 7.7 and 11.7 percent. Ours gave 9.8, 7.8 and 11.1. Together, theirs reach 30.9 and ours 29.5. Their four freedoms together, with a different fourth, reach 60.7 percent: more than half of the studies with nothing going on.

More faces of the stack, all illustrative. A student sees p equal to 0.08, drops one outlier, takes a logarithm, adds a covariate, and stops when it works. The honest version: write the analysis plan before opening the data. An EEG team plots both groups, spots where they differ, and tests that electrode and time window. The data chose the window, and the test reuses the same data. Neuroscientists call this double dipping, as Kriegeskorte and colleagues did in 2009. The honest version: choose the window from earlier work, or from independent data. A survey finds nothing overall, so the report says: among women under thirty, a difference. The honest version: name the subgroups beforehand.

Third, the tail. A one-tailed test asks only whether tea is faster. A two-tailed test asks whether it differs in either direction. Both are fine, when chosen beforehand. Now choose after seeing the sign. Here is one simulated study, 20 per group, no effect. Tea looks faster. The two-tailed p is 0.085: not significant. The one-tailed p, in the direction we saw, is half of that: 0.043. Significant. We chose the tail after the sign.

Over 10,000 empty studies, a tail declared beforehand gives 5.0 percent, whichever tail we declare. A tail chosen after the sign gives 9.9 percent. A nominal five that is really about ten: the 0.10 level of episode 6.4 in disguise. Put it on top of the stack, and 64.3 percent of empty studies come out significant.

Now the lesson behind all four. A p-value is read on a curtain of chance. The curtain on the left is the one we drew: the statistic at the planned size, with a cut at 1.96 that catches 4.7 percent. The curtain on the right is what peeking really uses: the largest statistic over twenty looks. Beyond the same cut, it holds 24.9 percent. Every decision taken after looking, a look, an outcome, an outlier, a tail, swaps the curtain without telling us. A p-value is valid only for the analysis you would have run whatever the data said.

Would Bayes save us? A Bayes factor can favour the null, as 16.5 shows. But it is computed for one analysis. If that analysis was chosen after looking, the factor inherits the choice. The plain remedy is to write the plan down before the data, which is called preregistration.

So four ways to move the curtain without saying so: peek, stack freedoms, choose the tail after the sign, and choose the analysis after the plot. The cure is the same each time: fix the rule first, or use a rule built for looking.
