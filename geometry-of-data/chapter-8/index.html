A matrix is an ellipse

A covariance matrix draws an ellipse, and the possible ellipses fill a cone. Subtracting matrices case by case is a real distance but the wrong ruler: it averages two same-area ellipses into a fatter circle, can leave the cone into a negative variance, and hears only loud channels. One channel in millivolts moves the distance from 3.75 to over one million.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, the thing we compared was a point, a sequence, or a distribution. Chapter eight compares a square table of numbers. A matrix. Most often, the covariance matrix of a recording, or the connectivity matrix of one person.

This table is not just a list of numbers. It draws an ellipse. And not every list of numbers draws one.

The whole chapter asks a single question. Which changes to the signal should leave the distance unchanged? Every distance in this chapter is a different answer. Today we start with the simplest answer, the straight line, and we find out where it fails.

First, the recording. A channel is the series of values measured by one sensor. In EEG, it is the voltage between one electrode and a reference electrode, at every instant. The EEG series shows this in its episode one point six.

Take two channels. At every instant, the two channels give one point. Here are three hundred instants, simulated, seed eighty-one. Together they form a cloud.

The covariance matrix of this cloud holds three numbers. The variance of the first channel, one point eight seven. The variance of the second, one point two four. And the covariance, one point oh four, which says how much the two channels move together. With sixty-four channels, you would need two thousand and eighty numbers, as in episode two point four.

These three numbers draw an ellipse. Its axes point in the directions where the cloud spreads. Its half-lengths are the square roots of the variances along those axes. Here, one point six three along the long axis, and zero point six eight along the short one. The matrix is the ellipse, and the ellipse is the matrix.

But not every triple of numbers draws an ellipse. The variances must be positive. And the covariance cannot be larger than the square root of the product of the two variances. Here, that limit is one point five two.

Put the three numbers in space, and the possible ellipses fill a cone. Everything inside the cone is an ellipse. Everything outside is not. Near the axis, the ellipses are round and fat. Near the wall, they are thin. On the wall itself, one direction has no spread at all, and the ellipse has collapsed into a line. Remember that wall.

Now we try the straight line of episode two point one. We treat the matrix as a plain list of numbers, and we subtract case by case.

Three ellipses, all with the same area. Ellipse A is long and horizontal, with variances four and one quarter. Ellipse B is the same, but vertical. Ellipse C is ellipse A, turned by forty-five degrees. Sizes are drawn on one common scale.

The straight line gives five point three between ellipse A and ellipse B, and three point seven five between ellipse A and ellipse C. The four rules are lit. It is a real distance. Yet three things go wrong anyway.

First, the average. Take the average of ellipse A and ellipse B, case by case. You get a circle, with a variance of two point one two five on both channels. Its area is two point one two five times the area of ellipse A, and of ellipse B. The average of two ellipses of the same area is fatter than both. In diffusion imaging, where these matrices describe brain tissue, this is called the swelling effect. The distance of episode eight point four was proposed to avoid it, by Arsigny and colleagues, in two thousand and six.

Second, going too far. Walk along the line from ellipse B to ellipse A, and keep going, a tenth of the length beyond ellipse A. The second variance becomes minus zero point one two five. A negative variance. We have left the cone, and the straight line did not notice. It happily hands you something that is not an ellipse.

Third, the wall. Flatten ellipse A until its small variance is zero. In other words, make a signal disappear. The straight line says it costs zero point two five, at most. The wall of the cone is very close.

Why does this matter? Because averaging trials, following a drift, and smoothing an estimate are things we do with matrices all the time.

Now the row of changes. For matrices, it takes its own form, and it will serve for the whole chapter. Rotated. Rescaled. Mixed. Inverted. Flattened. Under each copy, a light shows what the distance between ellipse A and ellipse C becomes.

Rotate both matrices by thirty degrees, and the distance stays at three point seven five. That is what the straight line ignores: a rotation applied to both.

Now express the first channel in millivolts instead of volts. The distance jumps from three point seven five to one million, eight hundred seventy-five thousand and two. Same recording, another unit.

Mix the channels with one fixed matrix, two, one, one, one. The distance becomes six point seven six. Flatten ellipse A, and it becomes three point eight eight.

Invert both matrices, and it stays at three point seven five. But that is luck. Four and one quarter are each other's inverse. Take ellipse A and a circle of variance two: the distance goes from two point six six to three point five one.

Let us fill the card. The object: a matrix, read as a list of numbers. The distance: the root of the sum of the squared gaps, case by case. What it ignores: only a rotation applied to both matrices. What it costs: one pass over the cases. And the trap.

The trap is that it only listens to the loud channels. Take eight channels with very unequal variances, from one hundred down to zero point zero one. Double the largest, from one hundred to two hundred, and the distance is one hundred. Double the smallest, from zero point zero one to zero point zero two, and the distance is zero point zero one. The same doubling, ten thousand times smaller.

So a covariance matrix is a point in a cone, not in a flat space. The straight line is a real distance there, and still the wrong ruler. It leaves the cone, it swells the averages, and it only hears the channels that shout.

PLATE 8.27:37

Measure in ratios

Measuring in ratios takes the logarithm of the variance ratios along the directions where two ellipses differ most, then combines them with Pythagoras. The wide and tall ellipses, with ratios sixteen and one sixteenth, are 3.92 apart. This affine-invariant distance ignores rotation, a millivolt rescaling, any fixed channel mixing and inversion, and grows without limit as a variance nears zero.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last episode, we met three ellipses. Each one is a covariance matrix. We measured them with the straight line, and the straight line went wrong in three ways.

So we change the question. We stop asking how far apart the numbers are. We ask which changes to the signal should never move the distance. This episode builds one answer, and the answer is to measure in ratios.

Start with a single number. A variance of one, and a variance of two. Then a variance of one hundred, and a variance of two hundred.

The straight line says the first step is worth one, and the second step is worth one hundred. But in both cases, the signal got twice as strong. It is the same change.

So we compare by division. How many times bigger is it? Then we take the logarithm of that ratio. For one to two, the natural logarithm of two is zero point six nine. For one hundred to two hundred, it is zero point six nine again. Doubling counts the same, wherever you start.

Think of a ruler whose ticks are not evenly spaced. Each tick is a doubling. That is a slide rule. Our ruler, RULY, has been re-marked. Every step she measures is now a ratio.

Now for two ellipses. They live in two channels, so one ratio is not enough. Here is the recipe.

First, look for the direction where the first ellipse is much stronger than the second. Measure the ratio of their variances along it. That is the largest ratio. Then look for the direction where it is the other way around. That is the smallest ratio.

Second, take the logarithm of each ratio.

Third, combine them with Pythagoras. Square each logarithm, add, take the square root.

Try it on the wide ellipse and the tall ellipse. Along the horizontal direction, the variances are four, and a quarter. The ratio is sixteen. Along the vertical direction, the ratio is one sixteenth. The logarithms are two point seven seven, and minus two point seven seven. Pythagoras gives three point nine two.

Now the wide ellipse and the tilted one. The two ratios are eight point nine two, and zero point one one. The distance is three point oh nine.

Those special directions have a name in linear algebra. They solve a generalized eigenvalue problem. And you may have met it in the EEG series. Common spatial patterns look for exactly these directions, the ones where one condition is strongest compared with the other. Blankertz and colleagues explain it in their review from two thousand eight. Same problem, new use: here we do not keep the directions, we keep the ratios.

Now the row of changes. Does this distance see them?

First, a rotation, applied to both matrices. The distance between wide and tall stays at three point nine two. Between wide and tilted, three point oh nine. The straight line did not mind rotations either.

Second, a change of unit. We write one channel in millivolts instead of volts. The distance stays three point oh nine. The straight line, on the same change, jumps from three point seven five to one million, eight hundred seventy-five thousand and two.

Third, a mixing of the channels. We take a fixed matrix, and we mix the channels with it, the same way for both recordings. Remember this one, because it is what the head does to EEG.

Every electrode receives a weighted sum of the sources in the brain. The weights stay the same from one moment to the next. That is a fixed mixing. And every channel is measured against a reference electrode. Changing the reference is, again, a mixing of the channels. The EEG series shows both.

After the mixing, the distance between wide and tall is still three point nine two. The straight line, on the same pair, climbs from five point three oh to twelve point four four. In the plan for this series, that last number was twelve point eight three. It came from a mixing matrix that was not written down. With the matrix we fixed for this chapter, we get twelve point four four.

Fourth, the inverse. We replace each matrix by its inverse. Nothing changes. Three point nine two.

Five changes, five answers. The distance did not move for the first four. That is the meaning of the word invariant.

Does it treat sizes fairly? Take two channels with the same variance. Going from one to two costs zero point nine eight. Going from two to four costs zero point nine eight again. With eight channels, whose variances range from a hundred down to a hundredth, doubling the largest one costs zero point six nine. Doubling the smallest one costs zero point six nine too. The straight line charged a hundred for the first, and one hundredth for the second. Here, a doubling is a doubling.

The fifth change is the last one. We flatten the wide ellipse, squeezing its small variance toward zero. That means making a signal disappear.

Squeeze it from a quarter down to zero point zero one, and the distance is three point two two. Squeeze it to one in ten thousand, seven point eight two. To one in a hundred million, seventeen point oh three. And exactly zero? There is no number. The distance is not defined.

The edge of the cone has gone to infinity. You can approach it for as long as you like, and you will never arrive. In the language of the four lights, all four are on. This is a real distance.

It has a name: the affine-invariant distance. It was written down several times, from several directions. Skovgaard, in nineteen eighty-four, derived it as a geometry of the normal distribution. Förstner and Moonen, in nineteen ninety-nine, used it as a distance between covariance matrices. And Pennec, Fillard and Ayache, in two thousand six, turned it into a full toolbox for diffusion tensors.

It makes the set of ellipses a curved space, with negative or zero curvature. That is the subject of chapter nine.

And its first reading waits for chapter eleven. Divide the distance by the square root of two, and you get the Fisher–Rao distance between two centred bell curves, as shown by Wells and colleagues in two thousand twenty. For our ellipses, that gives two point seven seven, and two point one nine.

Now the card. The object: a covariance matrix. The distance: the root of the sum of squared logarithms of ratios.

What it ignores: any invertible mixing of the channels, applied to both matrices.

What it costs: one generalized eigen-decomposition per pair, which is cubic in the number of channels. On this machine, one pair of sixty-four channels takes about one hundred and fifty microseconds. At two hundred fifty-six channels, it takes two point nine milliseconds. Four times the channels, about nineteen times the time. For large sizes, the cube takes over.

And its trap: it listens to small variances as much as to large ones. That includes the directions where there is nothing but noise. And it stops existing the moment a variance is zero.

Here is the point to keep. The invariance is the choice. Suppose the electrodes see a fixed mixture of as many sources as there are channels. Then this distance gives the same number on the scalp and on the sources. It does not know which channels you placed. It sees only what changed between the two recordings.

Measure in ratios, and a doubling is a doubling.

The mean of ellipses

A mean of ellipses minimizes summed squared distances, and each distance gives its own. For the wide and tall ellipses, the case-by-case mean is a circle of variance 2.125 with swollen area, while the ratio mean is the circle of variance one, with the same area. In a simulated toy, the ratio distance classified 87.0 percent of trials correctly, the straight line 51.9.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Episode one point six defined the mean without a single addition. The mean is the point that makes the sum of the squared distances to the data as small as possible. Maurice Fréchet wrote that down in nineteen forty-eight.

With the straight line, this gives the ordinary mean. The sum of squared differences has its lowest point at the total divided by the count, and you have known that since school. With another distance, it gives another mean. Episode six point six showed it for histograms: each distance has its own mean. Today it is the turn of ellipses.

Start with two plain numbers, one and one hundred. The straight line puts the mean at fifty point five, almost on top of the big one. Now measure in ratios, as in episode eight point two. From one to ten is a factor of ten. From ten to one hundred is a factor of ten. So the middle is ten. That is the geometric mean, the square root of one times one hundred. Same data, two means, and each one is right for its own distance.

Now the ellipses. This is the chapter's tool: a covariance matrix is an ellipse, and we want the ellipse in the middle of several. Take the wide one and the tall one, the same area. Average them case by case, and you get a circle of variance two point one two five. Its area is two point one two five times the area of either ellipse. The mean has swollen.

Now average them with the ratio distance. You get the circle of variance one. It has the same area as the two ellipses. Nothing has swollen.

Watch the area while we slide from the wide ellipse to the tall one. On the straight path, the area balloons up to two point one two five times, then comes back down. On the ratio path, the area never changes.

Here is a second pair: the wide ellipse and the tilted one. The ratio mean is an ellipse turned by twenty-two point five degrees, exactly halfway. Its variances are two point zero eight and zero point four eight, and its area is still the same. The case by case mean has variances three point four five and zero point eight, and an area one point six six times larger.

For two matrices, there is a formula. It takes square roots of matrices, and it is written on the screen. For three or more matrices, there is no formula. So we walk to the answer.

Start from a guess, say the ordinary mean. The idea is the one we just used on one and one hundred. Take the logarithm of each number, average the logarithms, and bring the result back with the exponential. That gives ten. For matrices, we do the same, with one difficulty: the logarithm depends on where you stand. Unfold every matrix flat, around the guess. That unfolding is a logarithm, taken from the point of view of the guess: the matrix is rewritten as a set of ratios, measured against the guess along its own axes. In the flat picture, the ordinary average makes sense again, so take it. Then fold the result back into the space of ellipses. That is the new guess. Repeat.

Here is the loop on six small ellipses. The guess starts fat, because the ordinary mean swells. The first turn already slims it down and turns it toward the middle. The next turns only polish: each step is about a tenth of the one before. After eight turns, it stops moving.

Does the mean always exist, and is it unique? Yes, here. When the curvature of the space is negative or zero, the sum of squares has a single lowest point. Pennec, Fillard and Ayache proved this for these matrices in two thousand six. Episode nine point two will show a space where it fails.

How many turns do we need? These matrices are simulated, seed eighty-three. Thirty matrices, with four, sixteen or sixty-four channels. The power of each source is spread by a factor of ten. We stop when the averaged unfolded matrix is smaller than one in a hundred million. Over twenty draws, that takes eight or nine turns, whatever the number of channels.

The number of turns depends on the scatter, not on the channels. With a spread of three, six turns. A spread of one hundred, seventeen to nineteen turns. A spread of one thousand, between fifty and ninety-three.

There is one more property worth knowing. Mix the channels of every matrix by the same invertible matrix. The ratio mean of the mixed matrices is the mixed ratio mean, exactly. We checked it on ten matrices of four channels: the two sides agree to thirteen digits. This is the invariance of episode eight point two, now carried to the mean.

Now a use. Every trial of an experiment gives a covariance matrix. Compute the mean matrix of each class. A new trial receives the class of the nearest mean. Barachant and colleagues described this for brain computer interfaces in twenty ten, and extended it to several classes in twenty twelve.

Here is a toy example, built for this episode. It is simulated, seed eighty-three. Four channels, four mixed sources. One source is strong, with variance thirty. Its power wanders from trial to trial, by up to a factor of ten. It is a nuisance. Another source is weak: variance zero point three in the first class, zero point four five in the second. That is a factor of one point five, and it is the only thing that tells the classes apart.

Fifty trials per class to learn, fifty to test, two hundred samples per trial, and twenty repetitions, each with a new mixing.

Here is every test trial of the first repetition, as one dot. Its score is how much nearer it is to the first class mean than to the second. Blue dots belong to the first class, pink dots to the second.

With the straight line, fifty-one point nine percent of trials are correctly classified. That is a coin toss. With the ratio distance, eighty-seven point zero percent.

Across the twenty repetitions, the straight line stays between forty and fifty-nine percent. The ratio distance ranges from sixty-two to ninety-nine, because each repetition has its own mixing and only fifty test trials per class. Why? The nuisance pushes every trial in the same direction, and the straight line follows it. In ratios, the same push costs the same to both class means, so it cancels out.

This example is built to show exactly that. Now swap the roles. Put the factor of one point five on the strong source, and let the weak source wander. The straight line gets ninety-four point six percent. The ratio distance gets eighty-six point zero. The straight line does as well, even a little better.

Here is the card of this mean. Remember the format: the object, the distance, what it ignores, what it costs, and its trap.

So what does it cost? A few turns, and at every turn, one decomposition per matrix. For thirty matrices of sixty-four channels, one turn takes seven point six milliseconds on this machine.

And the traps. First, the mean only exists if every matrix has all its variances positive. A dead channel has variance zero, its logarithm is minus infinity, and the loop breaks.

Second, one aberrant trial drags the mean toward itself, because we add squares. Take twenty clean matrices near the unit circle, and one more with all its variances ten times larger. It sits three point three away. It moves the mean by zero point one six.

So the mean is never a fact about the data alone. It is a fact about the data and a distance. Before you average matrices, choose the distance, and say it.

PLATE 8.47:03

Flatten it

Covariance matrices can be flattened in two ways before using ordinary tools. The log-Euclidean way takes each matrix's logarithm: wide to tilted becomes 2.77 against 3.09 for the ratio distance, and averages never swell. The second way flattens around one point, usually the mean, giving 2080 numbers for sixty-four channels. Its trap is that touching point.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Some of the tools you already know need a flat space. A straight line needs one. An ordinary average needs one. So does a linear classifier.

Covariance matrices do not live in a flat space. They live in a curved one, and episode 8.2 paid for it: one decomposition for every pair of matrices.

So here is another plan. Do not measure in the curved space. Flatten it first, then use everything you already own. There are two ways to flatten, and they are not the same.

The first way is called log-Euclidean. It comes from Arsigny and colleagues, in 2006 and 2007. Take the logarithm of each matrix. For an ellipse, that means the logarithm of its variances, along its own axes. The axes stay where they are. Only the lengths change. Then do the one thing you trust: a straight line between the two results.

You met the idea in episode 8.2. Going from a variance of one to two should count as much as going from one hundred to two hundred. A logarithm does exactly that.

Take our three ellipses of the same area. The wide one, the tall one, and the tilted one, which is the wide one turned by forty-five degrees. Between the wide one and the tall one, the log-Euclidean distance is 3.92. It is exactly the ratio distance of episode 8.2, because these two share the same axes. Between the wide one and the tilted one, it is 2.77, against 3.09 for the ratio distance. Close, but not identical.

Something else comes for free. After a logarithm, any point on the line is the logarithm of a real matrix, because undoing a logarithm always gives positive variances. You can walk as far as you like along the line, and you never fall out of the cone. The negative variance of episode 8.1 cannot happen.

The average now has a formula. Average the logarithms, then undo the logarithm. And it does not swell. The area of this average is the geometric mean of the two areas. For ellipses of equal area, the average has the same area. The swelling that Arsigny and colleagues wanted to avoid in diffusion imaging is gone.

It is not the same average as in episode 8.3, though. For the wide and the tilted ellipse, it is also turned by 22.5 degrees, with the same area, but it is more stretched. Its variances are 2.67 and 0.38, against 2.08 and 0.48 before.

Now the row of changes. Turn both ellipses by thirty degrees: 2.77, unchanged. Replace each matrix by its inverse: unchanged. Multiply both matrices by the same factor, say seven: unchanged. A global factor adds the same constant to every logarithm, and an inverse only flips their signs.

Now express one channel in millivolts instead of volts. The distance is 0.89. The straight line gave one million, eight hundred seventy-five thousand and two, so this is a lot calmer, but it does see the change. Mix the channels with one fixed matrix, the same for both recordings: 1.07. The distance moved, where the ratio distance stays at 3.09. So this first way ignores rotations, a global rescaling and inversion, but not an arbitrary mixing.

Last, flatten the wide ellipse, by pushing its small variance toward zero. The distance goes to 5.19, then 9.52, then 18.58. It never stops growing, so you can never reach the edge of the cone.

The second way flattens around one point, the way a map is drawn around one place. Picture a sheet of glass, bent like a saddle, resting on a flat marble slab. The glass touches the slab at a single point. Close to that point, glass and slab agree. Farther away, they drift apart.

The recipe comes from Tuzel, Porikli and Meer in 2008, and from Barachant and colleagues in 2012. First, choose the touching point. Take the mean of your own matrices, the one from episode 8.3. Second, bring every matrix to what it would be if that mean were a circle. Third, take the logarithm. Fourth, write the cells of the result into one list. For eight channels, that is 36 numbers. For sixty-four channels, 2080.

Every matrix is now an ordinary vector. All of chapter 2 applies. The straight line, the average, a linear classifier, a regression.

How wrong is the map? We measured it. Forty simulated matrices of eight channels, with seed 84, in three clouds: tight, medium and wide. For every pair, we compared the distance on the map with the true distance.

In the tight cloud, the matrices sit at 0.22 from their mean. The map never falls below 0.999 of the true distance. In the medium cloud, at 1.03, never below 0.988. In the wide cloud, at 2.08, never below 0.943.

The ratio never went above one. The map always shortens. That is the signature of negative curvature. The glass leaves the slab, so a walk along the glass is longer than the picture on the slab.

Now take the medium cloud again, and flatten it around the circle of variance one instead of its own mean. This cloud has its mean at 4.61 from that circle. The worst ratio falls to 0.69, and the median to 0.86. And this is exactly the first way. The logarithm, then a straight line, is a map that touches at the circle.

Now the card. What does it ignore? Curvature, so the long distances far from the touching point. What does it cost? One decomposition per matrix, and no longer one per pair. For forty matrices, that is forty, not 780 pairs. For a thousand matrices, a thousand, instead of almost half a million. The mean adds a few rounds: 4, 7 and 11 here.

The trap is the touching point. Two analyses unfolded around two different points do not give the same vectors. We took a neighbouring session, whose mean is 0.99 away from the first one. The same matrix gets two lists of numbers, and they differ by 99 percent of their own length, in the median. So write down where you put the point of contact. And never train around one mean and test around another, unless you recentre first.

A flat map of a curved space is right near the point where it touches. Put that point in the middle of your data, and say where you put it. It is the problem of every map of the Earth, and episode 9.1 begins there.

PLATE 8.58:09

Cheaper cousins

For centred bell shapes, the Kullback-Leibler divergence needs only a trace and a determinant and ignores any invertible channel mixing. It keeps its direction: 1.16 bits from the ellipse to the circle, 0.46 back. Comparing each matrix with the average gives the S-divergence, which broke the triangle in 10,123 of 20,000 two-channel triplets, unlike its square root.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In episode 5.3, we measured the gap between two distributions with the Kullback-Leibler divergence. Now the two distributions are bell shapes with several channels, both centred on zero. For these, the divergence depends only on the two covariance matrices. It is computed with a trace and a determinant, and nothing else. The trace asks how big one ellipse is when the other serves as the ruler. The determinant compares their areas. Together they give one number.

For one channel, you already know it. It is the Itakura-Saito divergence of episode 4.5, divided by two, and then by the natural logarithm of two, to count it in bits.

It keeps its direction. Take the ellipse with variances four and one, and the circle of variance one. From the ellipse to the circle, it costs 1.16 bits. From the circle to the ellipse, 0.46 bits.

Statisticians met this quantity long before we did. In 1961, James and Stein used it to judge an estimate of a covariance matrix. Their loss is twice this divergence, counted in natural units. It knows that guessing a variance too small is not the same mistake as guessing it too large.

What does it ignore? Any invertible mixing of the channels, applied to both matrices. The ratio distance of episode 8.2 ignores the same things. The reason is simple. The formula only uses the second matrix inverted and multiplied by the first, and a mixing cancels in that product, in the trace and in the areas. In an EEG recording, each electrode sums the brain sources with fixed weights, and changing the reference is also a mixing. So this divergence gives the same number on the scalp and on the sources.

Now we need a distance, and the direction gets in the way. Episode 5.4 gave the recipe. Compare each one with the one in the middle. For two matrices, the middle is simply their average. It is not a blend of the two bells, but the bell whose covariance is that average. For A and B, the average is a circle of variance 2.125. Add the divergence from A to that circle, and from B to that circle. The sum collapses into three determinants: the determinant of the average, and the determinants of A and of B.

This is the Jensen-Bregman log-det divergence of Cherian and colleagues, in 2011 and 2013. Sra named it the S-divergence. Those papers use natural logarithms. So in this episode only, this divergence is counted in nats, not bits. One bit is 0.69 nats.

Between A and B, the S-divergence is 1.51 nats. Between A and C, 1.01 nats. These are the divergence itself. Its square root, the true distance, is 1.23 and 1.01.

Now the row of changes. Rotate both matrices: 1.01. Express one channel in millivolts instead of volts: 1.01. Mix the channels: 1.01. Invert both matrices: 1.01. Nothing moves. Only flattening is seen. Push the small variance of A down to 0.01, and the distance to C rises to 2.48. Push it to one hundred-millionth, and it reaches 9.38. It never stops growing, so a flattened matrix is out of reach.

Now the four lights. Never negative: lit. Zero only for equal matrices: lit. The same in both directions: lit. The fourth goes out. The triangle rule falls.

Take three variances, 1, 10 and 100. From 1 to 10 is 0.55. From 10 to 100 is 0.55. Straight from 1 to 100 is 1.62, which is more than the detour, 1.11. With one channel, any three different variances break the rule.

How often, with ellipses? The die draws twenty thousand random triplets of ellipses, simulated, seed 85. With two channels, 10,123 triplets break the rule, about half. With four channels, 2,298. With eight channels, 71.

But take the square root, and the rule holds. In every draw, zero violations. And this is proved. The square root of the S-divergence is a distance, as Sra showed in 2012 and 2016. Its four lights are lit.

The thread of episode 5.5 comes back. Up close, all these measures agree. Take two matrices that are very close. The squared ratio distance is 0.00407. The Kullback-Leibler divergence is 0.00150 bits one way, and 0.00144 bits the other. Multiply by four, and convert to nats: 0.00416 and 0.00399. Eight times the symmetric divergence is 0.00407. The units are mixed on purpose, so you can see the factors that make them agree.

Far away, they do not. Take two variances whose ratio is 2, 10, 100, and ten thousand. The ratio distance is 0.69, 2.30, 4.61 and 9.21. The square root of the S-divergence is 0.24, 0.74, 1.27 and 1.98. That looks like a big gap, but part of it is only the factor from the close-up: the square root of eight. Multiply by it, and you get 0.69, 2.10, 3.60 and 5.59. They start together, and they part. The cousin crushes big gaps.

What does it cost? No decomposition, only determinants. We measured on this machine, in processor time, one thread, the median of many tries. At 64 channels, the ratio distance takes 155 microseconds. The three determinants take 35. That is about four times less. Counting operations explains the factor: generalized eigenvalues take about four d cubed operations, where d is the number of channels, and three Cholesky factorizations take about one d cubed. Keep each matrix's determinant in memory, and one pair takes 15 microseconds: about ten times less.

At 256 channels, the ratio distance takes 2.9 milliseconds. The three determinants take 0.77, nearly four times less. With the determinants kept, 0.26: about eleven times less.

We expected an ordinary determinant routine to gain nothing. Here it does gain: 42 microseconds at 64 channels. So the gain does not come from a clever routine. It comes from the way the calculation is written: determinants, not eigenvalues.

Cherian and colleagues report about four times less for matrices between 200 and 1,000 channels wide, and no gain at all for matrices of five by five. With eight channels, we measure only 1.3 times less.

Let us fill the card. The object: two matrices, drawn as ellipses. The distance: a trace and determinants. What it ignores: any invertible mixing of the channels. What it costs: determinants only, four to ten times less than eigenvalues. And the trap is to believe this is the same distance, only cheaper. It has the same invariance. It does not have the same idea of what is far.

Same invariance, another price, another idea of far. Up close, all these distances say the same thing. Chapter 11 will say why.

Ellipses as piles of sand

Seeing ellipses as piles of sand, the distance is the least total travel, computed from matrix square roots. The wide and tall ellipses are 2.12 apart, and moving a thousand real points each gave 2.13. It notices units and mixing but not rotation, and flattening an ellipse toward a line keeps the distance near 0.50, so it reaches the cone's edge.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we met cheaper cousins of the ratio distance, which keep its invariance and change its price. Today's distance asks a different question. The ratio distance asks about shape. This one asks about stuff.

A glassblower starts from a heap of sand. Melt it, and the heap becomes a gob of glass on the end of a pipe. Turn the pipe, press the gob, and it takes another outline. The glass is the same glass. It only moved. So how much of it had to travel, and how far?

Give that question to the ellipses of this chapter. Each ellipse is a cloud of grains. The wide ellipse is spread along the horizontal, and thin. The tall ellipse is its twin, standing up. The tilted one is the wide ellipse turned by forty-five degrees. All three have the same area. We read every distance in the unit of the data, say microvolts, and this is a simulated example.

On a line, episode six point three gave a shortcut for two bell curves with the same centre. The square of the distance is the square of the gap between the spreads. Variances of one and four are spreads of one and two. The gap is one, and so is the distance.

With several channels there is a formula of the same kind, and it needs only the two covariance matrices. It goes back to Dowson and Landau, and to Olkin and Pukelsheim, both in nineteen eighty-two, and to Givens and Shortt in nineteen eighty-four. The square of the distance is the total variance of the first matrix, plus the total variance of the second, minus twice the total of a square root. That root is taken of a sandwich: the root of the first matrix, then the second matrix, then the root of the first again.

You do not do this by hand. Look at what it does with one channel. The total variances are just the two variances, and the sandwich gives the product. What is left is the square of the gap between the spreads. It is the same formula as before, in more dimensions.

Now the three ellipses. From the wide one to the tall one: two point one two. From the wide one to the tilted one: one point three six. Remember that the straight line said five point three, and three point seven five. The numbers are not in the same unit, so they cannot be compared directly. The ordering is the same, though.

Why is this a story about sand? Because the formula hides a recipe. There is one straight stretch that turns the wide cloud into the tall one, at the smallest total travel. For these two ellipses it shrinks the horizontal by a factor of four and stretches the vertical by a factor of four. Every grain slides along a straight line, at constant speed, to the place the stretch gives it.

Stop halfway. Each grain has moved half of its trip. The cloud is now a circle, with a variance of one point five six. This is the transport mean of episode six point six: the barycentre, the point halfway along every trip. The area of this circle is one point five six times the area of the wide ellipse. The mean for the ratio distance did not swell at all. The case by case mean, remember, was a circle of variance two point one two five, and the area was two point one two five times bigger. Transport swells less, but it still swells.

Is the formula right? Let us move real grains. Draw a thousand points from the wide ellipse, and a thousand from the tall one. Pair them so that the total travel is as small as possible. Take the square root of the average squared trip. I did this a hundred times. The average is two point one three. In nine draws out of ten, the answer fell between two point oh six and two point two zero. The formula says two point one two. A finite sample never matches perfectly, as episode six point four found: two samples of the same source are never at distance zero.

Now the row of changes, on the wide and the tilted ellipses, which start at one point three six.

Rotated by thirty degrees, both of them: one point three six. Nothing changed. A rotation carries every grain the same way, so the trips keep their length. This is the one change that the distance ignores.

Rescaled: write the first channel in millivolts rather than volts. The distance becomes five hundred and forty-two. It does notice the unit. The straight line was worse. It said one million, eight hundred and seventy-five thousand and two.

Mixed: the two channels are blended by the same fixed matrix, for both ellipses. One point three six becomes zero point eight zero. The ratio distance did not care about mixing. This one does. The mixing matrix is two, one, one, one, and it is the same one in the whole chapter.

Inverted: one point three six again. Be careful here. That is an accident of our example. These ellipses have area one, in two channels, and the inverse of the tilted one is just the tilted one turned by a quarter. In general, an inversion changes this distance. So the light for this change stays on a question mark.

Flattened. Take the wide ellipse, and squeeze its small variance toward zero. A signal is disappearing. The ratio distance had said three point two two, then seven point eight two, then seventeen, and it kept growing without limit. Here, the distance from the wide ellipse to the squeezed one is zero point four zero, then zero point four nine, then zero point five zero, and never more. A flat ellipse is still a heap of sand, only laid along a line. Every grain is still there. This distance accepts matrices that have lost directions.

For more than two matrices, there is no closed formula for the mean. Agueh and Carlier defined it in twenty eleven, for any number of distributions. Álvarez-Esteban and colleagues, in twenty sixteen, showed how to find it by repeating one step until it stops moving.

This distance has two other lives. First, take matrices with total variance one. In quantum physics, those are the states, and the same formula is the Bures distance, from Bures in nineteen sixty-nine. Bhatia, Jain and Lim wrote it in the transport form in twenty nineteen. Be careful with conventions. They write the squared distance as two times one minus the fidelity, with the fidelity written without a square. Many quantum texts square it. The quantum series has its own episode on this. Say which convention you use.

Second, it is the formula behind the score used to judge image generators. Heusel and colleagues, in twenty seventeen, fit a bell to the descriptors of real images, and another to the descriptors of generated ones. The score is this formula, plus the squared gap between the two means. It is named after Fréchet.

Let us fill in the card. The object: a covariance matrix, seen as a centred bell. The distance: the least total travel of sand from one ellipse to the other, with the squared cost. What it ignores: only a rotation applied to both. What it costs: matrix square roots, so the cube of the number of channels. Its trap: it listens to the loud channels, and it depends on the unit.

On that trap, take one channel with a variance of one, and another with a variance of two, ten, a hundred and ten thousand. Transport says zero point four one, two point one six, nine, and ninety-nine. The ratio distance said zero point six nine, two point three, four point six one, and nine point two one. Transport sees the loud channel. The ratio distance sees the ratio. The four rules hold. Never negative, zero only for the same matrix, the same both ways, and no shortcut.

Here is what to remember. This distance sees an ellipse as a pile of sand, and measures what must be moved, in the unit of the data. A flattened ellipse is still a pile of sand. Of all the distances of this chapter, with the straight line, it is the only one that reaches the edge of the cone.

Which invariance do you need?

Five matrix distances are compared by which changes leave them unmoved: rotation, doubling, a unit change, fixed mixing, inversion and flattening. The straight line says 3.75 and is spared only by rotation; the ratio distance, 3.09, and the divergence root, 1.01, ignore all but flattening. With sixty-four channels and one hundred samples, two estimates of the identity are 12.6 apart.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Five distances, one chapter. Time to put them side by side.

The question is not which distance is best. The question is this. Which changes should your answer ignore?

Here is the test. Take the long flat ellipse from the start of the chapter, and its copy turned by forty-five degrees. Make one change to both of them. Then measure again. If the number did not move at all, the distance ignores that change, and the cell stays pale. If it moved, the cell turns pink.

There are six changes, one per column. Turn both ellipses by thirty degrees. Multiply every channel by two. Change the unit of one channel, from volts to millivolts. Mix the channels with one fixed matrix, the way electrodes mix brain sources. Invert both matrices. And flatten: push the smallest variance down toward zero.

Five distances make the rows. First, the straight line, cell by cell.

It says three point seven five. A rotation does not move it. Nothing else spares it. Doubling every channel gives fifteen. One channel in millivolts gives almost two million. A mix gives six point seven six. Inversion moves it too, but be careful. On this pair, by a symmetry, the number happens to stay the same. On random pairs, inversion changes it. So a pale cell has to hold on every pair, not only on one.

Second, take the logarithm of each matrix, then the straight line. Rotation, doubling and inversion leave it at two point seven seven. Changing one unit moves it to zero point eight nine. A mix moves it to one point oh seven.

Third, the distance in ratios. Three point oh nine. And nothing moves it. Not the rotation, not the doubling, not the unit, not the mix, not the inversion.

Fourth, the root of the symmetric divergence. One point oh one, and again, nothing moves it.

Fifth, transport. One point three six. Rotation does not move it, and nothing else spares it. Now the question the plan left open. Does transport ignore a global rescale? No. Double every channel, and the distance doubles. It follows the amplitude, so it follows the square root of the factor on the covariances. A unit change takes it to five hundred and forty-two. A mix takes it to zero point eight. Inversion moves it too.

The last column asks something different. Push the small variance of the flat ellipse down to one hundred-millionth, and measure how far it travelled. The straight line says one quarter, and it will never say more. Transport says one half, and it will never say more. The distance in ratios says seventeen, and keeps climbing. So does the logarithm. The root of the divergence says two point eight, and also climbs. At exactly zero, those three have no answer at all. So two distances accept a lost direction, and three forbid it.

One more column: the mean. Average the flat ellipse and the vertical one, each distance with its own mean. The straight line gives a mean with two point one two five times their area. That is the swelling of diffusion imaging. Transport gives one point five six. The logarithm, the ratios and the divergence give exactly one. Their mean has the same area as the ellipses you averaged.

So there is no best row. There is the list of changes your question must ignore. If your electrodes see a fixed mix of sources, you want a column that stays pale all the way down.

Each column is a decision about your recording. A rotation means you turned the axes of your sensors. A unit change means another amplifier gain on one channel. A mix means another reference, or another set of electrodes seeing the same sources. An inversion means you work with the precision matrix instead of the covariance. Ask which of these your conclusion must survive, and read down that column.

But the table hides something. It assumes you know the matrices. You never do. You estimate them.

So we measured the floor. Simulate one source whose covariance is exactly the identity. Cut the recording into two chunks, and estimate the covariance of each. Measure the distance between the two estimates. It should be zero.

It is not. With eight channels and one hundred samples, the distance in ratios says one point two seven. With one thousand samples, zero point three four. With sixty-four channels and one hundred samples, twelve point six. With one thousand, two point nine five. With ten thousand, zero point nine.

For scale, on sixty-four channels, doubling every variance costs five point five five. So with a hundred samples, two estimates of the same thing are more than twice as far apart as that. Transport gives four point six five, one point four five, and zero point four five. This is one draw. Fifty fresh draws at sixty-four channels and one hundred samples land between eleven point eight and twelve point eight, so it is not a lucky one.

Where does the floor come from? The true variances are all one. But the estimated variances, along their own axes, spread from zero point oh four to three point oh seven with one hundred samples. With one thousand, from zero point five nine to one point five three. Marchenko and Pastur described that spread in nineteen sixty-seven, and their bounds, drawn here, match. The distance in ratios listens to the small variances, and those are the worst estimated.

Fewer samples than channels, and it gets worse. With sixty-four channels and fifty samples, the matrix has at most forty-nine directions. The other fifteen have variance zero. The distance in ratios does not exist.

A reference does the same, quietly. Subtract the average of eight channels, and one direction disappears. Seven remain. Remove two more components, and five remain.

There are three remedies. Shrink the matrix toward a simpler one: that is Ledoit and Wolf, two thousand four, the estimator of episode two point four. Work in the directions that remain, in a subspace shared by all your matrices. Or choose a distance that accepts flattening, like transport. Sabbagh and colleagues compared those last two routes in twenty nineteen.

One last case: correlation matrices. Their diagonal is one. Take two small correlation matrices. Their ordinary average keeps the diagonal at one. The other means of this chapter do not. For the mean in ratios, the diagonal reads zero point eight one, zero point eight six, and zero point nine five. The mean is no longer a correlation matrix. These matrices have their own geometries: David and Gu, twenty nineteen, and Thanwerdas and Pennec, twenty twenty-two.

So before you read a distance between two matrices, measure the distance between two estimates of the same one. And write down the changes your question must ignore.

Your trials as ellipses

Established uses include the ratio distance on diffusion imaging, and covariance-based brain-computer interfaces, where unfolding around the mean raised average accuracy from 65.1 to 70.2 percent. Worth trying: read the distance between condition means against a floor, 0.45 against 0.28, and note that a divergence to a matrix without cross blocks equals mutual information, 0.159 bit.

TRANSCRIPTEN

This is the last episode of the matrices chapter, and it asks one question. Once you take the covariance of each trial of your experiment, every trial is an ellipse. So what do we already know about working with ellipses, and what is only an idea?

Two columns. On the left, Established. Every entry has a paper behind it, and we checked what the paper says. On the right, Worth trying. Each idea comes with a small computation of ours, simulated with seed eighty-eight. None of them is a result.

First entry. Diffusion imaging. In two thousand six, Pennec, Fillard and Ayache gave the space of these matrices an affine-invariant metric. That is the distance in ratios of episode eight point two. They applied it to diffusion tensor imaging, where every point of the brain carries a small matrix. The same year, Arsigny and colleagues proposed the logarithm of episode eight point four for the same images. They reported similar results in practice, with much simpler and faster computations.

Second entry. Brain-computer interfaces. In twenty twelve, Barachant and colleagues classified each trial by its covariance matrix. They used a public competition dataset: nine people, four imagined movements. Giving each class its mean, and sending a trial to the nearest mean, gave results comparable to the reference method, spatial filters and a linear classifier. Unfolding the matrices around their mean, and classifying there, did better. The average accuracy went from sixty-five point one to seventy point two percent. In twenty eighteen, Lotte and colleagues reviewed ten years of classifiers. They conclude that these methods have reached state-of-the-art performance on several problems.

Third entry. Functional MRI. In twenty ten, Varoquaux and colleagues built a probabilistic model for connectivity matrices on this space. They compared post-stroke patients, one by one, to a group of healthy people. Their model was more sensitive than the standard procedure. In twenty nineteen, Dadi and colleagues compared more than two hundred forty analysis pipelines, on two thousand people in six cohorts. One of their findings: describing connectivity in the tangent space, midway between correlation and partial correlation, was beneficial. In twenty twenty, Venkatesh, JaJa and Pessoa identified people from their resting-state matrices. With a geodesic distance, accuracy was over ninety-five percent, and the paper says it exceeded the correlation approach by twenty percent.

Fourth entry. Moving between sessions, or between people. Zanini and colleagues, in twenty eighteen, transformed the matrices of every session and every subject so that they are centred on a reference matrix, which makes them comparable. Rodrigues, Jutten and Congedo, in twenty nineteen, went further, with translation, scaling and rotation. They tested it on eight public datasets, two hundred forty-three people in all, and it beat the other geometry-aware methods.

Fifth entry. Two people. Korczowski, Congedo and Jutten, in twenty fifteen, studied two players of a collaborative brain-computer game. A Riemannian classifier, extended to use the statistics of both players, did better than the mean of the two players. A second variant did better than the best player of the pair.

Now the right column.

Idea one. Compute one matrix per trial and per condition. Take the mean matrix of each condition, and measure the distance between the two means. Then read it against a floor: the distance between the means of two random halves of the trials of one condition. In our simulation, eight channels, thirty trials per condition, one source is fifty percent stronger in the second condition. The distance between the means is zero point four five. The floor averages zero point two eight, and ninety-five percent of the halves stay below zero point three four.

Idea two. Follow a session as a path. One matrix per sliding window, and the length of the path travelled. A still session already travels fourteen point eight. A session whose covariance really changes halfway travels fourteen point eight too. The noise swallows the change, so the length alone is not what to read. The distance between the mean of the first half and the mean of the second half does better. For a still session it averages zero point three five, with ninety-five percent below zero point four one. With the change, it averages zero point five four.

Idea three. Two people recorded together. Put their channels side by side, and you get one big covariance matrix, a single point. Now take the same matrix with the cross blocks set to zero. That is the model where the two people ignore each other. The Kullback-Leibler divergence of episode eight point five, from the joint matrix to the one without cross blocks, is exactly the mutual information between the two people, if the signals are bell-shaped. We checked it on six channels, three per person. Zero point one five nine zero bit, computed both ways. The other order of the divergence gives zero point one eight, so the order matters. This is an identity, so it holds for any matrix. We found no paper that measures it on real pairs.

Idea four. Recentre the matrices of each person before comparing two people. We simulated two people who see the same sources through different mixtures. The same condition, measured on the two of them, is five point five and five point seven apart. Recentre each person on their own mean, and they are zero point three four apart, both times. The distance between the two conditions inside one person does not move: zero point five zero and zero point four five.

One question separates the two columns. How many samples per channel do you have? With sixty-four channels and a thousand samples, sixteen per channel, two independent estimates of the identity matrix are already two point nine five apart. With ten thousand samples, about one hundred fifty-six per channel, they are still zero point nine one apart. A gap between two conditions can only be read against that floor.

Back to the question. A trial is an ellipse, and for ellipses the ratio is the natural measure. Ask which changes must not move your distance, and how many samples per channel you have.

Established on the left. Worth trying on the right. Four ideas, and none of them is a result. The next chapter asks what it means to walk between them, in a space that is curved.
