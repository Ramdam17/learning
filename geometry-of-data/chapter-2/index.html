The straight line

Three people described by height and weight show that the straight line trusts your units. Measured in metres, A's nearest neighbour is B; write heights in centimetres and it becomes C, though nobody moved. The weight column made up ninety-two percent of the first distance. Dividing each column by its spread, a standardising choice, restores B as neighbour in both units.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Three people walk into a tailor's shop. Each one gets measured. The tailor writes down two numbers, and nothing else.

That is a vector. A vector is a row of numbers, one for each thing we measured. Person A is one point six zero metres tall and weighs sixty kilograms. Person B is one point nine zero metres and sixty-one kilograms. Person C is one point six two metres and sixty-four kilograms. Three vectors, two columns each. These people are simulated, and so is everything else you will see today.

Now a question. Who is A's nearest neighbour? To answer, we need a distance. And the first one anyone reaches for is the straight line.

Put height along one axis and weight along the other, with one unit drawn the same length on both. Each person becomes a point, a small tag pinned on the cloth. Draw the straight segment from A to B. It is the long side of a right triangle. One short side is the difference in height. The other is the difference in weight. And Pythagoras tells us the rest: square the two short sides, add them, take the square root. Euclid wrote that theorem down more than two thousand years ago, so this distance carries his name too. It is the Euclidean distance.

Let us do it for A and B. The height differs by zero point three metres. Squared, zero point zero nine. The weight differs by one kilogram. Squared, one. Add them: one point zero nine. Square root: one point zero four.

Now A and C. The height differs by two centimetres, that is zero point zero two metres. The weight differs by four kilograms. Squared, sixteen. The root is four point zero zero.

Here is the table of all the distances. The darker the cell, the farther the pair. So A is much closer to B than to C. One point zero four against four. B is the nearest neighbour.

A word on columns. Nothing here stops at two. With three columns you add a third square. With a thousand columns you add a thousand squares. Same recipe, as long as you like.

Now we change one thing. The tailor writes the heights in centimetres, not in metres. Nobody grew. Nobody lost weight. Same people, same bodies, same day.

Heights become one hundred and sixty, one hundred and ninety, and one hundred and sixty-two. Weights stay as they were.

A to B. The height differs by thirty. Squared, nine hundred. The weight differs by one, squared, one. Nine hundred and one, and the root is thirty point zero two. A to C. The height differs by two, squared, four. The weight differs by four, squared, sixteen. Twenty, and the root is four point four seven.

Here is the new table, next to the old one. Look at the row of A. Before, B was the neighbour. Now it is C. The neighbour flipped, and nobody moved.

Why? Look at what each column contributes to the sum. In metres, the weight column makes up ninety-two percent of the A to B distance, because kilograms are numbers like sixty and metres are numbers like one point six. In centimetres, the height column makes up almost all of it, ninety-nine point nine percent. The straight line adds the columns as if they were all measured in the same unit. They are not. So the column that carries the biggest numbers decides.

Now we can say what the straight line ignores. We take the three people and change them in five ways, and each time we ask one question: does the distance between A and B change?

First, shift everybody by the same amount: add ten centimetres to every height and five kilograms to every weight. The distance between A and B: unchanged. Second, multiply every column by one point six, as if we zoomed in. The distance becomes one point six seven. That is a change, but the neighbours stay the same. Third, turn the whole cloud by forty-five degrees. Unchanged again. Fourth, change the unit of one column, heights in centimetres. The distance jumps by almost twenty-nine, and the neighbour changes. Fifth, add a little random noise to every number. The distance moves a little, by about seven hundredths.

Notice what the straight line does not see. Moving the cloud, turning the cloud. Turning a cloud of heights and weights makes no physical sense, and yet the distance does not care. That is a clue. It treats every column as the same kind of thing.

What does it cost? Almost nothing. For each column, one subtraction, one square, and one addition. For a thousand columns: a thousand subtractions, a thousand squares, nine hundred and ninety-nine additions, and a single square root. It is about as cheap as a distance can get.

Now the trap. We have seen it. The straight line trusts your units. So what can we do?

Bring every column to the same spread before measuring. Take each column, subtract its average, and divide by its spread. Here the spread is the standard deviation across the three people. It is zero point one four metres for height, and one point seven kilograms for weight. Do it in metres or in centimetres, and you get exactly the same numbers. A to B: two point two seven. A to C: two point three six. In both units, B is the neighbour, though only by a hair.

Three people are far too few to estimate a spread, so take this as an illustration. But the lesson is not. Dividing each column by its spread is not housekeeping. It is a choice, and it is already a choice of distance. It says that one standard deviation of height counts as much as one standard deviation of weight. You may agree. You may not. Either way, somebody decided.

The documentation of a widely used machine-learning library makes the same point. On a wine dataset where one column runs up to a thousand and another stays below ten, neighbours depend almost entirely on the big column, until the columns are scaled.

And you can go further. The spread of one column is not the whole story. Columns can move together. Later in this chapter, we will meet a distance that learns the shape of the whole cloud.

Time to fill the card. The object is a vector: one number per measurement. The distance is Pythagoras, over every column. It ignores moving the cloud and turning it. It costs one subtraction, one square and one addition per column. Its trap is the units: change them, and the neighbours change.

And the four rules? Never negative. Yes. Zero only for the same point. Yes. The same in both directions. Yes. And no shortcut. Yes: going through a third point is never shorter than going straight. All four lights are on. This is a true distance.

Before you measure, ask what a number is measured in. The straight line cannot ask for you.

City blocks and chessboards

From the corner zero, zero to three blocks east and four north, a walker on streets covers seven, a crow five and a chess king four. These are the Manhattan, Euclidean and Chebyshev distances, all members of one family with a power p. Only the straight line ignores rotation. Below p equal to one the triangle rule breaks, and the median minimises Manhattan totals.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a city built on a grid. Streets run east and west, avenues run north and south, and every block is the same size. We want to get from the corner marked zero, zero to the corner three blocks east and four blocks north.

Three travellers set out. The first is Inch, and Inch can only walk along the streets. Three blocks east, four blocks north. Seven blocks in all. The second is Ruly, who flies straight over the roofs, as the crow flies. That is the straight line from last time, and it is five. The third is a chess king. A king may step one square in any direction, diagonals included, so it cuts the corner. Three steps go diagonally, and one more goes straight. Four moves.

Seven, five, and four. Same two corners, three different answers. That is the whole episode in a nutshell, so let us look at how each one is made.

Remember the straight line. Take the difference in each column, square it, add the squares, take the square root. Now look at the other two as recipes on the same differences. Here the differences are three and four. Walking along the streets, you simply add them: three plus four, seven. This is the Manhattan distance, named after the street grid of Manhattan. The king takes the largest of them: four. This is the Chebyshev distance, named after a Russian mathematician, and it is the number of king moves on a chessboard.

So we have three ways to combine the gaps column by column. Add them, square them, or keep only the biggest. And they are not three strangers. They belong to one family.

Here is the family. Raise every gap to the power p. Add up. Then take the p-th root. With p equal to one, you get the sum: the city blocks. With p equal to two, you get the straight line. And as p grows without limit, the biggest gap swallows all the others, and you get the maximum: the chessboard. This family carries the name of Hermann Minkowski, and p is the one knob that moves you along it.

To see what the knob does, draw every point that is exactly one step from the centre. With p equal to two, that is a circle, of course. With p equal to one, it is a diamond. With p equal to infinity, it is a square. And everything in between is a rounded shape that slides from diamond to circle to square as p goes up. In this simulated city, you can watch it happen.

Now let us run our row of changes, with all three distances on the same pair of corners, zero, zero and three, four. Shift both corners by the same amount: all three distances stay at seven, five and four. Blind to the shift. Amplify everything by one and a half: every distance grows by one and a half, so all three see it. Now turn the whole map by forty-five degrees. The straight line stays at exactly five. It does not care how the map is turned. But the city blocks give five point six six, and the chessboard gives four point nine five. Both of them noticed.

Here is the cleanest way to see it. Take the point one, zero. In a straight line, it is one step away. Turn the map forty-five degrees, so the point moves to about zero point seven one, zero point seven one. The straight line still says one. The sum says one point four one. The maximum says zero point seven one. Same point, same turn, three different answers.

So only the straight line ignores the orientation of the axes. The other two are tied to the axes: they depend on which direction the columns happen to point. Keep that in mind. It is the trap, and we will come back to it.

Back to the row. Stretch one column by a factor of two, and everything changes, all three. Add some noise, and all three move too. The straight line is the only one that tolerates turning.

Now what about p smaller than one? You can still compute the formula. Try p equal to one half, between zero, zero and one, one. The gaps are one and one. Take each to the power one half, that is still one. Add them: two. Then raise to the power two, which is one over one half. Four. The direct path costs four.

Now go through the corner one, zero instead. From zero, zero to one, zero: one. From one, zero to one, one: one. Two in total. The detour costs two, and the direct path costs four. A detour that is shorter than the direct road! The triangle rule is broken. For p equal to one or more, the family always obeys it, thanks to an inequality of Minkowski. Below one, it does not. So below one, you have a dissimilarity, but not a distance.

So which p should you choose? Here is the real question. Ten columns. One object differs from the origin by a single big gap of six, and nothing else. Another differs by a small gap of one in nine columns. Which one is nearer? Add the gaps, and the single big gap wins: six against nine. Use the straight line, and the nine small gaps win: three against six. Use the maximum, and the small gaps win by a mile: one against six. The distances do not even agree on which of the same two objects is nearer.

That is what p does. Choosing p means deciding how much one big gap should weigh against many small ones. The maximum listens only to the worst column. The sum listens to every column equally. And the straight line sits in between.

Cost is easy. All three do one subtraction per column. The sum takes an absolute value, the maximum takes an absolute value, and the straight line takes a square. Then one addition, or one comparison, per column. For a thousand columns, that is about the same work, and the straight line pays a single square root at the end. The three cost about the same.

Now a link back to the last episode of chapter one, where the average turned out to be a hidden choice of distance. The average is the point that makes the sum of squared straight-line distances as small as possible. There is a twin for the city blocks: take the median of each column on its own. That point makes the sum of the Manhattan distances as small as possible.

Here is a simulated cloud of eleven points, with one far outlier. The average is pulled toward the outlier. The median stays among the crowd. Count it up. At the average, the squared straight-line total is eighty-seven point three. At the median, it is ninety-three point six. The average wins on its own ground. But the city-block total is twenty-nine point seven at the average, and twenty-seven point four at the median. On that ground, the median wins. Each centre is the best one for its own distance.

Let us fill in the card. The object is a vector, a row of numbers. The distance is a member of the Minkowski family. What it ignores: shifting everything. Its cost is one operation per column. And the trap: outside the straight line, the distance depends on the axes, so every column has to mean something on its own. All four rules hold for p of one or more.

Before you pick a distance, ask which p you want, and whether your columns really are separate things.

Direction only

A text twice as long as another has the same word mix, yet the straight line says three point six one apart. The angle between the arrows ignores length and is zero. One minus the cosine breaks the triangle rule, but the angle itself works. The trap is the zero point: adding one hundred to every number moves the cosine from 0.71 to 0.9999.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two short texts about the same subject. One is twice as long as the other. Are they alike?

Let us turn each text into a vector. Choose two words, neuron and signal, and count how many times each one appears. The short text says neuron three times and signal twice. That is the vector three, two. The long text says neuron six times and signal four times. That is six, four. These counts are made up, to keep the picture flat. Real texts use thousands of words, and the recipe is the same.

Draw each vector as an arrow from the zero point. The two arrows lie on the same line. They point the same way. One is simply twice as long.

What does the straight line say? From the tip of one arrow to the tip of the other, the gap is three point six one. That is exactly the length of the short arrow. The straight line says these two texts are far apart, and the only reason is that one text is longer.

Now measure something else. The angle between the two arrows is zero degrees. Same direction, same mix of words. The cosine of that angle is one. The cosine similarity is simply this number: one for the same direction, zero for a right angle, minus one for opposite directions.

A cosine is a similarity, big when things look alike. Can we turn it into a distance, as in episode one point four? Take three arrows: one, zero; one, one; and zero, one. The cosines are zero point seven one, zero point seven one, and zero. One minus the cosine gives zero point two nine, zero point two nine, and one. The detour through the middle arrow costs zero point five eight. The direct road costs one. The detour is shorter, so the triangle rule fails. Same trap as before: one minus a similarity is not a distance.

But the angle itself works. It is forty-five degrees, forty-five degrees, and ninety. Forty-five plus forty-five is not less than ninety, so the triangle holds. The angle is an arc on a circle, and a detour along an arc is never shorter than the arc. The same arrows give the repair of episode one point four: the straight chord between two arrows of length one is the root of two times one minus the cosine. In twenty twenty-one, Erich Schubert wrote down a triangle inequality for the cosine similarity itself.

Now the row of changes. We take two arrows, four, one and two, three. Their angle is forty-two degrees. We change the picture in five ways, and ask: does the angle move?

First, shift everything by the same amount, two units on each axis. The angle drops to twenty-five degrees. It sees the shift. Second, multiply everything by one point six. The angle stays at forty-two. And stretch one arrow alone, by any factor you like: still forty-two. Third, turn the whole sheet by forty-five degrees about the zero point. The angle stays. Fourth, change the unit of one column, as in the last episode. Now the angle is seven degrees. A different story entirely. Fifth, add a little noise. The angle moves, by about ten degrees.

So the angle ignores length, and turning the sheet around zero. Nothing else. Every other change it sees.

Which brings us to the trap, and the trap is the zero.

Take the vectors one, two, three and three, two, one. The first rises, the second falls. Their cosine is zero point seven one: rather alike. Their correlation is minus one: exactly opposite. Same numbers, two opposite verdicts.

Now add one hundred to every number. The vectors become one hundred and one, one hundred and two, one hundred and three, and the reverse. They are almost the same arrow now, and their cosine climbs to zero point nine nine nine nine. The angle between them has shrunk from forty-four degrees to less than one. Nothing changed in the pattern. We only moved the zero.

That is why the correlation exists. The correlation is the cosine, computed after you subtract from each vector its own average. Add one hundred, and the average goes up by one hundred, and the correlation does not move. It is minus one before, and minus one after.

So the cosine depends on where zero sits. Temperatures in Celsius and in kelvin describe the same weather, and give different cosines. Before you use a cosine, ask what zero means in your data.

Time to fill the card. The object is a vector: a row of numbers, seen from a zero point. The distance is the angle between two vectors. It ignores their length, and turning them about zero. It costs one dot product and two lengths, and if every vector has length one already, just the dot product. Its trap is the zero: move it, and the angle changes.

And the four rules? Never negative, yes. The same in both directions, yes. No shortcut, yes. Zero only for the same point? Here the light goes out. A text and its double are at zero. That is the whole point, and the whole price: when you ignore length, two different vectors can be at distance zero. On arrows of length one, all four lights are on.

Does anyone use this? In nineteen seventy-five, Salton, Wong and Yang described each text as a vector, brought to length one, and compared two texts by their dot product or their angle. Today, a trained model gives each word or image a list of numbers, chosen so that similar objects get close vectors, and we often compare them by cosine. The World Models series shows this in its episode four point one.

But be careful. Harald Steck and colleagues, in twenty twenty-four, showed that for some models, the cosine between learned vectors can take almost any value you like. The training leaves the length of each learned vector free, and the cosine inherits that freedom. The cosine is not wrong. But what it means depends on the model.

Here is the rule of the day. The cosine ignores length, and nothing else. If the length is a nuisance, like the size of a text, it is the right choice. If the length carries the effect, you have just thrown it away.

The distance that knows the cloud

Two customers, P and Q, sit one point four one from the centre of a correlated cloud, but only Q is a stranger. The Mahalanobis distance, the straight line after making the cloud round, gives one point zero three for P and four point four seven for Q. It ignores unit changes, but with thirty observations and sixty-four columns the matrix cannot be inverted.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last episode, we ended on a promise. Later in this chapter, we would meet a distance that learns the shape of the cloud. This is that episode.

Back in the tailor's shop, we measure a lot more people. Two columns, height and weight. And this time we have already fixed the units: both columns are rescaled to the same spread, one unit each. These people are simulated, seed twenty-four.

Here is the cloud. It is not round. It lies along a diagonal, because the two columns move together. Tall people tend to be heavy ones. The correlation is zero point nine.

Now take two new customers. Call them P and Q. P sits in the direction of the cloud, one unit up and one unit across. Q sits across the cloud, one unit across and one unit down. Measure each one from the centre with a straight line. Both are one point four one away.

But look at the picture. P looks like a perfectly ordinary customer. Q is a stranger, far from anybody else. The straight line cannot tell them apart. It has no idea that the cloud has a shape.

A distance that does know the shape was written down in nineteen thirty-six, by the Indian statistician Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis. For P, it gives one point zero three. For Q, four point four seven. Same straight-line distance, very different strangeness.

Why? Here is the picture. Take the cloud and turn it, until its long axis is flat. Then press it along that axis, and stretch it across, until the cloud is round. Along the long axis the spread was one point three eight. Across it, only zero point three two. So we squeeze one direction and stretch the other. Now the cloud is a round patch, in new units. And in those units, we measure with the plain straight line.

Look where P and Q land. P hardly moves. It is still near the centre. Q is thrown far out. That is the Mahalanobis distance: the straight line, after the cloud has been made round. In symbols, the square root of the difference, times the inverse of the covariance matrix, times the difference again. The covariance matrix holds the shape of the cloud.

Now the question we always ask. What does it ignore? We take the cloud, and change it in five ways. And each time we ask: does the distance between P and the centre change?

Shift everybody by the same amount: unchanged. Multiply everything by one point six: unchanged, even though the straight line grew from one point four one to two point two six. Turn the whole cloud by forty-five degrees: unchanged. Now change the unit of one column, heights times one hundred. The straight line explodes to one hundred. Mahalanobis stays exactly where it was.

Only noise changes it. Add a little random noise to every number and the distance moves by about seven hundredths.

That is the problem from the straight-line episode, solved. Changing units, mixing the columns together, any invertible change of the columns: the distance does not care. There is one condition. The shape must be estimated again from the changed data. The shape moves with the cloud, so the distance moves with it.

So what does it cost? The straight line needed almost nothing. This distance first needs the shape. For two columns, the covariance matrix holds three numbers. For ten columns, fifty-five. For sixty-four, as in sixty-four EEG channels, two thousand and eighty. For a thousand columns, five hundred thousand, five hundred. And then the matrix must be inverted, which grows with the cube of the number of columns.

And all those numbers are estimated from the observations. With fewer observations than columns, the calculation is impossible as it stands. We tried it: thirty observations, sixty-four columns. The estimated matrix has rank twenty-nine. It cannot be inverted.

Even with a hundred observations, trouble. We drew data whose true shape is perfectly round, so every direction should have the same spread, one. The estimate says the spread runs from about zero point zero five to three. Some directions look nearly flat, so the distance blows up in those directions.

One answer pulls the estimate toward a round cloud. Ledoit and Wolf published such an estimator in two thousand and four. On the same data, the spread now runs from zero point nine four to one point zero two. Here the truth really was round, so the pull was strong. On real data, the pull is chosen from the data.

Now the trap. The shape is estimated from the same data we are trying to inspect. Suppose a few observations are wrong. One hundred regular points, and twenty-five outliers sitting together, far off across the cloud. The outliers pull the estimated cloud toward themselves. The ellipse swells in their direction. And the distance that was supposed to find them says they are normal. With the usual estimate, none of the twenty-five is flagged. Their average distance is one point nine seven, well inside the limit of two point seven two. This is called masking.

Estimate the shape on the most compact half of the data instead, as the minimum covariance determinant does, and the outliers stand out. All twenty-five are flagged, at an average distance of fourteen. Two regular points are flagged too, which is about what the limit allows. The method came in the mid-nineteen-eighties, and a fast algorithm made it practical in nineteen ninety-nine.

Time to fill the card. The object is a vector with correlated columns. The distance is the straight line, after the cloud has been made round. It ignores any change of units, and any mixing of the columns. It costs a covariance matrix: about half of p squared numbers, and an inversion. Its trap is that the shape is estimated from the data, so outliers can hide.

And the four rules? Never negative. Yes. Zero only for the same point. Yes, as long as the matrix can be inverted. The same in both directions. Yes. And no shortcut. Yes, because it is a straight line in new units. The squared version is not a distance, but the distance itself is. All four lights are on.

So the Mahalanobis distance is not one more distance. It is the straight line, measured in units learned from the data. It is worth exactly what the estimate of the shape is worth. So look at that estimate first.

When the numbers are counts

Three drawers of coloured buttons show the straight line failing on counts: drawers two and three share no colour yet come out closest at 1.73. Bray-Curtis divides the gaps by the totals and Canberra scales each colour; both ignore colours missing from both drawers. Bray-Curtis breaks the triangle rule in about five percent of triples, while Canberra keeps it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Some numbers are counts.

Counts are whole numbers. They are never negative, and a lot of them are zero. Species per site. Words per text. Spikes per neuron.

Let's picture them in the workshop. Three drawers of buttons. Each drawer is a site. Each colour of button is a species.

Drawer one holds no pink buttons, four blue ones and eight yellow ones.

Drawer two holds no pink, one blue and one yellow.

Drawer three holds a single pink button, and nothing else.

Which two drawers are alike?

Start with the tool we already have, the straight line from episode 2.1.

Drawers two and three come out at 1.73. Drawers one and two come out at 7.62.

So the closest pair is drawers two and three. Yet they share no colour at all, while drawers one and two hold the same colours, in different amounts.

The straight line measures the size of the gap.

A drawer with only a few buttons is close to everything, even to a drawer with nothing in common.

Counts need a distance that knows what a zero means.

In 1957, Roger Bray and John Curtis were studying forest communities in Wisconsin.

Their dissimilarity, as it is written today, is simple.

Add up the gaps, column by column. Then divide by the total number of buttons in the two drawers.

Drawers one and two. The gaps are 0, 3 and 7. That makes ten.

The totals are twelve buttons and two buttons. That makes fourteen.

Ten over fourteen is 0.71.

Drawers two and three. The gaps are 1, 1 and 1. That makes three. The totals are two and one. That makes three.

Three over three is exactly 1, its largest value.

There is another way to read it. Pair up the buttons.

In each colour, the smaller of the two counts is matched.

Drawers one and two match two buttons, one blue and one yellow.

Bray-Curtis is one, minus twice the matched buttons, over all the buttons. One minus four over fourteen.

Drawers two and three match nothing. So the answer is one.

A second recipe comes from Godfrey Lance and William Williams, in 1966 and 1967. It is called the Canberra distance.

It gives each colour its own scale.

For each colour, divide the gap by the sum of the two counts.

One button against two buttons. The gap is one, the sum is three. A third.

A hundred buttons against two hundred. The gap is a hundred, the sum is three hundred. Also a third.

So a small difference and a large one weigh the same, as long as the ratio is the same.

One button against none counts as one. A hundred against none also counts as one.

And a colour that is zero in both drawers counts as nothing at all.

Then add up the colours.

Drawers one and two: 0.6 and 0.78 from the blue and the yellow. A total of 1.38.

Drawers two and three: one, one and one. A total of 3, the largest value possible with three colours.

Both recipes ignore the same thing. A colour that is missing from both drawers.

It adds nothing on the top. It adds nothing on the bottom.

Ecologists call this a double zero. Two sites that both lack a species are not more alike for it.

We checked. Add a fourth colour that nobody has, and neither number moves.

Now the row of changes. We keep the same two drawers, one and two, and change them.

Shifted. Add one button of every colour to both drawers. The straight line does not notice. Bray-Curtis moves by 0.21. Canberra moves by 0.31.

Amplified. Double both drawers, as if you had scooped twice as hard. The straight line doubles. Bray-Curtis and Canberra stay put.

Rotated. Here we have a problem. Turning the axes would mix the colours, and make negative counts. There is no such thing as a rotated count. We leave this one with a question mark.

Rescaled. Multiply the yellow column by ten. Bray-Curtis moves by 0.05. Canberra does not move at all, because every colour has its own scale.

Noisy. Redraw every count at random, as counts really fluctuate. Here the dice come in. Over a thousand draws, Bray-Curtis moves by 0.14 on average and Canberra by 0.34.

The cost is the same as the straight line: one pass over the columns.

Now the traps. Bray-Curtis first.

Take three vectors: one, zero. Zero, one. And one, one.

The direct path from the first to the second is 1.

But going through the third costs one third plus one third.

The detour is shorter than the direct road. The triangle rule falls.

Bray-Curtis is a dissimilarity, not a distance. Over twenty thousand random triples of count vectors, about 5 percent break the rule. Simulated, seed 25.

There is a second trap, and it is about effort.

We simulated one community of ten species, three common and seven rare.

We sampled it twice, with the same effort. The average Bray-Curtis is 0.11.

Now sample the second site twice as hard. The community has not changed. But the average jumps to 0.34.

Bray-Curtis depends on the total you counted. And the total counted depends on your effort.

Canberra has a different trap. It gives the same weight to a small count as to a large one.

And small counts are the noisiest.

In the same simulation, seven of the ten colours are rare. Yet they supply 93 percent of the Canberra distance.

In Bray-Curtis, they supply 25 percent.

Canberra listens to the columns that say the least.

Does Canberra keep the triangle rule? Yes. Here is a short argument. It is ours, so we show it, we do not cite it.

For positive counts, the ratio is the hyperbolic tangent of half the gap between the logarithms.

An increasing function that curves downward turns a distance into a distance.

A zero is a point at the far end, one step from every other count.

And a sum of distances, colour by colour, is a distance.

We also tested twenty thousand triples. Not one broke the rule.

One last warning. Software does not agree on the scaling of Canberra.

Some programs divide by the number of colours, others by the number of non-zero colours. Check yours.

Let's fill the cards.

The object is a vector of counts. The distance is a ratio of gaps. It ignores the colours that both drawers lack. It costs one pass. Its trap is the effort, for Bray-Curtis, and the rare colours, for Canberra.

For Bray-Curtis, three lights are on, and the triangle is off. For Canberra, all four are on.

A zero is not a number like the others.

Absent from both sides does not mean the same.

When your data are counts, pick a distance that knows what a zero means.

And when only the share of the total matters, we are already in chapter 10.

PLATE 2.66:55

The trap of many dimensions

With a thousand columns, the farthest of a thousand random points is only one point one times as far as the nearest, against about seventy-one in two columns. Distances concentrate in a narrow spike, balls fill almost none of the box, and random directions are nearly perpendicular. Structure survives if it exceeds the noise. Draw the histogram of your distances before trusting a neighbour.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A tailor measures a customer with a tape. Two numbers, then three, then ten. Every new measurement is a new column in the row of numbers that describes that person.

Now picture a thousand measurements for every customer. Does the straight line still tell you who is close?

Not the way you hope. This episode is about a trap that opens quietly as the columns pile up.

Let us build it. We toss a thousand points at random into a square. Then pick one point, and measure its distance to the other nine hundred and ninety-nine.

Two numbers matter: the distance to its nearest neighbour, and the distance to its farthest one. We divide the second by the first. For each of the thousand points, we get a ratio, and we keep the typical one.

In a square, two columns, that ratio is about seventy-one. The farthest point is seventy times farther than the nearest. "Near" and "far" mean something.

Now a cube. Three columns. The ratio falls to twenty-two.

Ten columns: three point seven. One hundred columns: one point four. One thousand columns: one point one.

Think about that. With a thousand columns, the farthest point is only ten percent farther than the nearest. The neighbours are all on one thin ring around the chosen point. It is a dartboard where every dart lands in the same circle.

Here is the same story for all the pairs at once. We take every distance between two of the thousand points, and divide by their average. In two columns, the distances spread widely: the standard deviation is forty-eight percent of the average. With ten columns, nineteen percent. With a hundred, six percent. With a thousand columns, one point eight percent. The histogram has become a spike. Almost every pair of points is at almost the same distance.

This has a name. In the preface of his 1957 book on dynamic programming, Richard Bellman called the growth of difficulty with the number of variables "the curse of dimensionality". He was talking about the cost of computing. The name stuck, and it now covers this effect too.

For distances, the careful statement comes from Beyer and colleagues in 1999. They proved that, under a condition on the variance of the distances that is much broader than independent columns, the nearest distance approaches the farthest as columns increase. And they observed it in their experiments with as few as ten to fifteen columns. They also said that some data escape the effect. We will meet those data in a minute.

First, a second picture. Draw a ball that just fits inside a box. In two columns, it fills seventy-nine percent of the box. In three columns, fifty-two percent. In ten columns, a quarter of one percent. With a hundred columns, the share is about ten to the minus seventy: nothing.

Where did the volume go? To the corners. A corner of the box is half a unit, times the square root of the number of columns, away from the centre. That is five units with a hundred columns, while the ball stops at half a unit. Almost every random point sits in a corner, and corners are far from everything.

Third picture: angles. Take two directions at random, and measure the angle between them. We repeat that one hundred thousand times for each number of columns.

With three columns, the angle can be nearly anything: the middle ninety percent of the draws runs from twenty-six degrees to one hundred fifty-four. With ten columns, from fifty-eight to one hundred twenty-one. With a hundred, from eighty to one hundred. With a thousand columns, from eighty-seven to ninety-three degrees. Ninety percent of the pairs are within three degrees of a right angle. With ten thousand columns, from eighty-nine to ninety-one.

So in many dimensions, two random directions are almost perpendicular, and two random points are almost equally far. Nothing is near. Nothing is far.

Now the other side of the story. Take a thousand columns, but let the data vary along only two directions. The ratio of farthest to nearest is sixty-five. The columns are many, but the data are a flat sheet in disguise, and the sheet has plenty of contrast.

Now add a little noise to every column, one percent of the width of the data. The ratio drops to two point six. A thousand useless coordinates, each adding a whisper, make one loud noise together.

And what about real structure? Two groups of a hundred points, in a thousand columns. Inside a group, the distances run from eleven point seven to fourteen point one. Between the groups, the smallest distance is thirty-one point three. The groups are easy to tell apart. Structure survives in high dimensions, as long as it is bigger than the noise.

So what matters is not the number of columns. It is the number of directions in which the data really vary, and the noise that each useless column adds.

Can another distance help? Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim, in 2001, found that the Manhattan distance keeps more contrast than the straight line. We see a hint of it here: with a hundred columns, the relative gap between farthest and nearest is point five five for Manhattan and point four three for the straight line. With a thousand columns, point one five against point one two. A small gain. And a later study, by François, Wertz and Verleysen in 2007, found that going below p equals one does not always help. So there is no rule that smaller p is better.

One more symptom. In 2010, a study of hubs showed that some points become the neighbour of almost everyone. We tried it. List the ten nearest neighbours of each of the thousand points. With three columns, the most popular point appears in eighteen lists. With a thousand columns, one point appears in one hundred forty-three lists.

The cost, at least, is no surprise. The straight line still takes one subtraction, one square and one addition per column. A thousand columns means a thousand of each, for every pair. The computer does not mind. The trouble is not what the distance costs. It is what the distance means: an answer comes out, but it hardly separates one point from another.

So here is what to do. Before you trust a nearest neighbour, draw the histogram of your distances. If it is a narrow spike, the neighbours are not telling you much. Count the directions where your data really vary, not the columns. And remember that the cure is rarely a new distance. It is fewer, better columns.

The trap of many dimensions is that a ruler still gives a number, and the number still looks like a distance. It only stops telling you who is close.

Which units are yours?

Writing the same forty heights in centimetres instead of metres changes the nearest neighbour of thirty-nine clients, pure arithmetic. With two hundred random points, farthest over nearest falls from thirty-nine to one point one as columns grow. Chapter two closes with three questions: which units, whether one big gap counts more than many small ones, and how many directions carry something real.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Open a table of numbers. Pick a distance. Ask who is close to whom. You just answered three questions, and you probably answered none of them on purpose.

This is the last episode of the chapter, so it is about ideas. First we keep what is established. Then we try things out. At the end, the two lists stand side by side, and they never mix.

Start with what is established. Units.

Here are forty clients, simulated with seed twenty-seven. Each one has a height and a weight. Measure the height in metres, and the weight in kilograms. Find the nearest neighbour of every client. Now write the same heights in centimetres. Same people, same tape measure, one new unit.

Thirty-nine of the forty clients get a new nearest neighbour. Nobody moved. The weight used to decide almost everything, because metres are small numbers. In centimetres, the height shouts. That is arithmetic, not opinion. It is the episode two point one.

Second thing established: many columns. Take two hundred random points, with no structure at all. With two columns, the farthest point is about thirty-nine times as far as the nearest one. With a thousand columns, it is only one point one times as far. The histogram of all the distances, which was wide, becomes a narrow spike. Everyone is at about the same distance from everyone.

Beyer and his colleagues proved in nineteen ninety-nine that, under broad conditions, this happens as the number of columns grows. They also noted that some data escape it. Look at what escapes: a thousand columns again, but two groups hide inside. The histogram has two peaks. Distances inside a group are short, and distances between groups are long. The structure survives. Only the columns that carry nothing wash out.

Third thing established, from brain imaging. A pattern of activity is a vector, one number per voxel. Noise in neighbouring voxels is not independent. Walther and colleagues, in twenty sixteen, compared several ways to measure how different two patterns are. Taking the shape of that noise into account made the distances more reliable. Their paper prefers a cross-validated Mahalanobis distance.

That is what is established. Now come the suggestions. They are not results.

Suggestion one. Before any clustering, draw the histogram of your own distances. One narrow spike means that your neighbours may mean very little. Two peaks mean there is something to find. It costs one line of code.

Suggestion two. Redo one neighbour search with three distances: straight line, Manhattan, and cosine. Count how many of the five nearest neighbours stay the same. On a simulated table of a hundred rows and eight columns of mixed scales, straight line and Manhattan share about four of five. Cosine shares only three of five with either. Your own table will give other numbers, and those numbers are the point. This is a measurement you run on your data. It is not a rule.

Suggestion three. Say you have sixty-four correlated channels, and forty observations. The Mahalanobis distance needs the shape of the cloud, which is two thousand and eighty numbers. With forty observations, the estimated shape has rank thirty-nine, out of sixty-four. It cannot be inverted. One way out is to pull the estimate a little toward a round cloud. Ledoit and Wolf, in two thousand and four, give a formula for how much. Here it pulls about ten percent, and the smallest eigenvalue, which was zero, becomes zero point three eight. The distance exists again. Whether it helps your data, you have to check.

Suggestion four. Count data. Three sites, three species. Site two and site three share no species at all, yet the straight line puts them closest, at one point seven three. Sites one and two, which share the same species, are at seven point six two. Bray and Curtis, in nineteen fifty-seven, divide the sum of the differences by the sum of the totals. Sites two and three get one, the maximum. Sites one and two get zero point seven one. Try it, and look at the pairs that mostly share zeros.

Now the climax. For vectors, choosing a distance means answering three questions.

One. In which units are my columns? Because the biggest numbers decide.

Two. Should one big gap count more than many small ones? Because that is what the straight line, the sum and the maximum each decide.

Three. How many directions carry something real? Because the other columns only add noise. Write your three answers down before you run anything.

Now the two lists, side by side. In the left column, a calculation, a theorem, a measurement. In the right column, four things to try on your own data. Keep them apart. A habit is not a finding.

Chapter two began with a tape measure and three clients. It ends in the same workshop. Straight line, city blocks, direction only, the cloud, counts, many dimensions. Each one was a different answer to those three questions.
