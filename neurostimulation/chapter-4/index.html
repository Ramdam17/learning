tDCS

tDCS passes a weak constant current between two saline pads, like a slow canal between two ponds, and tilts the neurons on its path rather than firing them. The dose is set by intensity, pad size, duration and montage, and the second pad is never neutral. Motor after-effects are real on average but vary between people, while cognitive and clinical effects remain debated.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the greenhouse. Chapter four is about passing a weak electric current through the head, with nothing more than two patches on the scalp. We start with the oldest and simplest version: transcranial direct current stimulation, or tDCS. Remember the lesson of episode two point two. At these doses, the current does not fire neurons. It tilts them.

First, the principle, in one image. Picture a long garden with a pond at each end, joined by a slow canal. The water level is higher in one pond than in the other, so water creeps along the canal, always in the same direction, at a steady rate. That is the current. The ponds are the electrodes, and the plants growing along the canal are the neurons. The flow does not uproot anything. At most, the reeds lean a little with the stream.

In a head, the two ponds are two pads, soaked in saline and held against the scalp. One is the anode, where the current enters. The other is the cathode, where it leaves. The current is constant. It rises over a few tens of seconds, stays level for the whole session, and falls again. Under the pads, a reed that leans towards its threshold is nudged closer to firing, and a reed that leans away is nudged further from it. Which one it is depends on the direction of the neuron, as we saw in episode two point three.

Second, what is actually stimulated. Not a point. The current spreads through the scalp, the skull and the fluid, as in episode two point one, and reaches large regions between and under the pads. It also reaches the skin and the nerves of the scalp, and that is why people feel tingling or itching at the start. The latest safety guideline, from Antal and colleagues, counts more than three hundred thousand sessions of low-intensity electrical stimulation with no serious adverse event. Mild effects are common, and they appear with placebo stimulation too.

Third, the parameters. A tDCS protocol fits on the dose card. First, intensity. Studies usually use one to two milliamps. Second, electrode size. What matters at the skin is the current density, the current divided by the area. One milliamp through a pad of seven by five centimetres is about three hundredths of a milliamp per square centimetre. Third, duration. Single sessions typically last ten to thirty minutes. Fourth, the ramps at both ends. Fifth, the number of sessions. And sixth, the montage: where the two pads go.

Here is a real protocol, from the summary of the clinical trial behind a home-use device approved in the United States for depression. Two milliamps. Two round pads, five centimetres across, which works out at about a tenth of a milliamp per square centimetre. Thirty minutes a session. Five sessions a week for three weeks, then up to three a week for seven more weeks.

Montage can be refined. In high-definition tDCS, one small central electrode is surrounded by a ring of four return electrodes. In a head model, Datta and colleagues compared this ring with two large rectangular pads. With the pads, the field was diffuse, and its peak was not directly under a pad. With the ring, the field was more focal, with its peak under the central electrode. That is a model, not a measurement.

Fourth, what we know, and what is still debated. In 2000, Nitsche and Paulus applied weak direct current over the motor cortex, and measured the response to magnetic pulses. Motor excitability changed by up to forty percent, for several minutes after the current stopped. In 2001, they reported an increase of about a hundred and fifty percent, lasting up to ninety minutes after the end of stimulation. That is where the idea of a lasting after-effect comes from.

But how reliable is it? In 2016, Strube and colleagues tested fifty-nine healthy volunteers, with one milliamp of anodal stimulation for thirteen minutes. On average, excitability rose for forty minutes. Yet the people split into two groups: some went up, some went down. And on average, the cathode changed nothing. Beyond the motor cortex, the picture is weaker. In 2015, Horvath and colleagues pooled the single-session studies on healthy adults. In fifty-nine analyses of cognitive tasks, not one showed a significant effect.

Clinical use is where the debate has moved. In 2021, an expert panel led by Fregni gave tDCS its highest level of recommendation for depression. In 2025, the United States regulator approved a home-use device, on the strength of a ten-week trial. The treated group did better than sham by about two points on the main depression scale. But the regulator also noted a moderate level of uncertainty about the benefit, partly because of unblinding. At the end of the trial, seventy-eight percent of the active group guessed correctly that they had real stimulation, against forty-one percent of the sham group guessing sham. We come back to that in chapter nine.

Now the status stamp. As of November 2026. In the United States, the regulator approved a home-use tDCS device, for adults with moderate to severe major depressive disorder, alone or added to treatment, not for treatment-resistant depression. In Canada, the regulator lists active class two licences for tDCS stimulators, but its public record states no indication, so a depression indication in Canada is not confirmed. In Europe, one manufacturer states a C E mark under the medical device regulation, class two A, for adults with major depressive disorder. Evidence levels are not regulatory status, so they stay outside the stamp. This is not medical advice.

Here is the classic trap. A study says: we stimulated the left prefrontal cortex. The report names only the active electrode. But the current needs a return path, and the second pad is never neutral. The current passes under it too, and it can have effects of its own. In 2007, Nitsche and colleagues showed it. Making the reference pad larger spread its current thinly, and made stimulation under it functionally inefficient, without weakening the effect under the motor electrode. So a montage has two pads, and both belong in the methods, with their positions, their sizes and their polarity.

To sum up. tDCS pushes a weak, constant current through two pads, and tilts the neurons on its path. It reaches large regions, and the skin too. Its dose is set by intensity, pad size, duration, and montage. The motor after-effects are real on average, but vary between people. The cognitive and clinical effects are still debated. And a montage is never described by one electrode alone.

Next: alternating the current. Can a rhythm entrain a rhythm? Episode four point two, tACS.

PLATE 4.27:59

tACS

tACS swings the current like a rhythm, as a rocked beam catches hanging pots whose own swing is close to its pace; this catching is entrainment. In macaques a fraction of neurons lock to the current, more at higher doses. Skin, retina and peripheral nerves may carry part of the effect, and EEG recorded during stimulation is swamped by artefact at the same frequency.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the greenhouse. Last time the current was steady, and it leaned the neurons one way. This time we make the current swing, back and forth, like a rhythm. That is transcranial alternating current stimulation, or tACS. The question of this episode is simple. Can a rhythm from outside set the pace of a rhythm inside?

First, the principle, in one image. Picture a long beam under the roof of the greenhouse. Flowerpots hang from it on strings of different lengths. Each pot has its own natural swing: short strings swing fast, long strings swing slowly. Now rock the beam gently, at a steady pace. A pot whose own swing is close to that pace is caught. It starts to swing in step with the beam, locked to it, at a steady offset. A pot with a very different string hardly moves. And if you rock harder, the beam catches pots whose swing is further from its pace. That catching is entrainment. The beam is the current. The pots are neural populations, each with its own rhythm: theta, alpha, beta, gamma. It is a picture, not a model. Real neurons are noisy, not perfect pendulums.

In a head, the current is a sine wave. It flows from one pad to the other, then back, many times per second. Episode two point two gave us the size of the effect: at these doses, the field in the brain is at most about one volt per metre, and it moves the membrane by a fraction of a millivolt. With alternating current, that tiny tilt does not stay on one side. It leans one way, then the other, at the chosen frequency. A neuron close to threshold becomes a little more likely to fire at one moment of the cycle, and a little less at the opposite one. The spikes do not stop being theirs. They are only nudged in time.

Second, what is stimulated. The neurons under the current path, yes. But also the skin and the nerves of the scalp, which can feel the current and send a rhythmic signal of their own to the brain. And the retina. In 2008, Kanai and colleagues applied alternating current over the back of the head, and volunteers saw flickering lights, called phosphenes. In a lit room, the effect was strongest in the beta range. In the dark, it shifted to the alpha range. They read this as stimulation of the visual cortex. But Schutter and Hortensius found, in 2010, that a montage with an electrode on the forehead gave similar phosphenes, and stronger ones. They concluded that current spreading through the scalp was reaching the retina, and that this should be taken into account when studying the cortex.

Third, the parameters. First, frequency, usually chosen to match a brain rhythm. Second, amplitude. The studies in this episode mostly use between half a milliamp and two milliamps. Check how it is written: peak to baseline, or peak to peak. The second is twice the first. Third, phase. A turning arrow, as in the EEG series, shows where in its cycle each electrode is. With two sites, you can drive them in step, or out of step. In 2012, Polania and colleagues applied six hertz alternating current over the left frontal and parietal cortex, while volunteers did a memory-matching task. In step, reactions were faster than with placebo. Out of step, they were slower. Then the montage, and its two pads, as in episode four point one. And last, duration.

Fourth, what we know, and what is still debated. In animals, entrainment is shown. In 2020, Johnson and colleagues recorded single neurons in awake macaques, during ten hertz stimulation. The fields at the neurons were about four tenths to one and a third volts per metre. At half a milliamp, about nine percent of neurons locked onto the current. At one milliamp, about eighteen percent. At one and a half, more than a quarter. Twelve of the thirty-four neurons locked at some intensity. So the effect depends on dose, and no more than a fraction of cells follow. In 2019, Krause and colleagues, also in macaques, found that the current changes the timing of spikes, not their rate, and also in deep structures. Their control experiments could not attribute it to sensory stimulation, though other groups published a commentary disputing the interpretation.

In humans, in 2014, Helfrich and colleagues applied ten hertz current over the back of the head, while recording EEG. They reported more alpha activity, and target detection that depended on the phase of the current. After the current stops, alpha can stay higher for a long time: in 2016, Kasten and colleagues still found it seventy minutes later. But in 2015, Vossen and colleagues argued that such lasting changes are more likely plastic changes than a leftover echo of entrainment.

Now the debate on the route. In 2018, Voroslakos and colleagues found that in rats, at least one volt per metre was needed to change spiking. They estimated that in humans, conventional pads would need about four to six milliamps to reliably do it. In 2017, Lafon and colleagues stimulated thirteen patients with implanted electrodes, at low frequency, and found no stable entrainment. They blamed weak fields. And in 2019, Asamoah and colleagues went after the skin. In twelve healthy volunteers, a numbing cream on the scalp reduced the entrainment of a hand tremor. In another experiment with twelve volunteers, stimulation of the arm alone still entrained the tremor. They proposed that, at least for the motor system, tACS works through peripheral nerves. They did not test other functions. So there are three readings. The field acts directly on neurons. The field acts through the skin or the retina. Or the effect is too weak for the claims made. Which fits depends on the study, the dose and the target.

Now the status stamp. As of November 2026. The regulators' own databases were searched. In Canada, the active licence listing holds no device named for tACS or alternating current stimulation. In the United States, the device databases list no clearance or approval under a tACS name. So no tACS indication was found. In Europe, makers of stimulators able to deliver tACS state research use, and I could not verify a tACS indication. The home tDCS approval of episode four point one does not extend to tACS. This is not medical advice.

Here is the classic trap. You want to see entrainment, so you record EEG during the stimulation. But the current leaks into the recording, at the very frequency you are looking for, and far stronger than the brain signal. It is not a simple copy, either. Noury and colleagues showed in 2016 that heartbeat and breathing modulate the artefact in a non-linear way. So the usual clean-up leaves a residue, and a residue can look like entrainment. Helfrich's team built a method to separate the two, and it is a hard problem. Measuring before and after is easier, but it says nothing about what happens during. Episode ten point three comes back to this.

To sum up. tACS passes a weak, alternating current, at a frequency chosen to match a brain rhythm. In animals, a fraction of neurons lock onto it, more at higher doses. In humans, frequency, phase and montage matter. Skin, retina and nerves may carry part of the effect. No tACS indication was found in the regulators' databases. And EEG recorded during stimulation is full of artefact at the same frequency.

Next: noise instead of rhythm. What if the current is random? Episode four point three, tRNS.

PLATE 4.36:53

tRNS

tRNS delivers a random current across a wide band, a mist rather than a stream or a rhythm. The idea behind it is stochastic resonance: a marble in a shaken tray follows a weak tilt over its ridge only at the right amount of shaking. One visual study fits that inverted U, but a replication found nothing, and cognitive gains in healthy adults are small.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the greenhouse. In episode four point one the current was a steady stream. In episode four point two it was a rhythm. Now a third way to water the plants: not a stream, not a rhythm, but a mist. A current that is random, spread over a wide band of frequencies. That is transcranial random noise stimulation, or tRNS. The question of this episode sounds odd. Can adding noise help a weak signal get through?

First, the principle, in one image. Picture a seed tray from the greenhouse, with a marble resting in one hollow. A low ridge separates it from the next hollow. That ridge is the firing threshold. Now tilt the tray gently, back and forth, very slowly. That tilt is a weak signal. It is too small. The marble rocks, but it never crosses the ridge. Next, shake the tray a little, at random. Now and then the marble hops over, and it tends to hop when the tilt already leans its way. Its hops now follow the weak signal. Shake too little, and nothing happens. Shake too hard, and the marble hops everywhere, so the signal is lost. This curve, with a best amount of noise in the middle, is called stochastic resonance. The tray is a picture, not a model. A neuron is not a marble.

Second, what is stimulated. The same things as the other tES techniques. Two pads on the scalp, a current through the head, and a field in the cortex. In ten epilepsy patients, Huang and colleagues measured cortical fields of about eight tenths of a volt per metre at two milliamps. That is the range we met in episode two point two. The current is random instead of steady, but the field is just as small. And as with tACS, the skin and the nerves of the scalp feel the current too.

Third, the parameters. Terney and colleagues introduced tRNS in 2008. They stimulated the motor cortex of healthy volunteers with a random current between zero point one and six hundred and forty hertz, at one milliamp, for ten minutes, with no direct current offset. Then they split the band. The low part, up to about one hundred hertz, did nothing. The high part, from about one hundred to six hundred and forty hertz, raised the motor evoked potentials by roughly twenty to fifty percent, and the rise lasted about an hour. Since then, most studies use the high band, usually at one to two milliamps. A few add a direct current offset on purpose. Then come the montage and the duration, as always. And check how the amplitude is quoted. One recent study states one and a half milliamps, crest to trough.

Fourth, what we know, and what is debated. First, comfort. In 2010, Ambrus and colleagues found that half of the volunteers felt tDCS on the skin at four hundred microamps. For tRNS it took twelve hundred. So noise is often less noticeable. That helps blinding, but it does not guarantee it.

Now the mechanism. Terney's team suggested that the noise repeatedly opens sodium channels. That is a hypothesis. In 2015, Chaieb and colleagues tested it in a pilot with eight volunteers. A benzodiazepine, lorazepam, suppressed the rise. Drugs acting on NMDA receptors did not. The sodium channel blocker only showed a trend. Remember the membrane filter of episode two point five: fast components are strongly attenuated. So how a field at hundreds of hertz acts on neurons is not obvious.

Now the moment that gives this technique its name. In 2016, van der Groen and Wenderoth applied noise between one hundred and six hundred and forty hertz over the visual cortex, from zero up to one and a half milliamps, while volunteers detected faint images. Fifty-two people took part, across three experiments. For faint images, accuracy followed an inverted U, with a best level most often near one milliamp. At that level, detection was about ten percent better than with no noise. For clearly visible images, there was little or no gain. Stimulating the forehead gave no gain. And the best level differed from person to person. That is the signature of stochastic resonance. But nobody recorded the neurons, so the cortical origin is inferred from behaviour. It is a hypothesis that fits, not a proof.

And replication is mixed. In 2025, Edwards and colleagues ran a registered report. They tried to reproduce a benefit of tRNS on motion perception, in forty-two volunteers, at one and a half milliamps. They found none, neither against sham nor against forehead stimulation. A different task, so it does not cancel the visual result. But it shows the effects are not robust. For cognition in healthy adults, a 2026 meta-analysis pooled thirteen trials and four hundred and three people. The effect was small and not significant: a Hedges g of zero point one seven, with an interval from minus zero point zero six to zero point four. So, depending on the study, the answer is yes, no, or not clear.

Now the status stamp. As of November 2026. In Canada, the active licence listing holds no device named for tRNS or random noise stimulation. In the United States, the clearance and approval databases hold no device named for tRNS. So no tRNS indication was found. In Europe, some makers describe tRNS-capable stimulators as research instruments, and sources disagree on their CE status. Since December 2022, brain stimulation equipment without a medical purpose is class three under the medical device regulation. Research use only. No licensed indication. This is not medical advice.

Here is the classic trap. If stochastic resonance is real, there is a best noise level, and it differs between people. So a fixed dose for everyone sits on the wrong side of the hump for some. The same current could help one person and hinder another, and the group average hides both. A flat average does not mean nothing happened. It may mean the effects cancelled.

To sum up. tRNS is a random current, usually between one hundred and six hundred and forty hertz. In the motor cortex, one milliamp for ten minutes raised excitability for about an hour, in 2008. The sodium channel mechanism is a hypothesis. Stochastic resonance fits a visual detection study, but it is not proven, and replications are mixed. In healthy adults, cognitive gains are small and not significant. No tRNS indication was found.

Next: what if two currents cross deep in the head? Episode four point four, temporal interference.

Temporal interference

Temporal interference sends two kilohertz currents, which neurons ignore, through two electrode pairs so that they add into a slow envelope where the fields are comparable and aligned, like two sprinklers slightly out of tune. In mice it reached the hippocampus while sparing the cortex above. In human heads the envelope is a fraction of a volt per metre and effects are small.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the greenhouse. Every tES technique so far has the same weakness. The current is strongest under the electrodes, and it fades as it goes deeper. So how could you reach a plant in the middle of the bed, without soaking the ones at the edge? This episode is about one answer, called temporal interference, or TI.

First, the principle, in one image. Picture two sprinklers, one on each side of the bed. Each one pulses very fast, thousands of times a second. The plants cannot follow pulses that fast, so they ignore them. But the two sprinklers are slightly out of tune. In the middle of the bed, where both jets arrive, their pulses drift in and out of step. Sometimes they land together, and sometimes they cancel. So the water there swells and fades slowly. That slow swell is something a plant can follow. The sprinklers are a picture, not a model.

In episode two point five, we saw why. A membrane acts as a low-pass filter, and a neuron barely responds to a current of a few kilohertz. Two such currents, a few hertz apart, add up to a wave with a slow envelope. And we saw the warning. A passive filter alone cannot turn that envelope into a signal. Something else must, probably ion channels that rectify.

Second, what is stimulated, and why the middle. Each pair of electrodes makes its own field. Near a pair, its own field is strong and the other pair's field is weak. The envelope can be at most twice the weaker of the two fields, and only if both point along the same direction as the cell. So the envelope is small near the electrodes, even though the carrier is huge there. It is largest where the two fields are comparable and aligned. That can be deep. In principle, that is the target. In practice, the tissue on the way, which carries both fields at full strength, is also exposed.

Third, the parameters. The two frequencies, a few kilohertz each. Their difference, which sets the rhythm of the envelope. The current in each pair. The ratio between the two currents, which slides the peak toward the weaker pair. And where the electrodes sit.

Here are real examples. In two thousand and seventeen, Grossman and colleagues worked in anaesthetised mice, with electrodes on the skull. They used two thousand and two thousand and ten hertz, so the envelope beat ten times a second. In the cell-staining experiments, each electrode carried one hundred twenty-five microamps. With temporal interference, about half of the hippocampal cells showed the activity marker, and almost none in the cortex above. A two-kilohertz field alone activated almost nothing. In a second experiment, the authors changed the ratio of the two currents, and the evoked movement shifted from one body part to another. The carrier mattered too. The current needed to evoke a movement rose by about two hundred fifty microamps for each kilohertz, between one and four kilohertz. At five kilohertz, there was no response up to two milliamps.

Now the human head, which is larger. In two thousand and nineteen, Rampersad and colleagues simulated it. With two milliamps in total, the best peak of the envelope was about eight tenths of a volt per metre. Suprathreshold stimulation of cortical neurons is estimated at tens of volts per metre. They estimated that reaching twenty-eight volts per metre in the motor cortex would take about thirty-eight milliamps per pair. They concluded that temporal interference does not look viable as a noninvasive replacement for deep brain stimulation, though a small modulation at depth is possible. Their mouse model, which left out the skin, gave a peak of hundreds of volts per metre. A mouse head is small, so the mouse result does not scale.

Even so, human studies followed, and they used more current, because kilohertz currents feel weaker on the skin. The sensation threshold was about two milliamps, against about four tenths of a milliamp for ordinary alternating current. In two thousand and twenty-three, Violante and colleagues used two thousand and two thousand and five hertz, a five-hertz envelope, and two pairs of small pads on the head. In the main condition each pair carried two milliamps. In the other, one and three. In one perfused cadaver, at one milliamp, the envelope was about seventy-five percent larger in the hippocampus than in the cortex above, though the carrier was about fifty percent higher in the cortex. In twenty volunteers, the one-to-three ratio reduced hippocampal activity during memory encoding. In another twenty-one, the same ratio raised the odds of a correct answer by about twelve percent. A small effect, in a modest sample.

Also in two thousand and twenty-three, Wessel and colleagues targeted the striatum. They used four electrodes and two milliamps per channel. The envelope was shaped as theta bursts. Putamen activity rose, and fifteen older adults performed a motor task better, with larger gains. They write that a contribution from the carrier cannot be fully excluded. In two thousand and twenty-four, Beanato and colleagues reported a hippocampal and entorhinal study, with a navigation task. A systematic review published in two thousand and twenty-five, searching up to December two thousand and twenty-four, counted twenty published studies and eight hundred twenty participants. Side effects were mild, mostly tingling and itching. The reviewers asked for well-controlled larger trials.

So what is debated? First, the mechanism. Mirzakhalili and colleagues, in two thousand and twenty, showed in simulations that rectifying channels can produce the effect, and can also block conduction in tissue that was not the target. In two thousand and twenty-one, Esmaeilpour and colleagues recorded slices of rat hippocampus. Their model suggested that the envelope is stronger deep inside, while the unmodulated field peaks at the cortex. In two thousand and twenty-four, Caldas-Martinez and colleagues, in mouse cortical tissue, found that isolated pyramidal neurons stopped responding. They propose that it is a network effect, and that inhibitory cells may suppress shallow pyramidal cells, leaving the deep ones free. Second, focality. Von Conta and colleagues, in two thousand and twenty-one, modelled individual heads. The field in the target varied between people, and they argued for individual montages.

Here is the classic trap. Showing the envelope map alone. In the simulation of Violante and colleagues, the carrier in the hippocampus was about the same as under the anterior electrode in the cortex. The envelope is the part that the neuron follows, but the carrier fills the tissue on the way, at a high amplitude. A fair figure shows both.

As for the status. No licensed or cleared device for temporal interference was found in Canada, the United States or Europe. It is a research technique.

To sum up. Two kilohertz currents that neurons ignore make a slow envelope where they are comparable and aligned. In mice, that reached the hippocampus. In human heads, the envelope is a fraction of a volt per metre, the carriers are everywhere, and the effects are small. The question is still open.

Next: how much actually reaches the cortex? The fields that were measured inside human heads.

PLATE 4.58:42

How much actually reaches the cortex?

Fields measured inside the heads of epilepsy patients put the cortical field at a few tenths of a volt per metre at two milliamps, falling with depth; models match only after tuning. Neurons in slices and animals respond from roughly two tenths to one volt per metre, so human doses sit at the edge. Three readings remain defended: small but direct, peripheral, or too weak.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last stop in the greenhouse. Earlier in this chapter we pressed two pads on a head and sent in a weak current. Episode two point two gave us the rule of thumb: a field of a few tenths of a volt per metre moves the membrane by a fraction of a millivolt. This episode asks a plain question. How much field actually reaches the cortex? And how does it compare with what neurons need?

On the potting bench there is a gardener's balance. A beam, two pans, and a pivot in the middle. The gardener uses it to check one thing against another. We will use it the same way. On one pan, the fields that models predict. On the other, the fields that someone has measured. If the beam stays level, we can trust the models. If it tips, we cannot.

So first, how do you measure a field inside a living human head? You need electrodes inside the head. The only people who have them are patients with epilepsy, who are implanted for a few days so that surgeons can locate the source of their seizures. Researchers ask for permission, and apply weak stimulation through the scalp, and record the voltage between contacts. Divide the voltage difference by the distance between two contacts, and you get a field. That method brings three caveats. The patients are not a random sample of people. Their brains may differ from healthy brains, and the implants themselves change how current flows. And contacts record the field only along the direction of the electrode.

Here is what has been measured. In 2016, Opitz and colleagues recorded in two patients, and in two monkeys. Scaled to one milliamp, the strongest field along an electrode was about zero point three six volts per metre in one patient, and zero point one six in the other. The fields were strongest in superficial regions. In 2017, Huang and colleagues recorded in ten patients, with more than a thousand contacts, on the surface and in depth. At two milliamps, the cortical field reached about zero point eight volts per metre. That is the number from episode two point two, and it is the top of the range, not the average.

Their models are the first pan of the balance. Huang's team fed each patient's anatomy into a model, and compared the predicted field with the recorded one. Prediction and measurement were strongly correlated, with coefficients between zero point eight and zero point nine. But there is a catch. The models only matched after the tissue conductivities were adjusted, away from the values in the literature. So the beam is nearly level, and the models were tuned to get it there. That is a calibration. It is not independent proof.

Now the cadavers. In 2018, Vöröslakos and colleagues measured the field in eleven human cadavers, with contacts reaching three to seven centimetres deep. With current applied through the intact scalp, the field was about one eighth of a volt per metre for each milliamp. Two milliamps would give roughly a quarter of a volt per metre. That is the same order of magnitude as the living patients. But the caveat is real. Tissue changes after death, and the conductivities of a cadaver need not match those of a living head. So cadavers show what is possible. They do not replace measurements in the living.

What happens deeper? Fields fall with depth. In Huang's data, the field was strongest at the cortical surface and generally weaker below it. In 2022, Louviot and colleagues measured deep structures in eight patients, using small electrodes and one milliamp. The mean field was about zero point two one volts per metre in the amygdala, zero point one seven in the hippocampus, and zero point zero seven in the cingulate gyrus. So at one milliamp, deep fields are a few tenths of a volt per metre, or less. Montage matters: current shunted through the cerebrospinal fluid can sometimes keep deep fields comparable to cortical ones. In these data, deep fields are generally weaker than cortical ones. Models fill the gaps between contacts, and chapter seven shows how they are built.

Now the other side of the question. What does a neuron need? Here is a log scale, in volts per metre. In rat hippocampal slices, Reato and colleagues reported in 2010 that spikes locked to the phase of a field as weak as zero point two volts per metre, in a slice with an ongoing gamma rhythm. In the ferret slices of episode two point two, about half a volt per metre nudged a rhythm. In living rats, Özen and colleagues reported in 2010 that spikes were biased toward one phase of the stimulation at fields as low as about one volt per metre. And Vöröslakos found in 2018 that at least one volt per metre was needed to affect spiking.

Look at the scale. The measured human fields, from about zero point one to eight tenths, sit at the bottom of the animal range, or just below it. Not far below: within about one order of magnitude. That is why the debate is so sharp.

Be careful with this comparison. A slice has no skull and no skin. An anaesthetised rat has a different brain state from a person reading. And these thresholds describe what changed in the animal: spike timing, not behaviour. Nor is a rat's head a human head. In a 2019 modelling study, Alekseichuk and colleagues modelled the same stimulation in a mouse, monkeys and a human. The field fell sharply as the head grew, by up to a hundredfold between species. So compare fields, never milliamps.

This is where the literature splits into three positions. Each has named authors, and none is a straw man.

First position: the direct effect exists, but it is small. In 2019, Krause and colleagues recorded single neurons in macaques during alternating current. Spike timing changed, even in deep structures, and their control experiments argued against a sensory explanation. In 2020, Johnson and colleagues saw dose-dependent entrainment in macaques, at intensities that they judged feasible in humans. Huang's measurements fit this reading: the human cortex does receive fields at the lower edge of what works in animals.

Second position: part of the effect travels by another road. The current also crosses the skin, the retina and the cranial nerves, and these can carry signals to the brain. In 2019, Asamoah, Khatoun and McLaughlin showed, in rats and in humans, that stimulating peripheral nerves can cause tACS effects on the motor system. They noted that the question remains open for other functions. A 2018 review, led by Liu, called for indirect effects to be investigated in more detail.

Third position: the field is too weak for the claims made. Vöröslakos extrapolated from the cadavers that about six milliamps on the scalp would be needed to reach one volt per metre. In 2017, Lafon and colleagues stimulated epilepsy patients at low frequency, and found no stable entrainment of sleep spindles or theta rhythms. They pointed to the weak fields at the cortical surface.

These positions are not exclusive. A small direct effect can coexist with a peripheral one. Which weighs most depends on the study, the dose, and the target. And the human measurements mostly tell us the field. They do not tell us what neurons did with it.

Now the classic trap. A paper says: we stimulated at two milliamps, as in a study that worked in monkeys. But the milliamp is not the dose. The field at the target is. The same current gives a different field in a mouse, a macaque and a human, and in two humans with different skulls. Report the field if you can measure it, or the model that estimates it, and say which one it is.

To sum up. Measurements in patients and cadavers put the field at the human cortex, at two milliamps, at a few tenths of a volt per metre, and at most about eight tenths. It falls with depth. Neurons in slices and animals respond from about two tenths to one volt per metre. So the human dose sits at the edge of that range, not clearly inside it. The literature offers three readings: small but direct, partly peripheral, or too weak. All three are still defended, with data.
