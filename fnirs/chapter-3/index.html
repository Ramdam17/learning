Designing your montage

A montage starts from the question: tapping the right hand targets the left motor cortex. The 10-20 system, with its proportional grid, locates positions on any head, though scalp positions only approximate cortex. Sources and detectors alternate about 3 cm apart, with a mirror patch and short channels, and sensitivity tools such as AtlasViewer and fOLD show what the montage actually sees.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 3, Everything You Decide Before Pressing Record. And the first decision is the one you can never fix afterwards, where the optodes go. A channel in the wrong place stays in the wrong place, whatever you do to the data later. That plan is called your montage.

Start from your question, not from the cap. Your hypothesis points to a region of interest. Take our running example, tapping the fingers of the right hand. That should activate the hand area of the left motor cortex, with help from neighboring premotor and supplementary motor areas. So that's our target.

Next problem, you can't see the cortex. You only see a head. To get from one to the other, fNIRS borrows a tool from EEG, the 10-20 system introduced by Herbert Jasper in 1958. Find four landmarks, the nasion, the dip at the top of the nose, the inion, the bump at the back of the skull, and the two points just in front of the ears. Measure the distances between them, and place positions at 10 and 20% steps along those lines. Because the steps are proportional, the grid stretches to fit any head. Each position gets a name, a letter for the region like F for frontal and C for central, odd numbers on the left, even on the right, and Z on the midline. So C3 sits over the left central region, roughly above the hand area of motor cortex. Need finer steps? The 10-10 system offers about 80 positions, and the 10-5 system more than 300.

How good is that scalp-to-cortex link? In 2004, Okamoto and colleagues used MRI to project the 10-20 positions onto the cortex of 17 adults. On average, the projected points varied by around 8 millimeters between people. Useful but not a GPS. Brains differ and a scalp position is a good guess, not a guarantee.

Now place your optodes. Sources and detectors alternate like a checkerboard so that every neighboring source and detector pair forms a channel about 3 centimeters apart in adults. Cover your region plus a margin because the activation might sit a bit off target, and heads are never placed perfectly. A mirror patch over the other hemisphere is often worth it. It gives you a comparison, and some tasks activate both sides anyway.

Then add short channels. A detector about 8mm from a source sees mostly the scalp. Ideally, every region gets at least one, and the closer they sit to your long channels, the better they can clean them later.

But here's the real question, what does your montage actually see? Each channel's banana covers a patch of cortex, and the size of that patch depends on the distances, the head, and the anatomy underneath. Photon simulations in realistic head models can compute this sensitivity, channel by channel, and paint it on the brain. Overlay it on the cortex and you see what your montage really lights up, and where the blind spots are between channels and in the folds.

You don't have to run those simulations yourself. AtlasViewer, an open-source tool from the Homer team, shows the sensitivity of a probe you designed on an Atlas head. In fOLD, the fNIRS Optodes' Location Decider, published by Zimeo Morais and colleagues in 2018, does the reverse. You pick the brain regions you care about, and it suggests positions on the 10-10 or 10-5 grid with the best anatomical specificity, based on photon simulations in two-head atlases.

Finally, the practical constraints. Your device has a fixed number of sources and detectors, so it's always a trade-off between covering a lot and covering well. Thick hair makes some positions harder. Cables add weight and can pull on the cap. And whatever you plan, measure each participant's head, choose the right cap size, center it carefully, and if you can, digitize where the optodes really ended up. We'll come back to that in Episode 3.4.

So, start from a question, find it on the head with the 10-20 system, alternate sources and detectors at about 3 cm, add short channels, and check what your montage really sees before you record a single participant. Next time, designing an experiment for a signal this slow.

PLATE 3.24:13

Designing for a slow signal

Block designs give strong signals; event-related designs resolve single events, and jittered intervals let overlapping responses be separated, and task rhythms near 10 seconds collide with Mayer waves. Some tasks fake activation through systemic changes, as speaking alters breathing and stress raises blood pressure. Defenses include matched control tasks, recording the body, reporting both hemoglobins, and piloting.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You have your montage. Now, what will your participant actually do? With a signal that takes 5 seconds to peak and 15 to fade, timing is everything. And some tasks will fool you completely. Let's design an experiment for a slow signal.

There are two classic recipes. The first is the block design, alternate periods of task and rest. Say 20 seconds of finger tapping, then 20 seconds of rest, again and again. The responses pile up into a big plateau, a strong, robust signal, and a simple analysis. The rest has to be long enough for the signal to come back down, so at least as long as the task, often 15 to 30 seconds. The downsides, people anticipate get bored and habituate, and you learn little about any single event.

The second recipe is the event-related design, short stimuli one at a time. In a slow event-related design, you wait 15 to 20 seconds after each event, so every response plays out completely. Clean but long. In a rapid event-related design, events come every few seconds. The responses overlap, and the general linear model pulls them apart later. But that only works if the intervals vary. That's called jitter. With perfectly regular spacing, overlapping responses blend into an almost constant signal, and the model can't tell them apart. Jitter also stops participants from predicting exactly when the next event comes.

Remember the Mayer waves around one cycle every 10 seconds. A task that repeats on a regular 10-second rhythm lands right on top of them. 20 seconds on, 20 seconds off sits lower, near 0.025 Hz. Varying block durations a little helps too.

How many trials? The response is small and the noise is large, so more is better. Many blocks per condition or dozens of events. But fatigue is real, especially for children and patients. There's no magic number, run a pilot and estimate your power. We'll come back to that in Chapter 6.

Then order. Randomize or counterbalance your conditions so that slow drifts, fatigue, or learning never line up with one condition. And define your rest carefully. Rest is never nothing. A fixation cross or passive viewing kept identical throughout gives you a stable baseline.

Now the traps tasks that create a beautiful activation, without any extra brain activity. Speaking is the classic one. When we talk, we change the way we breathe. Scholkmann and colleagues measured this in 2013 in 24 adults reciting texts. Their breathing lowered the carbon dioxide in their blood, and that, rather than extra neural activity, was mainly responsible for the changes in their fNIRS signals. Holding your breath or straining does the opposite and pushes carbon dioxide and blood pressure up. Standing up, leaning forward or bending down shifts blood around the body. And exciting or stressful tasks raise heart rate and blood pressure. Blood pressure changes have been measured during tasks as different as solving anagrams, watching visual stimuli, and playing video games. Tachtsidis and Scholkmann call the results false positives. Worse, these same effects can also hide real brain activity, false negatives.

So how do you defend yourself? First, match your conditions. A control task with the same speech, the same movement, and the same excitement, minus the cognitive ingredient you actually care about. Second, record the body, breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, and carbon dioxide if you can, plus short channels. Third, always look at both hemoglobins. Deoxyhemoglobin tends to be more robust to these systemic effects, so report both. And fourth, pilot, pilot, pilot.

So, blocks for strength, events for detail, jitter for overlap, stay away from 10-second rhythms, counterbalance, and watch out for tasks that change how people breathe, move, or feel. Next time, getting a good signal on a real head.

PLATE 3.33:57

Getting a good signal on a real head

Preparing a real head means choosing the right cap size, centering Cz, parting hair under each optode, and dimming ambient light. A clean heartbeat in both wavelengths signals good contact, and the scalp coupling index quantifies it. Dark, dense or coarse hair and dark skin lower signal quality and lead to exclusions, so extra preparation and honest reporting of hair and skin matter.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You have a montage and a design. Now a real person sits down in front of you with a real head and real hair. This is where many studies are won or lost. So let's prepare a head step by step.

First, the cap. Measure the head circumference and choose the matching cap size. Then center it. The central point Cz should sit halfway between the nasion and the inion and halfway between the ears. A cap that's a centimeter off shifts every channel by a centimeter. Check it's symmetric, snug, and won't slide when the participant moves.

Second, the hair. Light has to go from the optode into the skin, not into a tuft of hair. So under each optode, part the hair with a small blunt tool like a plastic stick or a cotton bud until you see the scalp, then gently wiggle the optode down. Dark and dense hair absorbs and scatters a lot of light, so it takes the most care. Hardware can help. In 2012, Khan and colleagues tested brush optodes, loose optical fibers that thread through the hair like the bristles of a brush. Across 17 people with different hair colors and densities, they improved the signal-to-noise ratio by up to a factor of 10 and cut setup time by a factor of 3. Newer designs, like small combs that clip onto the optodes, follow the same idea.

Third, the light around you. Your detectors are built to catch a handful of photons, so room light and especially sunlight are noise. Dim the lights, keep away from windows, and if needed, add a dark overcap on top.

Fourth and most important, look at the signal before you press record. The best friend you have is the heartbeat. If a channel shows a clean cardiac pulse at both wavelengths, the optode is touching the scalp. That idea became a number, the scalp coupling index introduced by Luca Pollonini and colleagues. It measures how well the heartbeat matches between the two wavelengths of a channel. Close to one is good. Many pipelines flag channels below about 0.7. Watch for the two extremes too. A channel with too little light is lost in noise and a channel with too much light saturates the detector. And fix bad channels now, Part the hair again, press the optode, adjust the gain. 5 minutes here saves weeks of analysis later.

Fifth, the person. Optodes press on the head. Tell participants what to expect, check in with them regularly and keep sessions reasonably short, especially with children. A comfortable participant moves less and comes back.

Now, an honest moment. fNIRS does not work equally well on every head. Melanin, the pigment in skin, absorbs near-infrared light. so more melanin means less light reaches the detector. In coarse, curly, dense hair is harder to get through, and caps don't always fit it. The result is lower signal quality, and too often, people simply excluded from studies. In 2023, a review by Jasmine Kwasa and colleagues found that demographic and phenotypic details are rarely reported in fNIRS research, and that people of African descent with these hair types tend to be underrepresented.

What can you do? Plan more preparation time. Talk with participants about their hair in advance. Hairstyles like braids arranged to leave the optode positions free can help. Use optodes or attachments designed to get through hair and adjust the light intensity and detector gain within safety limits. And report it, skin tone, hair type, how many channels and participants you excluded and why. A finding that only holds for some heads is not a finding about the human brain.

So, the right cap, well-seated, hair parted, lights down, heartbeat checked, participants comfortable, and every head given a fair chance. Next time, recording like you'll need it later.

PLATE 3.44:17

Recording like you'll need it later

Recording for the future means sampling around 10 samples per second, sending timestamped markers on one clock, and recording accelerometer, breathing, pulse and blood pressure alongside. Optode positions are digitized, and a few minutes of rest are recorded. Files are saved in SNIRF and organized with BIDS, raw data kept untouched, with metadata so another lab can reproduce the analysis.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Your cap is on, your channels look good, the heartbeat is there. Before you press record, picture someone opening your files in two years. Maybe a colleague. Maybe a reviewer. Most likely you, having forgotten everything. Let's record like we'll need it later.

First, the sampling rate. The brain response is slow, but you still want to see the heartbeat clearly. It tells you about signal quality and you'll need to remove it later. In infants, the heart can beat more than twice per second. So record fast enough, typically around 10 samples per second, and never throw samples away during acquisition. You can always slow down later. You can never speed up.

Second markers. Every stimulus, every response, every change of condition needs a timestamp sent from the stimulus computer to the fNIRS recording. And test that link before the study. How long is the delay? Is it the same every time? A hardware trigger cable or software made for synchronizing devices like the lab streaming layer keeps everything on one clock. Save the event list in a separate file as well with clear condition names. And keep a lab notebook, a sneeze, a slammed door, a participant who fell asleep. Write it down with the time.

Third record the body. An accelerometer on the head catches movement. A breathing belt follows respiration. A pulse sensor, and if you can, continuous blood pressure follow the circulation. And for tasks that involve speaking, measuring carbon dioxide in the breath tells you whether breathing changed. Put them all on the same clock as your fNIRS. And don't forget your short channels. In chapter 4, these signals become your best cleaning tools.

Fourth, where did the optodes actually land? Your montage was a plan, the real cap is never perfect. After placing it, digitize the optode positions together with the landmarks, nasion, and inion, the points in front of the ears. A magnetic digitizer pen, photogrammetry from a set of photos, or a 3D scanner can all do the job. With real positions, you can project each participant's channels onto the cortex, instead of assuming a perfect cap.

Fifth, a few minutes of rest. Before the task starts, record a resting period. It gives you a baseline, a quality check, and data you might want later, for example, to study connectivity.

Now the files. For years, every manufacturer had its own format, which made sharing painful. Today, there's a common one, SNIRF, the shared near-infrared spectroscopy format. It was developed with the fNIRS community and the Society for Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy. It's built on HDF5, a mature scientific file format, and it covers continuous wave, frequency domain, and time domain devices. Major hardware and software now read and write it.

One file is in our study, though. To organize everything, participants, sessions, events, positions, and descriptions, there's BIDS, the brain imaging data structure. It started with MRI, then grew to EEG, MEG, and more. And in 2025, its extension to NIRS was published in Scientific Data. A data set in BIDS can be understood by tools and by people who never met you, and shared on public repositories like OpenNeuro.

In whatever the format, keep the raw data untouched forever. Record the metadata, the device, the wavelengths, the source detector distances, the cap size, the head circumference, and the participant details from last episode, like skin tone and hair type. Processing can always be redone. A lost raw file can't.

All this pays off in Chapter 6. It's what lets another lab reproduce your result, or lets you redo your own analysis when a reviewer asks.

So, sample fast, mark every event on one clock, record the body, digitize positions, start with rest, and save everything in SNIRF and BIDS, raw and untouched. That's chapter 3, everything before you record. Next chapter, cleaning up. First question, should this channel even exist?
