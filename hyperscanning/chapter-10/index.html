Bodies in sync

Heart, breath and skin are three body signals on three clocks: a beat, a breath, a slow wave of sweat, recorded with ECG or PPG, a chest belt and finger electrodes. Each mixes autonomic state, effort and emotion. In a simulation, two hikers who never interact show heart rates correlating at 0.96 because of the shared slope; removing it leaves minus 0.12.

TRANSCRIPTEN

High in the mountains, Ava and Otto have just reached the hut. They climbed the same slope, at the same pace. Now they sit by the stove, and their hearts and their breath are still fast.

This chapter is about that moment. Brains are not the only thing two people can share. Bodies move together too. This episode is the overview: which body signals we can record, what each one reflects, how fast it changes, and which sensor picks it up.

Each hiker brings three small helpers. Cardio is the heart. Puff is the breath. Palmy is the skin of the hand.

Start with Cardio. The heart gives us a beat about once a second at rest. We record it with an electrocardiogram, from electrodes on the chest. Or with PPG, a small light sensor on a fingertip or an earlobe, which sees the pulse in the blood. Both follow the autonomic nervous system, the part of the body that speeds the heart up and slows it down. Both also follow effort.

Puff is the breath. It moves in a cycle of a few seconds. A stretch belt around the chest measures it. It is the one signal here that we can also control on purpose, by talking, singing, or holding our breath. And it pushes on the heart, which we meet in episode 10.2.

Palmy is the skin. When the sweat glands of the palm are driven by the sympathetic nervous system, the skin conducts electricity a little better. Two small electrodes on the fingers or the palm record that, and we call it EDA. It is the slowest of the three. A reaction shows up a few seconds after its cause, and the level can drift for minutes.

So the three signals live on three clocks: a beat, a breath, a slow wave of sweat. And none of them reads a mind. A fast heart can mean fear, joy, or a flight of stairs.

Here is the good news. Physiological synchrony has its own literature, and it is older than brain hyperscanning.

In 1983, Levenson and Gottman recorded married couples while they talked. They measured the heart and the skin of both partners, and built a measure of physiological linkage. In the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, they reported that this linkage was related to how satisfied the couples were with their marriage.

In 2011, Konvalinka and colleagues recorded heart rates during a fire walking ritual in a Spanish village, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. The heart rates of fire walkers and of their related spectators moved together over the thirty minutes of the ritual. Those of unrelated spectators did not.

Then come the reviews. In 2017, Palumbo and colleagues reviewed the field in Personality and Social Psychology Review. They found synchrony in many contexts, and a literature that is fragmented, with open questions about methods and statistics. And in 2020, Mayo and Gordon, in Psychophysiology, argued that people both tend to synchronize and tend to drift out of sync, so more synchrony is not always better.

This field also has its traps. The first one is already in the hut.

Here is a simulation. Two hikers climb the same slope. They never talk, and they never look at each other. Each heart rate follows the effort of the climb, plus its own noise. Ava's heart and Otto's heart correlate at 0.96.

Now remove the slope from each trace. What is left correlates at minus 0.12, about zero. The two bodies were never in touch. The hill did it.

This is the fourth source on the compass, body coupling, and its first rival, the shared input. The next episodes keep the hill in mind.

Let's recap. Heart, breath and skin are three signals, with three clocks and three sensors. Each reflects the autonomic nervous system, effort and emotion, all at once. The field is old, and its main trap is shared effort.

Next, the two hearts side by side: episode 10.2, Hearts together.

PLATE 10.24:46

Hearts together

Heartbeats become R to R intervals placed on one shared timeline, and choirs and fire-walking rituals show real heart synchrony. But posture, speech, effort and above all breathing can pull two hearts together alone. In simulation, a shared breathing pacer lifts correlation from zero to 0.82, and strangers who followed the same pacer look just as synchronous as real partners.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The hut is warm now, and Ava and Otto have caught their breath. Today we listen to their hearts. And we ask one question. When two hearts keep time together, who is holding the beat?

Start with one heart. Every beat leaves a sharp spike in the electrocardiogram, the R peak. The time between two R peaks is the R to R interval. At rest it lasts about a second.

Think of a mountain lift. Each cabin is one heartbeat. The gap between two cabins is one interval. A long gap means a slow moment, and a short gap means a fast one.

Sixty divided by the interval gives the instantaneous heart rate. An interval of 0.89 seconds is 67 beats per minute.

Now watch the gaps. They are never all the same. That small, constant wobble is heart rate variability. It is a sign of a living, adjusting heart, and the guidelines of Berntson and colleagues, in Psychophysiology in 1997, warn how easily it is misread.

You can also get beats from PPG, a light sensor on a fingertip. It sees the pulse wave in the blood, a little later and a little blurrier than the spike. We then speak of pulse intervals, which stand in for the real thing.

Two hearts never beat at the same instants. So we cannot compare beat to beat. Instead we lay both lifts on one timeline, and resample each series evenly, for example four times per second. Then the two lines can be compared like any other pair of signals.

Does this show anything? Studies say people's hearts can move together. In 2011, Müller and Lindenberger recorded eleven singers and one conductor, in PLoS ONE. Phase synchronization of heart rate variability, and of breathing, increased during singing compared with rest. It was higher when the choir sang in unison than when it sang in several voices.

In 2013, Vickhoff and colleagues, in Frontiers in Psychology, asked singers to hum, to sing a hymn, and to sing a slow mantra. Unison singing of regular song structures made the hearts of the singers accelerate and decelerate simultaneously. And you met the fire walking ritual of Konvalinka and colleagues in 2011, in the last episode.

Now the careful part. Many plain things can move two hearts without any bond. The same posture, standing up or sitting down. The same speech, taking turns to talk. The same effort, like our climb. The same music.

And one cause sits very close to the heart. The heart follows the breath. When we breathe in, the heart speeds up. When we breathe out, it slows down. This is respiratory sinus arrhythmia. In 1981, Hirsch and Bishop, in the American Journal of Physiology, showed that its size depends on how deep and how fast we breathe.

So here is the consequence. Two people who breathe together get two hearts that swing together. Breathing itself is the next episode.

Here is a simulation of exactly that. Two people who never meet, never talk, never look at each other. First, each breathes freely, at their own pace. Their interval series correlate at minus 0.01. Nothing there.

Now a guide on the wall asks both to breathe six times a minute. Same two people, same hearts. The correlation jumps to 0.82.

Now remove from each series the part that moves at the breathing rhythm. What is left correlates at 0.07. The pacer did it, not the pair.

Is this one lucky dyad? No. Over sixty simulated dyads, free breathing gives a mean correlation of 0.00. Paced breathing gives 0.76. After removing the breathing part, 0.01.

And now the pseudo-pairs. Take Ava, and pair her with a stranger from another dyad who followed the same pacer. The mean correlation is 0.78. The strangers look just as synchronous as the real partners.

That is the lesson of the hut. A high correlation between two hearts is the start of a question, not the answer. Was it the stimulus, source one, here a shared pacer? Or body coupling, source four? Record the breath too, and test against pseudo-pairs, with the same protocol.

Let's recap. Beats become intervals, intervals go on one shared timeline, and the studies on choirs and rituals are real. But posture, speech, effort, music and above all breathing can pull two hearts together by themselves.

Next, the breath itself: episode 10.3, Breathing together.

PLATE 10.34:44

Breathing together

A chest belt records breathing like the bellows of an accordion. Conversation and singing impose the breath, through turn-taking and the score, and the breath then moves the heart and the fNIRS signal. In simulation, strangers who followed the same turn schedule or score look as synchronous as real pairs, while strangers on a different schedule fall to chance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Outside the hut window, the evening is cold. Ava and Otto sit at the glass, and each outbreath leaves a small cloud of mist. Two breaths, side by side. And one question. When two breaths line up, who set the pace?

First, how do we see a breath? The reference is a stretch belt around the chest. It stretches when the lungs fill, and relaxes when they empty. Think of an accordion. The bellows open on the way in, and close on the way out. The belt draws the width of the bellows.

Without a belt, there are estimates. In 2018, Charlton and colleagues reviewed the algorithms that estimate breathing rate from the electrocardiogram and from PPG, in IEEE Reviews in Biomedical Engineering. And fNIRS carries a trace of breathing too, but there it is a contaminant, which episode 5.4 covers.

Now the key point. Speaking and singing take over the breath. At rest, the body decides when to breathe. When we speak, the words decide. We breathe in quickly, then let the air out slowly, for as long as the sentence lasts.

In 2001, McFarland recorded the breathing movements of twenty people, in ten pairs, in the Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research. In conversation, the breathing of a listener changed to look more like speech breathing. It changed before the listener started to talk. And around the changes of turn, the breathing signals of the two partners were strongly correlated.

In 2014, Rochet-Capellan and Fuchs studied free conversations between women, in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B. Most turns were taken just after an inhalation. But over the whole conversation, they found no general coordination between the two breaths. The coordination showed up in short windows, when people were exchanging turns.

Here is a simulation in that spirit. Ava and Otto follow a scripted exchange. The yellow bands are the turns. Each person breathes in just before their own turn, and breathes out slowly while talking. The listener breathes quietly. Now count the breaths in of Ava that have a breath in of Otto within three quarters of a second. For this pair, that is 0.41. Over forty simulated pairs, the mean is 0.45.

Now they sing. The score is the yellow strip, with a mark where a phrase leaves room to breathe. The music decides when to breathe, for both singers. In 2011, Müller and Lindenberger recorded eleven singers and a conductor, in PLoS ONE. Breathing synchronized more during singing than at rest, and more in unison than in several voices. In the simulation, the mean over forty pairs is 0.76.

And the breath travels. In episode 10.2, we saw that the heart follows the breath, through respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Two breaths in step give two hearts in step. In fNIRS, speech changes breathing and carbon dioxide, and that moves the optical signal. One schedule can reach the breath, then the heart, then the fNIRS signal.

So what does a high breathing synchrony prove? Test it. Keep Ava, and pair her with a stranger from another pair, who followed the same turn schedule. In conversation, the mean is 0.46. The real pairs gave 0.45. The strangers look as synchronous as the partners. Now pair Ava with a stranger who followed a different schedule. The mean falls to 0.33, close to the chance level of 0.31.

Singing tells the same story. Strangers who sang the same score reach 0.77, against 0.76 for the real pairs. Strangers who sang another score fall to 0.27, close to the chance level of 0.21.

That is the lesson. The turn schedule, or the score, is a shared input. It is source one on the compass. It lives in the voice, the words and the music, and it reaches both bodies without any bond between them. So record the turns of speech and the score, and build the pseudo-pairs with the same structure. Only what stays above that null can be called body coupling, source four.

Let's recap. A belt gives the breath, and the electrocardiogram gives an estimate. Speaking and singing impose the breath, by the turns of speech in conversation and by the score in song. The breath then moves the heart and the fNIRS signal. And pseudo-pairs with the same structure show how much the schedule alone can explain.

Next, the skin: episode 10.4, Skin and sweat.

PLATE 10.44:21

Skin and sweat

Skin conductance has a slow tonic level, like a rain barrel filling, and quick phasic splashes arriving one to three seconds late. In a simulation, a shared warm-up makes raw traces correlate at 0.89 and pseudo-pairs nearly as much. Removing the slow level leaves a real link that beats pseudo-pairs, and shows that slowness leaves few independent samples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the mountain hut, Ava and Otto have a cup of hot tea each. Palmy, the skin helper, rests one small hand on each cup. This episode is about what the skin can tell us, and what it cannot.

The skin of the palm has sweat glands, and the sympathetic nervous system drives them. When it speeds up, the glands fill a little, and the skin conducts electricity better. Two small electrodes on the fingers record this. We call it electrodermal activity, or EDA, and we read it in microsiemens.

The signal has two parts. The tonic part is the slow level. It drifts up and down over minutes. The phasic part is the quick bumps on top of it. Each bump is one response. It rises over a second or two, then fades over several seconds. In 2010, Benedek and Kaernbach published a method in Psychophysiology to separate the two parts, based on a model of how sweat diffuses.

Picture a rain barrel under the hut's gutter. The water level creeps up all afternoon. That is the tonic part. Each drop that lands makes a splash. That is the phasic part. And the splash comes a little after the drop leaves the gutter.

That delay is the key habit to learn. In the usual scoring, a response is looked for one to three seconds after its cause. So when a door slams, Ava's skin does not answer at the same instant as Otto's. Each answer arrives late, by a different amount.

Now the literature. In 2007, Marci and colleagues recorded skin conductance in twenty patient and therapist pairs during a live therapy session. In the Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease, they reported that a measure of concordance between the two skin signals was related to how empathic the patient found the therapist, with a correlation of 0.47. They also tested their measure on hypothetical pairs, built from people who were never in the room together.

In 2017, Palumbo and colleagues reviewed interpersonal autonomic physiology in Personality and Social Psychology Review. They found that two bodies can become linked across many contexts, and that the literature is fragmented. And in 2012, a committee of the Society for Psychophysiological Research published recommendations in Psychophysiology on how to record and report electrodermal activity.

Here is a simulation, so none of this is real data. Ava and Otto never talk. They share seven events in eight minutes, each answered one to three seconds late. The hut also warms up, so both levels slowly rise.

The raw traces correlate at 0.89. That looks like a bond. But take Ava from one hut and Otto from another. These pseudo-pairs never met, and they still correlate at 0.88 on average. Only nine of forty real dyads beat the top five percent of the pseudo-pairs. The shared warm-up did it, not the pair.

Now remove the slow level from each trace. What is left correlates at 0.65 for this pair. The pseudo-pairs fall to 0.01 on average, and the top five percent stop at 0.18. All forty real dyads are above that line. The shared events are real, and now the comparison shows it.

There is one more cost of slowness. These traces hold nineteen hundred and twenty samples. But neighbouring samples are almost the same, so they are not independent. Before removing the level, a standard correction leaves about six independent samples. After removing it, about 104. That is the amount of evidence in eight minutes.

This is why measures built for fast brain rhythms do not carry over to the skin. A slow signal gives few independent samples, and a delay of seconds blurs who reacts first. Episode 10.5 looks at measures made for slow signals.

Let's recap. The skin gives a slow level and quick bumps, each arriving seconds late. It follows arousal, and it can follow shared emotion. But shared drift inflates a raw correlation, and slowness leaves little evidence, so a pseudo-pair comparison is needed.

Next, how to measure it: episode 10.5, Measuring bodily synchrony.

PLATE 10.56:27

Measuring bodily synchrony

Four methods follow bodily synchrony that shifts in lead and lag: windowed cross-correlation, cross recurrence, dynamic time warping and a coupled oscillator model. Each assumes a form of synchrony, and window length and maximum lag change the result. Picking the best lag per window inflates the value, so the null must run the same search.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Fresh snow lies around the mountain hut. Two hikers walked up here, and they left two trails of footprints. One trail runs a little ahead of the other.

Body signals behave like those trails. They are slow. They are irregular. And they are not stationary: for a while Ava is ahead, then Otto takes the lead. A measure of bodily synchrony has to follow that.

Here is a simulated pair of signals, four minutes long. Ava's signal leads, by up to three seconds. Halfway through, Otto takes over. Four methods can follow this, and we give each one a single image. You met windowed correlation for brains in chapter six, so we go fast.

First, windowed cross-correlation, introduced for behavioural time series by Boker and colleagues in 2002, in Psychological Methods. Cut both signals into windows of thirty seconds, every five seconds. In each window, slide Otto's signal against Ava's, up to a maximum lag of five seconds, and compute the correlation at every lag. Stack the windows and you get a map. Its ridge is the lag that fits best, and the ridge moves: from plus 1.5 seconds at the start to minus 2.75 seconds at the end. Averaged over windows, the correlation at zero lag is 0.38. With the best lag in each window, it is 0.82.

Second, cross recurrence quantification analysis, or CRQA. Marwan and colleagues reviewed recurrence plots in Physics Reports in 2007, and Coco and Dale adapted cross recurrence to two time series in Frontiers in Psychology in 2014. Each signal becomes a string of short trajectories, three points a few seconds apart. Then we ask which moments of Ava resemble which moments of Otto, within half a standard deviation. Every resemblance is a dark cell. If the two signals revisit the same states, dark cells line up along diagonals. Within twelve seconds of the main diagonal, 0.11 of the cells are dark. For pseudo-pairs the mean is 0.06, and the ninety-fifth percentile is 0.07.

Third, dynamic time warping, or DTW, from Sakoe and Chiba in 1978, in the IEEE Transactions on Acoustics, Speech, and Signal Processing. It was made for spoken words. Instead of one shift per window, it stretches and squeezes time, locally, to line the two signals up, within a limit of ten seconds. The warping path leaves the diagonal whenever one signal runs ahead. Here the distance that remains is 0.29, against 0.63 for a pseudo-pair. And the path leans the right way every time the lead is larger than half a second.

Fourth, a coupled oscillator model. Each person is a damped oscillator that returns to rest, and the partner's position pulls on it. Butler reviewed models of interpersonal emotion systems in 2011, in Personality and Social Psychology Review. In 2022, Butler and colleagues used a coupled oscillator model on electrodermal activity and on respiratory sinus arrhythmia in 53 couples. The model has one number for the pull. In a simulated pair, with a true coupling of 0.05, the fit gives 0.038 for Ava and 0.041 for Otto. For pseudo-pairs, the estimate stays within plus or minus 0.016.

Each method assumes a form of synchrony. Windowed correlation assumes a similar shape, at some shift. Recurrence assumes that the signals revisit similar states. Warping assumes the same shape, stretched. The oscillator assumes a rhythm that returns to rest. A signal without that form leaves the method blind.

Two settings change the result more than anything else: the window and the maximum lag. Here are sixty independent pairs, simulated, with no link at all. With ten-second windows and a ten-second lag limit, the mean is 0.91. With sixty-second windows and no lag search, it is 0.01.

That brings us to the trap. In each window, we keep the lag that gives the highest correlation. It is a maximum, so of course it is higher.

Take one pair of independent signals. At zero lag, the value is 0.03. With the best lag in each window, it is 0.41. Across 300 independent pairs, the best-lag value averages 0.42, and every one of the 300 lies above the ninety-fifth percentile of the zero-lag null, 0.21. A naive null makes everyone look synchronous.

The fair null applies the same search to the pseudo-pairs, as in episode 7.5. Its ninety-fifth percentile is 0.55. Our independent pair, at 0.41, sits inside it. The coupled dyad, at 0.82, stays above.

Now take two bodies. Interbeat intervals are slow, so we use windows of sixty seconds and a maximum lag of five seconds. In the simulated pair, the best-lag value is 0.25, and the null's ninety-fifth percentile, from 87 pseudo-pairs, is 0.51. There is no evidence of synchrony.

A respiration belt is faster. One breath lasts about four seconds, so the lag must stay under half a cycle. With windows of thirty seconds and a maximum lag of two seconds, a simulated pair whose breathing is weakly pulled together reaches 0.82, against a null of 0.48. Widen the maximum lag to five seconds, and the pair reaches 0.84, but the null rises to 0.56. The band moves with the search, so it is recomputed every time.

Let's recap. Four methods follow them, and each assumes a form of synchrony. The window and the maximum lag change the result. Choosing the best lag in each window inflates synchrony, so the null must use the same search.

Next, brain and body together: episode 10.6, Brain sync and body sync together.

PLATE 10.65:30

Brain sync and body sync together

Body signals leak into brain signals, so brain synchrony may only reflect two bodies. Simulations ask three questions: does it survive removing each person's own heart and breath, does body synchrony predict brain synchrony or the reverse, and do both tell the same story about behaviour. Recording the body is what answers them.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two hikers reach the mountain hut at the same moment. They climbed the same path at the same pace. Their hearts are still fast, and their breathing is still deep.

Now put a cap on each of them. Their brain signals rise and fall together. Is that two minds in tune? Or is it two bodies that climbed the same hill? Episodes 10.1 to 10.5 showed that bodies synchronise by themselves. This episode asks what that does to brain synchrony.

Here is the hut in cross-section. Each hiker has three floors. The top floor is the brain. The middle floor is the heart. The ground floor is the lungs. Arrows run between the floors. The lungs move the heart. The heart and the breath both leave a trace in the brain signal, especially in fNIRS, as episode 5.4 showed. And through the middle of the hut runs a yellow chimney, the shared climb. Like the shared stimulus of episode 7.3, it acts on both hikers at once.

So there are three questions, in order. They are this series' way of organising the problem. No single paper asks them in this order. One. Does brain synchrony survive when physiology is removed from each signal? Two. Does physiological synchrony predict brain synchrony, or the reverse? Three. Do the two tell the same story about behaviour?

Question one. Here is a simulation of forty dyads, each recorded for three minutes. These data are simulated. The brain signal is slow, like fNIRS. Each person's brain signal carries a little of their own heart rate and their own breathing. The two people shared the effort, so their heart rates correlate at zero point three three on average.

In the first study, the two brains are not linked at all. Even so, the correlation between the brain signals is zero point one nine. Now regress each person's own heart rate and own breathing out of their own brain signal. Their own, not the partner's. The correlation falls to zero point zero zero.

In the second study, the two brains share a real link. The raw correlation is zero point two nine. After the same removal, it is zero point two two. The first study ends at zero. The second keeps something. That is what survival looks like.

This is the worry that Tachtsidis and Scholkmann raised in Neurophotonics, in 2016. In fNIRS, responses that are not neural can be mistaken for brain activity, and they described ways to avoid and remove them. In practice, that means short channels, which see mostly the scalp, and recordings of heart and breathing. Two limits remain. Anything neural that moves with the body goes out with it. And a removal is only as good as the recording of the body.

A first study did record both. In 2022, Guglielmini and colleagues, in Neurophotonics, measured twenty-four pairs with fNIRS plus systemic physiology. The pairs sat with their eyes closed for ten minutes, then made eye contact for ten minutes. Eye contact changed the coupling between brains, between bodies, and between brain and body. The authors read this as entrainment of physiology between the two people.

Question two. Does physiological synchrony predict brain synchrony, or the reverse? Take forty simulated dyads. Plot each dyad's heart rate synchrony against its brain synchrony. In world one, the brain signals only echo the bodies. Across dyads, the correlation is zero point four zero. In world two, a third thing, shared engagement, drives both. Dyads that share more of the effort also couple more in the brain, and the body leaks little. The correlation is zero point five one.

Two worlds, one kind of plot. Now remove each person's own body from their brain signal. World one falls to minus zero point two two, which is no reliable relation with forty dyads. World two stays at zero point four nine. In world one, the body drives the brain signal. In world two, neither drives the other. The raw plot cannot tell which world you are in. The recording of the body can.

Question three. Do brain and body tell the same story about behaviour? In a third simulated study, forty dyads solve a puzzle together. By construction, the score follows the real brain link, and not the effort. The cleaned brain synchrony correlates with the score at zero point five five. The heart rate synchrony correlates at minus zero point one nine, which is nothing reliable. Same dyads, two stories. In real data, they may agree or disagree. You can only know if you recorded both.

Recording both is now practical. In 2026, Hong and colleagues, in Behavior Research Methods, published a guide to recording fNIRS and electrocardiogram together in caregiver and child pairs. They note that earlier work often studied neural and physiological synchrony separately.

So here is the recipe. With EEG, record the electrocardiogram too. With fNIRS, add short channels and a breathing belt. Keep one shared clock, as in episode 4.2. Then ask the three questions. Does it survive the removal? Which way does it go? And does the body tell the same story as the brain?
