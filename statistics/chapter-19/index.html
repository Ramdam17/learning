PLATE 19.17:08

Correlation, causation and randomization

Four stories can make a correlation of minus 0.5: A causes B, B causes A, a hidden cause, or selection. A simulated inert vitamin pill seems to add 9.2 points because people chose it. A coin flip that assigns the pill balances what we measured and what nobody imagined, bringing the effect back to minus 1.1.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the sprinkler garden. On mornings when the grass is wet, the flowers also look fresher. Did the sprinkler do both? Was it the rain? This chapter is about causes. Today: why a correlation cannot say which story is true, and the one tool that can.

Take two variables, A and B, measured on 300 simulated people. The correlation is about minus 0.5: when A is high, B tends to be low. Four stories could produce it. A causes B. B causes A. A hidden variable C causes both. Or nothing links A and B in the population, but the way the cases entered our data does.

Guess before you see. Here are four clouds, shuffled, with no labels. Which story made which? Now the labels. In the first world, A pushes B down, with a slope tuned to 0.58. In the second, B pushes A. In the third, C pushes A up and B down, with strength 1. In the fourth, A and B are independent, but a hospital records only the people whose A plus B passes 0.2: 44 percent. Each world has one knob, tuned so that the expected correlation is minus 0.5. The 300 people of each world give minus 0.42, minus 0.49, minus 0.51 and minus 0.48. The correlation does not tell them apart, and no formula can either. The data alone do not decide.

The same four stories hide in real questions.

Ice cream sales and drownings rise together in summer. A common cause: hot weather. A randomized test would hand out free ice cream by lot, and count the drownings. Nothing would change.

Coffee drinkers and heart disease. Or coffee drinkers smoke more: a common cause. A randomized test could give some volunteers decaf for a few months and measure their blood pressure. It cannot wait thirty years for heart attacks.

Among hospital patients, one disease seems to protect against another. That is selection: people are admitted for either illness. Berkson described it in 1946. We cannot randomize disease, so we must sample everyone, not only patients.

Firefighters and fire damage: more firefighters, more damage. The reverse story fits: bigger fires call more firefighters. Nobody will randomize that, so we compare fires of the same size.

Children who read at home do better at school. Reading may help, or families with time and money may do both. We could offer a reading program to a random half of the families.

Screen time and sleep. Screens may cost sleep, or poor sleepers may reach for a screen. Ask a random half to switch it off an hour earlier for two weeks.

Now the simulation that matters. A thousand simulated people. Some take a vitamin pill. The pill is inert: its true effect is exactly zero. People choose for themselves, and the health-conscious choose it more often. Health-consciousness also raises a health score. So do income, and a factor X that nobody has imagined, and both also steer the choice. 517 people take the pill and 483 do not. Their scores: 59.8 against 50.6. The pill seems to add 9.2 points, with an interval from 7.8 to 10.7. A false effect, made by who chose.

Can we adjust? Adjusting for age changes nothing: 9.2. Adjusting for everything we measured, age, income and a noisy questionnaire on health-consciousness, brings it to 6.0. Still far from zero, because nobody measured X. Only an oracle that knew every factor would reach 0.3.

Now a coin. The same thousand people. For each person, we flip a coin: heads, pill; tails, no pill. The coin knows nothing about anyone. 497 get the pill and 503 do not. Health-consciousness differs between the groups by 0.09 standard deviations, against 0.78 when people chose. Income, X and the other traits are balanced too, all within 0.13. The estimated effect of the pill is minus 1.1, with an interval from minus 2.7 to plus 0.4. Back near zero. The 9.2 compared people who chose; this compares people the coin assigned.

Balanced on average, not in every trial. Guess before you see: with 100 people, how often is the imbalance larger than 0.2 standard deviations? We run 5,000 simulated trials of 100 people. The difference in health-consciousness is centred on zero, with a spread of 0.20, and it exceeds 0.2 in 32 percent of trials. With 400 people the spread halves to 0.10, and with 1,600 it is 0.05. The coin does not promise a perfect split. It promises that the imbalance has no direction, and that its size can be calculated. That is what an interval is for. The estimated effect of the inert pill is centred on zero too.

The coin balanced X, and nobody had imagined X. It balances what we measured, and what we did not measure, and what we never imagined. That is what separates an experiment from an observation. A statistical adjustment corrects only what we thought of and measured. A coin corrects for everything, in advance.

A real case. In 1991, the Nurses' Health Study followed 48,470 women. Those currently taking hormone therapy had a relative risk of major coronary disease of 0.56, with an interval from 0.40 to 0.80: much lower. In 2002, the Women's Health Initiative randomized 16,608 women to hormones or placebo. For coronary heart disease, the hazard ratio was 1.29, with an interval from 1.02 to 1.63: if anything, higher. Both numbers can be right, because they answer different questions. The first compares women who chose hormones with women who did not, and a 1996 study found that, before any treatment, women who later used hormones already had a better health profile. The second compares women assigned by chance. Other differences were debated too, so choice is not the whole story.

A coin is not always possible. We cannot randomize smoking or genes, and sometimes it would be wrong to try. Later episodes ask what can be done without a coin.

Remember: a correlation fits many causal stories. A coin that decides who is treated balances what we measured and what we never imagined. Next time: Simpson's paradox.

PLATE 19.26:54

Simpson's paradox

A comparison can reverse when groups are pooled. At Berkeley in 1973, 44.5 percent of men and 30.4 percent of women were admitted, yet within departments women did as well or better, because more women applied to selective departments. Pooled rates are weighted averages of department rates. Whether to split depends on the causal story, not on the numbers.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the sprinkler garden. Last time, a coin balanced everything we had not measured. Today, a trap that catches careful people: a comparison can go one way in every group, and the opposite way when the groups are pooled. It is called Simpson's paradox.

Berkeley, fall 1973. The graduate school asks whether its departments treat women unfairly. Here are the applications to its six largest departments, 4,526 in all. Of 2,691 men who applied, 1,198 were admitted: 44.5 percent. Of 1,835 women, 557 were admitted: 30.4 percent. Counting every department, the figures are about 44 and 35 percent. Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell published this in Science in 1975. We use the six largest departments, the standard teaching dataset. The gap looks clear.

Guess before you see. Now split by department. Will the gap shrink, vanish, or reverse? In department A, 82.4 percent of the women were admitted, against 62.1 percent of the men. Across the six, women do better in four departments, and a little worse in two. The gap has gone.

How can both be true? Look at where each group applied. More than half of the men, 51.5 percent, applied to departments A and B, which admit more than 63 percent of all their applicants. Only 7.2 percent of the women did. Most women applied to departments C to F, which admit between 6.4 and 35.1 percent.

A pooled rate is a weighted average of the department rates, and the weights are where people applied. Here is each group as a jug. Each layer is a department, as thick as that group's share of applications. The pink in each layer is its admission rate. Pour the layers together, and you get 44.5 and 30.4. The arithmetic is right: the weights differ. Give women the men's weights, keep their own department rates, and their rate would be 51.6 percent. Give men the women's weights, and theirs falls to 30.2.

So the two statements do not contradict each other. The pooled gap compares two different mixes of departments. The department gaps compare like with like. One is not the average of the other.

The same reversal, as a scatter plot. A simulated study: three clinics, 40 patients each. Across is the daily dose of a drug, up is a recovery score. Inside each clinic, more drug goes with better recovery: slopes of 0.9, 0.9 and 1.4 points per milligram. But the clinics differ. Mild cases get low doses and recover well. Severe cases get high doses and recover less. Guess before you see: pool the three clinics. Which way does the slope go? Minus 1.0. More drug, worse recovery. Same points, same arithmetic. The clinics are the departments.

Simpson described this in 1951, in a paper on contingency tables. In 1972, Blyth wrote a paper called On Simpson's paradox and the sure-thing principle.

So which table do we believe? Both. They answer different questions. The pooled table answers: of the women who applied, what share got in? That is true, and it describes the university. The department table answers: does a committee treat a woman worse than a man? Departments decide alone, so that comparison is made inside a department. The authors concluded that the pooled gap did not come from the committees, but, apparently, from screening at earlier levels of education. Why did women apply where they did? That story is not in the numbers.

A medical case, with real data. In 1986, Charig and colleagues compared open surgery with a procedure through a small cut in the skin, for kidney stones. Overall, 78.0 percent of the open operations succeeded, against 82.6 percent for the small cut. But for small stones, open surgery wins: 93.1 against 86.7. For large stones, it wins again: 73.0 against 68.8. Why? Only 24.9 percent of the open-surgery patients had small stones, against 77.1 percent of the others. Large stones are harder for everyone, and they mostly went to open surgery. To choose a treatment for a patient, read the table split by stone size.

Two more cases, with invented numbers. Hospital X saves 90.0 percent of its patients, hospital Y only 71.3. But Y is a referral centre: 83.3 percent of its patients are severe, against 16.7 at X. Among mild cases, Y saves 98.0 percent against 96.0. Among severe cases, 66.0 against 60.0. For a sick person choosing a hospital, read the split table.

A web test. Design B converts 15.2 percent of visitors, design A only 11.0. But B was mostly shown on desktops, where everyone buys more. On mobile, A wins: 10.0 against 8.0. On desktop, A wins again: 20.0 against 16.0. A coin would have balanced the devices. Split by device.

Is splitting always right? No. Take a drug that lowers blood pressure, and low blood pressure protects the heart. Of the treated patients, 80.0 percent end with low blood pressure, against 30.0 percent of the controls. Heart attacks: 8.0 percent against 15.5. Now split by blood pressure after treatment. Among low pressures, 5.0 percent in both arms. Among high, 20.0 percent in both. The split says the drug does nothing. But the drug works through blood pressure. The grouping came after the treatment, so the pooled table is the right one.

So the rule is neither always split, nor never. Ask what made the groups. If the grouping came first and shaped both the choice and the result, compare inside groups. If the treatment made it, do not split. Drawing these stories as diagrams is for later.

Remember: a pooled table and a split table can disagree, and both are true. Which one answers your question depends on the causal story, and the numbers do not contain it. Next time: causal graphs.

PLATE 19.37:04

Causal graphs

A causal graph has a circle per variable and an arrow per cause. In a fork, adjust: age explains grey hair and glasses. In a chain, adjusting for fitness erases a real training effect. At a collider, adjusting creates a false link, as talent and luck among the famous reach minus 0.59. Adjusting for everything gave minus 0.27.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the sprinkler garden. Last time, we learned that the causal story is not in the numbers. Today we draw it. A causal graph is a picture with a circle for each variable and an arrow for each cause. With three variables, three shapes matter, and each one tells you what adjusting will do.

The first shape is the fork: one variable causes two others. Here is a simulated study of 1,000 adults. Age greys the hair, and age also strengthens reading glasses. Grey hair does nothing to eyesight: its true effect is zero. Yet across all 1,000 people, the correlation between grey hair and glasses is 0.86. This is the hidden variable of 19.1.

Now adjust for age. We draw a box around it, and the box means adjusted for. Compare people inside ten-year bands: the correlation falls to 0.14 on average. It is not zero, because inside a band, age still varies a little. Put age into the regression as a covariate, and the slope of glasses on grey hair is 0.04, with an interval from minus 0.06 to 0.14. The association was made by age.

Ice cream and drownings are the same fork, with summer heat behind both. So are shoe size and reading score in children, from 9.4, with age behind both, and the dose and recovery of 19.2, with case severity behind both. For a fork, adjust.

The second shape is the chain: a cause acts through an intermediate. A simulated training programme, assigned by a coin to 1,000 people, raises fitness. Fitness lowers the resting heart rate. The programme has no other route. Its true effect is minus 5.0 beats per minute. Measured: minus 5.2, with an interval from minus 6.0 to minus 4.4.

Now adjust for fitness, comparing only people with the same fitness. The effect is 0.0, with an interval from minus 0.8 to plus 0.8. It has vanished. Yet the programme works: it changes fitness, and fitness changes the heart. The effect was real, and adjusting for the step in between hid it. Chains are looked at closely in 19.4.

Another chain: evening screen time, a later bedtime, less sleep. Compare people with the same bedtime, and the screen would seem to do nothing. This drawing is only an illustration.

The third shape is the collider: two causes share an effect. Talent and luck both lead to fame. In a simulated population of 10,000, talent and luck are independent: the correlation is 0.02. Now look only at the 500 most successful, the top 5 percent. Among them the correlation is minus 0.59. Famous people with little luck have a lot of talent, and the reverse.

Adjusting for success in a regression does the same thing. The slope of talent on luck goes from 0.02 to minus 0.80. Nothing connects them. Looking at the effect made the link.

This is the fourth story of 19.1, the way the cases were chosen, and Berkson described it for hospital data in 1946. Here is the school version. 10,000 applicants have two independent scores. Keep only those whose sum passes a cutoff. Guess before you see: as the school gets pickier, does the correlation among those admitted get weaker, or stronger?

Keep half, 50.1 percent: minus 0.48. Keep 23.8 percent: minus 0.61. Keep 8.0 percent: minus 0.76. The pickier the school, the stronger the false link.

Hospitals do it too. Two diseases, independent in 100,000 people: their odds ratio is 0.97. Each disease raises the chance of admission, and 16,020 people are admitted. Among them the odds ratio is 0.11. Of admitted patients with disease A, 13 percent have B. Of those without A, 57 percent do. Look only at patients, and A seems to protect against B.

The garden has one too. Rain and the sprinkler are independent, and both wet the grass. On a day when the grass is wet, no rain means the sprinkler ran.

A real case. Light babies of smoking mothers survive more often than other light babies. In 2006, Hernández-Díaz and colleagues explained this as a collider: birth weight is affected by smoking, and shares causes with death.

Now the strong moment. Real studies have all three shapes at once. One simulated data set of 100,000 people: an exposure X and an outcome Y. A confounder C causes both. A middle step M carries the effect of X to Y. A common effect Z is caused by both. The true effect of X on Y is 0.50, as in 9.4. Guess before you see: what does a regression say, under each choice of covariates?

Same variables, same people, five different answers. Adjust for nothing: 1.06, too big, because of C. Adjust for C: 0.50, right. Add M: minus 0.01, the effect is erased. Adjust for C and Z: 0.06, nearly erased, because Z hears from both X and Y, so people with the same Z are tied together. Adjust for everything we measured, C, M and Z: minus 0.27, an effect with the wrong sign.

One choice is right, and it is not everything. Controlling for everything we measured is not a precaution. Depending on the drawing, a covariate removes a bias, erases the effect, or manufactures a bias. Rohrer made this case in 2018.

One more trap. Put X and C in one regression table. The coefficient of X is 0.50, and it reads as an effect, because the drawing was made for X. The coefficient of C is 0.70. But C acts on Y directly and through X, and its total effect is 1.10. The table shows only the direct part. Westreich and Greenland called this reading of every coefficient the Table 2 fallacy, in 2013.

These are the causal diagrams of Pearl, 1995. Greenland, Pearl and Robins brought them to epidemiology in 1999. There are formal rules to read any graph, and today three shapes were enough.

Remember: draw the story before choosing what to adjust. Adjust for a common cause. Do not adjust for the step in between, or for a common effect. Only the coefficient the drawing was made for reads as an effect. Next time: mediation.

PLATE 19.47:31

Mediation

Mediation splits an effect into direct and indirect parts. In a programme that raises fitness and lowers heart rate, the indirect path is 10.6 times minus 0.49, giving minus 5.2 of minus 7.3 total, or 71 percent. A hidden cause, like unmeasured motivation, or an unseen time order can produce a false indirect effect. Randomizing the start does not randomize the middle step.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the sprinkler garden. In 19.3, a training programme acted on resting heart rate through fitness, and adjusting for fitness erased the effect. Today we look at that chain closely. How much of an effect goes through the middle step, and how much does not?

Here is the programme again, simulated, with one change: it now has a second route. A coin gives it to 991 of 2,000 people. By construction, the programme raises fitness by 10.0 points. Each fitness point lowers resting heart rate by 0.5 beats per minute. And the programme lowers heart rate by 2.0 beats directly. So the true indirect effect is minus 5.0, and the true total is minus 7.0.

Now we measure. In 1986, Baron and Kenny proposed three regressions. First, heart rate on the programme alone. The total effect is minus 7.3, near the true minus 7.0. Second, fitness on the programme. The programme raises fitness by 10.6. Third, heart rate on the programme and fitness together. Each fitness point is worth minus 0.49 beats, and what is left for the programme, the direct effect, is minus 2.1.

Now the arithmetic. The indirect effect is the first path times the second: 10.6 times minus 0.49, which is minus 5.2. The direct effect plus the indirect effect is minus 2.1 plus minus 5.2, which is minus 7.3: the total. In a linear model, it always adds up. A bootstrap, as in 14.6, puts the indirect effect between minus 5.7 and minus 4.8.

The share mediated is the indirect effect over the total: 71 percent. But a ratio is fragile when its bottom is small. Take two kinds of studies, both with a true share of 71 percent, each repeated 1,000 times with 200 people. With a total effect of minus 7.0, the estimated share stays between 60 and 85 percent in four studies out of five. With a total effect of minus 0.7, it runs from minus 90 to plus 255 percent, and in 515 studies out of 1,000, it falls outside 0 to 100 percent.

The same drawing is everywhere, illustrative here. A tutoring scheme acts on scores through homework time. A drug lowers blood pressure through weight loss. A mindfulness course improves sleep through less rumination. A message changes behaviour through attitude. A neural signal carries a stimulus to a reaction time.

Now, what does this assume? The coin gave us the programme, but nobody gave us fitness. To read the second path as the effect of fitness, people with the same programme and the same fitness must be comparable in everything else: no hidden common cause of fitness and heart rate. Imai, Keele and Tingley wrote this down in 2010, and noted that it can fail even in a randomized experiment. Their example: 1,801 unemployed workers, randomly assigned to a job-search workshop or not. The workshop was randomized. Their confidence in searching for a job, the middle step, was not.

Here is what a hidden cause does, simulated. A lottery gives tutoring to half of 500 pupils. Tutoring raises homework time by 2.0 hours a week, and the exam score by 4.0 points directly. Homework time itself does nothing: the true indirect effect is zero. But motivated pupils do more homework and score higher, and motivation is not measured.

Run the three regressions. The total effect is 4.70, a little above the true 4.0: one study varies. Tutoring on homework time: 2.05. Homework time on score: 1.34 points per hour. The indirect effect, 2.05 times 1.34, is 2.75. The direct effect is 1.95. An indirect effect of 2.75, where the truth is zero.

Repeat 2,000 times, and turn a knob: how strongly motivation pulls the score. With no pull, the average indirect effect is 0.0, and the analysis cries wolf in 5 percent of studies. With a medium pull, the average is 1.5, found in 84 percent. With a strong pull, 3.0, found in every study, and the direct effect shrinks to 1.0, where the truth is 4.0. The lottery was fair. The answer is still wrong, because homework time was not randomized.

Imai and colleagues propose a sensitivity analysis: how strong would a hidden cause have to be to change the conclusion? They argue that a mediation analysis is not complete without one. Their paper also points to experiments that push the middle step itself, directly or indirectly.

A second trap is time. A coin assigns a mindfulness course to 500 people, simulated. It raises a sleep score by 6.0 points. Better sleep then lowers rumination, by 0.5 per sleep point. So the arrow runs from sleep to rumination, and the true indirect effect is zero.

But we measure both once, after the course. The total effect is 6.2, near the true 6.0. The course on rumination: minus 1.9. Rumination on sleep: minus 0.78. The indirect effect, minus 1.9 times minus 0.78, is plus 1.5. The direct effect is 4.7. The analysis says 24 percent mediated, where nothing is.

Repeat 2,000 times with 300 people. The indirect effect is found in 91 percent of studies, with an average of 2.4. Turn the knob on the arrow from sleep to rumination, from 0 to minus 1.0. The average indirect effect grows from 0.0 to 4.4.

Why? The course group ruminates less, and within a group, those who ruminate less sleep better. Both facts fit rumination first, and both fit sleep first. The order in time was never seen. Maxwell and Cole warned of this in 2007: cross-sectional analyses of mediation can be highly misleading. Of 72 mediation studies published in 2005 in five leading psychology journals, 28 were completely cross-sectional.

Now the strong moment. A mediation analysis is a causal claim about the intermediate. It says: change fitness, and heart rate changes by this much, whatever the programme. The coin tests the claim for the programme. For the middle step, only our assumptions test it. Randomizing the first does not randomize the second.

Remember: a mediation analysis splits an effect into a direct part and an indirect part. The split is a causal claim about the middle step, and randomizing the start does not randomize it. Next time, chapter 20 asks a different question: predict or explain. We begin with overfitting.
