Why would two brains sync at all?

Two pendulum clocks on one beam fall into the same rhythm, and hyperscanning asks whether two brains do likewise. Synchrony can arise from common input, coordinated behavior, mutual prediction or shared physiology. A simulation shows a task-driven raw correlation of 0.67 vanishing after removing the evoked response, so synchrony must exceed what the shared situation explains, tested against matched controls.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 7, Two Brains at Once. First question, and it's a big one. Why would two brains sync at all?

Let's start with a clock story. In 1665, Christian Huygens was ill in bed, staring at two pendulum clocks hanging from the same wooden beam. However he started them, after a while they ended up swinging with exactly the same rhythm in opposite directions. He called it an odd kind of sympathy. The explanation turned out to be physical, tiny vibrations traveling through the shared beam. Two oscillators, one coupling, one rhythm.

Now replace the clocks with two people and the beam with a conversation. Is there something that couples their brains? To ask that question, you need to record both brains at the same time. That is hyperscanning.

The word comes from Montague and colleagues in 2002. They linked two MRI scanners over the internet so that two people could play a game together while both brains were scanned. In 2010, Dumas and colleagues recorded two EEG systems while pairs of people spontaneously imitated each other's hand movements. During moments of interactional synchrony, they found a network of inter-brain synchronization in the alpha-mu band between right centro-parietal regions.

And fNIRS soon joined in. In one of the early fNIRS hyperscanning studies, Cui, Bryant, and Reiss asked pairs to press a button at the same moment as their partner. Wavelet coherence between the two right superior frontal cortices went up during cooperation, but not during competition. The same year, Jiang and colleagues found increased synchronization in the left inferior frontal cortex during a face-to-face dialogue, but not back-to-back and not during a monologue.

Exciting results. But hold on. Synchrony between two signals has many possible causes, and only some of them are interesting. Let's go through four routes.

Route 1, common input. Two people watch the same film or hear the same beep. Each brain responds to it on its own. Their signals rise and fall together, and no interaction is needed at all. Like two clocks each listening to the same radio time signal.

Route 2. Coordinated Behavior. When people move together, take turns speaking, or look at each other, their bodies do similar things at similar moments. Motor and sensory activity follows. The brains synchronize because the behavior did.

Route 3. Mutual Prediction. Each person builds a model of the other, and keeps adjusting to them. Their internal states become aligned. This is the interpretation most studies hope for, and it is the hardest to prove.

Route 4, the fNIRS special, shared physiology and shared artifacts. Breathing, heart rate, blood pressure and posture can all change together during a task. And both people move at the same moments, at the start of every block.

Let's see Route 1 in action with a simulation. Two brains never in contact. Same block design, 20 seconds of task, 20 seconds of rest. Each brain has its own independent noise. Correlate the two raw signals and you get R equals 0.67. Impressive and completely empty. Now remove the task-evoked response from each signal with a model and correlate the residuals. 0.12 negative. Noise level. Why it is not exactly zero is a story for later in this chapter.

Now a second pair, where we add a genuinely shared slow fluctuation on top of the task. The raw correlation is 0.81. After removing the task response, the residual correlation stays at 0.45. That is the part the task alone cannot explain.

So the key question is never, are these two brains in sync? It is, is there more synchrony than the shared input, the shared movement, and the shared physiology would produce on their own. That is why good studies compare against matched controls. Competition versus cooperation, back-to-back versus face-to-face, real pairs versus pseudo-pairs made of people who never met.

Researchers like Hamilton and Holroyd have pushed this point hard. Inter-brain synchrony could be a cause of smooth interaction, a consequence of it, or simply a byproduct of doing the same thing at the same time. A correlation alone cannot tell these apart. Measuring behavior and physiology alongside the brains helps a lot.

So why fNIRS for this? It tolerates movement, it is quiet, and people can sit face-to-face, talk, play, or teach a child. The price? It is slow. fNIRS sees coupling in fluctuations lasting several seconds, not millisecond phase locking. That shapes what you can claim.

Quick recap. Hyperscanning records two or more brains at once. Synchrony can come from common input, coordinated behavior, mutual prediction, or shared physiology. A big raw correlation can be entirely task-driven. The real question is synchrony beyond what the shared situation explains, tested with matched controls.

Next, before measuring any synchrony, both recordings must share the same clock. Two caps, one clock.

PLATE 7.25:19

Two caps, one clock

Hyperscanning needs both recordings on one timeline. One device with two caps gives a single clock, while two devices disagree through offset, drift and jitter. Simulations show that one or two seconds of misalignment can turn real synchrony into apparent anti-synchrony in faster bands, so hardware triggers or the lab streaming layer, shared markers and resampling align the data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Before you measure synchrony between two brains, there is a more boring synchrony to get right, the synchrony between your two recordings. If the two files do not share the same timeline, you are not measuring brains. You are measuring your equipment.

Option 1, one device, two caps. Some fNIRS systems let you split the optodes of a single instrument across two heads. Cui, Bryant, and Reiss did exactly that in their cooperation study. One device means one clock in one file, so alignment is free. The price, you share the channels between the two people and both are tethered to the same box, close together.

Option 2, two devices, one per person. More channels, more freedom, people can sit across a room. But now each device keeps its own time, with its own little quartz crystal. And clocks disagree in three ways.

First offset. The two recordings simply did not start at the same moment. Someone pressed start on one laptop, then walked over to the other. A second or two gone.

Second drift. One clock runs slightly faster than the other, so the gap grows over the session. Standard crystals are typically specified to within 20 to 50 parts per million. 20 parts per million is about 1.7 seconds per day, so roughly 70 milliseconds per hour. Small for fNIRS. The bigger danger is trusting a nominal sampling rate. If the true rate is off by just 0.1% and you reconstruct time by counting samples, you are off by 3.6 seconds after an hour.

Third, jitter, random timing errors that change from moment to moment. Offset and linear drift can be corrected afterwards. Jitter cannot, so it has to be avoided at acquisition.

Does a second or two really matter when the hemodynamic response itself takes several seconds? Let's simulate it. Two signals share a common fluctuation plus their own noise. In a slow band below 0.08 Hz, the correlation at 0 lag is 0.64. Shift one signal by one second and it barely moves, 0.61. At two seconds, 0.53.

Now take a faster band, like the one Cui and colleagues analyzed, from 0.08 to 0.3 hertz. At zero lag, 0.74. One second of misalignment, 0.3. Two seconds minus 0.34. The sign flips because at 0.2 hertz, two and a half seconds is half a cycle. A timing error can turn real synchrony into apparent anti-synchrony.

And with drift, it gets worse over time. Start Start with a 0.8 second offset and a 0.1% rate error. In 5-minute windows, the correlation in that band starts at 0.31 and decays to around 0 by the end of the hour, when the recordings are 4.4 seconds apart. Same brain, same coupling. Align the clocks properly, and the windowed correlation stays between 0.67 and 0.83 for the whole session.

So how do you get one shared timeline? Two main tools. Hardware triggers, one cable or one trigger box sends the same pulse to both devices and each records it in its own data. Or the lab streaming layer, LSL, described by Kothe and colleagues. It timestamps every sample and continuously estimates the clock offsets between computers on the same local network. With well-characterized equipment, it can reach near sample level synchronization. One caveat from its own authors, LSL cannot measure the internal delay of a device before the data reach the computer. Test your setup, don't assume it.

In practice, here is a simple recipe. Send shared markers at the start and at the end of the session, and ideally every few minutes. Read their times in each file. Two markers give you a straight line, the offset and the drift. Map one timeline onto the other, then resample both recordings onto one common time grid. Do the same for everything else in the room. The video you will code behavior from, the heart rate, the breathing belts, the task log.

A few more habits make a hyperscanning dataset analyzable. Use the same montage on both heads, so channel 12 means the same region in both people. Put short channels on both caps. Shared physiology is a real suspect, so measure it. Write down who wore which device and keep that mapping in the file names. And record a quick timing check at the start of every session, a few clapped hands or button presses seen by everything.

Quick recap. One device gives one clock for free at the cost of channels and freedom. Two devices need a shared timeline. Clocks disagree by offset, drift, and jitter. Even one second matters in the faster fNIRS bands and can flip the sign of synchrony. Use hardware triggers or LSL shared markers at the start and end and test it.

Next the clocks are aligned. Now how do we actually measure synchrony? Coherence, correlation and friends.

PLATE 7.34:59

Measuring synchrony

Several measures of inter-brain synchrony exist, and wavelet coherence is the most common in fNIRS. It decomposes each signal into time and period, checks stable phase relations, and yields a value from 0 to 1, with a cone of influence and phase arrows. Coherence is biased upward, so only comparisons count, and band, chromophore and channel pairs must be chosen in advance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The clocks are aligned. Now we want one number that says how much two brains are in sync. And here the field has been creative. A review by Hakim and colleagues in 2023 went through 215 hyperscanning studies and counted 27 different methods.

fNIRS was the most common modality with 119 studies and 89 of them used one method, wavelet coherence. Let's build up to it.

Start simple correlation. Take the same channel in both people and compute Pearson's R over the whole recording. Easy to compute, easy to explain. But it mixes every frequency together, slow drifts dominate it, and it gives you one number for the whole session even if the two people were only in sync some of the time.

A small upgrade cross correlation. Slide one signal against the other and look for the lag where they match best. Now you can ask who leads. In teaching studies, for example, researchers have looked for synchrony where the teacher's brain activity precedes the students.

Now, wavelet coherence. Three steps. Step one, decompose each signal into a map of time against period using small wave-shaped templates called wavelets. Each point of the map says how strongly the signal oscillates at that period, at that moment. Step Step 2. Compare the two maps point by point. Do the two signals oscillate at the same period, at the same time, with a stable phase difference? Step 3. Smooth that agreement over a little time and a little period and normalize it, so you get a value between 0 and 1. One means perfectly coherent. This method came from geophysics. Grinsted, Moore, and Jevrejeva published a popular toolbox for it in 2004.

Two details on the map. The cone of influence. Near the edges of the recording, long periods are contaminated by the borders, so you ignore that region. And the phase. Little arrows tell you whether the signals move in phase, in anti-phase, or one ahead of the other.

Let's try it. A simulated pair, 10 minutes long. 60 seconds of rest, 60 seconds of interaction, alternating. alternating. During interaction only, both signals share an oscillation with a period of about 16 seconds. On top, each brain has its own slow noise. Pearson's R over the whole recording, 0.18. Weak. The noise and the rest periods dilute it.

Now the coherence map. Bright bands appear around a period of 16 seconds, but only during the interaction blocks. Average the coherence between 12 and 22 seconds, inside the cone. During interaction, 0.59. During rest, 0.32.

Here is the trap. Take two completely independent noise signals, compute the same coherence, and you get 0.27. Not zero. Wavelet coherence is biased upwards. With finite smoothing, random signals always look a bit coherent. So an absolute value means very little on its own. What matters is the comparison. Interaction versus rest, cooperation versus a control condition, real pairs versus pseudo pairs.

Coherence also comes with choices, and every choice is a chance to fool yourself. Which frequency band? Ideally chosen from your task design before seeing the data and away from physiology. The heartbeat around 1 Hz, breathing around 0.2 to 0.3 Hz, Mayer waves near 0.1 Hz. Which chromophore? Report both HbO and HbR. Which channel pairs? Matching channels across people, or every channel with every channel? 20 channels each gives 400 pairs, times 2 chromophores. That is 800 tests before any frequency band. Decide in advance, use regions of interest, and correct for multiple comparisons.

A few other tools briefly. Phase-based measures, like the phase-locking value, are common in EEG, less so with slow fNIRS signals. Granger Causality asks whether one person's past helps predict the other's present. It has been used in fNIRS hyperscanning since early studies like Holper and colleagues in 2012. and newer approaches compare what the two brains represent, not just whether their signals go up and down together.

Quick recap. Correlation is simple but blunt. Cross-correlation adds lags and leaders. Wavelet coherence, the fNIRS favorite, shows synchrony across time and period from 0 to 1. But it is never 0, even for noise, so always compare against a reference. And choose your band, chromophores and channel pairs before you look.

Next the reference itself, pseudo pairs and the other traps of synchrony.

PLATE 7.45:05

The traps of synchrony

Synchrony is never zero, so the question is compared to what, since shared tasks, slow autocorrelated signals, shared physiology, shared movement and analytic flexibility all produce chance synchrony. Pseudo pairs, people who did the same task but never together, form the baseline: in a simulation, real pairs correlated no better than strangers. A permutation over dyads tests real against pseudo pairs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last two episodes, we saw that synchrony is never zero. Two brains doing the same task correlate. Two nice signals look coherent. So the question is not, is there synchrony? The question is, compared to what? This episode is about the traps and about the reference that protects you from most of them.

Trap 1, the shared task. Same design, same blocks, same stimuli. Both brains respond to them, and their signals go up and down together without any interaction. We saw this in episode 1 of this chapter.

Trap 2, slow signals. fNIRS signals are smooth, each sample looks a lot like the one before. That makes chance correlations much bigger than you would expect. Let's simulate 2,000 pairs of completely independent signals, 600 samples each. With white noise, the correlations cluster tightly around 0, a standard deviation of 0.04. With slow, autocorrelated noise, the spread is 4 times wider, 0.17. Almost a quarter of these unrelated pairs reach a correlation above 0.2 in one direction or the other. Your intuition about what counts as a big correlation is tuned for white noise. fNIRS is not white noise.

Trap 3. Shared Physiology Two people doing the same task can breathe at similar moments, their heart rates rise together, their blood pressure follows the effort. All of that lands in the scalp and in the fNIRS signal. Short channels help a lot here.

Trap 4. Shared Movement Both people move at the start of a block, both talk, both turn their heads. Correlated motion artifacts look exactly like correlated brain activity.

Trap 5. Flexibility. Many channels, many bands, two chromophores, several methods. Try enough of them and something will come out.

Now the reference. Burgess in 2013 combined EEG recorded from people individually into pseudo pairs, people who never interacted. He still found spurious hyper connections because the recordings shared genuine similarities caused by the experimental conditions. The lesson, you need a baseline that contains everything except the interaction.

That baseline is the pseudo-pair, also called random pair analysis. Take person A from dyad 1 and person B from dyad 5. They did the same task with the same timings in the same lab but never together. Their synchrony captures the shared task, the similar physiology, the typical movements, everything except the interaction itself. Real pairs must beat pseudo pairs.

Let's simulate a study. 20 dyads, the same block design for everyone, slow noise, and no coupling at all between partners. Mean correlation of the real pairs, 0.27. Test that against 0, and you get t equals 9.5, a p-value around 10 to the minus 8. A spectacular result and completely wrong. Now compute every pseudo pair, 380 of them. Their mean correlation, 0.3. The real pairs are no better than strangers.

To test properly, use a permutation. Shuffle the partners so that nobody keeps their real partner, compute the mean pseudo pair correlation, and repeat 5,000 times. That gives you the distribution you would expect without interaction. Here, the real mean is no higher than the shuffles. It even sits on their low side, p equals 0.97. Nothing.

Now add a small genuine coupling between partners and run the same pipeline on a new sample. Real pairs 0.35. Pseudo pairs 0.26. The real mean is beyond every one of the 5,000 shuffles, p below 0.001. That is a result you can defend.

Pseudo pairs have their own rules. They only make sense when everybody follows the same timeline, like a fixed block design. In a free conversation, everyone's timeline is different, so you need other surrogates like time shifting one signal or shuffling segments chosen with care. Build pseudo pairs within the same condition and with the same roles. A parent with someone else's child, not with another parent. And remember that the pseudo pairs are not independent of each other, So test with a permutation at the level of dyads, not by treating 380 pairs as 380 observations.

Quick recap. Synchrony always has rivals. The shared task, slow auto-correlated signals, shared physiology, shared movement, and analytic flexibility. A test against zero is almost meaningless. Compare real pairs to pseudo-pairs and test with a permutation over dyads.

Designing and reading a hyperscanning study

A hyperscanning study starts from a mechanistic hypothesis, contrasts conditions differing only in the interaction, records behavior and physiology, and counts dyads, not people. The analysis plan, including band, regions, pseudo-pair reference and permutation test, is pre-registered. A fictional abstract is dissected with critical questions, and a ladder of evidence runs from observed synchrony to shown causation.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last episode of Chapter 7. We have the tools and we know the traps. Let's put it together, how to design a hyperscanning study and how to read one critically.

It starts with the question, not with the setup. Two caps are not a hypothesis. Which kind of synchrony do you expect and why? Remember the four routes? Common input, coordinated behavior, mutual prediction, shared physiology. A good hypothesis names a mechanism, a region, a frequency band, and a contrast between conditions. For example, during cooperation, more coherence in the right prefrontal cortex than during competition, in the band of the task, beyond pseudo pairs.

Next, the conditions. The ideal contrast changes the interaction and nothing else. Same stimuli, same movements, same duration, only the social part differs. Classic contrasts, cooperation vs. competition, face-to-face vs. back-to-back, doing the task together vs. side-by-side but alone. Add a rest baseline and keep the timing identical across dyads so that pseudo-pairs are possible later.

Then record more than brains. Video so you can code behavior, turn-taking, gaze, imitation, performance. In physiology, short channels on both caps and ideally heart rate and breathing. Hamilton argued that integrating behavioral data is what can move hyperscanning beyond the hype. And measuring physiology is the only way to show that your synchrony is not just two hearts beating faster at the same time.

Sample size. In hyperscanning, the unit is the dyad, not the person. 40 participants means 20 dyads. Plan power on dyads using the effect sizes from chapter 6 and budget for dropouts. If one cap fails, you lose the whole pair.

Then write the analysis plan before collecting data and ideally pre-register it. Pre-processing steps. The band from the task design. The regions of interest and the channel pairs. The method for example wavelet coherence. The reference pseudo pairs. The test a permutation over dyads. And the correction for multiple comparisons.

Finally, link synchrony to something that matters. A brain number on its own is hard to interpret. Does more synchrony go with better performance, better learning, a closer relationship? Cui and colleagues reported that higher coherence was associated with better cooperation performance. That kind of link is what makes synchrony meaningful.

Now let's switch sides. You are reading a paper. Here is a fictional abstract. 24 participants wearing fNIRS while they talked. Inter-brain coherence was significantly greater than zero in 42 channel pairs. This reveals that conversation connects our brains.

Let's ask our questions. Compared to what? Greater than zero is meaningless since coherence is never zero. No pseudo pairs, no control condition. Red flag. How many tests? 42 significant pairs out of how many. Corrected how. Red flag. 24 participants is only 12 dyads. Small. Physiology. Talking moves the jaw and changes breathing, any short channels. Not mentioned. HbR. Not mentioned. And the claim conversation connects our brains. That is a causal, almost telepathic claim from a correlation.

Here is the rewritten version of a good abstract. 40 dyads. Pre-registered regions and band. Coherence was higher during face-to-face conversation than back-to-back, and higher in real pairs than in pseudo-pairs after correction. Effects held for HbO and HbR, and after short channel regression. Synchrony correlated with ratings of mutual understanding. Same topic, a completely different level of trust.

One last point, causality. Novembre and Iannetti argued that hyperscanning alone cannot prove that synchrony causes better interaction. For that, you need to manipulate synchrony, for example with multi-brain stimulation. Moreau and Dumas replied that stimulation is not the only route. Well-designed experiments, multi-brain neurofeedback, interactions with virtual partners, and computational models can also explain how synchrony works. Either way, be honest about which rung of the ladder your evidence reaches. Synchrony observed, synchrony beyond the right controls, synchrony linked to behavior, synchrony shown to cause.

Quick recap. Start from a mechanistic question. Contrast conditions that differ only in the interaction. Record behavior and physiology. Count dyads, not people. Pre-register the band, regions, reference, and test. Link synchrony to behavior. And when reading, ask, compared to what, how many tests, what about physiology, and does the claim match the evidence?
