PLATE 5.14:02

The GLM, built by hand

The general linear model describes a voxel's time series as a weighted sum of regressors plus noise, like copying a garment from a few pattern pieces. Columns for task onsets convolved with the response function, drift, confounds and a constant form the design matrix, and least squares finds the betas. Fitted independently in every voxel, the betas painted in place make a brain map.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 5, Task Analysis. Our new world is a sewing workshop. A tailor wants to copy a garment using only a few standard pattern pieces. Each piece can be scaled up or down but its shape is fixed. The question is simply how much of each piece to use. That is the general linear model. Today we build it by hand.

Take one voxel. Its time series is our garment, one value per volume. The model says that this series is a weighted sum of a few known shapes, the regressors, plus some leftover noise. Each weight is called a beta. Our job is to find the betas.

Let us cut the pattern pieces one column at a time. First, the task. Our participant taps a finger in blocks. We write down when each block starts and how long it lasts. That gives a box shape on and off. Then we convolve it with the hemodynamic response function from chapter 2. The box becomes a smooth, delayed curve. That is what we expect a responding voxel to look like.

If there are several conditions, each one gets its own column built the same way. We can add the temporal derivative of each to absorb small timing differences, as we saw in the slice timing episode.

Next, the drift. Slow cosines with periods of several minutes model the scanner's wandering baseline. This is the same as a high-pass filter. In SPM, the usual cutoff is 128 seconds.

Then the confounds from the denoising episode. Motion parameters, CompCor components, maybe spike regressors. And finally, a constant column for the mean signal.

Put the columns side by side, and you get the design matrix. Rows are volumes in time. Columns are regressors. Software often draws it as a gray picture, and learning to read it is a superpower. One glance tells you what the model believes.

Now we fit. We look for the betas that make the weighted sum of columns as close as possible to the data. Close means that the sum of the squared differences is as small as possible. This is ordinary least squares and it has an exact solution computed in one step. Picture one slider per column. The fit moves all the sliders at once until the copy matches the garment as well as it can.

What remains is the residual, the leftover fabric. It tells us how noisy this voxel is. A large beta with small residuals is convincing. The same beta with huge residuals is not. Dividing a beta by its uncertainty gives a T value, the subject of the next episode.

And now the moment that makes this whole chapter click. We do not fit one voxel. We fit every voxel, about 100,000 of them, independently, with exactly the same design matrix. Each voxel gets its own set of betas. Take the beta of the tapping column in every voxel, paint it at its place, and you have a brain map. A map is nothing more than a collection of regressions, one per voxel. This approach is called mass univariate, and it was set out for imaging by Karl Friston and colleagues in 1995. Five.

Three warnings come with it. The model assumes the shape of the response, so a region with a different shape is fitted badly. It assumes that the noise is independent from one volume to the next, which is false, and we fix it in the episode on autocorrelation. And if two columns are too similar, their betas become unstable, as we saw with correlated regressors in Chapter 3.

To sum up, the GLM explains a voxel's time series as a weighted sum of regressors. Task columns are onsets convolved with the response function. Drift, confounds, and a constant join them in the design matrix. Least squares finds the betas, and the residuals measure the noise. Repeated in every voxel, it paints a map. Next time contrasts asking the right question.

PLATE 5.24:11

Contrasts · Asking the right question

A contrast is a list of weights on the betas that turns a fitted model into a question. Weights of 1 and minus 1 test whether one condition exceeds another, dividing by the standard error gives a t-statistic, and an F-test asks several questions at once. A contrast is only as good as its control condition: rest is not zero, and factorial designs measure interactions.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we fitted the model and got one beta per column in every voxel. But a beta on its own answers no interesting question. To ask one we write a contrast. Think of it as the tailor's alteration ticket. One box per pattern piece and in each box a number that says what to do with that piece.

A contrast is a list of weights, one per column of the design matrix. We multiply each beta by its weight and add everything up. The result is a single number per voxel, the effect we care about.

Take our experiment from last time. Two conditions, tapping and reading. The contrast 1-0 asks, is tapping above the baseline. The contrast 1-1 asks something different, is tapping stronger than reading. All the other columns, drifts, motion and the constant get a weight of 0. They are in the model but not in the question.

With three conditions, the weights one-third, one-third, one-third ask about their average. In a rule of thumb, for a difference the weights add up to zero. For an average they add up to one.

Now how sure are we? The contrast value is divided by its standard error, which comes from the residuals and from the design matrix. That gives a t-statistic. A t-test asks one directed question. Is a bigger than b? The sign matters and so does the order. 1 minus 1 is not the same question as minus 1 1.

Sometimes we want to ask several questions at once. Does anything happen here in any condition? Is the response different between any of these three conditions? Then we stack several contrasts into a matrix and use an F-test. The F-test is undirected. It tells you that something differs but not what and not in which direction. To find out, you go back to the T-contrasts.

Contrasts carry an old idea, cognitive subtraction. It goes back to the Dutch physiologist Donders in the 19th century, who subtracted reaction times. In imaging, you compare a task with a control task that differs in exactly one process. Subtract the two maps, and what remains should be that process.

So the control condition is half of your question. Choose it badly, and you answer another question. The classic trap is rest. Resting people are not doing nothing. In a study published in 2001, Craig Stark and Larry Squire found that activity in the medial temporal lobe was substantially higher during rest than during several simple alternative tasks. Against rest, a memory effect there can shrink or even vanish. Zero is not zero.

Subtraction also rests on an assumption called pure insertion. Adding one process to a task should leave all the other processes unchanged. Back to the atelier. Sew a pocket on a jacket, and the jacket should be the same jacket, plus a pocket. But fabric does not behave like that. The pocket changes how the whole jacket hangs. In 1996, Karl Friston and colleagues argued that pure insertion is not valid for the brain, because cognitive processes interact.

Their remedy was factorial designs. Cross two factors, for example two kinds of task with two kinds of stimulus. Then the interaction is a contrast of its own, with weights 1, minus 1, minus 1, 1. It asks whether the effect of one factor depends on the other. And instead of assuming no interaction, you measure it.

A practical habit. Before scanning, write down the contrast you will test. If you cannot write the weights, the question is not ready yet. And when you read a paper, look for the contrast behind every map. Against what is the first question to ask.

To sum up, a contrast is a set of weights on the betas. A T contrast asks one directed question, an F contrast asks several at once. The control condition is half of the question, and rest is not zero. Pure insertion is fragile, and factorial designs let you measure interactions. PLATE 5.34:10

Autocorrelation and prewhitening

fMRI noise has a memory, like a chain stitch in which each loop passes through the one before. Ignoring this autocorrelation shrinks standard errors, inflates t values and lets false positives creep in, especially for slow designs. Prewhitening describes the noise, often with an AR1 model, and filters it back to white; a flat residual spectrum shows that it worked.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Our model has a hidden promise. When we computed a T value, we assumed that the noise in each volume is independent of the noise in the next one. Every volume a fresh roll of the dice. In fMRI that promise is broken.

Look at the residuals of a real voxel. If one volume is a bit too high, the next one tends to be too high as well. The noise drifts slowly with a memory. This is called temporal autocorrelation. It comes from slow physiological flow fluctuations, from breathing and heartbeat folded into low frequencies, from scanner drift that the filter did not fully remove, and from the smooth hemodynamic response itself.

Think of two ways to sew a seam. With separate tacks, each stitch is tied off on its own. Pull one out and its neighbors stay. With a chain stitch, each loop passes through the one before. Pull one and the whole row moves. fMRI noise is a chain stitch.

Why does it matter? Imagine counting the stitches to judge how strong the seam is. 200 tacks are 200 pieces of evidence. 200 chain loops are not. They hold together so they count as fewer. In statistics, we say the effective number of degrees of freedom is smaller than the number of volumes.

If we ignore this, the standard error of a beta is too small. The T values come out too large, and the map looks more significant than it is. False positives creep in, especially for slow designs, whose regressors share the same low frequencies as the noise. The beta itself is not biased. It is our confidence that is wrong.

The fix is called prewhitening. First, describe the noise with a simple model. The usual choice is an auto-regressive model of order 1, written AR1. It says that each noise value is a fraction of the previous one, plus a fresh random part. The fraction is the correlation between neighbors.

Then, transform the data and the design matrix with a filter built from that model. The filter undoes the memory. After it, the noise looks like separate tacks again, white noise, and the usual formulas are valid. White means that all frequencies carry the same power, like white light.

Each package does this its own way. SPM, by default, fits an AR1 plus white noise model, estimated once for the whole brain. SPM also offers an alternative called FAST, which uses a richer set of noise shapes. FSL estimates the autocorrelation locally in every voxel with smoothing, a method published by Mark Woolrich and colleagues in 2001. AFNI fits a slightly richer model, ARMA11, voxel by voxel.

These choices are not equally good. In 2019, Wiktor Olszowy and colleagues compared them on 11 data sets, 980 scans. AFNI whitened best, and SPM's FAST option did better than SPM's default. With FSL and default SPM, autocorrelated noise remained, mostly at low frequencies. And it led to false positive clusters, especially for slow designs. The authors recommended that software show diagnostic plots.

You can make that diagnostic yourself. Take the residuals after prewhitening and look at their power spectrum. If the model worked, the spectrum is flat. A bump at the low end means memory is left over. Short TRs make this harder because neighboring volumes are closer in time and more alike.

So three habits. Never switch prewhitening off to gain significance. Prefer a noise model that is estimated locally or a richer one, especially with a short TR. And check that your residuals are really white.

To sum up, fMRI noise has a memory, like a chain stitch. Ignoring it shrinks the standard errors and inflates the T values. Prewhitening models the noise, often as AR1, and filters it back to white. Packages differ, and a flat residual spectrum is the proof. Next time, from one brain to a group.

PLATE 5.44:21

From one brain to a group

Group analysis uses two levels: a GLM per participant gives one contrast image each, then a simple GLM across people treats those images as data. Fixed effects use only within-subject noise and speak for the scanned participants, so three strong responders among ten can light up the map. Random effects compare the effect to the spread between people and generalize to the population.

TRANSCRIPTEN

So far, everything happened inside one brain. But we rarely care about one person. We want to say something about people in general, about a population. That is the job of the group analysis.

Back in the atelier, the tailor is designing a size chart for a new shop. Measuring one client a hundred times tells you a lot about that client. It tells you very little about the next person who walks in. For a size chart, you need many clients and above all, you need to know how much they differ from each other.

The standard recipe has two levels. At the first level, we fit the GLM to each participant separately, exactly as in the last three episodes. For each person, we compute the contrast we care about, for example, tapping minus reading. The result is one contrast image per participant, a map of betas.

At the second level, these images become the data. In every voxel, we now have one number per person, and we fit a new, very simple GLM across people. The simplest one is a one-sample t-test. Is the average contrast across participants different from zero? This two-step procedure is called the summary statistics approach. It was described by Andrew Holmes and Karl Friston in 1998.

Here is the key question. Which variability goes into the denominator of the t-test? There are two kinds. The within-subject variability is the noise from scan to scan, inside each person. The between-subject variability is how much the true effect differs from one person to the next.

A fixed-effects analysis uses only the within-subject noise. It treats all the scans as if they came from one big brain. With fMRI, scan-to-scan noise is small compared to the differences between people, so fixed-effects gives huge T values. But its conclusion is limited. It says something about these participants and only these.

Let me show you why that matters. Imagine 10 participants. Three of them have a very strong effect. The seven others have none at all. In a fixed effects analysis, the three strong responders carry the whole group, and the map lights up. Yet most of the sample shows nothing. A random effects analysis looks at the spread between people. Seven zeros and three big values give a large spread and a modest average, and the effect is no longer significant. That is the honest answer about the population.

A random effects analysis treats participants as a random sample from the population. Their effects vary and that variation is what the test must beat. This is how you generalize beyond your sample. It is now the standard for any claim about people.

Mixed effects models go one step further. They combine both sources of variance and give less weight to a participant whose first-level estimate is noisy. FSL's FLAME does this, from work by Christian Beckmann and Mark Woolrich in 2003 and 2004. In practice, for most balanced designs, the simple summary statistics approach gives very similar results, and it is robust.

The second level is a GLM like any other. A paired t-test compares two conditions within people. A two-sample t-test compares two groups, for example, patients and controls. You can add covariates like age or a behavioral score and ask whether the brain effect follows them. The same contrast logic applies with weights on the group level betas.

Two practical warnings. With random effects, the number of participants is what gives you power more than the number of scans per person. Holmes and Friston already pointed to designs with more subjects, and the group map shows an average. It does not mean that every person has that pattern, as our three strong responders reminded us.

To sum up, group analysis has two levels, one GLM per person, then a GLM across people on their contrast images. Fixed effects speaks only for your participants. Random effects compares the effect to the spread between people and speaks for the population. Mixed effects weighs each participant by precision. Next time, 100,000 tests and the dead salmon.

PLATE 5.54:25

A hundred thousand tests

A brain map is one test per voxel, about a hundred thousand of them, so an uncorrected threshold of p below 0.001 lets roughly 100 false positives through on pure noise, as the dead salmon showed. Corrections trade strictness differently: Bonferroni, random field theory, false discovery rate, cluster inference, TFCE, and permutation tests that learn the null from the data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A brain map is not one test. It is one test per voxel, about a hundred thousand of them. And that changes everything about what significant means.

Picture an inspector at the end of the atelier, checking a long bolt of fabric square by square. Each square gets a quick test, is there a flaw? The test is good. It raises a false alarm only once in a thousand clean squares. But the bolt has 100,000 squares. So even if the fabric is perfect, the inspector will flag about 100 of them.

That is exactly what happens with a brain map thresholded at p less than 0.001, uncorrected. Run it on pure noise and you will still see about 100 voxels light up, scattered here and there. Each one looks like a finding.

The most famous demonstration involves a fish. In 2009, Craig Bennett and colleagues presented a poster, published the following year, about a dead Atlantic salmon. They put it in the scanner, showed it photos of people in social situations, and asked it what the people were feeling. With an uncorrected threshold of P less than 0.001 and clusters of at least three voxels, a few voxels in the salmon's brain came out significant. With a proper correction, nothing survived. The study won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2012. The image makes people smile. The lesson is serious.

So how do we correct? The first idea is to control the family-wise error rate, the chance of even one false positive anywhere in the map. The bluntest tool is Bonferroni. Divide your alpha by the number of tests. 0.05 divided by 100,000 is 0.0000005. It works, but it is too strict for fMRI because neighboring voxels are not independent. After smoothing, the real number of independent tests is much smaller.

Random field theory takes that smoothness into account. It treats the map as a smooth random field, estimates how smooth it is, and computes the threshold that keeps the family-wise error at 5%. That is what SPM reports as FWE corrected. It relies on assumptions, notably enough smoothness.

A second idea is the false discovery rate, or FDR. Instead of protecting against any single false positive, it controls the proportion of false positives among the voxels you declare active. At an FDR of 5%, you accept that about 1 in 20 of your active voxels may be a false alarm. It is more lenient and often more useful when you expect many true effects.

A third idea is to judge clusters, not voxels. Real activations tend to form blobs while noise forms specks. So, first apply a cluster-forming threshold, then ask how big a cluster must be to be unlikely under noise. This is cluster-level inference, very popular, and it has a trap that we will open in the next episode.

Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement, or TFCE, proposed by Stephen Smith and Thomas Nichols in 2009, avoids choosing that first threshold. Each voxel gets a score that combines its height and the size of the blob it belongs to across all possible thresholds.

Finally, permutation tests build the null distribution from your own data. Shuffle the labels, for example flip the sign of each participant's contrast at random, recompute the map thousands of times, and record the largest value each time. The 95th percentile of those maxima is your corrected threshold. Few assumptions, more computation. FSL's randomise does this.

Which one should you use? Decide before you look, report it clearly, and never pick the method that happens to give the prettiest map.

To sum up, a map is 100,000 tests, and an uncorrected threshold lets about 100 false positives through on pure noise. The dead salmon made it famous. Bonferroni is strict, random field theory uses smoothness, FDR controls the proportion of false alarms, cluster methods use the size of blobs, and permutations learn the null from your data. PLATE 5.64:41

Cluster failure

Eklund, Nichols and Knutsson tested cluster inference on resting-state scans analyzed as if a task had occurred, running about 3 million group analyses. Parametric cluster inference with a lenient threshold gave false positive rates far above 5%, because real noise blobs have heavier tails than the Gaussian assumption. The 40,000-study figure was an overstatement; strict thresholds or permutations are the remedy.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we opened a door, judge clusters not voxels. Today, we look at what happened when someone tested that door, very hard.

In 2016, Anders Eklund, Thomas Nichols and Hans Knutsson published a paper in PNAS called Cluster Failure. Their idea was simple and clever. Take resting state data, where nobody does any task. Pretend there was a task anyway, with a made-up block or event design. Then run a standard group analysis. Since there is no real task, any significant result is a false positive. If the methods work, about 5% of analyses should find something.

They used resting state scans from 499 healthy people from the Open 1000 Functional Connectomes project. They drew random groups of 20 or 40 and ran about 3 million group analyses with SPM, FSL, and AFNI across many settings.

The results were uncomfortable. Voxel-wise inference was fine, even conservative. But parametric cluster-level inference often went far above 5%. The worst case came with a cluster-defining threshold of p equal to 0.01. In one setting, the false positive rate reached 70%. Typical one-sample analyses with that threshold were often in the range of 10 to 20% or more. With a stricter cluster-defining threshold, P equal to 0.001, things were much better, though still not always perfect. Non-parametric permutation tests stayed close to 5%.

Why? Back to the atelier. Imagine a rule for spotting a real flaw in fabric. A lump bigger than this template is too big to be chance. The template was designed for a perfectly even weave. But real yarn has slubs, natural thick spots that spread further than expected. On slubby fabric, chance lumps are bigger, and the template flags them as flaws.

In fMRI, the template is the parametric cluster threshold. It assumes that the spatial auto-correlation of the noise has a Gaussian shape, with the same smoothness everywhere. Real fMRI noise has heavier tails. Correlation decays more slowly with distance than a Gaussian predicts. So noise blobs are larger than the theory expects and big enough to pass as clusters, especially when the first threshold is lenient.

There was also a bug. The team found an error in AFNI tool for cluster thresholds, 3dClustSim, which had been fixed in 2015. Fixing it lowered false positives somewhat, but it was not the main story. The main problem was the assumption, shared across packages. Now the nuance, because this paper was widely misread. The original text said the results question the validity of some 40,000 fMRI studies. The authors later corrected this. Not every study uses cluster inference, and the main problem concerns a lenient cluster-defining threshold. Thomas Nichols estimated that about 3,500 papers used that combination. That is around a tenth of the literature, not all of it. And a strong effect usually survives even with a biased p-value.

The AFNI team led by Robert Cox responded in 2017. They agreed on the problem and improved their tools with a new spatial autocorrelation model that has longer tails. Other groups proposed alternatives too.

So what do you do today? Use a strict cluster-defining threshold, p equal to 0.001 or lower, if you use parametric cluster inference. Prefer permutation-based inference or TFCE with permutations, which make fewer assumptions. Report your threshold, software, and version, and share your statistical maps so others can judge.

There is a deeper lesson. The study was only possible because thousands of resting state scans were openly shared and because the software was open. Open data found the problem and open code found the bug.

To sum up, using null resting state data, Eklund and colleagues ran 3 million group analyses. Parametric cluster inference with a lenient threshold gave false positive rates far above 5% because real noise blobs have heavier tails than assumed. The 40,000 figure was an overstatement, later corrected. Use strict thresholds or permutations and share your maps. Next time, Regions of Interest and How to Read a Brain Map

PLATE 5.74:19

Regions of interest, and reading a brain map

Whole-brain analysis pays for 100,000 tests, while a region of interest, chosen independently from an atlas, a localizer or earlier coordinates, trades breadth for power. A brain figure reads like a care label: statistic, threshold, color bar, orientation, coordinates. Thresholded maps hide near-miss effects, so unthresholded maps should be shared and sub-threshold results shown faded rather than removed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We end this chapter with two practical skills. Choosing where to look and reading what you see.

So far we tested every voxel the whole brain. That is the right choice when you have no strong idea of where the effect should be. The price is the correction for 100,000 tests, which we saw in the last two episodes.

But often you do have a hypothesis. A study of faces may care about the fusiform face area. A motor study may care about the hand area. Then you can define a region of interest or ROI and test only there. Back in the atelier, it is like laying a stencil on the fabric. You only inspect what falls inside the stencil. Fewer tests, so less correction and more power.

An ROI can be defined in several ways. From an anatomical atlas, for example, a labeled region in standard space, like our template friend Atlas. From a separate localizer run, a short scan designed only to find the region in each person, or from a sphere around coordinates reported by earlier studies.

There is one golden rule. The ROI must be chosen independently of the data you test. If you pick the voxels that responded most in your contrast, and then test that same contrast inside them, the result is guaranteed to look good. That is circular analysis, also called double dipping, and we will give it a full episode in chapter 9. Define the ROI before you look or with independent data.

Inside the ROI, you can average the signal over its voxels and run one test, or test each voxel with a small correction. Averaging is simple and robust, but it dilutes an effect that sits in only part of the region.

Now the second skill, reading a brain map. A published figure is like a garment care label. It packs a lot of information into a few symbols, and you need to read everyone.

First, the statistic. Is the color showing a T value, a Z score, a beta, or a percent signal change? They are not interchangeable.

Second the threshold. Which value and which correction? Voxel-wise or cluster-wise, FWE or FDR or uncorrected. A map at P less than 0.001 uncorrected, and a map at FWE 5% tell very different stories.

Third, the color bar. Where does it start and end? The same data can look dramatic or modest depending on the scale.

Fourth, orientation. In the radiological convention, the left of the brain is shown on the right of the image. In the neurological convention, left is left. A small L or R on the figure saves a lot of confusion.

Fifth coordinates. Peaks are reported in a standard space, often MNI. A coordinate tells you where the strongest voxel was, not where the whole effect lives. And as we saw in chapter 4, the same coordinate is not always the same area in every brain.

Finally the most important point. A thresholded map shows only what crossed a line. Everything below disappears, and an effect just under the threshold looks identical to no effect at all. Two studies can look contradictory, while their unthresholded maps are very similar.

So share the unthresholded map. NeuroVault, created by Chris Gorgolewski and colleagues, stores whole-brain statistical maps, so anyone can download them, compare them, and use them in meta-analyses. And in your figures, consider highlighting instead of hiding. In 2023, Paul Taylor, Gang Chen, and colleagues argued for showing results above threshold in full color, while keeping the rest visible but faded. Like a sheer fabric, you see the pattern on top and still see what lies beneath.

To sum up, whole-brain analysis needs heavy correction, and ROI trades breadth for power. Define the ROI independently from an atlas, a localizer or prior coordinates. Read every map like a care label, statistic, threshold, color bar, orientation, coordinates. Share unthresholded maps and highlight rather than hide. That closes chapter 5.
