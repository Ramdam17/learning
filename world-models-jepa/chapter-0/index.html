What does it mean to have a model of the world?

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the magic lantern room. A lantern throws a tiny picture of a room onto a hanging sheet. The room on the sheet is not real, yet everything in it follows the rules of the real one. Over this series, we will chase one big question: what does it mean to have a model of the world?

Meet Rovi, a small robot. Rovi rolls along a table, and at the edge of the table stands a glass. Rovi could push it. But before anything moves, a thought bubble opens above its head. In the bubble, the glass slides off the edge and falls.

Rovi has not touched anything. It imagined the push, and it saw the consequence. So it leaves the glass alone. That is the whole idea. A model of the world is a small version of reality that you can run in your head, or inside a machine, to see what would happen. In 1943, the Scottish psychologist Kenneth Craik suggested exactly this: an organism that carries a small-scale model of reality can try out alternatives before it acts. Rovi's bubble is a small-scale model. Imagining is cheaper than breaking a glass.

How can we tell whether someone has such a model? We watch what surprises them. Here is a young child. A ball rolls behind a screen, and it never comes out on the other side. The child stares. This example is illustrative, but the method is real. Psychologists have used it since the nineteen eighties. In a well-known study from 1985, five-month-old infants looked longer at an impossible event than at a possible one. How far to trust such experiments is still debated. The logic, though, is simple. Surprise is a measurement. It is the gap between what the model expected and what really happened.

Keep that gap in mind. Prediction on one side, reality on the other. It will come back again and again.

Now, the promise. We start very small, with a line fitted to a handful of points. A line has two knobs, and it already predicts one number from another. We will turn the knobs, and learn why a machine learns by making one number smaller. Then come necklaces of numbers, and slopes that tell a marble which way is downhill. Then neural networks, and the art of finding good representations. Then learning without anyone giving the answers. Then agents that act. And at the end of the road: machines that imagine what happens next, before they act, just like Rovi.

Some of these ideas have a history. In 2018, David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber published a paper called World Models, in which an agent learns a compact model of its world, and even trains inside its own dream. In 2022, Yann LeCun posted a position paper called A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence. It proposes an architecture named JEPA, which predicts the representation of what is missing instead of every pixel.

Is JEPA the right way? Nobody knows yet. It is one proposal among several, and the debate is open. In this series, we show the arguments and the evidence, with dates, and we do not pick a side.

To sum up. A model of the world is a small version of reality that lets you try things out before you act. Surprise measures the gap between what you expected and what happened. And learning, from a line on a few points to a robot that imagines, is about choosing what to predict. Next time: a model is a function with knobs.
