PLATE 1.13:56

Shocking fish and twitching frogs

A torpedo ray, a frog and a stack of metal discs lead to one question: can electricity talk to a nerve? Galvani saw frog legs twitch and proposed animal electricity, while Volta traced it to two metals and built the first battery. Both were partly right: a nerve carries an electrical signal, and an outside current can start one, though its dose was uncontrolled.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the cabinet of curiosities. Every shelf holds an object that someone once used to poke the nervous system. Today we visit the oldest ones. A fish, a frog, and a stack of metal discs. And one question that took centuries to ask properly. Can electricity talk to a nerve?

Start with the fish in the jar. The torpedo is a ray with an electric organ on each side of its body. It can deliver a real shock. In the first century, a Roman physician named Scribonius Largus collected remedies in a book called the Compositiones, around the year forty-seven. According to historians who have read it, he recommended a live torpedo against headaches, and against gout. This is reported tradition. We have the recipe, not a measurement. Nobody knows what the patients felt, or whether it helped. And Largus had no idea that the shock was electrical.

That idea came much later. In seventeen seventy-two, the English naturalist John Walsh travelled to the French coast near La Rochelle, and tested the torpedo with a series of experiments. His conclusion, published in seventeen seventy-three, was that the fish's shock behaves like the discharge of a Leyden jar, the early electrical capacitor of the day. Some contemporaries objected, because no spark was visible. The shock was electrical, even without one.

Now the frog. In the late eighteenth century, Luigi Galvani, an anatomist in Bologna, was studying the legs of dissected frogs. He noticed that they twitched when a spark from an electrical machine passed nearby, or when the nerve was touched at the right moment. In seventeen ninety-one he published his results. His interpretation was bold. The twitch, he said, came from an electricity carried by the animal itself. He called it animal electricity.

A physicist in Pavia, Alessandro Volta, did not accept this. He noticed that Galvani's best results came when the nerve and the muscle were joined by two different metals. Perhaps, said Volta, the electricity came from the metals. The frog was only a very sensitive detector.

To settle it, Volta built a new object. Discs of two different metals, stacked with brine-soaked cardboard in between. In a letter dated the twentieth of March eighteen hundred, he described this pile to the Royal Society in London. It was the first battery. It gave a steady current, with no animal involved.

Galvani's side answered with a result of its own. In seventeen ninety-four, an anonymous book from his circle reported muscles twitching without any metal at all. Galvani's nephew, Giovanni Aldini, defended the idea in public demonstrations in the early eighteen hundreds, long before research had ethical rules, a subject chapter eleven returns to.

So who was right? Both. Volta was right that two metals and a wet contact produce a current. That insight led to the whole field of electrochemistry. But Galvani was right that living tissue carries electrical signals. In the eighteen forties, Emil du Bois-Reymond used a very sensitive galvanometer, and picked up a change in the electrical state of a nerve while it carried an impulse. The nerve was not a wire. It produced its own signal.

Put the two stories side by side, and you get the principle of this whole series. A nerve carries an electrical signal. And a current applied from the outside can start one. Within a century, a fish, a frog, a pile of discs and a galvanometer had shown both halves.

What was missing? Control. Nobody knew how much current reached the nerve, or where. That is the thread we follow for the next seventy episodes.

PLATE 1.25:57

Maps drawn by stimulation

Stimulating the cortex drew the first maps of the brain, from Fritsch and Hitzig's dogs to Penfield's awake patients and the homunculus. A stimulation map shows that a point is sufficient to trigger a movement, not that it is necessary, like pressing one organ key and noting which pipe sounds. Activity also spreads along connections, and the result depends on how the cortex is stimulated.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cabinet of curiosities. Last time, we saw that a current from outside can start a signal in a nerve. Today the question moves into the brain. If a current can start a signal, can it also tell us where in the brain things happen? For a long time, that was what stimulation was for. It drew maps, long before it treated anyone.

Berlin, eighteen seventy. Two young physicians, Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig, publish a paper called On the electrical excitability of the cerebrum. Their institute had no animal facilities. According to the historian Charles Gross, they strapped their dogs down on Mrs Hitzig's dressing table.

They opened the skull, and touched the surface of the cortex with two fine platinum wires. The current came from a battery. It was galvanic current, the same kind that Volta's pile gave: brief pulses of direct current. They kept it as weak as they could. Their gauge, Gross reports, was their own tongue. They used the smallest current that they could just feel there.

Touch the front of the cortex, and a muscle twitched on the opposite side of the body. A neck, a foreleg, the face. Move the wire a little, and a different muscle moved. Touch the back of the cortex, and nothing happened. The sites that twitched formed a sparse but repeatable map of movements, laid out on the surface of the brain. Historians count this among the first strong evidence that the cortex is electrically excitable, and organized by place.

They also did the opposite test. They removed one of those sites. The matching movement was weakened, but not erased. Keep that detail in mind. We will need it in a few minutes.

Now, six decades later, to Montreal. The Montreal Neurological Institute opened in nineteen thirty-four, founded by the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield. Penfield operated on people with severe epilepsy. His problem was a surgeon's problem: which tissue to remove, and which tissue to spare. So he used the idea of Fritsch and Hitzig, on people. He placed a small electrode on the exposed cortex, and asked what happened.

For this, the patient had to be awake. The scalp and skull were numbed with local anaesthetic, and the brain itself has no pain receptors. So the patient could stay awake, and talk. Only a person who is awake can say: my thumb is tingling, or I felt something on my lip.

In nineteen thirty-seven, Penfield and his trainee Edwin Boldrey published their results in the journal Brain. They drew on a hundred and twenty-six patients, operated on between nineteen twenty-eight and nineteen thirty-six. Some of those operations came before the Institute even opened. Point by point, they marked on the brain which body part moved, or felt something, from toes to tongue.

Penfield wanted a picture that students could remember. A medical illustrator, Hortense Cantlie, drew it. The first figure, in nineteen thirty-seven, looked like an acrobat hanging by his knees. Body parts were drawn roughly in proportion to how much cortex answered for them, so the hands, the lips and the tongue came out huge. In nineteen fifty, Penfield and Theodore Rasmussen published The Cerebral Cortex of Man, with a revised, better-proportioned figure. That is the homunculus, the little man.

Penfield himself added a warning. Such drawings, he wrote, may easily become confusing if too much significance is attributed to their shape and comparative size. Later work agrees. In two thousand seventeen, the neurologist Marco Catani described the homunculus as a very rough approximation. The original maps show overlap between body parts, not tidy, separate territories. And a drawing made strictly from the data, one team found, would not look like a little man at all.

So here is the moment that matters. What does a stimulation map actually say?

Picture an organ, in a cabinet. A tester presses one key at a time, and writes down which pipe speaks. After every key, the chart is clear: this key, that pipe. The chart is exact. And it says one thing. Pressing this key is enough to make this pipe sound. In the vocabulary of logic, the key is sufficient.

It does not say that the pipe needs this key. Another keyboard, or a coupler, could sound the same pipe. To find out whether a key is necessary, you must switch it off, and listen for what disappears. Stimulation can only give you the first kind of answer.

The brain is the same, with an extra difficulty. A spot you stimulate does not act alone. Activity spreads along its connections, so part of what you see may come from other places. And what a spot seems to do depends on how you stimulate. In two thousand and two, Graziano and colleagues stimulated the motor cortex of monkeys for half a second at a time. Instead of single twitches, they saw coordinated postures, involving many joints at once, arranged across the cortex as a map of hand positions around the body.

Now you see why the detail from Berlin matters. When Fritsch and Hitzig removed a site, the movement was weakened, not erased. A place that can trigger a movement is not the only place that makes it.

To sum up. Fritsch and Hitzig showed in dogs, in eighteen seventy, that stimulating the cortex moves muscles. Penfield and his team did it in awake patients, and drew the best-known maps of the human cortex. A stimulation map says: stimulating this point is enough to trigger this movement. It does not say: this point is needed for this movement. Stimulation was a mapping tool before it was a treatment, and the Montreal procedure is still performed around the world today.

PLATE 1.35:59

From electroconvulsive therapy to TMS

Electroconvulsive therapy is a hammer that triggers a seizure in the whole brain and cannot choose a target. Scalp shocks fired the motor cortex but woke the pain nerves of the scalp, the doorman at the front door. A brief, strong magnetic pulse passes through bone almost untouched, and the 1985 Sheffield coil made a hand twitch with no incision.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cabinet of curiosities, two objects sit side by side on the same shelf. Both send electricity into a head. One was built as a hammer. The other was built to knock softly. Today we follow both, from nineteen thirty-four to nineteen eighty-five.

First, the hammer. In nineteen thirty-four, a Hungarian psychiatrist named László Meduna tried an idea. He believed that epilepsy and schizophrenia could not coexist in the same patient. So perhaps a seizure could relieve the illness. On the second of January, he gave his first injection of camphor to induce one. Camphor was later replaced by a drug called cardiazol, also sold as metrazol. The seizures worked, but they were unpredictable. And the wait between the injection and the seizure was frightening for patients.

In Rome, the psychiatrist Ugo Cerletti and his assistant Lucio Bini looked for a cleaner trigger. They tried electricity. In April nineteen thirty-eight, they gave the first electrical treatment to a patient. Accounts differ on the exact day. According to one detailed history, the first attempt on the eleventh produced no seizure, and a stronger shock nine days later did. Unlike cardiazol, the current acted at once, with no frightening wait. This was electroconvulsive therapy, or ECT. It spread across the world within a few years, and it largely replaced the chemical method.

The first decades were rough. At first, ECT was often given unmodified. The patient was awake when the current passed, and the seizure was a full convulsion of the body. By the early nineteen fifties, anaesthesia and muscle relaxants had changed this. Electrode placement and the shape of the electrical pulse were refined over the following decades, to spare memory. Today ECT is given under general anaesthesia, with a muscle relaxant.

So why does ECT still carry a bad reputation? Mostly because of its early years. Unmodified treatments, historical misuse, and a few famous films left an image that modern practice does not deserve. This episode is history, not a regulatory statement. The point here is the method. ECT is a hammer. It triggers a seizure in the whole brain. It cannot choose a target.

Now, a different ambition. Could we reach one place in the brain, and nowhere else? Picture the head as a house with a thick front door. That door is the skull. Right behind it lives a doorman. He is the pain nerves of the scalp. Deep inside, in one room, a lamp. That lamp is the motor cortex. Reaching the lamp means getting past the doorman without waking him.

In nineteen eighty, the physiologists Merton and Morton, in London, tried it with electricity. They placed electrodes on the scalp and gave brief, high-voltage shocks. A muscle in the hand twitched. For the first time, someone had fired the motor cortex of an awake person through the intact skull. They published it in Nature. But most of the current flowed through the scalp and woke the doorman. Later reviews describe the method as painful, because the shock activates pain fibres in the scalp. It was too uncomfortable for routine use.

The answer came from an old observation. In eighteen ninety-six, the French physician Jacques-Arsène d'Arsonval put his head near a large coil carrying an alternating current, at forty-two hertz. He saw flickering light. This was a magnetophosphene. In nineteen ten, the British physicist Silvanus Thompson repeated the experiment, with a subject's head inside a coil. A changing magnetic field passes through skin and bone almost untouched. Inside the head, it induces a small electric current. Much less current runs through the scalp, so the doorman stays asleep.

There was one catch, which we know only in hindsight. By the late nineteen forties, researchers had shown that those flickers came from the retina, not from the cortex. The field was strong enough to excite the eye. It was not yet strong enough to fire the brain. That needed a much stronger, much faster pulse.

That is what a small team in Sheffield built. Anthony Barker, Reza Jalinous and Ian Freeston discharged a large capacitor into a coil, in a single, very brief, very strong pulse. On the eleventh of May, nineteen eighty-five, The Lancet published their one-page letter. Its title says it all: Non-invasive magnetic stimulation of human motor cortex. Volume one, issue eight thousand four hundred thirty-seven, pages eleven-oh-six to eleven-oh-seven.

Hold the coil over the motor cortex, send one pulse, and a muscle of the hand twitches, a fraction of a second later. No incision. No electrode. And far less discomfort than the electrical shock of nineteen eighty. For the first time, one could fire a human motor pathway through the skull, without opening the head, and without the pain that had stopped the electrical method.

Notice what the twitch tells you, and what it does not. It tells you the pathway from the cortex to the muscle is working. It does not tell you what the stimulated tissue was doing. That distinction runs through the entire series. Chapter three opens the machine and shows how this pulse is made and how it is used.

To sum up. ECT, introduced by Cerletti and Bini in nineteen thirty-eight, replaced chemical seizures, and was later made safer with anaesthesia and muscle relaxants. It acts on the whole brain. Electrical stimulation through the scalp reached the motor cortex in nineteen eighty, but it was painful. Magnetic fields, from d'Arsonval's flickers in eighteen ninety-six to the Sheffield coil in nineteen eighty-five, passed through the skull and made the hand move.

The long road to deep brain stimulation

Deep brain stimulation grew from stereotactic frames and permanent lesions in the thalamus and pallidum for Parkinson's disease. After levodopa nearly emptied the operating rooms, Benabid found in 1987 that current above about one hundred hertz stops tremor as a lesion does. A lesion is a wall built once; stimulation is a door that can be opened or shut, though the electrode still means surgery.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cabinet of curiosities. The last shelf of this chapter holds a frame, a lesion, and a switch. It tells how a tool for destroying a small piece of brain became a device that can be turned down, or turned off.

Start with the frame. In nineteen oh eight, the surgeon Victor Horsley and the physiologist Robert Clarke published a method in the journal Brain. They had built a frame that holds an animal's head still. Inside it, any point of the brain is described by three numbers: three distances, along three perpendicular directions. Their first targets were the deep nuclei of the cerebellum.

In nineteen forty-seven, Spiegel and Wycis adapted the idea to people. Their frame was fixed to the patient's head with a plaster cast, and their paper described lesions in a deep nucleus of the thalamus. Historians disagree on which operation was the very first, so we only keep the year.

A frame lets you reach a point. What do you do once you are there? In the nineteen fifties, you destroy it. Surgeons made small lesions in the thalamus or in the pallidum to treat the tremor and stiffness of Parkinson's disease. In nineteen fifty-five, Hassler reported that thalamotomy worked better than pallidotomy for tremor, and Cooper took the same road. Before cutting, surgeons probed the exact spot with electrical stimulation. But the lesion itself was permanent. Once the tissue is gone, it is gone.

Then came a drug. In nineteen sixty-seven, Cotzias and colleagues reported that levodopa, given by mouth, had a dramatic and lasting effect on severely disabled patients. By one account, within five to ten years virtually all surgery for Parkinson's disease had stopped.

But years of treatment brought new problems: swings between good and bad hours, and involuntary movements called dyskinesias. Surgery came back. In nineteen ninety-two, Laitinen and colleagues reported pallidotomy in thirty-eight patients. Ninety-two percent had complete or almost complete relief of rigidity and slowness. It was a series without a control group, and the lesion was still permanent.

Now Grenoble. In nineteen eighty-seven, Alim-Louis Benabid and his colleagues were operating on patients with tremor. As usual, they probed the target with electrical stimulation before lesioning. They found that at frequencies above about one hundred hertz, the tremor stopped. When the current was withdrawn, the tremor came back. It was the effect of a lesion, without the lesion. That year they reported patients with tremor on both sides, with a lesion on one side and a chronic stimulator on the other, in the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus.

That is the turn of the story. A lesion is a wall of bricks: you build it once, and you live with it. Stimulation is a door in the same wall. It can be opened, half closed, or taken out of service. High-frequency stimulation acts like a lesion, but it is adjustable and reversible. Why it acts that way is still debated, and episode six-two returns to it.

In nineteen ninety-five, Limousin, Pollak, Benabid and colleagues published in The Lancet a new target. Three patients with advanced Parkinson's disease received electrodes in both subthalamic nuclei. Three months later, their motor scores had improved by between forty-two and eighty-four percent. Three patients, and no control group. But the door was open.

Now the authorisations. This is history, a list of dated firsts. It is not the status today. In Europe, a review reports a CE mark for tremor in nineteen ninety-three, and trade press reports one for Parkinson's disease in nineteen ninety-eight. We found no regulator record for either, so read both as reported. In the United States, the FDA approved the first system on the thirty-first of July, nineteen ninety-seven. It was for stimulation on one side of the thalamus, against tremor of the upper limb, essential or parkinsonian, not controlled by medication. On the fourteenth of January, two thousand two, the FDA approved stimulation on both sides, in the internal pallidum or the subthalamic nucleus, as an add-on treatment for advanced, levodopa-responsive Parkinson's disease. For Canada, we could not verify when the first licence was granted, so that line stays empty rather than guessed. Today's licensed indications are checked and dated in chapter twelve.

One trap. Reversible does not mean harmless. The stimulation can be switched off and its settings can be changed, but the electrode stays in the brain, and placing it is still brain surgery. How the lead, the box and the settings work is the story of chapter six.

To sum up. A frame turned the brain into coordinates. Lesions treated tremor, and levodopa nearly emptied the operating rooms. Then a current above one hundred hertz was found to act like a lesion, but adjustable and reversible. The first approvals followed in the nineteen nineties.
