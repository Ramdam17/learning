PLATE 3.124:00

The reference is a space

Every channel is a difference, so the reference is a point of view, like moving the zero mark on a tide gauge. Re-referencing shifts all channels together, preserving electrode differences but changing waveforms and polarities. Cz, linked mastoids and the common average each assume something different, and the N170 shrinks as the reference nears the occiput, so the choice must be justified and reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every EEG channel is a difference. The amplifier compares one electrode with another, and there is no absolute zero anywhere on the head. So the choice of that other point, the reference, is not a technical detail. It is a choice of space, a point of view on the data.

Think of measuring altitude. A mountain's height is always measured from some zero, usually sea level. Move the zero mark on the tide gauge, and every altitude you read changes. The landscape itself does not move. The biomedical engineer David Geselowitz used almost exactly this image, a lake whose water level rises and falls over a mountain range.

Let's write it down. A recorded channel is the potential at the electrode minus the potential at the reference. Re-referencing means subtracting something new, the same something from every channel, at every instant. At each moment, the whole map shifts up or down together. The differences between electrodes stay exactly the same. What changes is where zero sits, and therefore which places look positive, which look negative, and how big each waveform appears.

Let's try three common choices on the same data, an illustrative evoked response at the back of the head.

First, a vertex reference, at Cz. Cz is set to zero by definition. Anything happening at the vertex disappears there, and shows up, inverted, in every other channel.

Second, linked mastoids, the average of the two electrodes behind the ears. A classic for evoked potentials, and it keeps you comparable with decades of studies. But the mastoids are not silent. They sit close to temporal and posterior sources, so effects there are partly subtracted away.

This is not a hypothetical worry. Carrie Joyce and Bruno Rossion re-referenced the same face responses to several references in 2005. The face sensitive N170, measured at occipito temporal sites, shrank as the reference moved closer to the back of the head, while its frontal mirror image, the VPP, grew. Same brains, same data, a different story at each electrode.

Third, the common average. Subtract the mean of all electrodes. The idea comes from physics: over a closed surface surrounding all the sources, the potential sums to zero. So with many electrodes covering the whole head, the average approaches a neutral reference.

But we only cover the top of the head. With sparse or uneven coverage, the average is biased, and the bias is largest at the edges of the montage. Markus Junghöfer and colleagues called this the polar average reference effect. And if one channel is noisy, averaging spreads its noise into all the others. Clean your bad channels first.

The reference also travels into every later measure. Whatever the reference picks up, alpha, muscle, a heartbeat, is now shared by every channel. Spectra inherit it, and connectivity can rise between all pairs for no other reason than a common reference.

So which one is right? None of them is the true zero. Each makes an assumption: a silent point, a silent pair, or a well-sampled sphere. Pick the one that fits your montage and your question, keep it consistent across conditions and participants, and report it. In MNE-Python, it takes a single line of code.

Let's recap. EEG has no absolute zero. Re-referencing shifts every channel by the same amount at each instant, so differences between electrodes are preserved, but waveforms, polarities and apparent effects change. Cz, linked mastoids and the average each assume something different. The reference is a point of view. Choose it, justify it, and report it.

PLATE 3.135:03

Surface Laplacian and REST

The surface Laplacian is the local curvature of the scalp potential, like asking how much the sea bulges against its neighbours. It is reference free, estimates radial current, and acts as a spatial high pass filter that cuts volume conduction but attenuates broad sources. REST instead uses a head model to rebuild a reference at infinity, and is only as good as that model.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that every reference is a point of view, and that none of them is a true zero. Today, two tools that try to step out of that trap. The surface Laplacian, which gets rid of the reference altogether. And REST, which tries to rebuild a reference at infinity.

Picture the sea. A long, slow swell lifts the whole surface, and on top of it ride small, sharp ripples. Now imagine you only care about the ripples. You do not ask how high the water is. You ask, at each point, how much the surface bulges compared with its immediate neighbours. That question does not care about the water level, and it barely notices the swell.

That is the surface Laplacian. Mathematically, it is the second spatial derivative of the potential along the scalp: its local curvature. The simplest version, proposed by Bo Hjorth in 1975, takes each electrode and subtracts the mean of its nearest neighbours. Modern versions fit a smooth surface through all electrodes, usually with spherical splines, following François Perrin and colleagues in 1989, and compute the curvature of that surface.

Why is it reference free? Re-referencing adds the same value to every electrode at a given instant. A constant has no curvature. So whatever the original reference was, the Laplacian comes out the same.

And what does curvature mean physically? It estimates the current flowing radially through the skull and into the scalp, at each location. That is why it is also called current source density, or C S D. Where current leaves the head, we see a source. Where it enters, a sink.

Watch what it does to a topography. Here is an illustrative map of two nearby sources under a broad smear. The potential map shows one wide blob. The Laplacian map shows two sharp, separate spots. It acts as a spatial high pass filter: it keeps local detail and removes broad patterns.

That is a strength and a limit. Volume conduction spreads activity widely across the scalp, so a spatial high pass filter removes much of it. Neighbouring electrodes become more independent, which makes the Laplacian a good friend of connectivity analyses. But truly broad activity, from deep or very widespread generators, is attenuated too. The Laplacian favours superficial, focal sources.

A few practical rules. It needs good electrode density, and estimates at the edge of the montage are less reliable. It is sensitive to noisy channels, because a bad electrode corrupts the curvature of all its neighbours. And its units change, from microvolts to microvolts per square metre. In MNE-Python, it is computed by compute current source density.

Now the second tool: REST, the reference electrode standardization technique, published by Dezhong Yao in 2001. The idea is to measure every electrode against a point infinitely far away, a point where the brain's potential is truly zero.

Back at sea, it is like measuring wave heights not from a buoy that bobs on the swell, but from a fixed point far away, out at the horizon.

How can we reach infinity? With a head model. We assume the scalp data were produced by sources inside the head, through a known lead field. We estimate equivalent sources from the data as recorded, then project them back onto the electrodes as they would appear against infinity. Note that we never need to trust those sources individually. They are only a bridge.

The catch is that REST is only as good as its head model and its coverage. Errors in conductivities, positions or electrode density all leak into the result. In noise free simulations with a realistic head model, it can beat the common average. But in a 2015 comparison by Liu and colleagues, that advantage vanished when electrode positions were imprecise, and with a dense montage the average did better. In MNE-Python, it is a reference option that takes a forward model.

So which one, when? Use the Laplacian when you want sharp, local, reference free maps, or connectivity with less volume conduction. Use REST, or the average, when you want potentials you can compare with the rest of the literature, and broad patterns matter. Many labs report both potentials and their Laplacian.

Let's recap. The surface Laplacian is the local curvature of the scalp potential. It is reference free, it estimates radial current, and it acts as a spatial high pass filter that reduces volume conduction but also attenuates broad sources. REST rebuilds a reference at infinity using a head model, and it is only as good as that model.

PLATE 3.144:36

Sensor space, source space, component space

Sensors, sources and components are different projections of one channels by time matrix. A spatial filter is a recipe of weights over electrodes, like mixing juices, and covers references, Laplacians, ICA, beamformers and CSP. A pattern, how a source shows on electrodes, differs from a filter and is what to interpret, because no projection adds information beyond the data's rank.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have met many spaces in this chapter. Today we close it with a simple idea that ties a whole family of them together. Sensors, sources and components are not different data. They are different projections of the same data.

Start with what we record: a matrix. One row per electrode, one column per time sample. Sixty four rows, maybe a few hundred thousand columns. Every method in this episode starts from that matrix, and multiplies it by another one.

Picture a juice bar. Each electrode is a jar of juice. A new channel is a recipe: so much from this jar, so much from that one, and sometimes a negative amount, which means taking some away. That recipe is a spatial filter. A list of weights, one per electrode. Multiply, add up, and you get one new time series.

You have already used spatial filters without calling them that. Re-referencing to the average is a recipe: with sixty four electrodes, each channel minus one sixty-fourth of every channel. A bipolar montage is a recipe with two ingredients, plus one and minus one. And the Hjorth Laplacian from last episode: the centre electrode, minus a quarter of each of its four neighbours. Stack several recipes as rows, and you have a matrix that turns sensor space into another space.

Now the reverse direction. Suppose there is a source, somewhere, with its own time course. Through volume conduction, it reaches every electrode, with a different strength at each. That set of strengths is the source's pattern, its fingerprint on the scalp. The recorded data are, to a good approximation, a weighted sum of patterns, each multiplied by its source's time course, plus noise.

So there are two kinds of maps, and they are easy to confuse. A pattern tells you how a source shows up on the electrodes. A filter tells you how to combine the electrodes to recover the source. They are not the same. A good filter often gives large weights to electrodes far from the source, simply to cancel noise. Stefan Haufe and colleagues made this point in 2014: to interpret a linear model, look at its patterns, not at its filter weights. Patterns can be computed from the filters and the data covariance.

Three famous families are just three ways of choosing the recipes.

Independent component analysis, in chapter seven, chooses filters so that the outputs are as statistically independent as possible. Its unmixing matrix holds the filters. The columns of its inverse, the mixing matrix, hold the patterns, the topographies you inspect to spot a blink.

Source imaging, in chapter thirteen, uses a head model. A beamformer, for instance, builds one filter per brain location, designed to pass that location and suppress everything else. Minimum norm methods build a whole matrix at once that maps sixty four electrodes to thousands of points on the cortex.

Common spatial patterns, in chapter fourteen, chooses filters whose output variance is high in one condition and low in the other, like left hand versus right hand imagery. Despite its name, what it optimises are filters. The patterns come afterwards.

One rule holds for all of them. A projection cannot create information. If you have sixty four electrodes, there are at most sixty four independent directions in your data, and fewer after an average reference or an interpolated channel. Thousands of source points are an estimate drawn from those few dimensions. They do not hold more information than the sixty four channels they came from. That is why spatial resolution in source space has limits, and why data rank matters so much for ICA.

So when you meet a new method, ask three questions. What are its weights chosen to do? Where do you read its patterns? And how many dimensions really go in?

Let's recap. The data are a channels by time matrix. A spatial filter is a weighted sum of electrodes, and a matrix of filters moves you into another space: a reference, a Laplacian, components, sources. A pattern is how a source projects onto the electrodes, and it is what you interpret. ICA, beamformers and CSP are different recipes for choosing the weights. And no projection adds information.

That closes our tour of representation spaces. Next, a new chapter: the rhythms of the brain, and what lies beneath the peaks of the spectrum.
