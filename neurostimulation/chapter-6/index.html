Deep brain stimulation: lead, box and settings

A deep brain stimulation system is a thin lead with metal contacts, a cable and a pulse generator under the chest skin, delivering brief pulses about one hundred thirty times a second, continuously. Four dials set the dose: active contacts, amplitude, pulse width and frequency. What is stimulated is a volume, not a point, shaped by directional leads, and tuning remains largely empirical.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have gone underground. In the mine, the star is a long, thin wire with a small box at its other end: deep brain stimulation. This episode is about the hardware, and the dials on it. How it might work is the next episode. Who it is for belongs to chapter twelve. And how to model the field it makes comes in chapter seven.

Meet LEAD, laid out on the workbench like a miner's headlamp kit. At one end, the lamp: a thin lead, about one and a quarter millimetres across, depending on the model, with metal rings near its tip. These are the contacts. At the other end, the battery pack: a pulse generator, in a pocket under the skin of the chest wall. Between them runs an extension cable, tunnelled under the skin from the head to the chest. And a clinician's programmer talks to the box through the skin, to change its settings from the outside.

Here is the principle in one image. The battery pack sends brief electrical pulses, over and over, down the cable to the contacts. The pulses leave through one or more active contacts, and spread into the tissue around the tip. In the most common set-up, called monopolar, the active contact is the negative pole, and the metal case of the box is the return.

What do the pulses reach? Not only a nucleus. They reach the tissue around the active contacts: cell bodies, but above all axons, some passing through, some leaving the nucleus. In episode two point two, models showed axons following each pulse, even when the cell body was quiet. So stimulating one spot can reach a whole network. Whether that explains the benefit is the question of episode six point two.

Now the geometry. The classic lead has four contacts. Each is one and a half millimetres long. The gap between them is either half a millimetre or one and a half millimetres, depending on the model. So the four contacts together span seven and a half, or ten and a half, millimetres of tissue. Choosing the active contact is like choosing which lamp to light.

Then came the directional lead. Its contacts are cut into segments. A typical design has eight: a ring at each end, and between them two rows of three segments, each covering about a third of the circumference. The pattern is written one, three, three, one. Light a single segment, and the current is pushed to one side of the lead, instead of all around it. Think of a lamp with a shutter.

The first tests in patients were intraoperative studies, published in twenty fourteen. They compared the therapeutic window: the gap between the current that helps and the current that causes side effects. The window was wider with the best single segment than with a ring. In one study, about forty per cent wider. Directional leads for routine clinical use reached Europe around twenty fifteen, depending on the device.

Now the dose card. A DBS card has the same rows as any other. Most of them are settings you choose. First, which contacts are active. Second, the amplitude. Third, the pulse width. Fourth, the frequency. And one more choice: current mode, or voltage mode.

Amplitude. In one clinic's group of movement-disorder patients, the median setting was three volts, and the middle half of patients sat between about two and three and a half. Programmers raise the amplitude in small steps, a tenth to half a volt or milliamp at a time, up to about five, until side effects appear.

Pulse width. Sixty microseconds is the common default. Sixty to one hundred twenty microseconds is the usual range, depending on the study. Shorter pulses widened the therapeutic window in several studies.

Frequency. One hundred thirty hertz is the standard. Values up to about one hundred eighty five hertz are used, and some studies try lower rates, around sixty hertz.

And the duration row? Continuous. There are no sessions. The pulses run day and night, unlike a course of TMS.

These dials trade against each other. The charge in one pulse is the amplitude times the pulse width. Three milliamps for sixty microseconds is about zero point one eight microcoulombs. A longer pulse needs less amplitude to recruit the same fibres, but tends to bring more side effects. And settings decide battery life. A non-rechargeable box lasts about three to five years. Rechargeable boxes are estimated at fifteen to twenty five years, which is not yet confirmed.

Current or voltage? In voltage mode, the box fixes the voltage. The current that flows is the voltage divided by the impedance of the tissue around the contact. In one clinic's group, the median impedance was about seven hundred ohms, and it drifted down by a few tens of ohms a year. At three volts, that is about four milliamps, creeping up as impedance falls. In current mode, the box holds the current steady, and adjusts the voltage. This is why constant current is increasingly used. Whether the difference matters clinically is not yet clear.

One more change in the hardware. The lead that sends pulses can also listen. Since around twenty twelve, an investigational generator could record local field potentials from the contacts. Since around twenty twenty, commercial generators do it chronically, while stimulating. That is the door to episode six point three.

So what do we know, and what is debated? The benefit for some conditions is established, and chapter twelve gives each one with its regulatory stamp. How it works is debated: that is episode six point two. And how to choose the settings? A classic review called tuning an essentially empirical, ad hoc process. A twenty nineteen review says it still depends to a large extent on personal experience.

Here is the classic trap. Calling the target a point. The tip of the lead sits at a coordinate, but what is stimulated is a volume. Its size and shape depend on the amplitude, the pulse width, which contacts are active, the impedance, and the anatomy of this patient. Two patients with the same coordinates and the same settings do not get the same volume. Modelling that volume is chapter seven.

To sum up. A lead with contacts, a cable, and a box. Brief pulses, about one hundred thirty a second, running continuously. Four dials: contacts, amplitude, pulse width and frequency, plus a choice of mode. Directional leads shape the volume, and sensing lets the box listen.

How does DBS work? Nobody fully knows

One tap silences a humming radio, and four stories explain it for deep brain stimulation: pull the wire (a functional lesion), drown it out (a quiet cell body with a driven axon), shake the cable (distant axons carry the effect) and let it settle (slow plasticity). Each explains some findings and strains at others, since tremor changes in seconds but some benefits build over months.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mine. Episode six point one showed the hardware: a lead, a cable, and a box that sends pulses about one hundred thirty times a second. This episode asks the question the hardware cannot answer. Why does it work? The honest answer is in the title. Nobody fully knows.

Here is the picture. An old radio hums with static. You tap its case, and the hum stops. Why? Perhaps you knocked a wire loose. Perhaps the rattle of your tapping drowned out the hum. Perhaps the knock ran down the cable and shook a box in another room. Or perhaps, after weeks of tapping, the parts settled into a new place. Four stories for one tap. Each one is a hypothesis about deep brain stimulation. The radio is only a picture. It is not a model of the brain.

First, what gets tapped. In episode two point two, models showed that a pulse reaches cell bodies, but also axons, and that an axon can follow each pulse while its cell body stays quiet. Keep that in mind.

Then the rate. In nineteen eighty-seven, Benabid and colleagues in Grenoble were operating on the thalamus. In this operation, a surgeon destroys a small spot, to calm tremor. They observed that stimulating the same spot at one hundred hertz stopped the tremor. They also wrote that two hundred hertz seemed better than the one hundred thirty their stimulator could deliver.

In twenty eighteen, a team tested this in the operating room, in eleven patients. They sent short trains, two to five seconds long, through a tiny electrode in the thalamus. At two hundred hertz, tremor fell by about fifty-three per cent. At one hundred hertz, by about seventeen. And at most sites, neurons fired at the start of each train, then went quiet. So the rate matters, and tremor can change within seconds.

Now the first hypothesis. Pull the wire. A functional lesion. Stimulating the spot gave the same result as destroying it. So perhaps stimulation switches the spot off, and the switch can be reversed.

Some evidence fits. In slices of rat subthalamic nucleus, Beurrier and colleagues sent a one-minute burst at one hundred to two hundred fifty hertz. The neurons stayed silent for about six minutes, because the currents that keep them firing were blocked. No synapse was needed. In patients, during surgery, Dostrovsky and colleagues found that brief pulses in the pallidum quieted twenty-two of twenty-three nearby cells. But read the authors' explanation. The pulses probably excited the endings of fibres that release GABA, the brain's main inhibitory messenger. The cells were quieted by their inputs.

And here is the strain. In monkeys, Hashimoto and colleagues stimulated the subthalamic nucleus. The pallidal neurons it talks to did not fall silent. Their firing rate went up. A nucleus that is switched off should not do that.

The second hypothesis. Drown it out. Keep the cell body quiet, but drive the axon. McIntyre and colleagues modelled a thalamic relay neuron in two thousand four. With a strong enough pulse, the cell body was suppressed, but the axon sent spikes out at the stimulation rate. The nucleus still speaks. But it only repeats the rhythm of the pulses. It no longer passes on what it receives. Chiken and Nambu found something close in normal awake monkeys. High-frequency stimulation of the pallidum completely inhibited neighbouring neurons, through GABA receptors, and the responses that the cortex normally evokes in those cells disappeared. They call it disruption: the input and the output are dissociated.

In Parkinson's disease, a candidate for the thing being drowned out is the beta rhythm, between thirteen and thirty hertz. In eleven patients, Kühn and colleagues found that after stimulation stopped, beta in the subthalamic nucleus stayed lower for about twelve seconds, and the drop went with larger movements. Eusebio and colleagues recorded during stimulation itself, in sixteen patients. Above about one and a half volts, beta peaks shrank. At three volts, the median peak was down to about half. The strain: a rhythm that falls together with the benefit is not proof that it causes the benefit. And in Kühn's study, only patients whose benefit lingered after switching off were analysed.

The third hypothesis. Shake the cable. The effect is not where the lead is. Axons carry it away. In two thousand seven, Li and colleagues stimulated the subthalamic nucleus in anaesthetised rats. Deep-layer cortical neurons fired backwards, from their endings toward their cell bodies. That is called antidromic activation. The larger it was, the more slow waves and beta in the cortex were suppressed. In two thousand twelve, another group found antidromic spikes in the motor cortex of freely moving parkinsonian rats. And in two thousand nine, Gradinaru and colleagues used light to drive or silence parts of the circuit in parkinsonian rodents. The therapeutic effect could be accounted for by driving the axons that arrive at the subthalamic nucleus.

The strain: in that study, driving the subthalamic neurons themselves did not help. Eleven years later, Yu and colleagues repeated it with a faster light-sensitive protein, one that can follow one hundred thirty pulses a second. This time driving the subthalamic neurons did relieve symptoms, and only at high rates. So the axons matter. But the neurons at the electrode may matter too.

The fourth hypothesis. Let it settle. Look at the clock. Tremor can change in seconds. In Parkinson's disease, the main effect appears within hours of switching on, as Ruge and colleagues describe it. In dystonia, a condition of sustained twisting postures, the best effect often comes weeks to months after the start. In a trial of forty patients, Kupsch and colleagues compared stimulation with sham for three months. The movement score fell by about sixteen points with stimulation, and about one with sham. Ruge and colleagues followed patients at one, three and six months. A measure of inhibition in the cortex recovered toward normal over the same months as the symptoms. And the cortex's ability to change, its plasticity, moved too. They proposed that stimulation first disrupts abnormal signals, then slow relearning follows. That is a proposal, not a result. But a fast mechanism cannot explain a slow benefit. This hypothesis fills a gap the other three leave. One note. These are findings from studies, not statements about who can be treated. Licensed uses belong to chapter twelve.

Now put the four side by side. Cells near the electrode go quiet. Downstream cells speed up. A rhythm falls. The far cortex answers. The benefit arrives in seconds, or in months. Each hypothesis explains some of these, and strains at others. They do not exclude each other. In McIntyre's model, a single neuron already did two things at once: a quiet cell body, and a driven axon. A fair reading is that several may happen together, in different circuits, at different times. A systematic review in two thousand eight, by Montgomery and Gale, said it plainly: the mechanisms are unknown, and it is unclear which effects are the therapeutic ones. In two thousand sixteen, Chiken and Nambu still called the question open. Saying we don't know yet is part of good science.

Here is the classic trap. Reading the cell body as the output. A recording says the neurons near the electrode went quiet, and the headline says stimulation inhibits the nucleus. But in the model, a silent cell body and a driven axon are true together. So ask two questions. What was recorded: the cell body, or the output? And how long after switching on?

To sum up. One tap, four stories: pull the wire, drown it out, shake the cable, let it settle. High rates matter. Tremor changes in seconds, and some benefits build over months. The four are not rivals, and none is proven alone. And one candidate, the beta rhythm, can be read by the lead itself. That is the next episode.

Adaptive DBS

Adaptive DBS closes the loop: the contacts record local field potentials, the box compares the power of a rhythm with a threshold and adjusts the amplitude, like a vent that opens when a gas detector crosses its mark. In Parkinson's disease the signal is subthalamic beta, which follows slowness and rigidity but not tremor. Trials grew from eight patients to sixty-eight, the largest open label.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mine. In episode six point one, the lead learned to listen. Now we use that ear. Until now, our stimulator has worked in an open loop: the same pulses, day and night, whatever the brain is doing. Picture a mine gallery with a ventilation fan that runs at one fixed speed for the whole shift. It is safe, but it never knows whether the air needs it.

Adaptive deep brain stimulation hangs a gas detector on the wall. When the gas climbs past a mark on the dial, the vent opens wider. When the gas falls, the vent eases back. That is a closed loop: sense, decide, act. And it raises one question. What, in the brain, is the gas?

First the loop itself. Sense: the contacts that deliver the pulses also record the local field potential, the summed electrical activity of the tissue around the tip. Decide: the box measures the power of one rhythm, and compares it with a line, a threshold, set by the clinician. Act: when the power crosses the line, the box changes the stimulation amplitude, within limits the clinician also sets.

Remember the turning arrow from the EEG series. A rhythm is an arrow turning in circles. Its length is the power. Its angle is the phase. The adaptive stimulators in this episode read only the length. Reading the angle is chapter eight.

So which rhythm? In Parkinson's disease, so far, the answer is beta: activity between about thirteen and thirty-five hertz, recorded in the subthalamic nucleus. Why beta? First, it follows the symptoms. In two thousand six, Kühn and colleagues studied nine patients. The more levodopa lowered the beta peak, the more the motor score improved on the opposite side of the body: a correlation of zero point eight one. It held for slowness and rigidity. It did not hold for tremor. In a larger group of sixty-three patients off medication, Neumann and colleagues found a weaker link: zero point four four, between the total motor score and power from eight to thirty-five hertz.

Second, treatment moves it. Levodopa lowers low-frequency power in the basal ganglia, as Brown and colleagues showed in two thousand one. Continuous stimulation does too: in eleven patients, subthalamic beta stayed lower for about twelve seconds after stimulation stopped, and the drop went with better movement.

Third, beta is not a steady haze. It comes in bursts, like puffs of gas. Tinkhauser and colleagues showed in two thousand seventeen that a longer burst grows larger, and that longer bursts go with worse impairment. Continuous stimulation lowered beta across the board. Adaptive stimulation, in that study, cut the long bursts short.

Now the first proof. In two thousand thirteen, Little and colleagues closed the loop in eight patients. The leads were still connected to cables outside the head, in the days between the two operations. The beta signal was smoothed over four tenths of a second. When it crossed the threshold, stimulation switched on. They compared four conditions: no stimulation, continuous, random intermittent, and adaptive. Motor scores improved by sixty-six per cent when raters knew the condition, and by fifty per cent when they were blinded. That was twenty-nine and twenty-seven per cent better than continuous stimulation. And the stimulator was on fifty-six per cent less of the time. Random intermittent stimulation did worse, so the gain was not just less stimulation. But remember the setting: eight patients, a few days after surgery, a custom stimulator. A proof of principle.

Since then the loop has left the hospital. A twenty twenty-four pilot trial in Nature Medicine took four patients through a blinded, randomised crossover of adaptive and continuous stimulation. The signal was not beta. It was gamma activity, entrained by the stimulation, in the subthalamic nucleus or the motor cortex, chosen for each patient. Motor symptoms and quality of life improved. Four patients: a feasibility result.

The largest study is called ADAPT-PD. It enrolled sixty-eight people with Parkinson's disease in the United States, Canada and Europe, from December twenty twenty to July twenty twenty-two. All were first stable on continuous stimulation. Those who tolerated both adaptive modes, single threshold with one line on the dial and dual threshold with two, spent thirty days in each, without knowing which was which. Then they could continue for ten months. The main outcome was a self-reported diary: on-time without troublesome dyskinesia. The performance goal was met by ninety-one per cent of the forty people tested on dual threshold, and seventy-nine per cent of the thirty-five tested on single threshold. Energy delivered fell by fifteen per cent with single threshold.

Read the design. It was open label. The outcome was a diary, not a blinded rating. The margin that defined success was a post hoc threshold, as the paper itself states. And the main result is that adaptive stimulation was no worse than continuous. More on-time with dual threshold was an exploratory finding. The journal published it in twenty twenty-five, and calls it a non-randomised trial.

Where does this stand, as of November twenty twenty-six? In the United States, the F-D-A approved a premarket approval supplement on twentieth February twenty twenty-five. It adds an optional adaptive programming feature to one manufacturer's D-B-S system. The wording: bilateral stimulation of the internal globus pallidus or the subthalamic nucleus, as adjunctive therapy for levodopa-responsive Parkinson's disease of at least four years' duration, not adequately controlled with medication. In Canada, the Health Canada database lists two active class four licences for that manufacturer's sensing D-B-S systems, the first issued in October twenty twenty. It shows no separate entry for adaptive stimulation, and no indication text. In Europe, that manufacturer announced a C-E mark in January twenty twenty-five. A second manufacturer announced one in March twenty twenty-five, and that system is still investigational in the United States. Chapter twelve gives the indications.

Beta is not the only candidate. Gamma is another, as we just saw. Tremor itself, measured with motion sensors, is a third.

Now the limits. One: beta is not a tremor marker. In Kühn's data, the link was with slowness and rigidity. A loop that listens to beta is deaf to tremor. Two: the gas level changes for reasons that are not the disease. Levodopa lowers beta, which is the point. But movement lowers it too. In eight patients, beta fell before a voluntary movement. The box cannot tell whether beta dropped because the drug worked or because the hand moved. Three: the detector sees its own fan. Artefacts from the stimulation itself, and from the heartbeat, can contaminate the recording, and reviews list artefact removal as a requirement for any practical system. Four: every patient has their own beta peak and their own thresholds, so adaptive stimulation adds parameters to program.

Here is the classic trap. Treating beta as the disease. A correlation of zero point four four across patients means beta explains roughly a fifth of the variation in motor scores. And a correlation across patients is not a promise inside one patient. A detector that tracks the gas does not track every danger in the mine.

Now the bridge to chapter eight. Here, the loop reads the length of the arrow and adjusts a dose over seconds and minutes. In chapter eight, the loop reads the angle, and times each single pulse to the wave. That idea already has a precedent in the lead. In ten patients with essential tremor, Cagnan and colleagues delivered pulses at different phases of the tremor cycle. One half of the cycle reduced tremor amplitude by about ten per cent. The other half increased it by about the same. At the best alignment, the reduction reached twenty-seven per cent.

To sum up. Open loop means the same stimulation all the time. Closed loop is sense, decide, act. In Parkinson's disease, the main signal is subthalamic beta: it follows slowness and rigidity, falls with levodopa and with stimulation, and comes in bursts. Trials grew from eight patients in twenty thirteen to sixty-eight in ADAPT-PD, and the large one is open label. Beta is blind to tremor, and medication, movement and artefacts all move it.

Next: stimulating the surface of the cortex.

PLATE 6.49:16

Stimulating the cortex surface

Electrodes laid on the cortex to monitor epilepsy, like numbered stakes in a pasture, serve three jobs. They map, since stimulation-defined language sites are small, differ between people and sit just below the afterdischarge threshold. They treat, through a responsive device that senses a seizure pattern and answers with pulses, and they inform research in patients already implanted.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mine. So far, chapter six has been about devices that sit deep in the brain. This episode comes up to the surface, to the cortex itself.

Picture a hillside pasture, pegged out in a grid of numbered stakes. The stakes are driven in for a week or two, and they are in the field for one reason: the farmer needs to know what is happening there. Once they are in, they can be used in three ways. To map the pasture. To protect it. And, sometimes, to learn something new.

First, why would anyone put stakes on a cortex? Some people have epilepsy that drugs do not control. When surgery is possible, the team must find where the seizures begin, and which tissue speaks, moves or sees, before they take anything away. When recordings from the scalp cannot answer, electrodes go inside the head. The monitoring then lasts one to two weeks, according to a review by Chiong and colleagues.

There are two main designs. Subdural grids and strips are flat sheets of contacts, laid directly on the surface of the cortex. Stereo E-E-G goes the other way. Thin depth electrodes are placed through small holes along planned paths, so that the contacts sample deep tissue as well as the surface. In a two thousand twenty-two survey of United States epilepsy centres, ninety-two per cent of the one hundred four that answered used stereo E-E-G, and seventy-six per cent used it more often than grids. That figure comes from a two thousand twenty-five review by Ryvlin. Either way, the contacts can record, and they can stimulate.

Now, the first job: mapping. In episode one point two, a current on the motor cortex made a muscle move. That showed which sites are enough to cause a movement. Language mapping uses stimulation the other way round. The patient names pictures. The team sends a brief train of pulses through one pair of contacts. If the naming breaks down, or speech stops, that site is marked as important for language, and the surgeon will try to spare it.

In nineteen eighty-nine, Ojemann and colleagues mapped the left hemisphere of a hundred seventeen patients. A site counted as related to language when stimulation, at a current below the threshold for afterdischarge, caused repeated, statistically significant errors in naming objects. These sites were small, about one to two square centimetres. There was usually one in the frontal lobe, and one or more in the temporal and parietal lobes. They were much smaller than the traditional Broca and Wernicke areas, and they varied from person to person. The authors concluded that language cannot be reliably located from anatomy alone.

Here are typical settings. They depend on the centre. Fifty hertz, biphasic pulses, trains of a few seconds, and currents from one to about ten milliamps. In a stereo E-E-G study of ten children and young people, Arya and colleagues found that naming broke down at three point one milliamps on average.

And here is the safety signal. A train of pulses can set off an afterdischarge, a burst of epileptic activity that outlasts the stimulation. The team watches the recording for it. In that same study, the afterdischarge threshold averaged four point zero milliamps. The window between the two is narrow, so the recording is watched the whole time.

How does this compare with functional M-R-I? A review of nine studies that compared language fMRI with direct cortical stimulation, in brain tumour surgery, found sensitivity from fifty-nine to a hundred per cent, and specificity from zero to ninety-seven per cent, in the five studies that reported them. The authors judged that fMRI could not replace stimulation. In the stereo E-E-G study, stimulation agreed with a meta-analytic fMRI language map with a specificity of zero point eight seven, but a sensitivity of only zero point five seven. So stimulation can miss sites too. The two methods ask different questions. fMRI shows regions that are active during a task. Stimulation shows where a brief disturbance breaks it.

One more use of mapping. Stimulation at one hertz, a very low rate, can trigger a patient's habitual aura or seizure. That helps to confirm where the seizures begin.

Now, the second job: treating. What if the stakes could stay, and a sheepdog came with them? The dog sits quietly at the edge of the flock, and watches. It does nothing until one sheep starts to stray. Then it runs out, and nudges it back.

That is the responsive neurostimulator. It is implanted in the skull, level with the bone and under the scalp. It connects to one or two leads, each with four contacts. A lead is either a strip on the cortical surface, or a depth electrode placed at the seizure focus. The device records the electrical activity of the cortex all the time. A physician programs what counts as abnormal, from three detection tools: area, line length and bandpass. When the pattern is detected, the device sends a short train of pulses to the focus. The aim is to interrupt the pattern before the patient feels a seizure. The current can go up to eleven point five milliamps.

What does the evidence say? In the pivotal trial, reported by Morrell in two thousand eleven, one hundred ninety-one adults with drug-resistant partial epilepsy were implanted. A month later, they were randomised. For half, stimulation was on. For the other half, it was off. After twelve weeks, blinded, seizures had fallen by thirty-eight per cent with stimulation, in ninety-seven patients. With stimulation off, they had still fallen by seventeen per cent, in ninety-four patients. The difference was significant, with a p value of zero point zero one two. That seventeen per cent is why a sham group matters.

And in the long run? In two thousand twenty, Nair and colleagues followed patients from the earlier trials for nine years. Two hundred thirty of the two hundred fifty-six took part. At nine years, the median seizure reduction was seventy-five per cent. About eighteen per cent of all two hundred fifty-six had at least one year without seizures. But this study was open label, with no control group, and the journal rated it as class four evidence. A study like this cannot separate the effect of the device from everything else that changed over nine years.

Where does the device stand, as of November twenty twenty-six? In the United States, the F-D-A approved it on fourteenth November twenty thirteen, as premarket approval P one zero zero zero two six. It is indicated as adjunctive therapy to reduce seizure frequency in people eighteen or older with partial onset seizures. Testing must have localized no more than two seizure foci. The seizures must be refractory to two or more antiepileptic medications, frequent and disabling. No later change to the age limit was found, and use in generalised epilepsy is still investigational. In Canada, a search of the Health Canada medical devices database found no licence for this device. In Europe, no C-E mark was found. Chapter twelve gives indications and evidence.

Now, the third job: learning. Some patients, already implanted for their care, agree to research sessions. In two thousand four, Matsumoto and colleagues sent single pulses through contacts over the language areas in eight patients, and averaged the recordings from the other contacts. Stimulating the front language area produced a response in the side of the temporal and parietal lobe in seven of the eight. The responses clustered around contacts that, when stimulated, stopped speech. They called them cortico-cortical evoked potentials. It is a way to trace connections in a living human brain.

Patients like these have given us direct measurements of the electric field that scalp stimulation makes inside the head. Episode four point five used them. They are among the few direct measurements that exist.

What frames this kind of research? The electrodes are placed for clinical reasons, and the research happens during monitoring that the patient needs anyway. A study that asks for anything extra, such as an additional electrode, needs closer ethical review, say Chiong and colleagues. Consent for research is separate from consent for care. It is written before the research begins, and given orally again before each session. Patients are told that their care does not depend on taking part, and a patient who declines a session is thanked.

To sum up. Stimulation of the cortex surface does three jobs. It maps: language sites are small, they differ from person to person, and the current stays below the afterdischarge threshold. It treats: a responsive device that senses, then answers, with a sham-controlled trial and open label long-term data. And in people already implanted for their care, it informs research, under a separate consent.

PLATE 6.57:01

Electroconvulsive therapy, today

Modern ECT is a seizure caused on purpose under anaesthesia, muscle relaxant and monitoring, with the dose set as a multiple of each patient's seizure threshold. Electrode placement and pulse width shift the balance between speed of response and memory effects, so two courses both called ECT can differ as much as two drugs. Memory effects are real and their persistence is debated.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mine, one tunnel is different from all the others. Here the stimulation is not meant to nudge the brain. It is meant to cause a seizure, on purpose, under anaesthesia. This is electroconvulsive therapy, or ECT. Episode one point three told how it began. This episode is about how it is done today.

A modern session looks like a small procedure. Think of a pre-flight checklist. First, the patient receives a general anaesthetic, and falls asleep. Second, a short-acting muscle relaxant, so the body barely moves. Third, monitors for the heart, the blood pressure and the oxygen level. Fourth, electrodes on the scalp to record the EEG. Only then does the machine deliver a brief train of electrical pulses.

If the dose is high enough, a seizure follows, and the EEG shows it. Rhythmic discharges appear, slow down, and stop by themselves. Then the trace goes quiet for a while. The seizure typically lasts tens of seconds. In a Swedish registry of almost seven thousand patients, longer first seizures went with higher odds of remission, up to about a minute. That is an association, not proof. Seizure length is one clue among several that the dose was adequate.

The dose is set for each person. In the first session, the team looks for the seizure threshold, the lowest charge that triggers a seizure in that patient. Later sessions are set as a multiple of it. Right unilateral placement is typically given at about six times threshold. Bitemporal placement is given at much lower multiples: one and a half times in one large American trial, two and a half times in another. A course is usually two or three sessions a week. In one American trial of older adults, patients who reached remission needed seven point three sessions on average.

Placement is the first dial. Bitemporal puts one electrode over each temple. Right unilateral puts one on the right temple, and one near the top of the head on the same side. Bifrontal puts both on the forehead. In a trial of two hundred and thirty patients, Kellner and colleagues found remission rates of fifty-five, sixty-one and sixty-four percent, for right unilateral, bifrontal and bitemporal. Bitemporal acted faster. And in a large review from two thousand and three, bilateral placement was moderately more effective than unilateral. Placement can shift the balance between speed of response and memory effects.

Pulse width is the second dial. Brief pulses last about one and a half milliseconds. Ultrabrief pulses last three tenths of a millisecond. In two thousand and eight, Sackeim and colleagues assigned ninety people to four combinations. Remission was seventy-three percent with ultrabrief right unilateral, sixty-five with brief bitemporal, fifty-nine with brief right unilateral, and thirty-five with ultrabrief bitemporal. The ultrabrief right unilateral group had the mildest cognitive effects, and less retrograde amnesia. In the American trial of older adults, sixty-two percent of patients given ultrabrief right unilateral pulses reached remission, with a medication added. Remission rates differ between trials, depending on the study.

Now the part that matters most to patients. ECT can affect memory. Right after a course, tests of attention, memory and speed are often lower. A meta-analysis of eighty-four studies and almost three thousand patients found that most of these changes had cleared within about two weeks. But it also found no standard test of memory for past events, so the harder question is still open. In a New York study of three hundred and forty-seven patients, treated at seven centres, retrograde amnesia at six months was more severe and more persistent after bilateral than after right unilateral placement. An older waveform, the sine wave, also slowed reaction time at six months. Whether some gaps in autobiographical memory last for good is still debated.

Death is rare, but it is not zero. A pooled analysis of fifteen studies, covering about seven hundred and sixty-six thousand treatments, found about two deaths per one hundred thousand treatments. In the nine studies published after two thousand and one, there was one death in about four hundred and fifteen thousand treatments. The authors compared this with about three per one hundred thousand for anaesthesia in surgery.

Here is the gap. The public image of ECT is still the old one: unmodified seizures, and no anaesthesia. Practice today has anaesthesia, monitoring, and a dose measured against each patient's threshold. But the modern picture is not free of cost. Memory effects are real, they vary with the technique, and patients should be told. Both statements are true at once.

One alternative is under study. Magnetic seizure therapy triggers the seizure with a magnetic coil instead of scalp electrodes. It is still investigational. In a trial published in two thousand and twenty-six, in Canada and the United States, remission was twenty-two point five percent with magnetic seizure therapy, and twenty-seven point eight with ultrabrief right unilateral ECT, which met the non-inferiority margin. Autobiographical memory worsened in two point seven percent with magnetic seizure therapy, and seventeen point three with ECT. The trial stopped enrolling before its target size.

Any clinical mention needs a status. As of November two thousand and twenty-six: in the United States, in December two thousand and eighteen, the FDA reclassified ECT devices to class two, for catatonia or a severe major depressive episode, in major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder, in patients aged thirteen and older who are treatment-resistant or need a rapid response. Other uses remain class three. In Canada, a search of Health Canada's device licence database found no active licence for an ECT device. The one ECT licence found ended in October twenty nineteen. Who should receive ECT, and with what evidence, is a question for chapter twelve.

The classic trap is to treat ECT as one thing. Placement, pulse width, dose and waveform are parameters, and two courses both called ECT can differ as much as two drugs. When you read a study, fill in the dose card. A blank line is often where the problem is.

To sum up. ECT is a seizure caused on purpose, under anaesthesia and monitoring. Placement and pulse width shift the balance between speed and memory. The dose is a multiple of each patient's threshold. Adverse effects are real, and their persistence is debated. Magnetic seizure therapy is an alternative under study.

VNS and taVNS

Vagus nerve stimulation acts on a mostly sensory nerve reaching the brainstem, which projects to noradrenaline and serotonin centres, so a local stimulus has broad consequences, like a signal tower lighting fires across a country. The implanted neck cuff and the ear clip of taVNS share a nerve and little else, with different fibres and doses. For the ear, no marker yet proves recruitment.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mine, one more rail. This one does not go into the brain. It runs along the neck. On each side of the neck, beside the big blood vessels, runs the vagus nerve. It wanders from the brainstem down to the throat, the heart, the lungs and the gut. Today we hang a stimulator on that cable.

Picture a chain of signal fires. A nerve is a long road. At the far end of the road stands a signal tower. When a rider arrives, the tower lights fires on hills across the country, and each hill speaks to a whole region. Hold that picture. We will use it all episode.

Vagus nerve stimulation, V-N-S, comes in two forms. In the implanted form, a surgeon wraps a small helical electrode around the left vagus nerve, in the neck. A wire runs under the skin to a generator in the chest. In the transcutaneous form, taVNS, there is no surgery. A small electrode clip sits on the ear, on skin supplied by a branch of the vagus nerve.

First, the principle. Most of this road carries traffic toward the brain. Krahl and Clark, in two thousand twelve, put sensory fibres at about sixty-five to eighty per cent of all vagal fibres. The riders arrive at the signal tower: the nucleus of the solitary tract, in the brainstem. From there, direct or indirect projections reach the locus coeruleus, a main source of noradrenaline, and the raphe nuclei, a source of serotonin. These nuclei release their chemicals over wide regions. So the stimulation is local, in the neck, and the consequences are broad.

Second, what exactly is stimulated. The cuff does not pick fibres. The trunk holds large and small myelinated fibres, and many thin unmyelinated C fibres. At the currents used in patients, the myelinated A and B fibres are the likely recruits. Whether C fibres are recruited is debated, and animal work has pointed both ways.

And why the left side? The cardiac fibres of the left vagus mainly supply the atrioventricular node. Those of the right vagus mainly supply the sinoatrial node, which sets the heart rate. So the right side was thought riskier for slowing the heart. Careful with that story. In humans, the part of the neck trunk where the cuff sits does not include the cardiac branches, and in rats the right side suppressed seizures just as well. The left side is a convention with an anatomical reason, not a proof. An early label, quoted by Krahl and Clark, says the system is indicated only for the left nerve in the neck.

Third, the parameters. Intensity, in milliamps. Pulse width, in microseconds. Frequency, in hertz. And a duty cycle: how long on, how long off. For epilepsy, Krahl and Clark report that most patients are stimulated at thirty hertz, thirty seconds on, five minutes off. The paired version for stroke is different. In the V-N-S-REHAB trial, Dawson and colleagues gave half-second bursts, at eight tenths of a milliamp, with one hundred microsecond pulses at thirty hertz, during rehabilitation exercises. That was eighteen sessions in clinic over six weeks. The control group wore the same device, set to zero milliamps. For taVNS, a common research setting is twenty-five hertz, with pulses of two hundred to five hundred microseconds, thirty seconds on and thirty off, on the left ear. Intensity is either fixed, around half a milliamp in some studies, or set just under the pain threshold, which averaged about six milliamps in one study. Depending on the study. And the site: the ear.

Which part of the ear? In two thousand two, Peuker and Filler dissected fourteen ears from seven cadavers. They found a mix of four nerves, two cranial and two cervical, and no region supplied by three. The cymba conchae, the hollow at the top of the ear's bowl, is the region most often cited as vagal only. That claim leans on this one dissection. In two thousand eighteen, Burger and Verkuil pointed to an apparent inconsistency in that paper, and the choice between the cymba and the tragus is still argued. A two thousand nineteen review of the anatomy finds no consensus on which sites are the most densely supplied, and calls the concha and the inner tragus reasonable places to try.

Fourth, what is known and what is debated. For taVNS we still lack a marker that proves the nerve was recruited. Candidates include pupil size and a salivary enzyme, alpha-amylase. Results diverge. Keute and colleagues, in two thousand nineteen, studied thirty-three volunteers and found no change in pupil size or in the pupil's response to target sounds. D'Agostini and colleagues, in two thousand twenty-one, studied seventy-one people. They found no rise in tonic pupil size or in alpha-amylase. Sharon and colleagues, in two thousand twenty-one, used short, three point four second trials in twenty-four men, with the earlobe as sham. Pupil dilation peaked four to five seconds after onset, and was larger than for sham. Then Giraudier and colleagues pooled ten studies, three hundred seventy-one people. A meta-analysis of summary results found nothing significant. A model on the raw data found higher alpha-amylase with taVNS than sham. Reviews say the same thing, in different words: Burger and colleagues, in two thousand twenty, found no biomarker that clearly shows the nerve was activated, and Ludwig and colleagues, in two thousand twenty-one, found heterogeneous results on reaching the locus coeruleus. Designs differ too, short trials against stimulation throughout a task, so these studies are not strict replications.

Now the regulatory picture, as of November twenty twenty-six. In the United States, implanted V-N-S for epilepsy was approved in July nineteen ninety-seven, with its wording extended in two thousand seventeen to add-on therapy for patients four years and older with partial-onset seizures that do not respond to antiepileptic medications. For depression, the F-D-A approved a supplement on fifteenth July two thousand five, for adults with chronic or recurrent depression, in a major depressive episode, who have not responded to four or more antidepressant treatments. The paired system was approved on twenty-seventh August twenty twenty-one, to reduce arm deficits and improve motor function in chronic ischemic stroke with moderate to severe arm impairment. In Canada, Health Canada lists two active class four licences for the implanted system, the first from August nineteen ninety-eight. The database shows no indication text, and I found no licence for the stroke system. In Europe, the manufacturer announced C-E marks for epilepsy and for depression. For the ear devices, this episode makes no claim: they are research tools here. How well the licensed uses work is chapter twelve.

Here is the classic trap. Believing that stimulating the ear is the same as stimulating the nerve in the neck. They share a nerve and little else. The cuff wraps the trunk, with its mix of fibres, and sends current straight in. The clip sends current through skin to small branches, and we cannot yet prove the nerve fired. The fibres are not the same. The doses are not the same. So an effect seen with the cuff is not evidence for the clip, and the other way round.
