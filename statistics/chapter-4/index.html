The normal distribution

A Galton board shows the bell appearing when many small independent pushes add up: more rows widen the pile but keep its shape. Height and measurement error illustrate it, and one huge push breaks it into two humps. Z-scores measure position without saying abnormal. Bounded data, skewed data, counts and heavy tails are never normal, but means can be.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Why is the bell curve called normal?

It is not because it is healthy, or usual, or the way data ought to look. Today we find where the bell really comes from, and what it is good for.

Here is a board of pegs, a little like the one Francis Galton built in the eighteen seventies. Drop a ball at the top. At every peg it gets a small push, to the left or to the right, with equal chance. Pegs have no memory, so each push is independent of the others.

There are twelve rows, so twelve pushes. The ball lands in the slot that counts its pushes to the right. One ball can land almost anywhere, and nobody can say where.

Now drop a thousand. This is simulated.

Most balls land near the middle. Very few land far out. In this simulation, two hundred twenty-six balls landed in the middle slot. Exact arithmetic says to expect about two hundred twenty-six, so the pile and the arithmetic agree.

Not one ball went all the way to an edge. Twelve pushes in the same direction has a chance of about one in two thousand.

This pile is a bell. Each ball is the sum of twelve small, independent pushes. In seventeen thirty-three, Abraham de Moivre showed that counts of coin flips take exactly this shape.

Add up many small independent effects, and a bell appears.

Now guess before you look. If we add more rows, what happens to the pile? Does it get wider, does the shape change, or both?

Here are boards with one, two, four, twelve and thirty-six rows, with two thousand balls each. With one row, there are two piles and no bell. With two rows, three slots. With four, a rough hill. At twelve rows the bell is clear, and at thirty-six it is smooth.

The pile gets wider, as the standard deviation grows with the square root of the number of rows. But the shape settles. The width changes, and the shape does not.

The board is a toy, but the idea is real. Adult height depends on many small things. Imagine one hundred small effects, each adding or removing seven tenths of a centimeter. This is illustrative.

For five thousand simulated people, the mean is one hundred seventy-five centimeters, and the standard deviation is seven point one. The pile is a bell. That is why heights are often close to one.

A third example. Measure the same length five thousand times. Twenty-five tiny disturbances shift each reading: vibration, temperature, the eye of the operator. Say each one moves it by two tenths of a millimeter, up or down. This is illustrative. The errors pile up into a bell around zero, with a standard deviation of one millimeter.

Now let us break it. Keep twelve small pushes, and add one huge push, twelve times bigger than the others. This one push carries ninety-two percent of all the variation.

The pile now has two humps. Only two percent of the sums fall in the middle, where a bell of the same spread would put twenty-eight percent. A bell needs many small effects, and none that dominates.

A bell has only two settings. Where its center is, the mean. And how wide it is, the standard deviation. Slide the center, and the bell moves. Widen it, and the bell spreads. The shape never changes.

Take heights with a mean of one hundred seventy-five and a standard deviation of seven. About sixty-eight percent of people lie within one standard deviation of the mean. About ninety-five percent lie within two. About ninety-nine point seven percent lie within three.

In a hundred thousand simulated people, the shares are sixty-eight point oh, ninety-five point five, and ninety-nine point seven three percent.

Guess again. How many of the hundred thousand lie more than three standard deviations away? The exact chance is about one in three hundred seventy, so about two hundred seventy people. The simulation found two hundred sixty-six.

A z-score counts how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean. A person of one hundred eighty-nine centimeters is fourteen above the mean. That is two standard deviations, so z is two. A patient scores sixty-five on a clinical scale with mean fifty and standard deviation ten. That is z equals one point five. Two different units, one scale.

Who is more unusual? The tall person. About one in forty-four people is taller, and about one in fifteen scores higher on the clinical scale.

A common misreading says that a z of two means abnormal. In a bell, it only tells you where a value sits. It says nothing about good, bad, or healthy.

The bell is a model, not a law. Four kinds of data can never be normal.

First, bounded data. A quiz score out of one hundred, with mean eighty-eight and standard deviation nine point six. A bell with this mean and spread puts eleven percent of the students above one hundred. That is impossible.

Second, skewed data. These are the reaction times of chapter one. Beyond two standard deviations above the mean, the data hold four point one percent of the trials, and the bell would hold two point three.

Third, counts. Visits to a clinic last year, with a mean of point five seven. A bell would put eight percent of people below zero visits. In fact more than half had none.

Fourth, heavy tails. Beyond three standard deviations, a bell expects one value in three hundred seventy. These data give one in seventy-two. That is five times as often.

So must data be normal for a test to work? Often, no. For many tests, what must be close to a bell is the statistic, not the data.

Guess before you look. Will the pile of means keep the long tail? Take the skewed reaction times, with a long right tail. Draw fifty trials, compute the mean, and repeat five thousand times. The skewness falls from one point two six to zero point two three, and the pile of means is nearly a bell.

The same happens with the visit counts. Take one hundred people, compute the mean, and repeat. Skewness falls from one point two nine to zero point one two. It is the central limit theorem at work. It needs a big enough sample, and tails that are not too heavy.

Remember: the bell is what many small, independent effects add up to, and for many tests it is the statistic that should be close to it, not the data.

Next, we count things. The binomial and the Poisson.

Binomial and Poisson

Counting successes in a fixed number of independent trials gives the binomial, as with ten free throws at seventy percent. Rare events landing in a span of time give the Poisson, whose variance equals its mean, as with Prussian soldiers kicked by horses. A bar dipping below zero on counts signals the wrong model, and sick days show overdispersion.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Ten free throws. How many go in?

Last time, counts were one of four kinds of data that no bell can fit. Today we meet the two laws made for counting: the binomial, and the Poisson.

Here is a reel of film. Each frame is one free throw. A spark means the ball went in. Our player scores seventy percent of the time. This is illustrative, and every reel is simulated.

Ten frames, seven sparks: the count of hits is seven. Here are eight reels. They hold seven, nine, six, seven, five, nine, eight and seven hits. Nobody can say what the next reel will hold.

But a pile of many reels has a shape. Now ten thousand simulated reels.

Seven hits is the most common count. In this simulation, two thousand seven hundred twenty-four reels held exactly seven.

This pile is the binomial law. A yes or no trial is called a Bernoulli trial, after Jacob Bernoulli. Count the successes in a fixed number n of independent trials, each with the same chance p, and the count is binomial. If our player got hot, the chance would drift, and it would not be.

Why those heights? Take seven hits. There are one hundred twenty ways to place seven sparks among ten frames. Each way has a chance of zero point seven to the seventh, times zero point three cubed. One hundred twenty times that is twenty-six point seven percent. The simulation found twenty-seven point two. They differ by chance, as simulations do.

The average count is n times p: ten times zero point seven is seven. The variance is n times p times one minus p: two point one. The simulation gives seven point zero zero, and two point oh seven. A count is a sum of n trials, so means add and variances add.

Three more binomials, simulated. Twenty coin flips: a mean of about ten, a variance of about five. A block of forty reaction trials, each correct with chance zero point eight five: about thirty-four, and about five point one. A batch of fifty items, each defective with chance four percent: two, and just under two.

Now a different question. A neuron fires. How many spikes in one second? There is no n here. Nobody counted trials. There is only a span of time, and events that land in it at random.

To see the law, we cut the span into frames ourselves. Say the average is two events. Ten frames, each with chance zero point two. Fifty frames, each with four percent. Five hundred frames, each with four tenths of a percent. The mean stays two.

Ten thousand simulated reels of each. The dots are the Poisson law with a mean of two. With ten frames, the pile is squeezed, and its variance is one point six. With fifty, one point nine two. With five hundred, one point nine nine.

The gap to the dots is the share of the pile we would have to move: five point two percent, zero point nine three, zero point zero nine.

That limit is the Poisson law. Siméon Denis Poisson wrote about it in eighteen thirty-seven. Its formula has no n. The chance of k events is e to the minus lambda, times lambda to the k, divided by k factorial. For lambda two and k three, that is about eighteen percent.

Now the strong property. In a Poisson count, the variance equals the mean. Why? In the binomial, the variance is the mean times one minus p. When p is tiny, one minus p is almost one.

Guess before you look. Counts with a mean of ten. What is their variance? Ten, five, or twenty?

Here are simulated Poisson counts, ten thousand each, at means of zero point five, two and ten. The variances are zero point five, two point zero, and ten point three. At ten, a little high. Is that chance?

Nine means, from one half to twenty. At ten, a fresh set gives ten point one. Every point sits on the diagonal. Know the mean of a count, and you know its spread.

Now a real case. In eighteen ninety-eight, Ladislaus Bortkiewicz published The Law of Small Numbers. It counted Prussian soldiers killed by a horse's kick, per army corps and year, from eighteen seventy-five to eighteen ninety-four.

Fourteen corps were recorded. The classic table keeps ten, over twenty years: two hundred corps-years. In one hundred nine of them, nobody died. In sixty-five, one soldier. In twenty-two, two. In three, three. In one, four.

That is one hundred twenty-two deaths, a mean of zero point six one. A Poisson law with that mean expects one hundred eight point seven, sixty-six point three, twenty point two, four point one, and zero point six. We computed those. The data agree. The variance is zero point six one, the same as the mean.

Now the strong moment. Draw the mean, plus or minus one standard deviation, as people often do. For the horse kicks, the standard deviation is zero point seven eight. The bar runs from minus zero point one seven to one point three nine. Minus zero point one seven soldiers. Counts cannot be negative.

Next to it, the real counts. More than half of the corps-years sit at zero, and the pile leans to the right. A symmetric bar cannot describe this.

Here are four simulated sets of a thousand counts. With means of about zero point three and zero point six, the bar dips below zero. At two and at ten, it does not. For a Poisson count the standard deviation is the square root of the mean, so the bar dips below zero whenever the mean is below one.

A bar that crosses zero on counts is a warning: the model behind it is the wrong one.

Real counts often break the tie. Take sick days per employee per year. This is illustrative. Each person has their own rate. In this simulated set of ten thousand people, the mean is one point nine nine, but the variance is five point eight four, almost three times larger.

A Poisson law with that mean would put thirteen point seven percent of people at zero. This set has thirty-three point five. This is called overdispersion. The models for it come in chapter ten.

So how do we choose? A fixed number of trials? Binomial. Events landing in a span, with no n? Poisson. Then ask whether the variance is near the mean. Here are five spans, all illustrative.

Spikes of a neuron in a short window: a variance near the mean is plausible if the rate is steady. Emails per hour: doubtful, because the rate changes through the day.

Mutations per stretch of DNA: plausible, because each is rare and independent. Goals in a football match: plausible. Eye blinks per minute: doubtful, because people differ in their rate.

Remember: count the successes in a fixed number of trials, and you get the binomial. Count rare events in a span, and you get the Poisson, where the variance equals the mean. A bar that dips below zero on counts means the wrong model.

Next, when effects multiply instead of add: the log-normal, and heavy tails.

PLATE 4.37:05

Log-normal and heavy tails

On a Galton board, pushes that add make a bell, while pushes that multiply make a right-leaning pile called log-normal, which becomes a bell on the ladder of tens, the logarithm. Reaction times, drug concentrations and EEG power fit it. In a Pareto law, one value can dominate the sum, so the mean and standard deviation never settle.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the bell foundry, a bell appears when many small pushes add up.

But some things do not add. They multiply.

What shape does the pile take then? And what happens when a few values are enormous?

Today: the log-normal law, and heavy tails.

Here is the Galton board again. Twelve pushes, and every ball starts at one hundred.

On this board, each push adds a random amount, anywhere from minus ten to plus ten.

A thousand balls fall. The pile is a bell. Simulated.

The mean is 100.5, and the median is 100.3. In a bell, they sit in the same place.

Now a second board, with the same twelve pushes. This time, each push multiplies the value by a random factor, between about 0.6 and about 1.65.

A factor and its inverse are equally likely.

Watch the pile. It leans to the right, with a long arm.

The last bar counts every ball at 575 or more: 48 of them.

The mean is 165.8. The median is 100.1. The long arm pulls the mean away, as in episode 2.2.

Why the lean? A push of plus twenty percent adds two hundred to a ball at a thousand, and two to a ball at ten. Growth feeds on itself.

Adding makes a bell. Multiplying makes this shape.

Limpert and colleagues, in 2001, gathered examples of it from across the sciences, and explained it with boards like this one. It is called log-normal.

Count how many times you multiplied by ten.

One is zero tens. Ten is one ten. A hundred is two tens. A thousand is three.

That count is the logarithm, in base ten.

Slide along the ladder. Fifty is one point seven tens. Three hundred and sixteen is two and a half.

Here is the useful part. A push that multiplies by 1.25 always moves you by the same distance on the ladder, 0.097, whether you start at a hundred or at a thousand.

Equal multiplications become equal steps, and steps add up.

So redraw the multiplicative pile on the ladder. Each ball sits at its count of tens.

The bell is back. The mean of the logs is 2.00, and so is the median. The skewness falls from 3.6 to 0.1.

A quantity whose log is a bell is called log-normal.

Put the mean of the logs back on the value scale, and you get the geometric mean: 99.7.

It sits with the median, 100.1, and not with the mean, 165.8. Different questions: the mean shares out the total, the median and the geometric mean describe a typical ball.

Episode 4.6 teaches transformations. Here, we only see that the log straightens a multiplicative pile.

Where does this shape appear? Wherever effects multiply. Every example here is illustrative.

Reaction times: attention and fatigue each speed or slow the answer by a proportion. Report the median, or the geometric mean.

A drug's concentration in blood: absorption and clearance each scale it. Same shape, same summary.

Bacteria after growth: each doubling multiplies the count. Work with the logs of the counts.

The power of an EEG band: it is positive and strongly right-skewed, and analysts usually take its log.

Now another kind of long tail. Here one value can dominate the others.

Incomes, where a few people earn far more than the rest. City sizes. File sizes, where a handful of files fill the disk. Word frequencies. Insurance claims, where one disaster can set the whole year's bill.

For these, the median and the quantiles are honest. The mean can hide the one value that is the story.

To see it, take the Pareto law. It is named after the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, who studied how incomes are spread. We use a shape of 1.1.

Each draw is now a building, as tall as its value, measured in medians. A hundred draws from each of three laws: a normal, a log-normal, and a Pareto.

In the normal city, the tallest building carries 1.4 percent of the total height.

In the log-normal city, 13.7 percent. In the Pareto city, 20.3 percent.

Those are single samples. Repeat them, and take the typical share. At a hundred draws it is 1.4 percent, 7.2 percent, and 19 percent. That log-normal city was a tall one.

Now grow the city. At a million draws, the normal's largest value carries 0.00017 percent. The log-normal's, 0.007 percent. The Pareto's, 3.5 percent.

And in one Pareto sample out of ten, more than a quarter of the whole sum sits in a single draw.

When one observation can dominate the sum, what happens to the average?

Draw a million values, one at a time, and keep the running mean. For this Pareto law, the exact mean is 11. For the log-normal, it is 1.65.

The log-normal runs settle. At a million draws, they read 1.647, 1.646 and 1.650.

The Pareto runs do not. At a million draws, they read 8.1, 20.1 and 7.9. None is near 11.

After the first thousand draws, each Pareto run jumped by at least one percent about forty times. Each log-normal run jumped once.

In the second run, a single draw of about 9.3 million carried 46.2 percent of the total.

The standard deviation is worse. After a million draws, the Pareto runs read 498, 9,513 and 459. The log-normal runs read 2.16, 2.15 and 2.17.

In episode 2.5, heavy tails shook the standard deviation more than the mean. Here, on paper, the variance is not even finite.

In episode 1.5, a ratio's average never settled. Here, it is the law itself.

So, with heavy tails, report the median and the quantiles, show the largest values, and ask whether one value is the story.

Remember: when effects multiply, the natural scale is the logarithm. When tails are heavy, one observation can dominate the sum, and the mean and the standard deviation stop being stable summaries.

PLATE 4.46:58

t, chi-square and F

A machine drawing normal samples produces three curves. The t arises when the spread is estimated from the sample, giving heavy tails at small n; the chi-square sums squared standardised gaps; the F is a ratio of variances. Degrees of freedom count free pieces. These curves describe a statistic when nothing is going on, and they break when data are heavy-tailed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Where do t, chi-square and F come from?

Three curves appear in almost every statistics book: t, chi-square and F. They look like strangers. In fact they come out of one machine, fed the same dough.

The machine draws samples from a normal population, and computes one statistic from each. This is simulated. We pile up ten thousand of them, for each die we push the dough through.

First die: the t. Recall the standard error: the spread divided by the square root of n. If we knew the true spread, the gap of a sample mean from the truth, divided by the standard error, would follow a bell. But nobody knows the true spread. So we estimate it from the sample itself, and divide by that. The result is called t.

A blood pressure average against a target is a t. So is the gap between two classes' average scores, divided by its estimated standard error.

Guess before you look. With samples of three people, how often does t land beyond plus or minus 1.96? For a bell, it is 5 percent.

Here is n equals 3: 19.2 percent. Almost four times more. The pile has heavy tails. Now n equals 5: 12.2 percent. Ten: 7.8. Thirty: 6.0. The tails thin out, and the pile creeps toward the bell, but slowly.

This family of curves is the t law. It was found by William Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery. He published it in nineteen oh eight, in the journal Biometrika, under the pen name Student. The paper is called "The probable error of a mean."

Where do the heavy tails come from? Test it. Take the very same samples, but divide by the true standard error, which the machine knows. For n equals 3: 5.2 percent. The bell is back. For 5, 10 and 30: 5.0, 4.9 and 5.1. So the heavy tails come from estimating the spread.

Why? With three people, the estimated spread falls under half of the truth in 22.7 percent of the samples. A small number in the denominator makes t huge.

How much a sample knows about the spread is counted in degrees of freedom: the number of free pieces of information. Here are three everyday cases.

Three numbers whose mean is fixed at ten. Choose the first two freely: seven, twelve. The third is forced to be eleven. Two free pieces.

Five people and five seats in a row. The first four choose; the last takes what is left. Four free pieces.

A table with two rows and two columns, and fixed totals. Fill one cell, and the other three follow. One free piece.

A sample of n, with its own mean taken out, has n minus one free pieces, as in episode 2.3. So the t law for n people has n minus one degrees of freedom: 2, 4, 9 and 29 for our four piles.

Second die: the chi-square. Take one standardised gap, a gap measured in standard deviations, and square it. A square is never negative, and is usually small. Add k of them: that sum is a chi-square with k degrees of freedom.

Guess before you look. When we add ten squares, where does the average of the sums land? Here are k equals 1, 2, 5 and 10. The averages land on 1.0, 2.0, 5.0 and 10.0. The average of a chi-square is its degrees of freedom.

It is used on counts. A die rolled 60 times, with the six face counts compared to ten each: add each squared gap over its expected count. This is illustrative. In the machine, the average is 5.0: five degrees of freedom. A genotype count of 80 offspring against a three to one ratio: two classes, one degree of freedom, and the average is 1.0.

It is used on spread too. Ten reaction times, and their variance divided by the true variance, times n minus one: nine degrees of freedom, and the average is 8.9.

Third die: the F. Take two samples from the same population, and divide one variance by the other. Two machines fill bags of flour. If both are equally steady, the ratio of their variances should sit near one. So should the variances of two classes' scores, if their marks are equally scattered. The F curve says how far from one is still ordinary.

With samples of 5 and 5, the median is 1.0, and the middle 95 percent of the ratios run from 0.10 to 9.7. With 10 and 10: 0.25 to 4.0. With 30 and 30: 0.47 to 2.1. So the pile is centred near one, and wider when the samples are small.

Careful with two numbers. For samples of 5 and 5, the average of the ratios is 2.0, not 1.0. A long right tail drags the average up; the median stays at one.

Now, one place where it breaks. These curves hold when the data are normal. Take samples of 20 from a heavy-tailed law. This is simulated. Each statistic is judged against its own curve, which promises 5 percent of false alarms. The t of the mean gives 5.3 percent. Close. The chi-square of a variance gives 16.0 percent. The F gives 16.4. On normal data, the same three give 4.8, 5.6 and 5.2.

So a curve is true only when its assumptions hold. And in this simulation, the curves for variances lean on the bell much harder than the t of the mean does.

Notice what these three curves are. They do not describe data. They describe what a statistic does when nothing is going on, and the assumptions hold.

Here is a clinic, illustrative: ten patients, and a target pressure of 130. Their mean is 132.3, and t is 0.69. The pile of ten thousand simulated clinics, in a world where the truth is 130, has the t curve laid over it. About 51 percent of the clinics land at least that far out. So this value is ordinary. Run the clinic again and again, and t wanders. The curve says how far it wanders when the truth is 130.

A test compares the statistic we saw to the curve. That curve is a curtain of chance, which chapter 6 builds.

Remember: t, chi-square and F do not describe data. They describe a statistic when nothing is going on and the test's assumptions hold. Degrees of freedom count the free pieces.

So before we trust a curve, we should check its assumption.

Checking an assumption

A Q-Q plot sorts values and plots each against its position in a perfect bell, within a grey band; 62.9 percent of perfect samples still stray outside it. Shapiro-Wilk asks only whether a departure is detectable, not whether it matters: it misses half of skewed samples of fifteen yet flags harmless ones with thousands. Check residuals, not pooled values.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Is my data normal enough?

A bell is a model, not a law. So before a test that leans on a bell, someone has to ask: does the bell fit well enough? There are two ways to answer. One is a picture. The other is a test. One of them is easy to misread.

We start with the picture, and we build it. Here are forty clinical scores. This is simulated, from a perfect bell with a mean of fifty and a standard deviation of ten.

Step one: sort them, smallest first. Each value now has a rank.

Step two: ask the bell. If forty values came from a bell, where would the smallest usually sit? The rule is rank minus one half, divided by forty. For rank one, that is zero point zero one two five. A bell has one point two five percent of its mass below a spot at minus two point two four standard deviations. That is where the smallest value belongs.

Step three: plot each value against its bell position. Our smallest score, twenty-nine, is one point nine two standard deviations below the mean of this sample. So its dot sits at minus two point two four across, and minus one point nine two up.

Do this for all forty. If the data are a bell, the dots follow the diagonal. This is a quantile-quantile plot, a Q-Q plot. Wilk and Gnanadesikan published a paper on such plots in nineteen sixty-eight.

The dots follow the line, but they wobble. How much wobble is normal? We draw two thousand more perfect bell samples of forty, and mark where each rank lands ninety-five times out of a hundred. That is the grey band. Here are twelve of those samples.

Guess before you look. Among perfect bell samples, how many have at least one dot outside the band? It is sixty-two point nine percent. A single stray dot is normal. The band shows how much wobble to expect. It is not a gate.

Now let us read the departures. Here are four samples of three hundred, each with a histogram and its plot. For three hundred values, a bell expects the largest to sit near two point nine four standard deviations above the mean.

First, heavy tails. Both ends fly away from the line. The lowest value is at minus four point five six, and the highest at seven point one five. There are more extreme values than a bell allows.

Second, a skewed sample. Guess before you look. The histogram has a long right tail. Which way does the plot bend?

The right end shoots up, to five point four four. The left end falls short: the lowest is only minus one point two six, where a bell expects near minus three. A long tail on one side, a short tail on the other, and the dots curve like a banana.

Third, light tails. These values spread evenly between two bounds. Both ends fall short of the line.

Fourth, one wild value. Two hundred ninety-nine ordinary values, and one entry error, at seven point two. That is not a shape problem. It is a value to go and check.

Now the test. Shapiro and Wilk published theirs in nineteen sixty-five. It boils the plot down to one number, W. W is near one when the dots hug a line, and smaller when they bend. Our forty scores give W of point nine eight three.

To judge a W, we ask the curtain of chance. Nil draws it for samples of fifteen: twenty thousand simulated bells, whose W values pile up near one. The p-value is the share of perfect bells that give a W as low as ours, or lower. On perfect bells, the test raises a false alarm in five point two percent of ten thousand samples, close to the five percent it promises.

So the test asks one question: could a perfect bell bend this much? It does not ask whether the bend is big enough to matter.

First case. Fifteen patients, and their days to recovery. This is illustrative: a right-skewed law, with a long tail. Here is the first sample that the test lets through. The dots curve like a banana. The test gives W of point eight nine nine, and p of point oh nine two. No alarm.

Was that bad luck? Repeat it ten thousand times. The test catches the skew in fifty point two percent of the samples. It misses half.

Does the miss matter? The usual ninety-five percent interval for the mean should cover the true mean ninety-five times out of a hundred. It covers it ninety-one point six percent of the time. Among the samples the test let through, eighty-seven point one.

Second case. Five thousand values from a bell made lopsided by a hair. Its skewness is zero point one four. To the eye, the dots are a straight line. The test gives W of point nine nine eight seven, and p of about five in ten thousand. Alarm.

Repeat it ten thousand times. The test catches it ninety-five point two percent of the time. The interval covers the true mean ninety-five point two percent of the time. Nothing is wrong.

Now turn one knob: the sample size, with the skewed law of the first case. The pink line is the share the test catches: thirty-two percent at ten values, eighty-five at thirty, all of them from a hundred on.

The blue line is how often the interval covers the mean: ninety-one percent at ten values, ninety-four at a hundred, ninety-five at a thousand. The test is quietest where the harm is largest, and loudest where the harm is gone. With more data, the pile of means turns into a bell by itself, as in episode one point six.

One more thing: what the assumption is about. Two groups of a hundred, with scores around thirty-five and sixty-five, and a standard deviation of ten. This is illustrative. Pool all two hundred values, and the plot is an S, with p of point oh oh three.

But a comparison of means leans on the errors around each group's mean. These are the residuals: each value minus the mean of its own group. Their plot is a straight line. W is point nine nine one, and p is point two five.

Repeat it five thousand times. The test flags the pooled values ninety-seven point nine percent of the time, and the residuals four point four percent, which is just the false alarm rate.

A common misreading says: p above point oh five, so the data are normal. No. It says only that nothing was detected. With fifteen values, the test is half blind.

Remember: a normality test asks whether a departure can be detected, not whether it matters. So look at the plot, and check the assumption on what it is really about, often the residuals.

Next, when the plot says not normal, what do we do? Transform, or not.

Transform or not

Square root, logarithm and ranks each straighten skewed hormone data, but they change the question: a 4.54 difference becomes a ratio of 1.34 in geometric means. The log helps tests on multiplicative data, yet for hospital costs the budget needs the raw mean. Adding a constant before the log with many zeros changes the answer itself.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Your data lean to the right, and the normality test complains.

So you bend the numbers, and test again. Is that allowed? Sometimes yes. And sometimes the bent numbers answer a different question from the one you asked.

Today: transform, or not.

In 4.3, we saw that the log straightens a pile built by multiplying. In 4.5, we saw that a normality test asks whether a departure can be detected, not whether it matters. Now we bend the numbers on purpose, and watch what each bend does.

Here are two groups of 30 positive values: a hormone concentration, in nanograms per millilitre. Simulated, and illustrative. Group A is blue, group B is pink.

Look at the errors around each group's mean, in standard deviations. The pile leans right. Its skewness is 1.7.

Move one: the square root. The square root of a value is the number that, multiplied by itself, gives it. It pulls the big values in. The skewness falls to 0.9.

Move two: the logarithm. Count how many times you multiplied by ten, as in 4.3. The skewness falls to 0.2.

Move three: ranks. Replace each value by its place in the line: 1 for the smallest, 60 for the largest. The pile is flat, and the skewness is near zero. Any increasing move leaves the ranks unchanged, so ranks never depend on the scale you chose.

Every move straightens something. But look at what happens to the answer.

On the raw scale, group B averages 16.66, and group A 12.12. The answer is a difference: B is 4.54 higher.

Guess before you look. On the log scale, what kind of sentence will the answer be?

The groups differ by 0.127 steps of ten. Put that step back on the value scale, ten to the power 0.127, and you get 1.34. The answer is now a ratio: B is 1.34 times A, in geometric means.

Not the same sentence. A gap of 4.54 nanograms, or 1.34 times. When effects multiply, the ratio is the natural way to say it. On ranks, the sentence changes again: who tends to be higher? That is episode 7.4.

There is one dial that holds these moves: raise every value to a power. Box and Cox described this family in 1964.

Power 1 changes nothing. Power one half is the square root. Power 0, by convention, is the log. Power minus 1 is one over the value.

Turn the dial. The skewness reads 1.7, then 0.9, then 0.2, and at minus 1 it overshoots, to minus 1.1.

The data can choose the power. For each power, we ask how well the transformed groups fit a bell with a common spread. The best power is minus 0.15. The data cannot tell apart anything from minus 0.45 to 0.20, and zero is inside. So the data say: multiply, take the log.

Another data set. Areas of cells, 30 per group, each the square of a length. Illustrative. The dial picks 0.45, with a range from 0.20 to 0.80, which holds one half. The square root of an area is a length, and lengths add.

And minus 1 has a use too. The reciprocal of a time is a speed.

Does the log help the test? Measure it. 10,000 simulated studies, two groups of 15, multiplicative data.

With no difference at all, the raw t-test raises a false alarm in 3.6 percent of studies, the log version in 4.9.

With a true ratio of 1.5, the raw test finds it in 12.6 percent of studies. The log version, in 18.8. The raw test is not wrong about false alarms. It is just blind. On the log scale, effects add, and the noise is even.

Now a case where the log answers the wrong question. Hospital costs, in dollars, illustrative. Two treatments, 200 patients each. Guess: which looks cheaper?

On the log scale, treatment B. Its geometric mean is 941, against 1,032. A ratio of 0.91.

But the mean cost per patient is 2,005 for B, against 1,439. The total bill is 400,927 dollars, against 287,859. Ten patients carry 36 percent of B's bill.

No contradiction. The log describes a typical patient. The budget is the total, and the total is the mean times the number of patients. For the budget, do not transform. And never try powers until the normality test passes: that polishes the picture, not the question.

One trap remains. A log needs positive values. The log of zero is undefined: how many times do you multiply by ten to reach zero? Never.

So people add a small constant first. But which one? Take colony counts on 40 plates per group, illustrative. 14 plates of A are at zero, and 31 of B.

Change the constant, and watch the ratio of geometric means. With 0.01: 0.045. With 0.1: 0.12. With 1: 0.31. With 10: 0.67. With 100: 0.95. The p-value drifts from 0.0004 to 0.28.

The constant is not a detail. It is the answer. And it carries a unit, so changing the unit changes the result. With many zeros, the question is really two: does a plate have any, and how many? That is a model for counts, chapter 10.

Here is the rule in six places. All illustrative.

Reaction times: effects scale by proportions. Ask about ratios and take the log, or study speed, the reciprocal.

A hormone concentration: effects multiply, so the question is a ratio. Log.

Costs and incomes: effects multiply, but the budget is a sum. Keep the raw mean, or resample, chapter 14.

EEG band power: positive and skewed, effects multiply. Log, and a difference of logs is a ratio of powers.

Areas and volumes: an area is a length squared. Take the square root of an area, the cube root of a volume.

Counts with many zeros: not a job for a transformation. A model for counts, chapter 10. And ratings from 1 to 5, or wild outliers: ranks, episode 7.4.

Remember: a transformation changes the question. Choose it because effects add on the new scale and the question still makes sense there, not to pass a normality test.
