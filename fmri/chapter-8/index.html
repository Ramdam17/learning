Ultra-high field and cortical layers

Stronger magnets give more signal and allow submillimeter voxels, enough to sample the six layers of cortex, where feedforward input and feedback arrive at different depths. Standard BOLD is biased toward draining surface veins, like a bright city washing out a telescope's outer ring. Blood-volume contrast such as VASO reduces this bias, but distortions, heating and scarcity of scanners remain the price.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the Observatory. In this chapter, we look at what fMRI can do beyond the classic group map. First stop, stronger magnets and the layers of cortex.

Picture an old telescope pointed at Saturn. Through a weak lens, you see a yellow smudge with a vague halo. Swap in a better lens and the halo splits into separate rings, each with its own brightness. That is the promise of ultra-high field fMRI.

Most research scanners run at 3 Tesla. Ultra-high field usually means 7 Tesla or more. In October 2017, the first 7 Tesla scanner was cleared for clinical use in the United States for the head and the limbs. A stronger field gives more signal, and the BOLD contrast itself grows with field strength. You can spend that gain on smaller voxels, well below one millimeter.

Why does that matter? Because the cortex is a thin sheet, only about two to four millimeters thick, and it is organized in six layers. Different layers do different jobs. In sensory cortex, signals coming up from the thalamus, the feedforward input, arrive mostly in the middle layer. Feedback from higher areas lands mainly in the superficial and the deep layers. So if you could read activity layer by layer, you could tell a signal going up from a signal coming down.

Here is the first rule. At 3 mm, one voxel swallows the whole thickness of cortex, and every layer is blended. At 0.8 mm, you get perhaps three samples across the sheet. That is just enough to sketch a profile from the surface to the white matter, and only with careful analysis.

Now comes the glare. Back at the telescope, a bright city on the horizon washes out the outer ring. In the brain, the city is the large veins on the cortical surface. The standard BOLD signal, measured with gradient echo, picks up draining blood that flows from deep layers towards the surface. So BOLD profiles grow stronger towards the surface, whatever the neurons were doing underneath.

The fix is a different contrast. VASO, short for vascular space occupancy, measures changes in blood volume rather than oxygenation. It is far less sensitive to those surface veins. Think of it as a filter against light pollution. You lose some signal, but the rings come back where they belong.

A beautiful example comes from Laurentius Huber and colleagues in 2017 in the primary motor cortex. Tapping the fingers activated the superficial layers, which receive input from other cortical areas, and the deep layers, which send commands down to the spinal cord. Being touched without moving activated mainly the superficial layers. Input and output separated inside a strip of cortex a few millimeters thick.

And in vision, Kok and colleagues in 2016 showed people illusory shapes, outlines that the brain fills in by itself. The response in primary visual cortex appeared selectively in the deep layers, where top-down feedback is expected. Descending and ascending made visible.

Now the price. Distortions grow with field strength, especially near the sinuses. The radio waves become uneven, leaving darker patches, often in the temporal lobes. Tissue heating limits which sequences you can run. Some people feel dizzy when moving into the bore. Head motion, even a fraction of a millimeter, can smear one layer into the next. And 7 Tesla scanners are still rare and expensive, so few labs have one.

So to recap, 7 Tesla buys signal and signal buys smaller voxels. The cortex has layers, with input and feedback arriving at different depths. Surface veins bias BOLD towards the top, and blood volume methods like VASO reduce that bias. And every gain comes with distortions, heating, and fewer machines.

PLATE 8.24:08

Real-time fMRI and neurofeedback

Real-time fMRI shows participants a signal from their own brain while they are scanned, like adjusting a hotel shower with a delayed hot-water pipe. The blood response makes feedback arrive seconds late. Only sham, alternative-region and no-feedback controls show that learning is real and specific rather than breathing, motion or effort.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, every analysis happened long after the scan. Today, we close the loop. Real-time fMRI lets people watch a signal from their own brain while they are still inside the scanner.

Picture an old hotel shower. You turn the knob towards warm and nothing happens. The hot water is still traveling down a long pipe. So you turn further and a few seconds later you are scalded. You turn back and soon you are freezing. With practice, you learn to make a small move and wait. That is what neurofeedback with fMRI feels like.

Here is the loop. The scanner acquires a new volume every one or two seconds. Software receives it immediately, corrects head motion, removes slow drifts, and averages the signal in a target region. The result is shown to the participant, often as a thermometer, a few seconds later. The participant tries a mental strategy, imagining a movement, a memory, or a feeling, and watches whether the bar rises.

The idea is old. Robert Cox and colleagues showed real-time brain mapping as early as 1995. Today, open toolboxes make it possible on standard research scanners.

Now the long pipe. The blood response peaks roughly five seconds after the neurons fire. Add the time to receive and process the volume. So the bar always shows what the brain was doing several seconds ago. Participants have to learn with delayed information. Some studies show the average over a whole block rather than a jumpy bar. Others accept the delay and train people to anticipate.

The target can be a single region, or it can be a pattern. Shibata and colleagues in 2011 used a decoder in early visual cortex and rewarded people when a particular pattern appeared. Participants improved on a visual task without knowing what they had been trained on.

There are hopeful results. In 2005, deCharms and colleagues trained people to control the rostral anterior cingulate cortex, a region involved in pain. Healthy volunteers and patients with chronic pain could learn some control, and their pain ratings changed with it. But larger follow-ups did not confirm the clinical benefit. That is a pattern the field knows well, promising pilots harder replications.

So what makes a convincing study? Controls. Think of the office thermostat that is not connected to anything. People turn it, feel more comfortable, and nothing has changed in the room. Neurofeedback needs the same test. In sham feedback, participants see a signal that is not their own, for instance, a recording from another person. Other controls give feedback from a different brain region or practice the same mental strategy without any feedback. Without these, motivation, expectation, and practice can look exactly like learning.

There is another trap. A meta-analysis by Emmert and colleagues in 2016 found that a common network, including the anterior insula and the basal ganglia, was active during regulation whatever the target region. Trying hard has its own signature, so check that the change is specific to the target.

And mind the noise. Breathing and head motion can push the signal up or down. If a participant learns to hold their breath, the bar may rise without any change in neurons. A review by Heunis and colleagues in 2020 found that fewer than a third of studies reported standard real-time denoising steps. A consensus checklist called CredNF now lists what to design and report.

So to recap, real-time fMRI closes the loop between brain and screen. The feedback always arrives seconds late. The target can be a region or a decoded pattern. And only sham, alternative region, and no feedback controls can show that learning is real, specific, and not just breathing.

PLATE 8.33:54

Precision fMRI · One brain, many hours

Group averaging blurs individual brain layouts, like stacking different fingerprints into a smudge. Precision fMRI scans a few people for hours each, so individual networks emerge sharply, as in the Midnight Scan Club. These layouts are stable across sessions and differ from the average, supporting surgical mapping and within-person designs alongside large samples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Most fMRI studies scan 30 people for 10 minutes each and average them. Precision fMRI flips the recipe. A few people scan for many hours each.

Picture a detective with fingerprints. If you press 30 different fingers onto the same card, one on top of the other, you get a grey smudge. Every finger has ridges but they never line up so they cancel out. Now press the same finger 40 times. Each print is faint and patchy, but the ridges fall in the same place. Stack them and every loop and whirl appears sharp.

A group map is the smudge. Each brain has its own layout of networks, shifted and folded a little differently. Averaging aligns them only roughly so the borders blur, and small features that exist in every single person can vanish from the average.

To see the ridges of one brain, you need a lot of data from that brain. Short scans of 5 to 20 minutes are too noisy to map an individual reliably. In 2015, Laumann and colleagues analyzed one highly sampled person, scanned repeatedly for more than a year, with around 14 hours of data. The individual parcellation was reproducible, and it fit that person far better than the group atlas.

Then came the Midnight Scan Club. Gordon and colleagues in 2017 scanned 10 people in 10 separate sessions each. Every session started at midnight, partly because scanner time was much cheaper at night. Each person gave about 5 hours of resting state and more than 5 hours of tasks.

What did they see? Every person had the familiar large networks. But the borders moved and some people had small patches belonging to an unexpected network, sometimes called network variants. These variants were stable across sessions when enough data was used. Other work found the same thing with the default network, which splits into two interleaved networks once you look within a single person.

How much data is enough? It depends on the measure. For resting state networks, reliability keeps improving well beyond the usual 10 minutes. One study reported correlations above 0.8 for network variants with about 40 minutes of clean data per person. Gratton and colleagues in 2018 showed that networks are dominated by stable features of the group and of the individual, and change only modestly from day to day or task to task. Which is good news, the individual layout is a real, durable thing.

Why does this matter? First, for patients. A surgeon needs to know where language sits in this brain, not in the average brain. Precision maps also open the door to targeting stimulation or following one person's illness over time.

Second for statistics, when each person is measured many times, you can test effects within that person with much less noise. That makes within person designs a natural answer to the power problem we will meet in chapter 9. Some questions need thousands of people. Others are better answered by a handful of people, each measured very well.

There are limits, of course. A few people do not represent a population. Long scanning is tiring, and sleepiness and head motion creep in over hours. In the Midnight Scan Club itself, one participant was excluded for motion and drowsiness. And the scanner time is expensive, even at midnight.

So to recap, group maps blur individual layouts. Precision fMRI collects hours of data from each person. Individual networks are stable and different from the average, and this approach complements large samples rather than replacing them.

PLATE 8.43:50

Two brains, two scanners · Hyperscanning

Hyperscanning records two brains during one interaction, from two scanners linked over the internet in 2002 to dual-head coils fitting two people in one magnet. fMRI brings whole-brain depth, including deep structures, but at the cost of natural interaction and speed, unlike EEG and fNIRS. Shared stimuli can fake synchrony, so pseudo-pairs provide the control.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last episode, we looked at one brain for many hours. Today, we look at two brains at the same time while they interact. This is hyperscanning.

Picture two chess players in two different cities. They cannot sit at the same table, so they play by telephone. Each move is announced, the other player answers, and the game goes on. Now imagine you could watch both players' brains during every move. That was the idea behind the very first fMRI hyperscanning.

In 2002, Read Montague and colleagues linked two scanners through the internet, one in Houston and one in Atlanta. They called the technique hyperscanning. Two people played a simple game of deception against each other, while both brains were scanned in synchrony with the game. The hardware and software kept the two scanners and the two players on the same clock.

Why go to such trouble? Because social interaction is not one brain watching a recording. Each person's actions become the other person's stimulus, and both adapt in real time. With hyperscanning, you can model the exchange itself. Who reacted to whom? How a decision in one brain predicts a signal in the other, and how this changes as trust builds or breaks. Later studies used this setup for economic games such as the trust game played over many rounds.

Then came a bolder idea, two people in one scanner. Ray Lee and colleagues in 2012 built a pair of head coils that fit inside a standard bore, so two people could lie side by side and make eye contact. In 2020, Renvall and colleagues described a two-helmet coil, eight channels per person, and scanned 10 pairs face-to-face while one partner touched the other. The price was lower signal than a standard 32-channel head coil, but the method worked.

So what does fMRI bring to two-person neuroscience compared with EEG and fNIRS? Its strength is space. You see the whole brain, including deep structures like the striatum, the amygdala, or the insula, which reward and emotion studies care about.

But the constraints are severe. People lie on their backs, they must stay still, and they usually cannot see each other. Conversation is limited by the noise and by jaw motion. And the signal is slow, sampled every second or two, so fast exchanges like gestures or turn-taking in speech are blurred together.

EEG and fNIRS sit at the other end. They allow real face-to-face interaction, even walking, talking, playing music together. EEG follows milliseconds, but they mostly see the surface of the cortex. The three methods answer different questions, and the best choice depends on which question you ask.

One trap is shared by all of them. If two people see the same screen, hear the same sounds, and follow the same rhythm, their brains will look synchronized even if they never interact at all. Think of two chess players in different games who glance at the clock at the same moment. The fix is a control, pseudo-pairs made of people who were never paired but went through the same task. Real interaction should beat them. The fNIRS series covers these synchrony pitfalls in detail, and they apply here too.

So to recap, hyperscanning records two brains during one interaction. It began in 2002 with two linked scanners. Dual coils can now fit two heads in one magnet. fMRI offers depth and spatial detail, but at the cost of natural interaction and speed. And shared stimuli must be ruled out with pseudo pairs.
