PLATE 18.17:10

How big?

A p-value says whether an effect exists, not how big it is. One sleep difference can be told as 24.9 minutes, a Cohen's d of 0.55, a correlation of 0.27, or an odds ratio of 2.71. Size must be judged in the units of each field, with an interval. A tiny effect can be hugely significant while a big one is not.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the cooperative's fruit press. Last time, we saw what noise does to the numbers we report. Today's question comes after every test. The p-value says whether there is an effect. How big is it?

Take a simulated sleep trial. Forty people use a sleep app, forty do not, and we measure their sleep in minutes. The app group sleeps 24.9 minutes longer. That is the effect in its original units, and it is the clearest way to say it.

But 24.9 minutes means little without the spread. The people differ by 45.2 minutes. Divide the difference by the spread: 24.9 over 45.2 is 0.55. This is Cohen's d: the effect counted in standard deviations, a unit that does not depend on minutes, or points, or millimetres.

A third way is a correlation. Code the group as zero or one, and correlate it with the sleep. For equal groups, r is d over the square root of d squared plus 4. Here, 0.55 over 2.07 is 0.27. The data give 0.27 too.

A fourth way is an odds ratio: the odds of a good night in the app group, over the odds in the other. A standard rule gives it from d: e to the power d times 1.814. Here, 2.71. The rule assumes a logistic-shaped outcome, so it is a translation, not a measurement.

One difference, four ways: 24.9 minutes, 0.55 standard deviations, a correlation of 0.27, an odds ratio of 2.71. None is truer. Each speaks to a different reader.

What does d look like? Two hills of sleep, one per group, the same width. At d equal to 0.2, they almost coincide. Guess before you see: pick one person from each group at random. At d equal to 0.8, how often does the person from the higher group sleep more? 71 percent. At 0.5, 64 percent. At 0.2, 56 percent. At d equal to 2, the hills barely touch, and it is 92 percent. These values are exact for two bell curves, and a million simulated pairs agree.

In 1988, Jacob Cohen proposed 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 as small, medium and large. He also wrote that these words are relative, not only to each other, but to the area of behavioural science. So let us say the size in the units of each field. These examples are illustrative.

A drug lowers blood pressure by 8 millimetres of mercury, with a spread of 12. The d is 0.67. For a doctor, that is a lot: a 2016 analysis of 123 trials found that every 10 millimetres of lower systolic pressure went with about 20 percent fewer major cardiovascular events.

A teaching method adds 3 points on a test of 100, with a spread of 15. The d is 0.2. If it costs nothing, a school may adopt it. If it costs a year of training, it may not.

Two groups of adults differ in height by 13 centimetres, with a spread of 6.5. The d is 2. Nobody asks whether that matters. It is a fact about people.

A reaction-time effect of 20 milliseconds, with a spread of 50, gives a d of 0.4. For a sprinter at the start, a lot. For someone waiting for a web page, nothing.

A page change moves the click rate from 5.0 to 5.1 percent. The odds ratio is 1.02, and the d is about 0.01. But with 10 million visitors, that is 10,000 more clicks. In its own units, the size is what a business debates.

Every effect size is an estimate, so it needs an interval. The true d is 0.5. A study of 10 people per group gives 0.14, with an interval from minus 0.74 to 1.02. It fits no effect, and a huge one. With 40 per group: 0.45, from 0.01 to 0.90. With 160: 0.44, from 0.22 to 0.67. With 640: 0.48, from 0.37 to 0.59. Four times the people, half the width. Over 20,000 studies of each size, these intervals contain the true value between 94.5 and 95.2 percent of the time.

There is a small catch. In small studies, d is a little too big. We simulate 200,000 studies with a true d of 0.5. With 5 people per group, the average d is 0.556. With 10, it is 0.521. With 20, 0.511. In 1981, Larry Hedges gave a correction: multiply d by 1 minus 3 over 4 times the degrees of freedom, minus 1. With 10 per group, the degrees of freedom are 18, and the factor is 0.958. The result is called g. Its average is 0.499 with 10 per group, and 0.502 with 5. From about 20 per group, the correction hardly matters.

Now the moment that matters. A teaching method truly adds 0.6 points on a test of 100, a d of 0.04. We test 50,000 students per group, in a simulation. We measure 0.76 points. The d is 0.05. The p-value is about one in a quadrillion. Overwhelmingly significant.

Next, a drug that truly lowers blood pressure by 8, a d of 0.67. We test 8 people per group. This simulated study is the first one, among our seeds, that is not significant, and 76 percent of such studies are not. We measure 10.8, and the d is 1.00. The p-value is 0.066. Not significant.

A careless headline says: the tiny effect works, the big effect does not. These are two different questions. The p-value asks whether we can tell the effect from zero, given the noise and the number of people. The size asks how far from zero. The first study is sure about a tiny effect: its interval for d runs from 0.04 to 0.06. The second knows little about a big one: from minus 0.04 to 2.04, from nothing to enormous. When a study is not significant, what can we say? That is for next time.

Remember: the p-value says whether an effect stands out from the noise. The size, with its interval and in the units of the field, says whether it matters. Next time: no difference, or not enough data?

PLATE 18.27:11

No difference, or not enough data?

Two trials of a do-nothing supplement both give p equal to 0.30, yet one interval is narrow and the other fits a rise of 17 millimetres. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Declare a negligible bay beforehand, like parking lines, and test equivalence with two one-sided tests and a 90 percent interval. Otherwise no difference means not enough data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cooperative's fruit press. Last time, a big effect was not significant. A trial ends with a p-value of 0.30. The headline says: no difference. Is that true?

Take a simulated trial of a supplement against a placebo, measuring systolic blood pressure in millimetres of mercury. These numbers are illustrative. People differ by 12 millimetres, and the supplement does exactly nothing. Two teams run the trial.

Team A tests 500 people per group. The supplement group ends 0.78 millimetres higher, with an interval from minus 0.71 to plus 2.27. The p-value is 0.30. Team B tests 10 people per group. Its difference is 5.80 millimetres, with an interval from minus 5.73 to plus 17.33. The p-value is 0.30 as well.

Two studies, one p-value. But look at the intervals. Team A's interval is narrow: the effect is within a couple of millimetres. Team B's interval is huge. It fits no effect at all, and it fits a rise of 17 millimetres. A p-value of 0.30 says only that zero is inside the interval. It does not say what else is inside. In 1995, Douglas Altman and Martin Bland gave this a title in the British Medical Journal: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

To say no difference, we must first say how big a difference we would still call nothing. So before the data, choose the smallest effect that would matter. Here, say it is 3 millimetres, in either direction. Think of a parking bay. The lines are painted first, before any car arrives. Each study is a car, as long as its interval. If the whole car sits between the lines, the effect is negligible. Team A's car fits. Team B's car sticks out on both sides.

The test for this has two parts. The first asks: is the effect above minus 3? The second asks: is it below plus 3? Each is one-sided, each at 5 percent. If both say yes, the groups are equivalent. In 1987, Donald Schuirmann built this procedure for drugs, and in 2017, Lakens wrote a practical primer for psychologists.

There is a shortcut. Both tests pass when the 90 percent interval lies inside the bay. Not 95. Each test puts 5 percent in one tail, so together they leave 90 percent in the middle. So the 95 percent bar answers: is zero outside? The 90 percent bar answers: is the effect inside the bay? Two questions, two bars.

Team A's 90 percent interval runs from minus 0.47 to 2.03, inside the bay. The two one-sided p-values are under one in a million, and 0.0018. The larger one is the equivalence p-value: 0.0018. Team B's 90 percent interval runs from minus 3.72 to 15.31. Its one-sided p-values are 0.063 and 0.69. The larger is 0.69. Same p-value, different answers.

Does this test work? Simulate studies where the true effect is exactly zero, with a bay of 3 millimetres, and count how often a study concludes: equivalent. Guess before you see, with 10, 150, 280 and 500 people per group. With 10 per group: none of 20,000 studies. With 150: 39.2 percent. With 280: 81.0 percent. With 500: 97.9 percent. Even when the truth is exactly zero, a small study cannot say so. So the bay also sets the size of the study. To have an 80 percent chance of showing equivalence when the truth is zero, this bay needs about 280 people per group.

Now the opposite. The true effect is exactly 3 millimetres, right on the line, so the groups are not equivalent. How often does a study wrongly conclude equivalent? With 10 per group, never. With 150, 4.9 percent. With 280, 4.6. With 500, 5.1. About 5 percent at most: that is the guarantee of the test.

This is where the method comes from. A generic drug must act like the brand drug. Regulators compare how much drug reaches the blood, as a ratio of averages, and a common rule asks that the 90 percent interval of that ratio lies between 80 and 125 percent. On a log scale, those limits are the same distance from one. Illustrative example: a ratio of 0.98, with a 90 percent interval from 0.91 to 1.06. Inside the bay.

Now the four outcomes, on one axis, in units of the bay.

Different, and not equivalent. A cheaper teaching method should be no worse. The smallest loss that matters is 3 points out of 100. The study finds minus 6, and the interval excludes zero and reaches past minus 3. It is worse. If the only question is no worse, one bound is enough: that is a non-inferiority test.

Equivalent, and not different. An EEG marker, compared between two groups, 150 people each. The smallest difference that matters is 0.5 microvolts. The study finds 0.1, with an interval that includes zero and a 90 percent interval inside the bay. We can say: no difference that matters.

Both. A new sensor against a reference thermometer, 100 pairs. Half a degree Celsius is the smallest error that matters. The sensor reads 0.12 degrees higher. That differs from zero, but it is a real difference too small to matter.

Neither. A side effect, in a trial with 60 people per arm. Nausea in 4 on the new drug, 3 on the old one. A difference of 2 points would matter. The study finds 1.7, with an interval from minus 6.7 to plus 10.0. It did not find a side effect. It did not rule one out either. There are not enough data.

The Bayes factor, from episode 16.5, is the other road to this question. It asks which hypothesis predicted the data better, and it can favour the null. But it needs a choice too: how wide the alternative is, and you must report it.

So when you read no difference, ask two things. Is the interval narrow? And what did the authors write down, before the data, as too small to matter? Without both, no difference means only: not enough data.

Remember: to conclude no difference, you need a narrow interval and a definition, written beforehand, of what would be negligible. Next time: meta-analysis, combining many studies.

PLATE 18.37:26

Meta-analysis

Ten studies, none significant alone, can be pooled. Each is weighted by one over its squared standard error, so the precise trial carries 80 percent of the weight in the example. The pooled diamond excludes zero, and a vote would say no effect in 93.3 percent of sets. When studies truly differ, the random effects model with tau gives honest intervals.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cooperative's fruit press. Last time, an interval told us whether to believe "no difference". Today there are ten studies of the same effect. Can they say together what none of them can say alone?

Here is a simulated case. A bedtime routine, and its effect on sleep, in standard deviations as in 18.1. Ten studies, from 20 to 200 people per group. Each study is a dot with its 95 percent interval. Stack the ten, and you have a forest plot. All ten intervals cross the line at zero. Not one study is significant alone.

Now combine them, but not with a plain average: a study of 200 people per group is more precise than a study of 20. Each study gets a weight, one divided by its squared standard error. On the plot, the weight is the size of the square. In 1976, Gene Glass gave this practice its name: meta-analysis.

Try two illustrative trials of a blood pressure drug. Trial X finds a drop of 4 millimetres, with a standard error of 2. Trial Y finds 1 millimetre, with a standard error of 1. The weights are one over four, which is 0.25, and one over one, which is 1. The weighted mean is 0.25 times 4, plus 1 times 1, divided by 1.25. That is 2 divided by 1.25: 1.6. A plain average would say 2.5. The precise trial carries 80 percent of the weight.

For the ten studies, the weights run from 2.3 percent for the smallest study to 23.5 percent for the largest. The pooled estimate is 0.13 standard deviations, with an interval from 0.03 to 0.22. That is the diamond at the bottom. It is narrower than any single interval, and it excludes zero. Ten intervals that cross zero, and one that does not.

Was that a lucky set? It was found, not built: the first in a long run of simulated sets with no significant study. So simulate again, 20,000 sets of ten, with a true effect of 0.20 and the same sizes. Count votes: if fewer than half the studies are significant, the vote says no effect. The vote says no effect in 93.3 percent of the sets. The pooled interval excludes zero in 98.2 percent. In 91.5 percent of all the sets, the vote says no and the pooling says yes. A meta-analysis is not a vote.

Does pooling find effects everywhere? Same sizes, true effect exactly zero, 20,000 sets. The pooled interval excludes zero in 4.9 percent: the usual false alarm rate.

The next question is what we assume about the studies. The first model says: one true effect, shared by every study. Differences between them are only chance. This is the fixed, or common, effect model. The second says: every study has its own true effect, drawn from a distribution. We estimate its mean, and its width, which is called tau. This is the random effects model. In 1986, Rebecca DerSimonian and Nan Laird described a simple way to fit it in clinical trials.

How would we know which one fits? Measure the spread between the studies. The statistic Q adds up how far each estimate sits from the pooled one, in units of its own error. If only chance is at work, Q is about the number of studies minus one. I squared turns the excess into a share. Higgins, Thompson, Deeks and Altman defined it in 2003: the part of the spread of the estimates that is not just chance. They tentatively called 25, 50 and 75 percent low, moderate and high.

Two simulated sets. In the first, ten drug trials, every true effect is 0.30. Q is 3.1, tau is zero, I squared is 0 percent. Both models give 0.25, with an interval from 0.15 to 0.34. In the second, ten classrooms try a teaching method, and the true effects differ: their spread is 0.25. Q is 27.1, tau is 0.22, I squared is 67 percent. The fixed model says 0.23, from 0.13 to 0.32. The random model says 0.27, from 0.09 to 0.44. Its interval is almost twice as wide.

Why? The random model adds tau squared to every study's variance, so the weights flatten. The largest study falls from 23.7 to 13.6 percent. The smallest rises from 2.3 to 5.3. Which interval is honest? Guess before you see. Repeat the second set 10,000 times, and ask how often each interval holds the mean true effect, 0.30. The fixed interval holds it 64.4 percent of the time. The random one, 91.4. When studies truly differ, the fixed interval is too confident.

Now the strong moment. In 16.6, we met eight schools, from Rubin, 1981: real data. Put studies in the role of schools. A random effects meta-analysis of those same eight numbers is the hierarchical model of 16.6: a common level, and a width tau.

Q is 4.7, on 7: no more than chance would give. So tau is zero, and I squared is 0 percent. The pooled effect is 7.7 points, with an interval from minus 0.3 to 15.7. That is exactly the complete pooling of 16.6. But 16.6 found a level of 7.9, and a tau with a median of 5.2, from 0.2 to 20.7. Why the difference? Here, tau is a single number computed from the data, and it lands on the floor. There, tau is a whole posterior, which keeps every value the data still allow. Eight studies cannot pin tau down. I squared of 0 percent does not mean the schools are identical. It means the data cannot tell.

Three more contexts, all simulated. Exercise and mood: 12 trials, 6 significant alone, pooled effect 0.38, from 0.28 to 0.48. An EEG effect across 9 labs: pooled 1.14 microvolts, from 0.28 to 1.99. And ten small aspirin-type trials, with a risk ratio, none significant alone. The pooled risk ratio is 0.96, from 0.79 to 1.16. The truth is 0.80, and still the interval includes 1. Over 10,000 such sets, pooling excludes 1 in only 58 percent. Pooling narrows an interval. It does not create data.

One more limit: a meta-analysis can only combine the studies that exist. The missing ones are episode 18.4.

Remember: weigh each study by its precision, and measure how much the studies differ before you trust one common number. Next time: the missing studies.

PLATE 18.47:25

The missing studies

A simulated machine runs 100 studies of a zero effect, and only the 5 significant ones are published. Pooling the shelf gives 0.59, while pooling all 100 gives minus 0.01. This is the file drawer problem. Funnel plots and Egger's test can hint at missing studies but are not verdicts. Preregistration and registered reports help.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cooperative's fruit press. Last time, a meta-analysis combined ten studies of one effect. It could only combine the studies that exist. Today we ask about the ones that do not.

Here is a simulated machine. It runs 100 small studies of an effect from psychology, and the effect is exactly zero. Each study has between 10 and 60 people per group.

Every study goes through a gate. Is the result significant, with the effect favouring the treatment? If yes, it is published, and goes on the shelf. If not, it drops into a drawer.

Of the 100 studies, 5 clear the gate. The other 95 are in the drawer, and one of them is significant the wrong way.

Now do the meta-analysis of 18.3 on the shelf only. The 5 published studies pool to 0.59, with an interval from 0.36 to 0.82. A clear effect, from an effect that does not exist. Pool all 100 instead: minus 0.01, from minus 0.06 to 0.04. Zero, as it should be.

Robert Rosenthal described this in 1979. He called it the file drawer problem.

Was that a fluke? Repeat the whole machine 10,000 times, each with its own 100 studies. This run had 5 published studies, but on average it is 2.5. In 7.8 percent of the literatures, nothing is published at all. In all the others, the published studies, pooled, exclude zero in 99.8 percent, and their average pooled effect is 0.58. Pooling all 100, the interval excludes zero in 4.4 percent: the usual false alarm rate.

Is the problem only false alarms? Give a drug, tested against a placebo, a small real benefit of 0.20, and repeat 10,000 literatures of 100 studies. About 13 studies are published in each. Their pooled effect averages 0.55, which is 2.75 times the truth. All 100 together average 0.20. The published interval holds the truth in 2 literatures out of 10,000. This is the overshoot of 6.5: only the studies that overshoot clear the gate.

Can we see the missing studies? Sometimes. Plot each study's estimate against its precision, with the standard error on a reversed axis, so the largest studies sit at the top. This is a funnel plot. Here are 100 simulated studies, true effect 0.20. Small studies scatter widely at the bottom, large ones cluster at the top, and the funnel is symmetric around the truth.

Now keep only the published ones: 14 dots, all in the right wing. The funnel is lopsided.

In 1997, Egger, Davey Smith, Schneider and Minder turned the picture into a test. Regress each estimate, divided by its standard error, on its precision. An intercept away from zero measures the asymmetry. On all 100 studies: intercept 0.23, p-value 0.62. On the 14 published: intercept 2.94, p-value 0.011.

Guidelines say to use it only with at least 10 studies. So take 10 published studies from a hard gate, 10,000 times. The test flags asymmetry in 73.9 percent. Not 100.

Now let the drawer leak: one non-significant study in four is published too. The bias is still there: the published studies average 0.31, against a truth of 0.20. But the test flags it in only 5.0 percent, no better than chance. With 30 published studies, 5.8 percent.

And asymmetry can have another cause. Here nothing is hidden, but the small studies come from a different population, where the true effect is 0.50, against 0.20 for the larger ones. The test flags asymmetry in 20.5 percent. A lopsided funnel is a question, not a verdict.

Rosenthal asked: how many null studies, hidden in drawers, would cancel a published result? Take the 14 published studies. Their z values add up to 35.89. Squared, that is 1,288.09. Divided by 2.706, 476.01. Minus the 14 studies themselves, 462.01. About 462 null studies. It sounds reassuring.

But the real drawer holds 86 studies, and they do not average zero. The published estimate is 0.55, still far above the truth of 0.20. A later critique showed that it treats the drawer as unbiased, and a software manual now calls it mainly of historical interest.

Is this only a simulation? No. In 2008, Turner and colleagues compared FDA records with journal articles for 12 antidepressants. The FDA had 74 registered trials, and 31 percent were never published. In the journals, 94 percent of the trials looked positive. The FDA's own analysis found about half. The apparent effect was 32 percent larger overall.

In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 psychology studies from three journals. 97 percent of the originals had significant results. 36 percent of the replications did. The replication effects were about half the size of the originals.

Four more fields, simulated with the same machine. An EEG effect across 40 labs, true effect 0.10: 3 published, pooled 0.52. A nutrition claim, 80 studies, true effect 0.05: none is published at all. An education intervention, 60 studies, true effect 0.15: 6 published, pooled 0.56. And a candidate gene, 100 studies, true effect 0.10: 3 published, pooled 0.49.

Now the strong moment. In 12.1, we met three kinds of missing: completely at random, at random, and not at random. An unpublished study is the last kind, in its worst case: it is gone because of its own result. And not at random cannot be fixed from the data alone. A meta-analysis inherits the biases of what was published.

What helps? Record the plan before the results. In 2005, a group of medical journal editors said they would consider a trial only if it was registered before its first patient. A registered report goes further: the journal accepts the plan before the results exist, whatever they turn out to be. Preregistration is the subject of 22.1.

Remember: a meta-analysis is only as honest as the studies that reach it, so ask what is missing, and why. Next time: correlation, causation and randomization.
