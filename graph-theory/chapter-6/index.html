PLATE 6.14:39

Counting friends

In Florence's marriage network of fifteen families and twenty marriages, degree counts each family's partners: the Medici have six. Weights give strength, and arrows split degree into in-degree and out-degree. The friendship paradox shows most families have fewer partners than their partners average, 2.67 against 3.54, because well-connected vertices are counted more often.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Florence, in the early fourteen hundreds. The Strozzi are richer than the Medici. Yet it is the Medici who end up ruling the city. How?

A clue hangs on the wall of this loggia. Every shield is a family. Every ribbon is a marriage between two families, and in those days a marriage was an alliance.

John Padgett and Christopher Ansell drew this picture in nineteen ninety-three. Shields become vertices. Marriages become edges. The version everyone uses today has fifteen families and twenty marriages.

Now the first measurement of the chapter, and the simplest. Count the ribbons on a shield. That number is the degree, the same degree you met in episode one point one.

Imagine that every family keeps a dance card, with one line for each partner. The Medici card has six lines. The Strozzi and the Guadagni have four each. Most families have three lines or fewer, and four families have just one.

Now each circle swells with its card. The area follows the count, and the number sits above in blue. Add up the lines on all fifteen cards and you get forty, which is twice the twenty marriages, because each marriage is written on two cards. That is the handshake rule from episode one point one. On average, a family has two point six seven partners.

Is the family with the most partners the most central? It is the oldest answer, and a good first one, because degree needs only counting. Two refinements first.

First, links can differ in strength, as in episode one point three. Here is an invented example. Ola, Bo and Cy each have three links, but the links of Bo are thick and the links of Ola are thin. Add up the weights and you get the strength. Ola has three, Bo has nine and Cy has eight. Same degree, very different strength.

Second, links can have a direction, as in episode one point two. In this invented example, an arrow means that one person asks another to dance. Ben is asked by four people and asks one. His in-degree is four and his out-degree is one. Ben is popular. Dan asks four people, and nobody asks him. His out-degree is four and his in-degree is zero. Dan is active. So in-degree measures popularity, and out-degree measures activity. Every arrow has one head and one tail, so the two totals match, nine and nine.

Back to Florence, and a surprise hiding in these cards. Pick a family. Count its partners. Then ask each partner how many partners it has, and average the answers. Take the Acciaiuoli. They have one partner, the Medici, and the Medici have six.

Do this for every family. Eleven families out of fifteen have fewer partners than the average of their partners. One is level, and only three have more: the Medici, the Strozzi and the Guadagni. Average these averages over all fifteen families, and you get three point five four, against two point six seven for the families themselves.

This is the friendship paradox. Why does it happen? It is not about popularity, and it is not about self-esteem. It is about counting. Look at the cards again. The Medici are written on six cards. A family with one partner is written on one. Draw one name from the forty on the cards, and the Medici come up six times in forty, fifteen percent. Draw a family straight from the list of fifteen, and each one comes up one time in fifteen, under seven percent.

Well-connected families are counted more often, and when you ask your partners, you are drawing from the cards. The name you draw has, on average, three point three five partners. If everyone had the same number of partners, the paradox would vanish.

Scott Feld described it in nineteen ninety-one, in the American Journal of Sociology. Chapter twelve will put it to work. To protect a population, you can ask random people to name a friend, and vaccinate that friend, who is likelier to be well connected.

Is counting partners the whole story? The Medici have six, and the Strozzi and the Guadagni four. A card tells you how many partners a family has. It does not tell you where those partners stand.

To sum up. Degree counts the links at a vertex. With weights it becomes strength, and with arrows it splits into in-degree and out-degree. And on average your partners have more partners than you do, because well-connected vertices are counted more often.

Next time, standing in between. A family can be well placed, and not only well connected.

PLATE 6.26:20

Standing in between

Betweenness counts the traffic a vertex stands on: every pair sends a letter along shortest routes, and each family passed through keeps a coin. In Florence the Medici collect 47.5 coins and score 0.52, while the rich Strozzi hold barely nine. Removing the Medici splits the network into three pieces. Position, not wealth, explains their power.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Florence, in the year fourteen twenty-seven. The city taxes every household, and every household must declare what it owns. This great survey is called the catasto.

The question is a puzzle of history. The richest of the great families are the Strozzi, with one hundred and forty-six thousand lira. The Medici declare one hundred and three. In fourteen thirty-three, a faction led by the Albizzi has Cosimo de' Medici arrested and sent into exile. One year later, in fourteen thirty-four, Cosimo comes back, his rivals are banished, and his family leads Florence for generations. How does the poorer family win?

Look at the wall of this loggia. Each shield is a family. Each ribbon is a marriage between two families. In fifteenth-century Florence, a marriage was an alliance, and the great families chose their partners with care.

John Padgett and Christopher Ansell drew exactly this picture in nineteen ninety-three. Shields become vertices. Marriages become edges. The version everyone uses today has fifteen families and twenty marriages.

Last time, we counted friends. The Medici have six marriages, the Strozzi and the Guadagni four each. The Medici are ahead, but six against four is not a landslide. Today we ask a different question. Not how many links you have, but how much traffic you stand on.

Here is the game. Every pair of families sends one letter to each other. A letter travels along the marriages, and it always takes a shortest route, the one with the fewest steps. Every family that a letter passes through keeps one coin as a toll. The sender and the receiver keep nothing.

First letter. The Acciaiuoli write to the Strozzi. The shortest route goes through the Medici, then through the Ridolfi. Three steps. The Medici keep one coin, and the Ridolfi keep one coin.

Sometimes two routes tie. The Ginori write to the Tornabuoni. Through the Albizzi and the Medici, it takes three steps. Through the Albizzi and the Guadagni, it also takes three steps. Nothing tells us which one the letter takes, so the coin is split. The Albizzi are on both routes and keep a whole coin. The Medici and the Guadagni keep half a coin each.

Now let every pair write. Fifteen families make one hundred and five pairs, so one hundred and five letters cross the city. Watch the coins pile up. Each family grows with its coins. The area of its circle is proportional to the count, and the count is written above it in blue.

When the last letter lands, the Medici hold forty-seven and a half coins. The Guadagni hold twenty-three. The Albizzi hold nineteen. The rich Strozzi hold barely nine. And four families hold no coin at all. Each of them has a single marriage, so a letter can end at their door, but it can never pass through.

This count has a name. It is the betweenness of a vertex, defined by Linton Freeman in nineteen seventy-seven. To compare networks of different sizes, we divide by the number of pairs that do not include the family itself. With fifteen families, that makes ninety-one pairs. The Medici score zero point five two. They collect the toll of more than half of the pairs around them. The Guadagni score zero point two five. The highest possible score is one. It belongs to the centre of a star, through which every letter must pass.

Here is the moment. On degree, the Medici were ahead. On betweenness, they score twice as much as the next family. Why? The ranking has reshuffled. The Strozzi were tied for second on degree, and they fall to seventh on betweenness. The Medici married into families that did not marry each other. Take the Medici away, and the network falls apart into three pieces. The Acciaiuoli are left alone. The Salviati and the Pazzi are cut off. Now take the Strozzi away instead. Nothing breaks. Every family can still reach every other.

Padgett and Ansell put this in words: the Medici alone spanned the gaps inside the elite. Betweenness puts a number on that idea. This picture does not show their money. It shows their position.

Now look at the Salviati. They have only two marriages, fewer than the Strozzi. Yet their betweenness, zero point one four, is higher than the Strozzi's, zero point one zero. The reason is simple. The Pazzi married only into the Salviati, so every letter to or from the Pazzi must cross the Salviati's door. A family with few links can stand on a lot of traffic, if it is the bridge between two groups. And the Guadagni, second on this measure, declared only eight thousand lira in fourteen twenty-seven. Degree counts your links. Betweenness counts the traffic you stand on.

Two warnings before we leave. First, betweenness assumes that whatever moves on the network moves along shortest routes. A letter may do that. A rumour spreads every way at once. Episode six point five returns to this question. Second, when the edges carry weights, the shortest routes depend on how you turn a strength into a length. One over the weight, or one minus the weight, can change who stands in between. You met this trap in episode two point three.

One more thing. You can charge the same toll on the edges instead of the vertices. The busiest edges are the bridges between groups, and in chapter eight we will cut them, one by one, to find communities. And counting every route of every pair sounds slow on a large network. In two thousand and one, Ulrik Brandes found a way to get all the scores in about the time of one walk outward from each vertex. That is what network software runs today.

To sum up. Send a letter between every pair of vertices, along the shortest routes. Give each vertex a coin for every letter that passes through it, and split the coin when routes tie. The total is betweenness. A vertex with few links can score high if it is a bridge. In Florence, the Medici stood in between, and the rest of the elite had to go through them.

Next time, a third way to be central. Not having many friends, not standing in between, but being close to everyone. We will measure how fast each family can reach all the others.

PLATE 6.35:48

Close to everyone

Closeness measures how fast a vertex reaches everyone: the inverse of its average distance. The Medici lead with 0.56. One unreachable family makes every distance infinite, so harmonic closeness averages inverses instead, scoring the unreachable as zero. A vertex close to everyone need not be the one in between, though in Florence both measures point to the Medici.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the Medici stood in between. Today, a different question. Who can reach everyone the fastest?

Imagine a piece of news starting at one family. How many marriages must it cross to reach every other family? Some families are one step from many others. Others sit at the edge, and the news has to walk.

This question is old. In the late nineteen forties, the social psychologist Alex Bavelas was studying how the pattern of who can talk to whom changes the work of a small group. He proposed a number for each member: how far, in steps, that member is from all the others. Gert Sabidussi wrote it in the language of graphs in nineteen sixty-six, as the sum of the distances.

Let us compute it. Take five people in a line, A to E. Stand on C and count the steps to everyone else. One to B, one to D, two to A, two to E. The total is six. Now stand on A. One, two, three, four. That makes ten.

This total is the farness. Divide it by the number of others, four, and you get the average distance. C averages one and a half steps. Person A averages two and a half. Closeness is the inverse of the average distance. C scores zero point six seven, B and D score zero point five seven, and the two ends score zero point four. Short distances give a high score.

Now the Florentine families. Hold the network by the Medici, and let everyone else fall onto shelves. Each shelf is one step further down. Six families land on the first shelf. Five land on the second. Three land on the third. Six times one, plus five times two, plus three times three, makes twenty-five. The Medici are one point seven nine steps from the other families, on average.

Now hold the network by the Pazzi instead. One family on the first shelf, the Salviati. One on the second. Five on the third, four on the fourth, and three on the fifth. Forty-nine in all, an average of three and a half steps.

Here is closeness for every family. The Medici lead with zero point five six. The Ridolfi follow with zero point five. The Pazzi come last with zero point two nine. The scores sit in a narrow band, because in a small network every distance is short.

Now a problem. So far, every family could reach every other. But the full table of Padgett and Ansell has sixteen families. The sixteenth is the Pucci, who have no marriage in this network. They are alone, like the islands of episode two point four.

The distance from the Medici to the Pucci is infinite. So is the distance from every other family. Every average becomes infinite, and every closeness becomes zero. One lonely family has erased the whole ranking.

Here is the fix. Do not average the distances. Average their inverses. A family one step away counts one. A family two steps away counts one half. Three steps away, one third. And a family you can never reach counts zero, because one over infinity is zero.

For the Medici, that is six at one step, five at two steps, three at three steps, and the Pucci at zero. Six, plus two and a half, plus one, plus nothing, makes nine and a half. Divide by the fifteen others, and the Medici score zero point six three. This is harmonic closeness.

The Pucci get zero, and that is honest. The others keep a sensible ranking. The Guadagni come second with zero point five four, and the Ridolfi third with zero point five three. Marchiori and Latora used the same trick in two thousand, to describe a whole network by the harmonic mean of its distances. Boldi and Vigna studied it as a centrality in two thousand fourteen. They describe it as a correction of Bavelas's closeness, made to take unreachable vertices into account. In their axioms, it was the measure that passed every test.

Now the moment. Do closeness and betweenness always crown the same vertex? Look at this small town. It is invented for the picture. A dense town on the left, and a lane leading out to a far hamlet on the right. Vertex H is the gate between the town and the lane.

The closest vertex to everyone is G, deep in the town. G is one point four four steps from the others, and scores zero point six nine. But the most in-between vertex is H. It scores zero point three nine on betweenness, because everything bound for the lane must cross the gate. And H is only tied for third on closeness, with zero point six.

Two measures, two winners, and neither is wrong. G asks how fast it can reach everyone. H asks how many routes depend on it.

And in Florence? On closeness, the Medici lead. On betweenness, the Medici lead too. Zero point five six, and zero point five two. Both measures agree, but only because of this graph. The Medici sat at the centre of the network and also on its bridges. Whether measures agree depends on the graph. Episode six point five explains why they can disagree.

To sum up. For each vertex, add up its distances to all the others and divide by their number. The inverse is closeness. When some vertices are out of reach, add up the inverses of the distances instead, with zero for the unreachable. That is harmonic closeness. A vertex close to everyone is not always the vertex in between. In Florence, both measures pointed to the Medici.

Next time, four families look identical. The Acciaiuoli, the Ginori, the Lamberteschi and the Pazzi have one marriage each. But is one marriage always worth the same?

PLATE 6.45:07

Important friends

Four Florentine families with one marriage each look tied, yet marrying the Medici is worth far more than marrying the Salviati. Give everyone a score of one, repeatedly sum the neighbours' scores and divide by the biggest: that is eigenvector centrality. On the Web, a random surfer who sometimes jumps gives PageRank, and the jump rescues the surfer from dead ends.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Four Florentine families have exactly one marriage each. The Acciaiuoli, the Ginori, the Lamberteschi and the Pazzi. On a count of links, they are tied.

But are they equal? The Acciaiuoli married the Medici. The Pazzi married the Salviati, who have only two marriages themselves. One link into the Medici is surely not worth the same as one link into the Salviati.

Here is an old idea. You are important if you are linked to important people. That sounds like a circle. To know my score, you need my friends' scores. To know theirs, you need mine. And yet we can compute it.

Give every family the same score, one. Then play a round. Every family takes the sum of its neighbours' scores. The numbers grow, so we divide them all by the biggest one, and the top family is back to one. Then play another round, and another.

After the first round, each score is just the degree, divided by six. The Medici stay at one. The Acciaiuoli, with a single partner, fall to zero point one seven.

In the second round, something new happens. A family is rewarded for having partners that have many partners. Round after round, the circles breathe in and out, and then they settle. From round twenty-seven on, not a single score changes at the second decimal.

At the end, the Medici still sit at one. The Strozzi follow with zero point eight three, then the Ridolfi with zero point seven nine. And look at the Guadagni. They were tied for second on degree. Now they are fifth. Their partners have two point five partners on average, against three for the Strozzi.

Now the four families with a single marriage. The Acciaiuoli score zero point three one. The Pazzi score zero point one zero. Same number of links, almost three times the score, because the one friend of the Acciaiuoli is the Medici.

This is eigenvector centrality. Phillip Bonacich proposed it in nineteen seventy-two, and a close idea comes from Leo Katz, in nineteen fifty-three, where distant friends count for less. The standard software gets the same scores as our rounds, to within a millionth. The name comes from linear algebra: the scores that stop moving are an eigenvector of the table of episode one point five. Chapter eleven explains how.

Now the Web. Pages are vertices, links are arrows, and the idea changes shape. A page is important if important pages link to it. In nineteen ninety-eight, Sergey Brin and Lawrence Page told this as a story about a surfer. A random surfer opens a page, clicks one of its links at random, and keeps going. The share of time spent on each page is its PageRank.

Here is a tiny web, invented for the picture. Six pages. A crowd of six hundred surfers starts at random places. Each step, every surfer clicks one link at random.

The crowd spreads along the arrows. But look at page E. It has no links out. Whoever reaches E can never leave. Step after step, surfers pour into it. After forty steps, five hundred and eighty-six of the six hundred are stuck there. The pages that people link to score almost nothing. These scores make no sense.

The fix is the jump. At every step, with probability fifteen percent, the surfer gets bored and jumps to a page picked at random among all six. Otherwise, the surfer clicks a link. Brin and Page usually set the chance of following a link at zero point eight five.

Run the crowd again. Now a surfer can escape from E. The crowd spreads, keeps moving, and settles. The circles stop changing, even though no surfer stops walking.

Page C gets thirty percent of the time, because three pages link to it. Page A gets twenty-four. Page E keeps eleven percent. And page F, which nobody links to, still keeps four percent, because the jumps land there. The software computes the exact values, and the crowd lands within half a percentage point of them.

A surfer who clicks at random is a random walk, the subject of chapter twelve. And the settled shares are an eigenvector again, of a table with the jump built in, the subject of chapter eleven. So the circle at the start and the surfer are one idea. Importance flows along the links, and the jump keeps it from draining into a dead end.

Counting friends, standing in between, having important friends. Each one answers a different question. So which of these measures should you trust? It depends on what travels along the links.

To sum up. Give everyone a score of one. Let each vertex take the sum of its neighbours' scores, divide by the biggest, and repeat until nothing moves. That is eigenvector centrality. On the Web, a surfer who follows links and sometimes jumps spends a share of time on each page. That is PageRank, and the jump rescues the surfer from dead ends.

Next time, we ask what each of these measures assumes about what flows through a network.

PLATE 6.55:06

What each centrality assumes

Degree, closeness, betweenness and eigenvector all put the Medici first among the Florentine families, then disagree. Each measure quietly assumes something different moving through the network: parcels on best routes, a coin passed at random, news copied to everyone, or an echo along every walk. There is no most central family, only one for a given flow, so say what flows first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We now have four ways to say who matters in a network. Degree counts the links. Closeness asks how near you are to everyone. Betweenness counts the best routes that pass through you. Eigenvector asks whether your friends matter. Put them side by side on the Florentine families. Do they agree?

On one thing, yes. The Medici come first in all four rankings, alone every time. After the Medici, the rankings fall apart. The Salviati have two marriages. By degree they are tenth. Measured by betweenness, they are fourth. The Peruzzi have three marriages, and share fourth place by degree. Seen through betweenness, they are eleventh. The Guadagni and the Strozzi both have four marriages. The Guadagni are second by betweenness and fifth by eigenvector. The Strozzi are seventh by betweenness and second by eigenvector.

So which measure is wrong? None of them. Each one answers a question about something that moves through the network. And each one quietly assumes what moves, and how. Stephen Borgatti made this point in two thousand five. He described a flow by two things: the routes it may take, and the way it spreads. A measure fits some flows, and misleads on the others. Here are four flows, in plain words. Each one matches a measure.

First, a parcel. It travels from one person to another along the best route, and it can only be in one place at a time. Betweenness tallies the best routes that run through each family. So betweenness assumes parcels on best routes. You met these routes in episode six point two.

Second, a coin passed at random. Whoever holds it picks a partner by chance, and hands it over. Watch it go. After ten thousand hands, the Medici have held the coin fifteen percent of the time. That is six links out of forty, which is fifteen percent. The Pazzi, with one link, held it under three percent of the time. The long-run share of a blind coin is the degree, divided by forty. So degree assumes a coin that wanders blindly.

Third, a piece of news. In every round, everyone who knows it tells all their friends at once. Nobody is skipped, and nothing is lost. So you hear it in the round that equals your distance from the source. Here the news starts at the Peruzzi. It reaches the last family, the Pazzi, in round five. Closeness averages that waiting time over every possible source. For the Medici the average wait is one point eight rounds. For the Peruzzi it is two point seven. So closeness assumes news that spreads everywhere in parallel, with the clock running.

Fourth, an echo. Now every copy is passed on again in every round, along every link, and it may also go straight back where it came from. Count the copies each family holds. After one round, the count is the degree. After a few more rounds, something settles. The share of copies held by each family stops moving. After twenty rounds the Medici hold twelve percent of all copies, and the order of the families is the eigenvector order. This is the relay of episode six point four, in disguise. Eigenvector centrality assumes copies that wander along every possible walk, backward steps included.

Four flows, four measures, one network. Change the flow, and the right answer changes. Ask who hears a rumour first, and closeness is your measure. Ask who sits on the best routes, and betweenness is. There is no most central family. There is only the most central family for a given flow. So before you choose a measure, say what flows.

This matters most when nobody knows what flows. Take a brain network built from correlations. Two regions are linked when their signals rise and fall together. That is a similarity, not a message. Here is a small simulated example. One region sends a signal to three others. There are three wires. But the three receivers move together, because they share a source. So the correlation network has six links, and three of them join regions that share no wire. Zalesky and colleagues showed in two thousand twelve that correlation networks are more clustered than random ones, by construction. In such a network, betweenness counts best routes as if signals travelled along them. Avena-Koenigsberger and colleagues pointed out in two thousand nineteen that routing along best routes requires global information about the whole network. A brain region may not have that. A betweenness hub in a correlation network is a claim about similarity, not proof of traffic. The EEG and fMRI series of this collection put these measures to work on real recordings.

To sum up. Betweenness assumes parcels on best routes. Degree assumes a coin passed at random. Closeness assumes news copied to everyone at once. Eigenvector assumes an echo that goes everywhere, backward included. Say what flows, then choose the measure.
