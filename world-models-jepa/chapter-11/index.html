LeCun's 2022 proposal

LeCun's 2022 position paper, version 0.9.2, proposes three ideas: learn mostly by observation without labels, predict in a representation space rather than pixels, and plan with the resulting model at several scales. Like a shadow that keeps the pose and drops the stitching, a JEPA predicts necklaces, not images. The episode sorts what the text claims, assumes and leaves open, including its hardest unsolved modules.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The lights go down on the world models of chapter ten, and a new stage appears. Welcome to the shadow theatre. Everything in this chapter is about one question: what should a machine predict, and in which space? We begin with the document that put that question on the table, in the summer of 2022.

Here it is, standing alone on the stage. It is called A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence, and it was written by Yann LeCun. The version we will use is 0.9.2, dated the twenty-seventh of June 2022. It sits on OpenReview, where it was posted that very day, and it is sixty-two pages long. The version number matters. A zero point nine sits below version one, and the OpenReview page lists no later version. The page has gathered a long thread of comments since.

Notice what the author says it is. In the very first lines, he calls it a position paper, and says that it is not a technical or scholarly paper in the traditional sense. He also says that almost all the ideas in it have been formulated by other people before him, and that he does not claim priority for them. What he offers is a proposal for how to assemble them into a consistent whole.

So this video does not ask whether the proposal works. It asks what the document says. And it sorts that into three piles: what the text claims, what it assumes, and what it leaves open. We open three curtains, one for each of its main ideas.

First curtain. Learn mostly by observation, without labels.

The paper starts from a puzzle. An adolescent can learn to drive a car in about twenty hours of practice. Meanwhile, the best machine learning systems of the time needed enormous amounts of data, and millions of reinforcement learning trials in virtual worlds, and they still fell short of human reliability. The answer the paper suggests is that people and animals learn models of how the world works.

And mostly, they learn them by watching. The reason is practical. Interactions with the real world are expensive and dangerous. An agent should learn as much as it can without interacting, so that it needs very few costly trials to learn a task. If you have followed chapter five, you will recognise this as self-supervised learning. The data carries its own answer, and nobody has to write the labels.

Second curtain. Predict in a representation space, rather than in pixels.

Here is the idea that gives the chapter its name. The paper calls it the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, or JEPA for short. Two encoders turn two pieces of the world into two necklaces. A predictor guesses the second necklace from the first. And the error is measured between necklaces, not between pixels.

Think of the shadow on the screen. The puppet has stitches, buttons and strings. The shadow keeps the pose and the direction of travel, and drops the rest. The paper makes the same point in its own words: predicting in representation space avoids predicting every detail, because the encoder can drop what is irrelevant.

The text is also clear about the price. A JEPA is not generative. You cannot easily use it to draw the next frame. It captures the dependencies between two things, and it does not paint either one. That is the trade, and chapter six showed why many people might accept it: painting every pixel is costly.

Third curtain. Plan with that model, at several scales.

Once an agent has a world model, it can use it. It imagines a sequence of actions, the model predicts what follows, and a cost tells the agent how bad each imagined future is. The agent picks the sequence with the lowest cost and plays the first step. You met this loop in chapter nine, under the name model-predictive control, and the paper uses that very name.

One requirement is specific to this paper. Everything in the architecture is meant to be differentiable, so that a gradient can flow from the cost back to the actions. That is how reasoning and planning are made compatible with gradient-based learning.

The other word in the curtain is several scales. A plan to travel across a city is not a list of muscle twitches. The paper proposes a hierarchy of JEPAs, where higher levels predict further ahead and in coarser terms. It is called H-JEPA, and it waits for chapter twelve.

That chapter will also open the full architecture. For now, we only name it. The paper describes six modules: a configurator, a perception module, a world model, a cost, a short-term memory, and an actor. That is the whole list, and we stop there.

Now the three piles.

What does the text claim? That it offers an overall architecture in which every module is differentiable. That a non-generative, hierarchical JEPA can serve as the predictive world model. And that such models can be trained with methods that are not contrastive, so that representations are both informative and predictable. The author lists these as the main original contributions, and he says that the rest is mostly not new.

What does it assume? That a good part of what an agent needs can be learned from watching. The paper itself calls this the main open question: how much can be learned from passive observation, how much needs moving around, and how much needs full agency. It also assumes that gradients can find good actions. And it takes a position in a debate. It says that scaling up generative models is not enough, and gives two reasons. That is an argument, and not a measurement.

What does it leave open? A lot, and the author says so in a section on what is missing. Can a hierarchical JEPA really be built and trained from video? How should the latent variable be kept small? How does the actor find good action sequences when the cost is not smooth? And the configurator, the module in charge of setting the others up, is called the most mysterious part. The text says it will probably take many years to work out.

Where did it go from there? In January 2023, a first model for images appeared, called I-JEPA. We will open it in episode 11.4. But the proposal itself stays a proposal. Each step since then tests one piece of it.

To sum up. A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence is a position paper, version 0.9.2, from June 2022. Its three curtains say: learn mostly by observation, predict in representation space, and plan with the model at several scales. It claims an architecture, it assumes that observation goes a long way, and it leaves many things open. Next time: the JEPA recipe, the four ingredients of the idea, one piece at a time.

PLATE 11.24:50

The JEPA recipe

A JEPA sees one part of the world and predicts the representation of a hidden part, never repainting it. Four pieces make it up: a context encoder, a twin encoder that follows it as a moving average, a predictor told where the hidden block sits, and a loss on necklaces. Like a shadow, it keeps what is predictable and must be guarded against collapse.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the shadow theatre. Behind the screen, a puppeteer moves a puppet covered in buttons, stitches and strings. The audience sees only its shadow. The shadow is simpler than the puppet, and yet it keeps what matters: the pose, the shape, where the puppet is heading. Today's recipe is built on that idea. It is called the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, or JEPA for short. By the end of this video, you will know its four ingredients, and the one trap that can ruin it.

Start with the intuition. Take a picture, and cover a part of it. Now you have two pieces: the part you can see, and the part you cannot. A painter would try to repaint the hidden part, pixel by pixel. JEPA never does that. It predicts a short description of the hidden part, a necklace of a few beads. It sees one part of the world, and predicts the representation of another part, without ever repainting it.

Let's build the diagram, one piece at a time. On the left, we have an image, with a few blocks hidden. The part we can see is the context. It goes into an encoder, our friend ENKO, and a necklace comes out. That is the context necklace.

Now the hidden part. We call it the target. It goes through a second encoder, drawn with a dotted outline: ENKO's twin. It produces the target necklace, which is the answer we want to hit. In I-JEPA, the paper we will open in episode 11.4, the twin actually looks at the whole image, and the target blocks are cut out of its output. They are not hidden at the input.

The twin does not learn by itself. Its weights are a slow moving average of the weights of the first encoder. This is the exponential moving average, or EMA. The twin follows its sibling, with a delay. The red bar on its arrow means that no gradient flows into it. Only the average changes it.

Between the two necklaces stands PREDI, the predictor. PREDI receives the context necklace, and also the position of the hidden block: where to look. In return, PREDI projects a ghost necklace, its guess for the target.

Last piece: the scale. It weighs the ghost necklace against the target necklace. When they agree, the scale balances. When they do not, it tilts, and the model learns. In I-JEPA, the loss is an average L2 distance between the predicted and the target representations. Notice where the scale stands. It stands in representation space. Never in pixel space.

Compare this with two diagrams you already know. In the autoencoder of chapter 6, a decoder repaints the image, and the scale sits in pixel space. Every pixel counts, even the ones nobody could guess: the exact shade of a leaf, the grain of a sensor. In methods like SimCLR, from chapter 7, two views of the same image become two necklaces, and the scale compares them directly. There is no predictor. JEPA keeps the comparison between necklaces, and adds a predictor. So it can learn from what it sees, in order to guess what it does not.

What does this teach the model? What is predictable. Think of the shadow again. The position of the puppet's arm can be guessed from the rest of the puppet. The stitching of its coat cannot. If the necklace has no room for the stitching, the predictor is never asked to guess it. The unpredictable details can simply be left out.

I-JEPA, by Assran and colleagues, was presented at CVPR in 2023. It uses one large block of the image as the context, and predicts the representations of four hidden target blocks. The encoders and the predictor are Vision Transformers. And it needs no hand-crafted augmentations.

Now the trap. It is called collapse. Suppose both encoders learn to ignore the image, and always output the same necklace, say with every bead the same grey. Then PREDI can simply copy it. The scale balances perfectly, and the loss is zero. A perfect score, and it means nothing. The model has learned nothing about the world.

This is the collapse we met in chapter 7. It is a danger for any method that compares representations, and the recipe cannot do without a guard. The I-JEPA paper relies on an asymmetric design to avoid it: the slow twin, and the predictor. Other guards exist, and they are the subject of the next video.

To sum up. A JEPA sees a part of the world, and predicts the representation of the hidden part. It has four pieces: a context encoder, a twin encoder that follows it as a moving average, a predictor told where to look, and a loss measured on necklaces. It learns what is predictable, and it must be protected from collapse. Next time: avoiding collapse in JEPA.

PLATE 11.36:37

Avoiding collapse in JEPA

Three braces keep a JEPA from collapsing, each with a price. The slow twin with stop-gradient works but relies on a delicate recipe, VICReg's written variance and covariance rules need tuned weights, and SIGReg pushes the cloud of necklaces toward a round Gaussian, tested through random one-dimensional shadows. Avoiding collapse does not mean recovering hidden causes, which remains open.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the shadow theatre. Imagine that every shadow on the screen is the same grey blob, whatever puppet stands behind it. The audience learns nothing, and yet the loss is perfectly happy. That is collapse. Today we put three guards on the same diagram, and for each one we ask the same question: what does it cost?

Here is our JEPA from last time. The context goes into ENKO. The twin ENKO writes the target necklace. PREDI casts a ghost necklace, and the scale stands in representation space. Think of the diagram as a frame on the stage. Each guard is a brace bolted onto the same frame. Three braces, three prices.

Brace one: the slow twin. You met it in episode 7.5, with BYOL. The target encoder is not trained by the loss. The red bar on its arrow is the stop-gradient: no learning signal flows into the twin. It only follows its sibling, late and smooth, as an exponential moving average. I-JEPA, the paper of the last episode, uses exactly this guard.

The price of brace one. First, a second copy of the encoder must live in memory. Second, the decay rate tau is a setting: in BYOL it starts at zero point nine nine six and rises toward one. Third, nothing in the loss forbids the cheat. BYOL reports collapse when the predictor is removed. Why the pieces work together was long unclear, and the 2021 theory of Tian, Chen and Ganguli covers only simple linear networks. This guard works, and it is held up by a recipe.

Brace two: written rules. VICReg, from episode 7.6, looks at a whole batch of necklaces. Rule one: every bead must vary from dancer to dancer. That is the variance rule. Rule two: no bead may simply repeat another. That is the covariance rule. Here we lay the two rules on the necklaces of our JEPA. No slow twin is needed, because the rules look at the crowd directly.

The price of brace two. The weights. In the VICReg paper, the invariance and variance weights are twenty-five, the covariance weight is one, and the first two came out of a grid search. Remove the variance term, and the paper reports immediate collapse. And the rules check only spread and pairs of beads, never the shape of the crowd.

Brace three: a bell. This is the idea of LeJEPA, by Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun, posted on arXiv in November 2025. The authors show that the best shape for a cloud of necklaces, to minimize the risk of later tasks, is an isotropic Gaussian: round, centred, and equally spread in every direction. Their regularizer is called SIGReg, for sketched isotropic Gaussian regularization.

How do you test a cloud that lives in a thousand dimensions? With shadows. Put a lamp behind the cloud, and look at its shadow on a line. That is a random projection: one number per necklace. Compare the histogram of these numbers with a bell curve, using a classical test, the Epps and Pulley test. Then turn the lamp, pick a new random direction, and do it again. Average the results. If every shadow is a bell, the whole cloud is a Gaussian. That is the lemma of Cramér and Wold.

Here is a toy, illustrative. Two beads, a hundred and sixty necklaces, thirty-six directions of the lamp. A round cloud scores zero point four, in thousandths. A cloud of four clusters, rescaled to have the same mean and the same variance in every direction, scores two point nine, and it jumps when the lamp looks along the clusters. A collapsed cloud scores one hundred thirty. A test of mean and variance cannot tell the first two apart. The paper warns that such a test leads to shortcuts.

The loss then has one extra weight: one minus lambda times the prediction loss, plus lambda times SIGReg. The paper says it needs no stop-gradient and no teacher, and that its time and memory grow linearly with the batch and the dimension. With a frozen huge Vision Transformer pretrained on ImageNet, it reaches seventy-nine percent in linear evaluation.

The price of brace three. There is still a weight, and a number of directions to draw. The test runs on a minibatch, so it only estimates the true distribution. And a Gaussian is a choice. The paper argues it is the best one, under its assumptions, and assumptions are where guarantees live.

Look at the three braces together. The first is an engineering recipe. The second is a set of written rules. The third is a statement with a theorem behind it. The trend is toward fewer tricks and more guarantees. And a guarantee opens a bigger question. Not only: does the JEPA avoid collapse? But: when does it find the hidden causes of what it observes?

Two preprints from 2026 take this question seriously. In May 2026, David Klindt, Yann LeCun and Randall Balestriero asked: when does LeJEPA learn a world model? They prove that, when the hidden variables evolve with stationary transitions plus additive noise, LeJEPA recovers them up to a linear map. This is called linear identifiability. Within that class of worlds, the Gaussian is the only distribution for which the guarantee holds. And when the conditions are only approximately met, it fades gradually.

In September 2026, Yuhang Liu, Zhuo Huang and Javen Qinfeng Shi studied JEPAs that also see the actions of an agent. Their title: I act therefore I am. They argue that accurate prediction alone does not mean the hidden causal states were recovered. Under their conditions, the states are recovered up to a component-wise invertible transformation and a permutation. A key condition is that the actions make the transitions vary enough.

Both are preprints on arXiv, and both rest on assumptions. So the honest summary is this. A JEPA that does not collapse is not yet a JEPA that has found the causes. Under which conditions it does is still an open question, and the answers so far depend on the world.

To sum up. Three braces on the same frame. The slow twin, with its stop-gradient: it works, and it depends on a recipe. The written rules of VICReg: they work, with weights to tune. SIGReg: a bell, with one weight and a theorem. Every brace has a price. Next time: I-JEPA, hidden blocks of an image.

I-JEPA: hidden blocks of an image

I-JEPA sees one large block of an image, with holes, and predicts the representations of four hidden blocks, like writing a note about each missing pane of a stained-glass window instead of repainting it. The targets come from a slow twin, and the predictor is told only where each hole sits. It learns without hand-crafted augmentations, and the masking strategy decides most of the result.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the shadow theatre. Last time we bolted three braces onto our JEPA, so that it would not collapse. Today the recipe leaves the stage and meets a real picture. This is I-JEPA, the Image-based Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, by Assran and colleagues, presented at CVPR in 2023. The idea fits in one sentence: see one big block of an image, with holes, and predict the representations of a few hidden blocks.

Start with the intuition. Picture a stained-glass window, with a lamp behind it. Now lift out four panes, and leave the rest in place. A restorer would try to repaint the missing panes, colour by colour. I-JEPA does something else. For each hole, it writes a short note, a necklace of beads, describing what that pane contains. It never paints the glass. And it is told only one thing about each hole: where it is.

Let's build the diagram. First, the picture is cut into small square patches, like panes. A Vision Transformer reads them, the architecture of episode 3.5. I-JEPA uses it three times: for the context encoder, for the target encoder, and for the predictor.

Now the masking. The paper samples four target blocks. Each one has a scale between zero point one five and zero point two, as a fraction of the image, and an aspect ratio between zero point seven five and one point five. Then it samples one context block, a big one: a scale between zero point eight five and one. Any part of the context that overlaps a target is removed. What is left is full of holes: the paper reports that it keeps, on average, about a quarter of the patches.

The context goes through the context encoder, our friend ENKO, and a necklace comes out for each visible patch.

Now the twin. In I-JEPA, the twin reads the whole image, and the target blocks are cut out of its output. The paper insists on this: the blocks are masked at the output of the target encoder, not at its input. The twin's weights are a moving average of ENKO's, with a momentum of zero point nine nine six, rising to one during training. No gradient flows into it.

PREDI is a narrow Vision Transformer, with an embedding width of three hundred eighty-four. It receives the context necklaces, plus one mask token for each patch to predict, each carrying the position of its hole. It runs once for each of the four blocks, and casts a ghost necklace for each. Last, the scale. The loss is the average L2 distance between the predicted and the target patch representations, averaged over the four blocks.

Compare this with the neighbours. Masked autoencoders, or MAE, also hide parts of an image. But a decoder repaints the missing pixels, and the scale stands in pixel space. Data2vec is the closest relative: it predicts the representations of missing patches, computed by an online target encoder. And joint-embedding methods like DINO or iBOT need hand-crafted augmentations: many cropped and recoloured views of every image. I-JEPA processes a single view, with no augmentation, and keeps a predictor.

What does it learn? To look inside, the authors trained a separate generative model to decode the predictor's output. What stayed the same across samples was the high-level part, such as the back of a bird, with the right pose. What varied was the exact low-level detail, and the background. The predictor captures where things could be, and leaves the stitching out. The shadow, not the puppet.

Now the numbers, as the paper reports them. First, a linear probe on ImageNet-1K: freeze the encoder, and train only a straight classifier on top. I-JEPA with a huge Vision Transformer, trained for three hundred epochs, reaches seventy-nine point three percent. MAE with the same architecture, trained for sixteen hundred epochs, reaches seventy-seven point two. Data2vec, also sixteen hundred epochs, seventy-seven point three. Methods with hand-crafted augmentations are still ahead at this size: DINO reaches eighty point one, and iBOT eighty-one point zero. With a larger input, four hundred forty-eight pixels, I-JEPA reaches eighty-one point one.

Second, the cost. The paper says a huge I-JEPA trains in under twelve hundred GPU hours. That is over two and a half times faster than the smallest iBOT, and over ten times more efficient than a huge MAE. Each step is about seven percent slower, because the targets are computed too, but I-JEPA needs roughly five times fewer iterations. And with full fine-tuning, it reaches eighty-seven point one, against eighty-seven point eight for MAE, with five point three times fewer epochs.

Now the trap: the masking strategy decides everything. The authors trained a small model, for three hundred epochs, and scored it with a linear probe on one percent of the ImageNet labels. With their multi-block masking, fifty-four point two. Hide a single big block: twenty point two. Hide random patches: seventeen point six. Hide three quadrants: fifteen point five.

The size of the targets matters too. Shrink the target scale to between zero point zero seven five and zero point two, and the score falls to nineteen point two. One target block gives nine point zero; four give fifty-four point two. A context shrunk to a scale between zero point four and one gives thirty-one point two. And on a huge model, cutting the targets from the output of the twin gives sixty-seven point three, against fifty-six point one when they are hidden at the input. Pixels as targets, in another test, give forty point seven, against sixty-six point nine for representations, with more epochs for pixels.

To sum up. I-JEPA sees one big context block, with holes, and predicts the representations of four hidden target blocks, cut from the output of a slow twin, with PREDI told where each one is. It learns semantic features without hand-crafted augmentations, at a lower compute cost than methods that repaint pixels. And its masking is the whole game. V-JEPA: what happens next in a video

V-JEPA punches a tube through a stack of video frames and predicts what was inside as a representation, never as pixels; hiding a continuous block over the whole clip stops the model copying a neighbouring frame. Scaled up, it predicts the next action. A later version applies the loss to visible tokens too, giving finer maps, so the line on what JEPA ignores moves.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the shadow theatre. Last time, I-JEPA looked at one picture. Today the picture starts to move. This is V-JEPA, the V is for video. The first paper is by Bardes and colleagues at Meta, from February 2024. The idea fits in one sentence: hide a tube of a video, and predict what is inside it, as a representation, never as pixels.

Start with the intuition. A video is a stack of pictures. Picture the reel of a film, with the frames stacked like a deck of slides. Now push a hole punch straight through the whole stack. Every frame loses the same patch, and what comes out is a tube of film. V-JEPA must write a short note, a necklace, about what was inside that tube, using only the film that is left. It never repaints a single frame.

Why a tube, and not scattered dots? Because videos are redundant. Hide a patch in one frame only, and the same patch sits untouched in the next frame, ready to be copied. The paper says that masking a large continuous block, over the full duration of the clip, limits this leak, and makes the task harder.

Let's build the diagram. A clip has sixteen frames, taken one in every four: about three seconds. It is cut into blocks of sixteen by sixteen pixels, spanning two frames. A Vision Transformer reads them. ENKO, the slow twin and PREDI are the same cast as last time. Only the time axis is new.

Now the masking. Short-range masks are the union of eight blocks, each covering fifteen percent of every frame. Long-range masks are the union of two blocks, each covering seventy percent. Either way, about ninety percent of the clip is hidden. And each block lasts the whole clip: that is the tube.

ENKO sees only the visible tokens. The twin reads the whole clip, and the targets are cut from its output. PREDI receives the visible necklaces, plus a mask token for every hidden position, carrying its place in space and in time. It is a narrow transformer, width three hundred eighty-four. The loss is the average absolute distance, L1, between predicted and target necklaces.

Compare with the neighbours. VideoMAE, OmniMAE and Hiera hide parts of the video too, but they repaint the missing pixels. Then V-JEPA 2.1, March 2026, by Mur-Labadia and colleagues, and an honest point. In V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2, the loss is applied only to the masked tokens. Which is why a familiar sentence needs a date: JEPA does not predict details. That describes V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2, where the loss touches only the hidden part. In 2.1, the loss also touches the visible part, and the maps get fine. The targets are still representations, not pixels. What the papers show is that the loss on every token gives finer maps. What they do not show is where the right boundary lies for other tasks. The line moves with the versions.

Anything newer? Among Meta's releases, 2.1 is the latest found, as of November 2026. And LeVJEPA, in August 2026, drops the twin, the stop-gradient and the predictor altogether, and uses SIGReg instead. Its authors report matching V-JEPA 2 with much less compute: a claim from its abstract.

To sum up. V-JEPA hides tubes of a video, and predicts their representations, from a slow twin, with PREDI told where and when. V-JEPA 2 scales it to over a million hours, and predicts the next action. V-JEPA 2.1 puts the loss on every token, and gets finer maps. TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the shadow theatre. Last time, V-JEPA watched video and learned to guess what happens next. But it only watched. Today the puppet gets a rod. We add the action to the predictor, and then we let a robot plan with it, toward a goal given as a picture. By the end of this video you will know how the model is built, what it was trained on, what the robot did, under which conditions, and how a much smaller model learns the same kind of thing from pixels.

Start with the intuition. Picture the puppeteer behind the screen, holding a rod. Watching the show is one thing: you see the shadows, and you guess the next one. Acting is another. Before moving the rod, the puppeteer asks a different question: if I push it this way, what will the shadow look like? An action-conditioned model answers that question. Take the shadow of now, add the movement of the rod, and predict the shadow of next. In the words of episode ten point one, a state and an action go in, and the next state comes out. Here the state is a necklace, and the shadow is never turned back into a picture.

Let's build the diagram. This is V-JEPA 2-AC, from the V-JEPA 2 paper by Assran and colleagues at Meta, posted in June 2025. The AC stands for action-conditioned. First piece: ENKO, the video encoder of V-JEPA 2, already trained on video. In this step it is frozen. Frozen means that its weights are never changed again.

Second piece: PREDI, the predictor. It is a transformer of about three hundred million parameters, and it now receives three things for each frame: the necklace of the frame, the position of the robot's gripper, and the action. The action is a vector of seven numbers: the change in the gripper's position and orientation, plus the gripper itself. PREDI casts a ghost necklace for the next frame.

The scale sits in representation space, as always. It weighs the ghost against the real necklace of the next frame, with an L1 distance. The paper adds a second term, a short rollout, in which PREDI feeds on its own guesses for two steps.

What did PREDI train on? About sixty-two hours of video from Droid, a public dataset of a Franka robot arm, recorded by people teleoperating it. The paper says it uses no extra information: no reward, and no label saying which task is being done. Clips last four seconds, at four frames per second. And the arms used for the tests stood in two labs, neither of which appears in Droid.

Now the planning. It is the loop of episode nine point four. The robot receives its goal as a photograph: what the scene should look like at the end. ENKO turns the photograph into a goal necklace. Then CEM, the cross-entropy method, goes to work. Imagine many sequences of actions. For each one, PREDI rolls out a ghost necklace. The energy of a sequence is the L1 distance between its last ghost and the goal necklace. Keep the best sequences, refit the bell curves around them, and repeat. Then play only the first action, look again, and plan again.

In the paper's planner, there are eight hundred samples, ten rounds, and a horizon of one action. Each action takes sixteen seconds to compute. For pick-and-place, one goal picture is not enough. The paper gives three: the object grasped, the object near its target, and the object placed. The robot aims for each one in turn.

Now the numbers, as Table 2 of the paper reports them. Two Franka arms, one in each lab, tested without any training in those labs. Each cell is ten trials, with the task varied from trial to trial. Reaching a goal: one hundred percent in both labs. Grasping a cup: seventy in lab one, sixty in lab two. Grasping a box: thirty and twenty. Picking and placing a cup: eighty in both. A box: eighty in lab one, and fifty in lab two. The baseline, Octo, a robot policy fine-tuned on Droid, averages fifteen percent for the cup and ten for the box.

Table 3 compares planners, in lab two, ten tasks per skill. An action-conditioned Cosmos video model, with eighty samples, needs four minutes for each action. It scores zero percent on picking and placing, a cup or a box. V-JEPA 2-AC, with ten times more samples, needs sixteen seconds. It scores eighty percent for the cup, and fifty for the box. This is the comparison we met in episode ten point five, and it is the authors' own, in their setup.

Now read these numbers with their conditions. The tasks are short: reach, grasp, move one object. The scenes are simple: a cup or a box on a table. The goal is a picture, and for pick-and-place, three pictures. The authors chose the camera position by hand, and say that the base of the robot is often out of view. They also say long horizons are hard, because errors pile up when a model feeds on its own predictions. And ten trials per cell is a small sample. These are results from one paper, on two arms. They say what happened in these tests. They do not say what a robot can do in general.

Anything newer? As of November 2026, two papers build directly on it, each reporting its own results. V-JEPA 2.1, from March 2026, keeps the recipe with a better encoder. In its Table 6, grasping goes from sixty to seventy percent with the same planner settings, ten tasks, in one lab. RoboJEPA, from October 2026, trains the predictor on twenty-three public datasets, twelve kinds of robot, and about fifteen thousand hours of video, up to eight billion parameters, still over a frozen encoder, with a single goal picture. On a real Franka arm, thirty episodes per task, the largest model succeeds on sixty-seven percent of grasps and twenty-seven percent of pick-and-place, in its Table 3. Its authors add that the text-goal policies they list beside it are references, not direct comparisons.

Now a different route. V-JEPA 2-AC starts from a large pretrained encoder. LeWorldModel, from March 2026, by Maes, Le Lidec, Scieur, LeCun and Balestriero, trains everything at once, from raw pixels. It is a JEPA with two loss terms. First, the prediction term: the squared distance between the predicted next necklace and the real one. Second, SIGReg, from episode eleven point three, which pushes the cloud of necklaces toward a Gaussian, so that they cannot collapse. The authors say it uses no stop-gradient, no slow twin, and no other stabilizing trick.

It has about fifteen million parameters, and it trains on a single GPU in a few hours. It plans with CEM again: three hundred sequences, thirty elites, a horizon of five steps, with a goal picture. The authors report planning up to forty-eight times faster than DINO-WM, a world model built on a large pretrained encoder. Full planning takes under one second. One reason is that LeWorldModel needs about two hundred times fewer tokens for each image.

What does it score? On Push-T, a pushing task, ninety-six percent, against ninety-two for DINO-WM and seventy-eight for PLDM, in their Table 5, with a goal twenty-five steps ahead and a budget of fifty steps. But on the simple TwoRoom task, both of those baselines beat it, and on OGBench-Cube, DINO-WM is slightly ahead. The authors list limits too: planning stays on short horizons, it needs offline data that covers the environment, and it needs action labels.

To sum up. V-JEPA 2-AC adds the action to the predictor, over a frozen encoder, with about sixty-two hours of unlabeled Droid video. A robot then plans with CEM toward a goal picture, and plays the first action. In the paper's tests: two arms, short tasks, simple scenes, picture goals, ten trials per cell. LeWorldModel learns a small JEPA from pixels with two loss terms, and plans in under a second, on benchmark control tasks. Neither result should be generalized. PLATE 11.76:03

The JEPA family

JEPA is a family defined by its structure: a context encoder, a target encoder, a predictor, a masking rule, a guard against collapse, and a loss on embeddings rather than pixels. Each branch bends the pieces to its data, from video and sound to point clouds, time series, brain signals and language. Recent theory asks when this structure recovers the world's hidden causes.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the shadow theatre. So far in this chapter we have met the recipe, the braces against collapse, and one model that looks at pictures. Today we step back and look at the whole family. Picture a family tree painted on the workshop wall. Its trunk is the shared structure, and each branch is a different kind of data.

Start with the trunk. What do all these models have in common? Six things. A context encoder, which reads the visible part. A target encoder, which reads the part to predict. In many models it is the slow twin, but not in all of them. A predictor, PREDI, which guesses the target's necklace. A masking rule, which decides what is hidden. A guard against collapse. And a loss that compares necklaces, never pixels. Each branch keeps this skeleton. Mostly, it changes how the data is cut into pieces, and how the pieces are hidden.

The first branch is images. I-JEPA, by Assran and colleagues, appeared in January twenty twenty-three. We met it earlier in this chapter, so let us move on to video.

V-JEPA, from Bardes and colleagues, was posted in February twenty twenty-four. The paper says it learns with only a feature prediction objective: no text, no negative examples, and no reconstruction. It was trained on two million videos from public datasets. With a frozen backbone, its largest model reports eighty-one point nine percent on Kinetics four hundred, and seventy-two point two on Something-Something version two. In June twenty twenty-five, V-JEPA two was pre-trained on over one million hours of internet video and images. It reports seventy-seven point three on Something-Something version two.

Now leave the eyes, and listen. A-JEPA, posted in November twenty twenty-three, turns a sound into a spectrogram, a picture of frequencies over time, and cuts it into patches. What is new is the mask. It respects time and frequency, and it follows a curriculum, instead of random blocks. The authors report state-of-the-art results on several audio and speech classification tasks.

Point clouds come next: sets of three-dimensional points, like the ones a laser scanner gives. Point-JEPA, from April twenty twenty-four, faces a problem. Points have no natural order, so choosing a neighbouring block is costly. Its answer is a sequencer. It orders the patch embeddings, so that closeness can be read from the indices. The authors report results competitive with the state of the art, without reconstructing the input.

Then time series. A paper called Joint Embeddings Go Temporal introduces TS-JEPA. It predicts the embeddings of masked segments of a signal, from the segments that are not masked. The authors argue that reconstructing masked inputs is sensitive to noise. It was a workshop paper at NeurIPS twenty twenty-four, posted on arXiv in September twenty twenty-five.

Two branches matter for us, because they grow on brain data. Brain-JEPA, posted in September twenty twenty-four, is a foundation model for fMRI. It adds a functional coordinate system for brain regions, called gradient positioning, and a masking built for fMRI time series. For EEG, S-JEPA, short for Signal-JEPA, was posted in March twenty twenty-four. Its masking hides blocks of neighbouring electrodes. Next time we look at these brain models closely, with their baselines and their limits. For the sensor side, see EEG episode fourteen point one. For the scanner side, see the fMRI series.

Now a branch that grows toward language. VL-JEPA, from Chen and colleagues, with Yann LeCun and Pascale Fung, was posted in December twenty twenty-five. A usual vision-language model writes its answer token by token. VL-JEPA predicts a continuous embedding of the target text instead. A light decoder turns embeddings into words, but only when needed. In a controlled comparison with a standard token model, same vision encoder and same data, the authors report better performance with fifty percent fewer trainable parameters.

The last branch is not a kind of data. It is a question: why does any of this work? In November twenty twenty-five, Balestriero and LeCun proposed LeJEPA, with a regulariser, SIGReg, that pushes the necklaces toward an isotropic Gaussian. In May twenty twenty-six, Klindt, LeCun and Balestriero asked: when does LeJEPA learn a world model? They prove that it can linearly recover the hidden variables of a world, when those variables follow stationary transitions with additive noise. In September twenty twenty-six, Liu, Huang and Shi asked the same question for JEPAs that see actions. In November twenty twenty-six, Xin Li argued that a JEPA-based proof of the Platonic representation hypothesis rests on assumptions that fail in practice. All of this is on arXiv, as of November twenty twenty-six. Weigh it as a field still taking shape.

Now step back and look at the tree again. No single model defines it. The structure does: a context encoder, a target encoder, a predictor, a masking rule, a guard against collapse, and a loss in latent space. Each branch bends the pieces to fit its data: a time-frequency mask for sound, a sequencer for points, a coordinate system for brain regions, text embeddings as targets. Even the guard changes. LeJEPA drops the slow twin and the stop-gradient, and uses a bell-shaped regulariser instead.

To sum up. JEPA is a family defined by its structure. Its branches cover images, video, audio, point clouds, time series, brain signals and language. The twenty twenty-six theory asks when that structure finds the hidden causes of the world. Next time: JEPA on brain data.

PLATE 11.87:35

JEPA on brain data

The JEPA recipe carries over to EEG and fMRI by hiding blocks of electrodes and time and predicting their embeddings: a disc of neighbours, tubes through time, or stretches of time. Frozen linear probes show gains on some tasks, near-chance results on others, and sometimes a simple baseline does as well. Three pitfalls: subject leakage, weak baselines, and benchmarks far from your own question.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the shadow theatre. This time, the puppet is a brain, and the screen is a recording: electrodes on a scalp, or a scanner around a head. We only ever see the shadow of what the neurons do. In episode 11.2 we learned the recipe: see one part, and predict the representation of another part. By the end of this video, you will know three ways to hide part of an EEG, what the results claim, and where to be suspicious.

Why would anyone try? Because labels are costly, as we saw in episode 5.2. An expert must read hours of EEG to label it. Unlabelled recordings sit by the tens of thousands in hospital archives. And every lab has its own montage: its own electrodes, in its own places. A model trained on one cap often cannot read the next. A JEPA trained on unlabelled recordings, from many caps, is the hope.

Here is the diagram of episode 11.2 again. Only one thing changes. The squares of the picture become blocks of electrodes and time. One square is one electrode, during one slice of time. ENKO reads the squares that remain. The twin sees the whole recording. PREDI guesses the necklaces of the hidden blocks.

What changes from paper to paper is what gets hidden. Our picture for it is a choir. Each singer is an electrode, and each bar of the song is a slice of time. Hiding part of the recording means silencing part of the choir. The conductor must then guess, not the notes, but the chord that the silent singers would have sung.

First, S-JEPA, by Guetschel, Moreau and Tangermann, in March 2024. It silences neighbours. It picks one electrode at random, and hides a disc of electrodes around it, as wide as forty, sixty or eighty percent of the head. The neighbours cannot help, because they are silent too. The model must listen to the far side of the head.

Second, EEG-VJEPA, by Hojjati and colleagues, in July 2025. It borrows from video. It hides tubes: large blocks of electrodes that stay silent through the whole time axis, just as V-JEPA hides tubes in a video. A group of singers is silent for the entire song.

Third, Laya, by Panchavati and colleagues, in March 2026. It silences stretches of time instead: blocks of half a second to a second, covering sixty percent of the recording. For collapse, it does not use a slow twin. It uses SIGReg, the regulariser of LeJEPA, which spreads the necklaces out so that they cannot all become the same. Each electrode is described by its coordinates, so any montage fits.

How do we know what the models learned? With a probe, as in episode 4.3. We freeze the encoder, and train only a small linear readout. If a simple readout works, the information was already in the necklaces. S-JEPA was probed on seven held-out participants, from a dataset of fifty-four people, in 2024. It reached ninety-four percent accuracy on a visual task, but only sixty-five percent on imagined hand movements, where standard pipelines do better. The authors call their work exploratory.

Laya went wide. Its pretraining used twenty thousand nine hundred and forty subjects, with seventeen different electrode layouts. Under a frozen linear probe on fourteen tasks, its clinical tasks reach a mean balanced accuracy of zero point six, against zero point five seven eight for LaBraM. On sleep staging, every model lands between zero point one seven and zero point two seven. That is close to chance. A good result and a bad one, in the same table.

Now, scale. STST-JEPA, in July 2026, pretrained on forty-seven thousand seven hundred and three sessions, from people aged five to eighty-one, split by person. A frozen probe predicts age with an average error of three years, on three thousand three hundred and sixty-seven held-out sessions. But this is the best value seen during training, in a cohort full of children, so it cannot be compared with adult benchmarks.

EEG-JEPA, at the end of July 2026, mixes six kinds of silence: gaps in time, a band of channels, a focal spot, a left and right pair, a dead sensor, and a dead pair. Pretrained on thirty-nine thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight recordings, it raises the frozen balanced accuracy over fourteen tasks from forty point five, for a model that repaints the waveform, to fifty point four percent. With forty-four thousand four hundred and eighty-six recordings from three corpora, it reaches fifty-two point nine.

Now a counterpoint, in structural MRI. Neuro-JEPA, in June 2026, adapts the V-JEPA 2 recipe to three-dimensional scans. It was pretrained on one million five hundred and fifty thousand scans, from two hundred and eighty-two thousand patients in one health system. Its authors report something sobering. Across forty-one tasks from twelve public datasets, existing foundation models did not consistently beat a simple convolutional network trained from scratch.

And for fMRI, there is Brain-JEPA, from September 2024. It was pretrained on forty thousand one hundred and sixty-two UK Biobank participants, aged forty-four to eighty-three, each brain cut into four hundred and fifty regions. One block of regions and time stays visible. The hidden targets are of three kinds: other regions, other times, and both at once. After fine-tuning to predict age, its correlation is point seven one eight, against point six three two for an earlier model, BrainLM.

Now the pitfalls. First, leakage. A person has many sessions, and a brain is a signature. If sessions of one person fall on both sides of the split, the model recognises the person instead of the disease. STST-JEPA split by subject. Laya removed the benchmark participants from pretraining. Neuro-JEPA split by patient. This is the subject of EEG 14.6.

Second, weak baselines. EEG-VJEPA, on eighty-eight people with Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, or healthy controls, scored eighty-three percent after fine-tuning. A support vector machine on hand-crafted features reached ninety-three point five. The authors say so themselves.

Third, benchmarks far from your question. An average over fourteen public tasks says little about your own cohort.

To sum up. The recipe of episode 11.2 carries over to brain data: hide blocks of electrodes and time, and predict their necklaces. Three kinds of silence: a disc of neighbours, tubes through time, and stretches of time with SIGReg. The gains are real on some tasks, close to chance on others, and a simple baseline is sometimes just as good. In EEG 14.1, these models come back as self-supervised learning on brain data, where the linear probe is the linear decoder you know. In EEG 14.6, you will meet cross-validation and leakage in full.
