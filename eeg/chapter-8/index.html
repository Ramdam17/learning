Averaging · Pulling a whisper out of noise

Averaging pulls a quiet announcement out of a noisy station: the response is locked to the stimulus and adds up, while background EEG cancels, falling with the square root of the number of trials. The average assumes the same response on every trial. Latency jitter lowers and widens peaks, and phase-varying oscillations can vanish, so the average is the consistent part, not the typical trial.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter eight: event-related potentials. Our first stop is the oldest trick in the book. Averaging, or how to pull a whisper out of noise.

Picture a busy railway station. Every few minutes, a quiet voice announces the next train. You cannot hear it over the crowd. But if you recorded the station every time the announcement played, and laid all the recordings on top of each other, the voice would come out, and the crowd would fade away.

That is exactly the situation with an E-R-P. The brain's response to a single stimulus is small: often a few microvolts. The ongoing EEG around it, from everything else the brain is doing, is often ten times larger. On a single trial, you usually cannot see the response at all.

So we repeat the stimulus many times, cut an epoch around each one, line them up on the stimulus, and average them, sample by sample.

Here is why it works. The response we care about is locked to the stimulus: it comes back at the same moment after every stimulus, with the same sign. When we add the trials, it adds up. The background EEG is not locked to the stimulus: at a given moment, it is sometimes positive and sometimes negative. When we add the trials, it partly cancels.

How fast does the noise shrink? If the noise is independent from trial to trial, its amplitude in the average falls with the square root of the number of trials. Four trials halve the noise. A hundred trials divide it by ten. But to halve it again, you need four hundred.

Let's watch it happen. One trial: nothing but noise. Ten trials: a shape begins to emerge. Fifty trials: the bumps are clear. Two hundred trials: a smooth, regular waveform. This is the event-related potential.

The idea is not new. George Dawson presented a summation technique for detecting small signals in a large irregular background in 1951, and averaging became the foundation of evoked potential research. Today the computer does in a second what Dawson's apparatus did with a bank of condensers.

Now, the hidden assumption. Averaging assumes that the response is the same on every trial: the same shape, the same size, at the same latency. Everything that differs from trial to trial is treated as noise.

What if the response is real on every trial, but arrives a little earlier or later each time? This is called latency jitter. When we average jittered responses, the peaks no longer line up. The average becomes lower and wider than the response on any single trial. A sharp, tall component can turn into a small, smeared bump.

And what if the brain produces an oscillation that starts at the stimulus, but with a different phase on each trial? Then the peaks of one trial meet the troughs of another, and the average can be almost flat, even though every trial contained a strong response. We come back to this in chapter nine, with induced activity.

So the average is not the typical trial. It is the part of the response that is consistent across trials, in both time and sign.

A few practical consequences. Average only trials that belong together: one condition, one participant, correctly marked events. Keep the number of trials similar across the conditions you compare, because fewer trials mean more residual noise. Check that the event markers are on time, as in episode six point three: a constant delay shifts the whole E-R-P, but a variable delay creates jitter. And look at single trials too, for example with an E-R-P image, which we meet in episode eight point six.

In M N E, the average is one line: epochs, then average. The result is an Evoked object, with one waveform per channel.

To recap. Single-trial responses are buried in ongoing EEG. Averaging keeps what is time-locked and consistent, and the noise falls with the square root of the number of trials. The method dates back to Dawson in 1951. It assumes the same response on every trial, so latency jitter shrinks and widens the peaks, and phase-varying oscillations can vanish. The average is the consistent part, not the typical trial.

A zoo of components

ERP components are named by polarity and latency or by function, and the episode tours P1, N1, N170, mismatch negativity, P300, N400, error-related negativity and late positive potential, each with a typical latency, topography and paradigm. These values are typical, not fixed. A peak is not a component: overlapping components sum, so enlarging a P3 can make a neighbouring N2 peak look smaller and earlier.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A zoo of components. The E-R-P waveform has bumps and dips, and many of them have names. Today we take the train along the timeline and stop at the main stations.

First, the naming system. Most components are named by their polarity and their order or latency. P for positive, N for negative. P1 is the first positive peak; N170 is a negative peak around one hundred and seventy milliseconds. Some have descriptive names instead, like the mismatch negativity or the error-related negativity.

Our line starts at the stimulus, at zero milliseconds.

First station: P1, around one hundred milliseconds after a visual stimulus, at occipital sites over the visual cortex. It is sensitive to physical features like brightness and contrast, and it grows when attention is directed to the stimulus location.

Second station: N1. After a visual stimulus, it follows the P1, around one hundred and fifty to two hundred milliseconds. After a sound, the auditory N1 peaks around one hundred milliseconds, at fronto-central sites. Both are larger for attended stimuli.

Third station: N170. A negative peak around one hundred and seventy milliseconds, at lateral occipito-temporal sites, and much larger for faces than for most other objects. It was described in detail by Bentin and colleagues in 1996, and it is a workhorse of face perception research.

Fourth station: the mismatch negativity, or M-M-N. Play the same sound again and again, then slip in a different one. Between about one hundred and two hundred and fifty milliseconds, the deviant sound produces an extra negativity at fronto-central sites, even when the listener is not paying attention. It was reported by Näätänen and colleagues in 1978, and it reflects the brain detecting a change against what it predicted.

Fifth station: P300, or more precisely the P3b. A large positivity, typically between three hundred and six hundred milliseconds, maximal at parietal sites. It appears when a rare, task-relevant target appears among frequent standards: the classic oddball task. It was first described by Sutton and colleagues in 1965. Its amplitude grows with rarity and relevance, and its latency is often linked to how long it takes to evaluate the stimulus.

Sixth station: N400. A negativity around four hundred milliseconds, at centro-parietal sites, larger for words that do not fit their context. "He spread the warm bread with socks." Reported by Kutas and Hillyard in 1980, it is a key measure of meaning processing.

Seventh station: the error-related negativity, or E-R-N. This one is locked not to the stimulus but to the response. Within about one hundred milliseconds after a mistake, a sharp negativity appears at fronto-central sites. It was described independently by Falkenstein and colleagues, and by Gehring and colleagues, in the early nineteen-nineties.

Last station: the late positive potential, or L-P-P. A slow positivity starting around three hundred milliseconds and lasting for several hundred, at centro-parietal sites, larger for emotional pictures than for neutral ones.

Each station comes with three facts: a typical latency, a typical topography, and a paradigm that brings it out. Latencies and topographies vary with the stimulus, the task, the age of the participants and the reference, so treat these numbers as typical, not fixed.

Now the most important idea of the episode. A peak is not a component.

A component is the activity of a particular brain process, with its own time course and its own scalp distribution. What you see at an electrode is the sum of several components that overlap in time. A peak in the waveform is just where that sum happens to be largest.

Here is an example. Two components overlap: an N2 and a P3. Make the P3 bigger, and the N2 peak looks smaller and earlier, even though the N2 itself did not change. The visible peak moved because its neighbour grew.

So when two conditions differ at a peak, ask which component changed. Topography helps, because different components have different scalp maps. Difference waves help, as we see in episode eight point four. Methods like I-C-A, or the regression approaches of episode eight point five, can help too.

To recap. Components are named by polarity and latency, or by what they do. P1, N1, N170, M-M-N, P300, N400, E-R-N and L-P-P each have a typical latency, topography and paradigm. These values are typical, not fixed. And a peak is not a component: overlapping components shape the peaks we see.

Next time: how to measure an E-R-P.

PLATE 8.34:24

Measuring an ERP

Measuring an ERP means choosing a rule, like deciding whether a train's time is its arrival or departure. Peak amplitude is biased upward by noise and so by trial count, while mean amplitude over a window is more robust. Fractional area latency uses the whole component, and jackknife averages help latency comparisons; windows and electrodes must be chosen before looking at effects.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Measuring an E-R-P. We have a nice average waveform. Now we need numbers: how big is the component, and when does it happen?

Think of a train timetable. To compare trains, you need a rule: do you note the moment the front of the train enters the station, the moment it stops, or the middle of its stay? Each rule gives a different time. E-R-P measures are the same: each one is a rule, and the rule matters.

Let's start with amplitude.

Measure one: peak amplitude. Find the highest point of the component inside a time window, and report its value. It is intuitive, and it is what our eyes do.

But it has a hidden problem: noise. The peak is the maximum of the waveform, and noise adds random bumps. The maximum tends to land on a bump that noise pushed up. So with more noise, peak amplitude becomes larger on average, even if the true component did not change.

Why does that matter? Because noise depends on the number of trials. A condition with fewer trials is noisier, so its peak looks bigger. Clayson, Baldwin and Larson showed this kind of bias in simulations in 2013. Two conditions can differ in peak amplitude simply because they had different numbers of trials.

Measure two: mean amplitude. Choose a time window, and average the voltage over all samples inside it. Random noise pushes some samples up and others down, so it cancels out instead of being selected. Mean amplitude is not biased by the number of trials in the same way, and it is generally more robust. It is usually the recommended default.

Its weakness: you must choose the window. And if the window also covers parts of neighbouring components, they leak into the measure.

Now, latency.

Measure three: peak latency. The time at which the peak occurs. Same problem as peak amplitude: noise can move the highest point by tens of milliseconds, especially for broad components with a flat top.

Measure four: fractional area latency. Compute the area under the component inside the window. Then find the time point that divides this area at a given fraction, for example fifty per cent. That time is the latency. Because it uses the whole component instead of one sample, it is much less sensitive to noise. You can also use a fractional peak latency, the time at which the waveform first reaches, say, half of its peak, which estimates onset.

For latency differences between conditions, a technique called the jackknife can help. You compute the measure on averages that each leave out one participant, which are much less noisy, and then correct the statistics. Kiesel and colleagues described this approach in detail in 2008.

Now the most important rule. Choose the time window and the electrodes before looking at your effects.

Why? Because the waveform always has a place where the conditions differ by chance. If you look at the data, see where the difference is largest, and put your window there, you are almost guaranteed to find a significant effect. Luck and Gaspelin made this point vividly in 2017, in a paper titled: how to get statistically significant effects in any E-R-P experiment, and why you shouldn't.

So where do windows come from? From previous studies of the same component. From a pre-registration. From a collapsed localiser: the average of all conditions together, which does not favour any condition. Or from independent data.

And always report: the measure, the window, the electrodes, the reference, the filter, and how many trials went into each average.

In M N E, Evoked objects have a get peak method, and you can compute a mean amplitude by cropping a window and averaging the data.

To recap. Peak amplitude is intuitive but biased by noise, and so by trial numbers. Mean amplitude is more robust, but needs a well-chosen window. Peak latency is noisy. Fractional area latency uses the whole component. The jackknife helps with latency differences. And choose windows and electrodes before looking at the effects.

Next time: difference waves, and what overlapping components do to them.

PLATE 8.43:40

Difference waves and overlap

A difference wave subtracts one condition from another, like subtracting two photos of a platform so only the moving train remains. It isolates what differs, but two changes can cancel, and a latency shift can masquerade as an amplitude effect with two opposite lobes. It does not name the component, and noise of both averages adds up, so original waveforms should be shown too.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Difference waves. One of the simplest tools in E-R-P research, and one of the most powerful. But it can also fool you.

Here is the idea. You have two conditions, A and B. For example, rare targets and frequent standards in an oddball task. You subtract the average waveform of B from the average waveform of A, sample by sample. The result is the difference wave.

Everything that is identical in both conditions cancels out: the response to the screen flash, the eye movements, the parts of the processing that do not care about the condition. What remains is the activity that differs. The mismatch negativity, for example, is usually measured on a difference wave: deviant minus standard.

Think of two photos of the same railway platform, taken a minute apart. Subtract one from the other, and the platform, the clock and the benches disappear. Only what moved stays: a train leaving, a passenger walking.

Difference waves are useful in three ways. They isolate the effect from overlapping components that do not change. They make the effect's timing and topography easier to see. And they reduce several electrodes and time points to one clear waveform to measure.

But here come the traps.

Trap one: two changes can cancel. Imagine that condition A increases a positive component, and also increases a negative component at the same time. In the difference wave, the two changes partly cancel. You see a small effect, or none at all, although two real processes changed.

Trap two: a latency difference looks like an amplitude difference. Take the same component in both conditions, with exactly the same size and shape. Now shift it by thirty milliseconds in condition A. Subtract. The difference wave shows a negative bump, then a positive bump. If you measure mean amplitude in a window over the first half of the component, you find a significant amplitude effect. But nothing changed in size. Only the timing changed.

So when you see a difference wave with two opposite lobes next to each other, think: could this be a shift in latency? Compare the latencies directly, for example with the fractional area latency of episode eight point three.

Trap three: the difference wave tells you that something differs, not which component. As we saw in episode eight point two, the same difference can come from different components that overlap. Look at the topography of the difference: a parietal maximum around four hundred milliseconds does not mean the same thing as a fronto-central one.

Trap four: noise adds up. The difference of two averages contains the noise of both. If each average has some noise, the difference has more. Difference waves need enough trials in both conditions.

A last practical point. Always show the original waveforms next to the difference wave. Readers need to see whether the difference comes from A going up, B going down, or a shift in time.

In M N E, you can compute a difference wave with combine evoked, with weights one and minus one.

To recap. A difference wave is A minus B, sample by sample. It removes everything the conditions share and isolates what differs. But two changes can cancel, a latency shift can look like an amplitude effect, the difference does not name the component, and the noise of both averages adds up. Always show the original waveforms too.

Next time: what to do when responses overlap in time: regression E-R-Ps.

PLATE 8.54:22

When responses overlap · Regression ERPs

When events follow each other quickly, responses overlap, like trains arriving every two minutes. If timing differs between conditions, as with fast and slow button presses, averages show false effects. Regression ERPs model the whole recording as a sum of event responses through a time-expanded design matrix, separating them and allowing continuous predictors, but they need jittered timing between events.

TRANSCRIPTEN

When responses overlap. Regression E-R-Ps.

Averaging works well when trials are far apart. But in many experiments, events come quickly. A stimulus, then a response half a second later, then the next stimulus. The brain's reaction to one event is still going on when the next one arrives. The responses overlap.

Think of trains arriving at a busy station. If they come every hour, each one has the platform to itself. If they come every two minutes, the end of one train is still at the platform when the next pulls in. A photo of the platform shows a mix of both.

Why is overlap a problem for averaging? Because the average of epochs around event A also contains pieces of the responses to the events around it. If those neighbours are always at random times, they blur into a slow smear. But if their timing differs between conditions, the smear differs too, and it creates a difference between conditions that has nothing to do with the brain's response to A.

Here is a classic case. In condition A, people respond quickly. In condition B, they respond slowly. The stimulus-locked average contains the motor response at a different latency in each condition. The difference wave shows an "effect" that is really the button press arriving at a different time.

The solution: stop treating each epoch separately, and model the whole continuous recording at once. This is the regression approach, described for E-R-Ps by Nathaniel Smith and Marta Kutas in 2015. If you watched the fNIRS series, this is the same idea as the general linear model.

Here is how it works. We assume that each type of event produces its own response waveform, unknown, lasting for, say, one second. The recording is the sum of all these responses, each placed at the time of its event, plus noise. Where two events are close, their responses simply add.

To estimate the waveforms, we build a large design matrix. Each column stands for one time point of one event's response: zero milliseconds after a stimulus, four milliseconds after, and so on. Each row is a time point of the recording. We put a one wherever an event of that type happened that many milliseconds earlier. This is sometimes called time expansion.

Then we solve one big regression. The estimated weights, column by column, are the response waveforms, now separated from each other. The overlap is explained by the model instead of being folded into the average.

Because it is a regression, we can add more than event types. Continuous predictors like word frequency, reaction time or stimulus contrast. Interactions between conditions. The result is sometimes called an r-E-R-P, a regression E-R-P.

Now, before and after. In our illustrative example, the stimulus-locked averages for the fast and the slow conditions differ around four hundred milliseconds. After modelling the stimulus and the response as two separate events, the stimulus responses in both conditions look the same. The difference came from the timing of the button press, not from how the stimulus was processed.

What does this approach need? Variable timing between events: if two events always occur at exactly the same interval, the model cannot tell their responses apart. Jittering the interval in the design, as we discussed in chapter five, makes overlap correction possible. It also assumes that responses add up linearly and have the same shape every time, which is the same assumption as averaging.

In Python, M N E offers linear regression raw, which estimates regression E-R-Ps directly from the continuous data and the events.

To recap. When events are close, their responses overlap, and averages mix them. If timing differs between conditions, the mix creates false differences. Regression E-R-Ps model the continuous recording as a sum of event responses and separate them. They can include continuous predictors. They need jittered timing between events. And in M N E, linear regression raw does it.

Beyond the average · Single trials and frequency tagging

An ERP image stacks single trials as coloured rows, like a departure board. Sorting by reaction time reveals response-locked activity as a curved band and stimulus-locked activity as a vertical one, and exposes jitter. Frequency tagging presents stimuli rhythmically, giving a sharp spectral peak with high signal-to-noise ratio, and the fast periodic oddball makes the rhythm itself the test.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Beyond the average. Single trials, and frequency tagging.

The average E-R-P is a summary. It hides how the response varies from one trial to the next. Today, two ways to see more: looking at all the trials at once, and making the brain respond at a frequency we choose.

Part one: the E-R-P image.

Take all the epochs from one channel. Instead of averaging them, stack them as horizontal lines, one trial per row, and colour each point by its voltage: pink for positive, blue for negative. This is an E-R-P image, popularised by Scott Makeig, Tzyy-Ping Jung and colleagues around two thousand.

Think of a departure board, with one row per train. Read across a row, and you follow one trial in time. Read down a column, and you see the same moment across all trials.

In the order the trials were recorded, the image often looks like noise. But now sort the rows by something meaningful, like reaction time, from the fastest at the top to the slowest at the bottom.

Suddenly a pattern appears. A curved band of positive voltage follows the reaction times: early on fast trials, late on slow trials. This activity is locked to the response, not to the stimulus. And a straight vertical band stays at the same latency on every row: that part is locked to the stimulus.

In the average, these two kinds of activity are mixed together, and the response-locked part is smeared by the variable reaction times. The E-R-P image separates them at a glance. It also reveals latency jitter, as we discussed in episode eight point one, and occasional odd trials.

A light smoothing across neighbouring rows makes the pattern easier to see. In M N E, plot image draws an E-R-P image from epochs, with an option to sort the trials.

Part two: frequency tagging.

So far, we waited for the brain's response to fade between stimuli. Now we do the opposite. We present the stimulus rhythmically, fast, for example a flicker at seven and a half hertz.

The visual cortex follows the rhythm. The EEG contains a response at exactly the stimulation frequency, and at its harmonics. This is the steady-state visual evoked potential, or S-S-V-E-P. Norcia and colleagues reviewed its use in vision research in 2015.

In the spectrum, the response appears as a sharp, narrow peak at exactly seven and a half hertz. The background EEG is spread across all frequencies, so the noise in that one narrow frequency bin is tiny. The peak stands out clearly above its neighbours.

That is the magic of frequency tagging: an excellent signal-to-noise ratio in a few minutes of recording. A common measure is the signal-to-noise ratio of each frequency bin relative to the neighbouring bins.

And we can tag different things with different frequencies. Flicker the left image at one frequency and the right image at another, and you can follow attention to each side from the same EEG.

A clever variation is the fast periodic oddball. Bruno Rossion and colleagues showed images at about six per second, and made every fifth image a face. A response at about one point two hertz, and its harmonics, can only come from the brain telling faces apart from the other images. The rhythm itself becomes the test.

Frequency tagging has limits. The stimulus must be repeated rhythmically, which does not suit every question. It tells you how strongly the brain follows the rhythm, not the detailed time course of a single response. And the frequency choice matters: some frequencies drive the visual system better than others, and alpha around ten hertz can get in the way.

To recap. An E-R-P image shows every trial as a coloured row. Sorting by reaction time separates stimulus-locked from response-locked activity, and reveals jitter. Frequency tagging presents stimuli rhythmically, and the response appears as a sharp spectral peak with a high signal-to-noise ratio. Different frequencies can tag different stimuli, and the fast periodic oddball turns the rhythm into a test.
