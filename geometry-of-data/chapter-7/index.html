A similarity that behaves

A ring of lanterns around a cluster cannot be split by a line: the best of 720 directions gets 75 percent right. Adding the squared distance from the centre as a third column separates them perfectly. The kernel trick gives dot products in such a hidden space without visiting it, so a kernel distance is the root of k(x,x) plus k(y,y) minus twice k(x,y).

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the lantern festival. Last time, in episode one point four, we tried to turn a similarity into a distance, and it did not go well. One minus the correlation looked like a distance. It was not one. It broke the triangle rule. A square root repaired it. Today we ask the question behind that repair. Is there a whole family of similarities that always behaves? There is. They are called kernels. And a kernel does more than behave. It tells you where your data secretly live.

Here is a puzzle. A cluster of lanterns hangs in the middle of a field. A ring of lanterns surrounds it. Each lantern is a point with two columns, its east-west and its north-south position. This is a simulation, seed seventy-one. Can one straight line separate the ring from the cluster? No. I scanned seven hundred and twenty directions, with every possible cut. The best line gets seventy-five percent of the lanterns right. It can peel off one side of the ring, and nothing more. Now add a third column: the squared distance from the centre. Raise each lantern on a pole, as high as that number. The cluster stays low. The ring floats high. A flat sheet slides between them. One hundred percent right. The straight line was never the problem. The space was.

But look at what we paid. We built a new space, with a new column for every lantern. What if the new space had a million columns? Or infinitely many? Here is the trick. We never need to visit the new space. We only need the dot products inside it. Take two points of the plane. One, one, and two, minus one. Their dot product is one. Squared, it is still one. Now send each point to three columns. The first number squared. Root two times the first times the second. The second number squared. The point one, one becomes one, root two, one. The point two, minus one becomes four, minus two root two, one. Multiply column by column and add. Four, minus four, plus one. The answer is one. The same number, and we never left the plane. The square of the dot product in the plane is exactly the dot product in the three-column space. I checked five hundred more pairs. The two sides never differed by more than ten to the minus thirteen. This is the kernel trick. A function of two points that, secretly, is a dot product somewhere else. The idea goes back to Aizerman, Braverman and Rozonoer, in nineteen sixty-four. In nineteen ninety-two, Boser, Guyon and Vapnik carried it into classifiers with a large margin.

And a dot product gives a distance. In the hidden space, the squared distance between x and y is the similarity of x with itself, plus the similarity of y with itself, minus twice their similarity. Then take the root. That is a straight line, in a space you never visit. Ruly measures it. It is also the formula of episode one point four. Correlation is a kernel: it is the dot product of two standardized signals. Put it in the formula, and out comes the square root of two times one minus r. The repair was a kernel distance all along. Alpha, beta and gamma again. One minus r gives zero point two nine, zero point two nine, and one. The two short sides reach only zero point five nine. The triangle breaks. The kernel distance gives zero point seven seven, zero point seven seven, and one point four one. The two short sides reach one point five three. The triangle closes. Not every similarity can do this. Take three objects. The first is very like the second, zero point nine. The second is very like the third, zero point nine. The first and the third have nothing in common, zero. No space can hold that. Its table has a negative eigenvalue, minus zero point two seven. The distances it implies are zero point four five, zero point four five, and one point four one. The triangle breaks again. A kernel is a similarity whose table never does that, whichever objects you pick. That is the same as being a dot product in some space, a result usually credited to Moore and Aronszajn. So a kernel distance gets three lights for free. Never negative. The same both ways. No shortcut. The zero light needs one more thing: the kernel must never confuse two different objects. Note that this space is fixed by the kernel. It is not learned from your data. It is not the latent space of a neural network. That is another story, told in the World Models series.

The most common kernel is a bell. Two identical points have similarity one. As they move apart, similarity falls, at a speed set by a width. Think of each lantern's glow. The similarity of two lanterns is how much their glows overlap. Three lanterns, at zero, one and ten. With a width of one, the distances are zero point eight nine, one point four one and one point four one. The lantern at ten is far beyond the glow of the others. Beyond a few widths, everything is equally far: root two. With a width of zero point one, every distance is one point four one. Each lantern is alone in the dark. With a width of one hundred, the distances are zero point zero one zero, zero point zero nine zero and zero point one zero zero. That is the straight-line distances, one, nine and ten, divided by one hundred. The glow covers everything, and the kernel adds nothing. It is the ruler again. Same three points, three widths, three geometries. I use the convention e to the minus d squared over two sigma squared. Some libraries use gamma instead of sigma, so check yours.

Now fill in the card. The object: anything you can write a kernel for. The distance: the root of k of x with x, plus k of y with y, minus two k of x with y. What it ignores: for the bell, everything that happens beyond a few widths. What it costs: one similarity for each pair, so a table that grows with the square of the number of points. Its trap: the width. For the bell, all four lights are on. I drew twenty thousand random triples of points. Zero violations of the triangle. The usual habit is to set the width to the median of the distances between the points. On our ring and cluster, that median is two point eight three. It is a habit, not a theorem. Its origin is unclear, and a two thousand seventeen paper by Garreau, Jitkrittum and Kanagawa studies what it does, not why we do it.

One last thing. We never said what the points are. A kernel needs two objects and a number, and nothing else. Kernels exist for strings of letters, for graphs, for trains of spikes. Whenever you can write a similarity that behaves, you have a geometry. A kernel is a similarity that guarantees a distance. Choosing the kernel means choosing the hidden space, and the hidden space decides what close means. Next time: The distance between two clouds.

The distance between two clouds

Two crowds with the same average but different spreads are told apart by the maximum mean discrepancy: send points into a kernel's hidden space, average, and measure the gap. Five guests each give 0.77, 0.32 and 0.39, so the squared distance is 0.31. The plain dot product notices the difference four times in a hundred, a bell of width one every time.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two crowds walk into the lantern festival. Every guest carries a lantern.

Both crowds are centred on the very same spot. But one crowd is huddled together, and the other is spread wide. Compare their averages, and you find nothing at all. The averages are identical.

Chapter five compared histograms, bin by bin. Chapter six moved earth from one pile to another. Today we compare two clouds of points with no bins, and no plan. We build a distance that sees the difference between these two crowds.

Last time, we met kernels. A kernel is a similarity that hides a flat space. Send every point into that hidden space, and the similarity becomes a dot product.

So here is the plan. Send every point of the huddled crowd into the hidden space, and take the average. Do the same for the wide crowd. Then measure the straight line between the two averages.

That straight line is called the maximum mean discrepancy, and people write it M M D. Gretton and colleagues studied it in a famous paper in two thousand twelve.

We can never visit the hidden space. But on a line, with a bell-shaped kernel, we can draw it. Every point becomes a glow: a little bell of light, centred on that point.

The average of a crowd is then its skyline. All the glows, added up, and divided by the number of guests. The huddled crowd makes a tall, narrow skyline. The wide crowd makes a low, wide one.

The distance between the crowds is the distance between the skylines. Square the gap at every position, add it all up, and you get the squared distance, up to a fixed constant. We checked it on this example, and the two numbers agree.

In practice, nobody draws skylines. You only compute three averages of similarities. Take five guests in each crowd, and fill a grid with their similarities, using a bell of width one. Every pair counts, even a guest with themselves.

First, average the block where the huddled crowd meets itself. It is bright: zero point seven seven. Second, average the block where the wide crowd meets itself. It is dim: zero point three two. Those guests are far apart, so they hardly resemble each other.

Third, average the two blocks where one crowd meets the other: zero point three nine.

The squared distance is the first average, plus the second, minus twice the third. Zero point seven seven, plus zero point three two, minus twice zero point three nine, gives zero point three one. The distance is the square root: zero point five six.

Here is the question hiding in that formula. Do the guests of one crowd resemble the guests of the other as much as they resemble their own crowd? If yes, the distance is close to zero.

Now let us change the kernel. The simplest kernel is the ordinary dot product. Its hidden space is the space we started from. The average of each crowd is just its mean, and the distance is the gap between the two means. Nothing more.

Take our two crowds again. Same mean, one twice as spread, a hundred points each. We shuffle the labels two hundred times to see what chance alone would give, and ask how often the test notices the difference. We repeat this five hundred times.

With the plain dot product, the test fires four times in a hundred. That is exactly the false alarm rate. It never notices. With a bell of width one, it fires every single time.

Same machinery, another hidden space, and the difference becomes visible.

Why does the bell work? Because its hidden space is rich enough to keep the whole distribution inside the average. Two distributions that have the same average in that space are the same distribution. Fukumizu and colleagues, and then Sriperumbudur and colleagues, worked out which kernels have this property. Bell kernels do. A polynomial kernel does not.

So with a rich kernel, all four lights come on. The distance is never negative. It is zero only for identical distributions. It is the same in both directions. And there is no shortcut, because it is a straight line in a flat space. With the plain dot product, the second light stays off: two different crowds can sit at distance zero.

Now, what does this distance ignore? Anything that happens at a scale much smaller, or much larger, than the width of the bell. That width is the knob, and it decides everything.

Here are the detection rates for our two crowds. Width zero point zero one: twenty-four percent. Zero point one: eighty-one. One: a hundred. Ten: twelve. A hundred: four.

Too narrow, and every guest lives alone in a tiny glow, so no two glows overlap. Too wide, and both skylines become the same smooth lump. A very wide bell behaves like the plain dot product, and the difference disappears again.

The usual habit is to take the median distance between the points. On our crowds it detects the difference every time. But it is a habit, not a theorem.

Now what does it cost? One similarity for each pair of points, so the square of the number of points. No bins to choose, and no plan to solve.

And what is the floor? Two samples drawn from the very same distribution are never at distance zero. The floor goes down like one over the square root of the number of points, whatever the distributions. That is a result of the paper.

We measured it here, on the same uniform clouds as in episode six point four. In two columns, a hundred points per cloud give a floor of zero point zero seven nine, and a thousand points give zero point zero two four. In ten columns: zero point zero nine seven, then zero point zero two seven.

Ten times more points divide the floor by about three, in two columns as in ten. In episode six point four, the transport distance in ten columns lost only a quarter: from zero point seven three to zero point five five.

Now, the traps. The first trap is to choose the width after you have seen the result. Try ten widths, keep the best, and your five percent is no longer five percent. Methods that choose the kernel from the data do it on one part of the points, and test on the other part.

The second trap is to believe that high dimension costs nothing. The floor falls just as fast, that is true. But Ramdas and colleagues showed that, for differences of equal difficulty, the power of these tests goes down as the dimension goes up. That does not say that kernel tests fail in high dimension. It says each extra column makes a given difference harder to see.

There is also a strange feature. The unbiased estimate of the squared distance is often negative. When the two samples come from the same distribution, sixty-three percent of the draws fall below zero. A squared distance below zero looks absurd, but it is normal: the estimate leaves out each point's comparison with itself, and noise does the rest. It is not a mistake.

Back to the row of changes. Same signal as in episode five point one: four thousand raw amplitude samples. A bell kernel, with its width taken once on the original samples: zero point seven two.

Shifted: zero point two eight. Amplified: zero point two three. Delayed: zero. Shuffled: zero. Noisy: zero point zero three.

The two zeros are the same as with bins. The kernel compares amplitudes one at a time, and the order of time never enters. But there were no bins to choose this time.

So the maximum mean discrepancy compares two clouds by comparing two averages. But they are the averages of a huge number of features at once.

With the simplest kernel, you compare the means. With a rich kernel, you compare everything. The width of the bell is the price of that freedom.

Energy

The energy distance needs no kernel and no width: double the average straight-line ribbon across two flocks and subtract the averages inside each. Here 8.49 minus 1.39 minus 1.43 gives 5.67, so the distance is 2.38. Ten hertz against eleven reads 1.41, against thirty 6.32. If the starting distance is not of negative type, the formula can return minus 0.33.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two clouds of points. Do they come from the same distribution?

Last time we answered with a kernel, and the kernel came with a width to choose. This time we choose nothing. All we need is the distance between two points.

Here is the recipe. Take two flocks of lanterns, five blue and five pink. Tie a ribbon between every pair of lanterns, and measure each ribbon with the straight line.

There are three kinds of ribbons. The ribbons across, from a blue lantern to a pink one. The ribbons inside the blue flock. And the ribbons inside the pink flock. Average each kind.

On our flocks, the average across is 4.25. Inside the blue flock, 1.39. Inside the pink flock, 1.43. Now take twice the average across, and cut off the two inside averages. Twice 4.25 is 8.49. Take away 1.39, take away 1.43. What is left is 5.67.

That is the squared energy distance. Its square root, 2.38, is the energy distance between the two flocks.

Why does it work? If both flocks come from the same distribution, a ribbon across looks like a ribbon inside. Twice the across is exactly the two insides, and nothing is left. If the flocks drift apart, the ribbons across stretch and the ribbons inside do not. What is left grows.

Székely and Rizzo called it energy, after the potential energy of two bodies in Newton's physics, which is also a function of the distances between them. Baringhaus and Franz built a two-sample test from the same ingredients in 2004.

With the straight line between points, the energy distance is zero only when the two distributions are identical, in any dimension. No bins. No transport plan. No width. The price is one distance for every pair of points.

Let us test it on two spikes. A spike at 10 hertz against a spike at 11 hertz: the energy distance is 1.41. Against a spike at 30 hertz: 6.32.

The gap grew twenty times. The distance grew about four and a half times. For two spikes, the squared energy distance is twice the shift, so the distance grows like the square root of the shift. Far-away mass is counted, but it is not shouted.

On a line, there is a second way to read the same number. Remember the staircases of episode 5.7. Each cloud climbs one step for every point. The biggest gap between two staircases was Kolmogorov and Smirnov. In episode 6.3, the area between them was the transport.

Now take the gap at every place, and square it. Take the area under those squares, and multiply by two. That is the squared energy distance again. The area under the squared gap is a very old distance, due to Cramér in 1928. Székely and Rizzo point out that on a line, it is exactly the ribbon recipe.

We can check it. Two simulated clouds of 60 points each. The ribbon recipe gives 0.626. Twice the area under the squared gap gives 0.626. Two different computations, one number.

And there is a third reading. In 2013, Sejdinovic and his colleagues showed that the energy distance is also a maximum mean discrepancy, the distance of the last episode, for a kernel built from the distance itself. On our clouds, the squared discrepancy is 0.313. Twice that is 0.626 once more.

So the same gap between two staircases can be read three ways. Its height. Its area. Or the area of its square.

Now the row of changes. The signal of episode 5.1, seen through its amplitudes, with its five copies. The energy distance from the original, computed on the raw samples: 0.43 for shifted, 0.25 for amplified, 0 for delayed, 0 for shuffled, and 0.04 for noisy.

Delayed and shuffled stay at zero, as they did for histograms. The cloud of amplitudes lost the order in time before any distance could look at it. But this time, nobody chose a bin.

There is also a link with episode 6.5. Blurred transport, with a huge blur, tends to half the squared energy distance. On the three-bin example, the squared energy distance is 0.36. With a blur of 1,000, the blurred transport is 0.18. Half.

One more fact. Take the squares of the distances, instead of the distances themselves. The formula collapses. It becomes twice the squared gap between the two means, and nothing else.

Here are two clouds with exactly the same mean. Their spreads are 1.06 and 2.30. With the straight line, the squared energy distance is 0.234. With the squared distance, it is zero. That is the simplest kernel of the last episode again: the plain mean. The straight distance is what lets the formula see the whole cloud.

Now an idea. We have a shelf of distances, from chapters 2 to 4. Could we plug any of them into the recipe, and get a distance between clouds?

Only some of them. The distance you start from must be of what is called negative type. In one sentence: once you take its square root, you can draw it in a flat space. For the result to be zero only between identical distributions, you need a slightly stronger condition. Lyons proved in 2013 that flat Hilbert spaces have it.

Here is what happens when the condition fails. A small graph with five nodes. Two on one side, three on the other, and every node is tied to every node on the opposite side. The distance is the number of edges on the shortest path.

Across, the distance is 1. Between the two nodes on one side, it is 2, so inside that flock the average is 1.00. Inside the flock of three, the average is 1.33. Now apply the recipe. Twice 1.00, minus 1.00, minus 1.33. We get minus 0.33.

A squared distance of minus 0.33. There is no square root of that. The formula computed without a complaint, and gave us nonsense. That is the trap: the formula always computes, even when it no longer defines a distance.

So here is the card. The object: two clouds of points. The distance: the square root of twice the ribbons across, minus the ribbons inside each cloud. It ignores nothing about the cloud itself, only the order in which the points came. It costs one distance for every pair of points.

With the straight line, all four rules hold. The square root obeys the triangle rule: on 3,000 triples of small clouds, we found no violation.

The energy distance is the distance by kernel that you get without choosing a kernel. You start from a distance you already have. And the condition fits in one sentence: the starting distance must be drawable in a flat space once you take its square root.

Dependence as a distance

Dependence can be measured as a distance between observed pairs and the same pairs with Y shuffled. With energy this gives the distance correlation, with a kernel the HSIC, and they are one tool. When Y is X squared, ordinary correlation is zero while distance correlation is 0.49. Raw values drift toward one as dimension grows, so correct the bias.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two measurements, taken on the same objects. Are they linked?

It is a classic question of data analysis, and we usually answer it with a correlation. This episode answers it with a distance.

Take a hundred objects, and give each one two numbers, X and Y. Plot every object as a point. Here Y is exactly the square of X. The link is perfect. Yet the ordinary correlation is zero, because the curve goes down and then up, and a straight-line score cannot see that.

So let us ask the question differently. If X and Y had no link at all, any Y could sit with any X. So shuffle the Ys. The same X values, the same Y values, but the pairs are broken.

Now we have two clouds of points. The pairs as we observed them, and the pairs as they would be with no link. The dependence is the distance between these two clouds.

You already know two ways to measure the distance between clouds. Chapter 7 has just given them to you. With the energy distance, you get the distance correlation, by Székely, Rizzo and Bakirov, in 2007. With a kernel, you get the Hilbert-Schmidt independence criterion, HSIC, by Gretton and colleagues, in 2005.

They look like two different tools. They are one tool. Sejdinovic and colleagues showed in 2013 that the energy version is the kernel version, for the kernel that a distance builds. On our parabola, the squared distance covariance is 0.023. The kernel version gives 0.0057, and four times that is again 0.023.

A bell kernel finds 0.032 for the same points, while in a thousand shuffles of the Ys, none goes above 0.012. Give the kernel the plain dot product instead, and the rescaled HSIC collapses to the squared ordinary correlation: 0.25 for a straight link of correlation 0.5, and exactly zero for our parabola. It is the lesson of episode 7.2 again. The plainest kernel sees only the plainest difference.

Now, how is it computed? With the table of distances.

Take twenty-four objects. Build one table for X: each cell says how far two objects are apart in X. Build another for Y, with the same objects in the same order.

Look at the Y table. Two objects placed symmetrically around zero have the same square, so they sit at distance zero. The table folds in the middle, like a mirror.

Next, centre each table. Subtract the average of each row and the average of each column, and add back the overall average. A cell no longer says how far. It says: farther than usual, or closer than usual.

Now multiply the two tables cell by cell, and average. If pairs that are close in X are also close in Y, and pairs that are far in X are far in Y, most products are positive. The two tables resemble each other, and the average is large.

That average is the distance covariance. Rescaled to run from 0 to 1, it is the distance correlation: 0.49 for our twenty-four objects.

Now shuffle the Ys. The Y table scrambles. Its cells no longer line up with the X table, the products cancel, and the value falls to 0.30. It does not fall to zero. We will come back to that floor.

Three links, a hundred objects each. The parabola: ordinary correlation 0.00, distance correlation 0.49. Points on a circle: 0.00 and 0.20. A straight link with ordinary correlation 0.50: distance correlation 0.48. The distance correlation sees all three.

And it is zero only when X and Y are independent. That is a theorem, for variables with finite averages, not something we observe on three examples.

X and Y may even have different numbers of columns. Take twenty channels on one side, and thirty on the other. No subtraction connects them. But the tables do not care: both are a hundred by a hundred. One number comes out: 0.88. These thirty channels are noisy, curved mixtures of the twenty, simulated. After shuffling the objects, the value never goes above 0.68. Chapter 12 picks up exactly here.

Time to fill the card of this distance. The object: two sets of measures on the same objects, with any number of columns. The distance: how far the observed pairs are from the re-paired ones, read as the resemblance of two centred tables.

What does it ignore? The sign, and the shape, of the link. A rising curve and a falling curve get the same value. Look at the row of changes. Shift X, stretch Y, flip Y, list the objects in another order: 0.49 every time. Only noise moves it, to 0.46.

But it does not forgive a change of unit on one channel among several. Take five channels, one of them useless. Multiply the useless one by 100, and 0.30 falls to 0.15.

Three of the four rules hold for this distance. The triangle is not a question we ask here, because it compares two clouds, not three variables.

What does it cost? Two tables, so the square of the number of objects. A thousand objects take 0.005 seconds on this machine, two thousand take 0.021, four thousand take 0.088. Four times more for each doubling. And ten thousand objects need 800 megabytes for each table.

The trap is bias. Take X and Y independent, with thirty objects. With one column each, the distance correlation averages 0.31, though there is no link at all. With ten columns, 0.75. With a hundred, 0.96. With a thousand, 0.996. Even with a hundred objects and a hundred columns, 0.89. The raw value runs toward one as the dimension grows.

The corrected version, from Székely and Rizzo in 2013 and 2014, stays within one hundredth of zero on average, in every one of these cases. It can be slightly negative, and that is normal.

So the raw value compares neither to an ordinary correlation, nor between data sets of different sizes. Our 0.88 between twenty and thirty channels has a corrected value of 0.61. Always set the raw number against the shuffles.

Independence is a distance that equals zero. And dependence is read in the second visual code of this series: two tables of distances over the same objects, and how much they resemble each other.

Chapter 12 starts exactly from there, to compare representations that share no common frame.

So: shuffle the Ys to build the cloud with no link. Measure the distance between the clouds, with energy or with a kernel. Read it as the resemblance of two centred tables. And mind the bias.

One witness, three families

Total variation, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, earth mover's and the kernel distance answer one question: who is the witness? Each is the best gap between two averages for a function obeying a rulebook: between zero and one, a step, slope at most one, or smooth. On three bins the scores are 0.30, 0.30, 0.60 and 0.39. A free witness scores one on two samples of the same source.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Three chapters ago, we started comparing two distributions. First, we counted bin by bin. Then we moved mass. Then we compared them through a similarity.

They looked like three different worlds. Today they meet. They are three answers to a single question. Who is the witness?

Let us meet our two histograms again. Three bins, at positions one, two and three. The pink one holds point five, point three and point two. The blue one holds point two, point three and point five. This data is illustrative.

Now a witness walks in. A witness is a function. It gives a number to every position. We average that number over the pink histogram, and then over the blue one. The score of the witness is the gap between the two averages.

A good witness gives high numbers where pink is heavy, and low numbers where blue is heavy. The distance is the best score that any witness can reach. But the witness is not free. It must follow a rulebook, and the distance depends on the rulebook. Alfred Müller wrote this idea down in nineteen ninety-seven. He called these distances integral probability metrics.

Let us change the rulebook, four times.

Rule one. Any function, as long as it stays between zero and one. The best witness gives one to the first position, where pink is heavier, and zero to the others. The pink average is point five. The blue average is point two. The score is point three zero. This is the total variation of chapter five. Some books let the witness go from minus one to one. Then the score doubles, so always check the convention.

Rule two. Only steps. The witness says one up to some position, and zero after it. Stopping after position one scores point three zero. Stopping after position two scores point three zero as well. A tie. This is the distance of Kolmogorov and Smirnov, from episode five point seven. Notice the nesting. Every step is also a function between zero and one, so the freer rulebook can only score higher, or equal.

Rule three. The slope may never exceed one. Now the witness can climb, but only gently. The best one goes down by one at each position. The pink average is point three. The blue average is minus point three. The score is point six zero. This is the earth mover's distance of chapter six, seen from the other side, through the duality of Kantorovich and Rubinstein.

Rule four. Only smooth functions, the ones a kernel can build. We take a bell of width one. The best witness is a smooth bump. It is high near the first position, and low near the third. Its score is zero point three nine. This is the maximum mean discrepancy of this chapter.

Four rulebooks, four scores. Do not compare the numbers, because the units differ. Compare what each witness is allowed to see.

A freer witness sees more. That sounds like good news. It has a price.

Take two samples from the very same source, ten points each. The witness of rule one is free. It gives one to every pink point, and zero to every blue point. The score is one, the largest possible, for two samples of the same source. Two samples never share a point. The free witness isolates every point, and declares the samples completely different.

The kernel witness cannot do that. A bump is wide. On these same ten points, it scores only point one eight. This is also a theorem. In twenty twelve, Sriperumbudur and colleagues showed that the estimates for the transport and kernel distances are consistent, but not the estimate for total variation.

Now the floor. It is the score between two samples of the same source, and it should be zero. In one column, with a hundred points per sample, the kernel floor is point zero eight. With a thousand points, it falls to point zero two six. It falls like the inverse root of the number of points.

Now ten columns. The transport floor is two point six at a hundred points. At a thousand points, it is still two point zero. The kernel floor goes from point one one to point zero three five.

Does it matter? Shift one cloud by point three in each of the ten columns. The true transport distance is point nine five. Measured on two thousand points, transport says two point one. The floor swallows the signal. The kernel says point one seven, far above its floor.

So which families fit this picture? From chapter five, only one. Total variation. Sriperumbudur and colleagues also showed that it is the only non-trivial divergence that is the best score of a witness class. So Kullback-Leibler and Hellinger do not fit.

Now the bill. Bin by bin makes one pass over the data. But you choose the bins, and bins know nothing about positions. In ten columns, with twelve bins per column, there are sixty-two billion bins.

Transport knows the positions. But it is expensive. In episode six point four, the measured cost grew with a slope of two point two. On two thousand points in ten columns, it took point seven three seconds, measured here.

The kernel needs one similarity for each pair of points, a cost like the square. The same two thousand points took point zero six seconds. Its floor falls fast. Its price is a bandwidth to choose.

A trick makes the kernel cheaper. Replace it by random cosines. This is the idea of Rahimi and Recht, from two thousand seven. On two clouds of a thousand points, a hundred random features give the distance to within five percent, and a thousand features to within one percent. That is the median over three hundred draws. The saving appears when the points outnumber the features. With two thousand points, it is only twice as fast, point zero three seconds.

Earth mover's says point five, point three four, zero, zero, point zero four. The kernel, with a bell of width one unit of amplitude, says point two seven, point two zero, zero, zero, point zero three.

Three witnesses, one blind spot. All three see only the histogram of values. The order in time is gone before any witness speaks.

So here is the whole chapter in one sentence. To choose a distance between distributions is to choose which differences you count as real. The rulebook decides.

And the witness can be drawn. Here is the best witness of rule four. Positive near the first position, negative near the third, and flat in between. It does not only say that the two histograms differ. It shows where.

Two clouds, not two means

Comparing means is the simplest kernel, and it sees nothing when two clouds share a centre: with spreads one and two, a linear-kernel test flagged a difference five percent of the time, a bell kernel of width one ninety-nine percent. Worth trying: the energy distance flagged three-measure groups 57 percent of the time, where three Bonferroni t-tests flagged 5.5 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

This is the last episode of the kernels chapter, and it asks one question. When you compare two conditions, what exactly are you comparing?

Usually the answer is their means. Average the trials of the first condition, average the trials of the second, and subtract. It feels like no method at all. But it is a method. It is the simplest kernel of this chapter, the linear one. With that kernel, the mean of a cloud is all that survives. So comparing means is comparing clouds through a very small window.

Picture two clouds of lanterns hanging along a line. They have the same centre. One cloud is tight. The other is twice as wide. Balance each cloud on a single pin, and both pins land on the same spot. The two means are zero point zero seven and zero point one six. Yet nobody would call these the same cloud.

So we tested it. One hundred points per cloud, spreads one and two, the same mean. We ran a test with the linear kernel, which is exactly a comparison of means. Over three hundred repeated experiments, it flagged a difference five percent of the time. That is the false alarm rate we allowed. It sees nothing. Then we swapped in a bell kernel of width one. It flagged the difference ninety-nine percent of the time. Same data. Only the window changed.

Now the two columns. On the left, Established. Each entry has a paper behind it. On the right, Worth trying. Those are suggestions, each with a small simulation, and none of them is a result.

First entry. Geerligs, the Cam-CAN team and Henson, in twenty sixteen. They took resting-state fMRI from two hundred and fourteen people, scanned twice. For each region of the brain, the usual method averages the signals of its voxels into one signal, then correlates. Distance correlation keeps all the voxels. For functional connectivity, it was more reliable from one session to the next, with each of the three sets of regions they tried. For structural covariance, the result was mixed. The averaged correlation did better for two of the three sets.

Second entry. Park and colleagues, in twenty thirteen, wrote a tutorial on kernels for spike trains. Such kernels exist. So the geometry of this chapter applies to trains of action potentials.

Third entry. Pan and colleagues, in twenty eleven, and Long and colleagues, in twenty fifteen. When training data and new data come from two different sources, they add the maximum mean discrepancy to the learning objective. It pulls the two clouds together.

Fourth entry. Song and colleagues, in twenty twelve. The Hilbert Schmidt criterion scores how strongly each measure depends on a label. The method keeps the measures that depend on it most.

One remark separates the columns. All of this needs a kernel, or a distance that supplies one. Dynamic time warping, from episode four point two, is not a distance, so nothing is guaranteed. A kernel built straight from it is not always a kernel. Cuturi, in twenty eleven, built alternatives that are positive definite.

Now the right column. Suggestion one. Compare the clouds of trials of two conditions with the maximum mean discrepancy, from Gretton and colleagues, instead of comparing their means. Then shuffle the condition labels many times, to see what chance looks like. On our draw, the test on means gives a p-value of zero point six eight. The discrepancy is zero point one one. Ninety-five percent of one thousand shuffles stay below zero point zero two seven. The p-value is zero point zero zero one.

Suggestion two. Compare two groups described by several measures at once, with the energy distance. It has no width to set. We built two groups of forty, with three measures each. Same means, same spreads. But in one group the first two measures rise together, and in the other they move against each other. The means differ by zero point two eight, minus zero point zero five, and minus zero point zero two. The t-tests give p-values of zero point two two, zero point eight four, and zero point nine four. The energy distance gives zero point zero three six on this draw. Repeated two hundred times, it flags the groups fifty-seven percent of the time. Three t-tests with a Bonferroni correction flag them five point five percent of the time.

Suggestion three. Measure the distance correlation between the multichannel signals of two people, in its corrected version, by Székely and Rizzo. Get the floor from pseudo-pairs: people who never interacted. We simulated twenty pairs, with twenty channels against thirty, and slow drifting signals. The true pairs score zero point five six. The pseudo-pairs score zero point two zero on average, and their ninety-fifth percentile is zero point two four. It is not zero, because slow signals are not independent samples. That is why the floor matters.

Suggestion four. Draw the witness function, to see where two conditions differ, and not only whether they differ. On our two clouds, it is positive in the middle, where the tight cloud is denser, and negative in the tails, where the wide cloud reaches.

Four ideas, and none of them is a result.

Back to the question. Comparing conditions by their means is the simplest kernel. It is one choice among others, and it sees neither the spreads nor the shapes. So ask your data: is what interests me in the mean?
