The shortest path on a surface

Montréal and Paris are 5,504 kilometres apart along the surface and 5,334 through a tunnel, so the arc is 3.2 percent longer. A geodesic stays in the space where data can be: on a sphere, an arc of a great circle. Curvature shows from inside: a triangle with three right angles sums to 270 degrees, and a circle lacks over a third of its length.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the globe-maker's workshop. On the big globe, two pins: Montréal and Paris. How far apart are they?

The answer depends on who you ask. In this series, a distance is always a choice of space, and today we meet a choice that every globe-maker makes without noticing.

Let us ask two walkers. The first is the inchworm. It stays on the surface, and it walks.

Draw two lines from the centre of the Earth, one to each pin. They make an angle of 49.5 degrees. With an Earth radius of 6,371 kilometres, a 49.5 degree arc of the surface is 5,504 kilometres long. That is the inchworm's walk.

The second walker is the ruler. It goes straight, through the Earth, in a tunnel. The tunnel measures 5,334 kilometres. Cut the Earth in half along the route, and you see why it is shorter: the tunnel is the chord of the arc. At its deepest point, it runs 585 kilometres under the ground.

The straight line exists. But nobody can take it. A distance has to be measured in the space where the data can actually be. For a point on the Earth, that space is the surface.

The shortest path that stays inside the surface has a name: a geodesic. On a sphere, a geodesic is an arc of a great circle. That is the circle you get by cutting the sphere through its centre. Pull a rubber band tight between two pins on a globe, and it settles on that arc. Any other path that stays on the surface is longer.

Up close, the arc and the chord agree. Far away, they do not. Take a sphere of radius 1. For two points 60 degrees apart, the arc is 1.047 and the chord is 1.000. At 90 degrees, 1.571 against 1.414. At 180 degrees, two opposite points: 3.142 against 2.000.

Back on the Earth, the numbers say the same thing. Between our two pins, the arc is 3.2 percent longer than the chord. For two points 1,000 kilometres apart, it is only 0.1 percent longer. For two points 100 kilometres apart, a thousandth of a percent. So the straight line is a good ruler for close points, and a poor one for distant points. You met a first hint of this in episode 8.4, where a curved space had to be flattened before a straight line could be used.

Now, three ways to measure the curve of a surface, without ever leaving it.

First, a triangle. Put one corner at the pole. Put the two others on the equator, a quarter turn apart. Every corner of this triangle is a right angle. Three right angles: 270 degrees, instead of the 180 degrees of a flat triangle. This triangle covers exactly one eighth of the sphere. The bigger the triangle, the bigger the excess. A tiny triangle has almost none.

Second, a circle. Around the pole, draw a circle whose radius is a quarter of a meridian: 10,008 kilometres, measured along the surface. That circle is the equator, and its length is 40,030 kilometres. The formula of the plane, two pi times the radius, promises 62,879 kilometres. More than a third is missing. There is less room on the sphere than the plane would give.

Shrink the circle, and the gap shrinks too. With a radius of only 1,000 kilometres, just 0.41 percent is missing. A small circle on a sphere looks flat.

Third, a map. No flat map keeps all the distances. A map can keep angles, or it can keep areas, but it cannot keep every distance at once. Peel a globe, and the paper has to tear or stretch. Every flat world map you have seen is a compromise.

This follows from a theorem of Gauss, published in 1828. Curvature is a property of the surface itself. It can be computed from distances measured on the surface, without ever seeing how the surface sits in space. Picture a very small ant that never leaves the surface. With a ruler, it could still find out whether its world is curved. And the curvature of a sphere is not the curvature of a plane. So no flat sheet can copy it faithfully.

Three spaces will serve as references from now on.

The sphere has positive curvature. There is less room than in the plane: circles are shorter, and triangles are fat.

The plane has zero curvature. Its triangles add up to 180 degrees.

The hyperbolic space has negative curvature. There is more room than in the plane: circles are longer, and triangles are thin. The cone of ellipses from chapter 8, measured in ratios, was of this third kind.

Let us fill the distance card for the geodesic.

The object: points on a surface. The distance: the shortest path that stays on that surface.

What it ignores: the way the surface is placed in the space around it. Roll a flat sheet into a tube, and every geodesic length stays the same.

What it costs: a formula when the space is known, and a shortest-path computation when it is not.

And its trap: at a small scale, everything is flat. Curvature only shows at the scale where your data spread out. Montréal and Paris are far enough apart for the curve to matter. Two streets in the same neighbourhood are not.

So here is what to keep. Curvature does not say how a surface is folded in space. It is measured from the inside, with circles and triangles. A dataset can be tested the same way: measure its triangles and its circles, and ask which of the three spaces it looks like.

The ruler answers for the space around the data. The inchworm answers for the space the data are in. Which one is right depends on where your data can be.

PLATE 9.26:14

Data on a sphere

Angles between directions squeeze toward ninety degrees as columns grow: the spread falls from 39.2 degrees at three columns to 1.81 at a thousand. The mean of directions has two definitions: averaging arrows gives 26.57 degrees, minimizing squared arcs gives 30.00. Two opposite poles make every point of the equator a mean.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Some data live on a sphere. Nobody chose it. It is what is left after you throw something away.

Take a list of numbers and keep only its direction, as in episode 2.3. That is a point on a sphere. Take a histogram and pass it through a square root, as in episode 5.2. That is another one. Take the voltage at every electrode of a scalp at one instant, and scale the whole map to the same amplitude. A third sphere. Or take a plain direction in space.

On every one of these spheres, the distance is the angle between two points. This episode is about four things that angle does to your data.

First fact. In many columns, angles squeeze toward ninety degrees. Episode 2.6 announced it. Here is the measurement. Watch what happens to the shape of the angles as the number of columns grows.

We draw 100,000 pairs of random directions, and measure the angle of each pair. With 3 columns, the angles spread over the whole range: the standard deviation is 39.2 degrees. With 10 columns, 19.1. With 100 columns, 5.8. With 1,000 columns, only 1.81 degrees.

So an angle of 70 degrees does not mean the same thing in every dimension. With 3 columns, it is half a standard deviation below ninety, and a third of random pairs are closer than that. It is banal. With 1,000 columns, it is eleven standard deviations below ninety. None of our hundred thousand random pairs came anywhere near it. There, 70 degrees means very close. Never read an angle without the number of columns.

Second fact. The mean has two definitions. And a mean of directions matters: it is the typical wind, the typical gaze, the template that a cluster of maps is summarised by.

Take three points on the equator: two at longitude 0, and one at longitude 90 degrees.

The first definition: average the arrows, then push the result out to the surface. Two arrows point along the first axis, one along the second. Their average points at 26.57 degrees. Notice where that arrow went: through the inside of the ball, where no data can live. We repaired it afterwards.

The second definition is the one of episode 1.6. Look for the point that makes the sum of squared distances as small as possible, with each distance measured along the surface. Now the inchworm does the walking. Each point at longitude theta pays two squared arcs to the first pair, and one to the last point. The cheapest point is at exactly 30.00 degrees.

The two answers differ by 3.43 degrees. The second one is also truly the best: its total cost is 1.645 squared radians, against 1.656 for the first. They agree when points are grouped. They separate as soon as the points spread. Slide the third point away to 150 degrees, and the two means are 26 degrees apart.

Third fact. The mean may not be unique.

Put two points at the two poles. Average the arrows: they cancel. The average is zero, and zero has no direction. Now minimize the squared arcs. Every point of the equator is a quarter turn from both poles, and every one of them costs exactly 4.935. Nothing does better. Every point of the equator is a mean. Which one would you report?

On the sphere, a mean is guaranteed to be unique only if the data fit in a cap of radius less than 90 degrees, that is, inside an open half sphere. That is the condition of Kendall, from 1990, taken up by Afsari in 2011. The two poles sit exactly on the edge. Inside a small cap, three points on the equator have one mean, and only one. Outside, a unique mean can still happen, but nothing promises it.

In the cone of ellipses of chapter 8, the mean was always unique. That is the difference between negative curvature and positive curvature. Positive curvature costs uniqueness.

Fourth fact. Sometimes two opposite points are the same object.

Think of a map of potential on the scalp. Flip the sign of every electrode, and you get the inverted map. On the sphere, it sits at 180 degrees: the opposite point. In resting EEG, the map keeps roughly the same shape for a fraction of a second, then jumps to another one. These few shapes that come back are called microstates. The EEG series presents them.

In their analysis, a map and its inverted copy count as the same state, because the sign of the map follows the oscillation of the signal. That is the choice of Pascual-Marqui, Michel and Lehmann in 1995, and it is explained in the review of Michel and Koenig in 2018. The sign is a nuisance here, not information. The distance falls to zero. The space is no longer the sphere. It is the sphere with opposite points glued together. And two maps can never be more than 90 degrees apart.

But it is a choice. The dissimilarity of Lehmann and Skrandies, from 1980, used for evoked potentials, the response to an event averaged over many trials, does the opposite. A map and its inverse get the largest gap, 2. Murray, Brunet and Michel describe it in 2008. Same maps, two spaces. Say which one you use.

Now the card. The object is a direction. The distance is the angle. The angle ignores the length of the vectors, so the amplitude. It costs one dot product. And its traps are two: reading an angle without knowing the dimension, and averaging directions that are widely spread, then trusting the result.

On a sphere, the mean has two definitions, and they separate as soon as the data spread. Sometimes there is no single answer. Positive curvature costs uniqueness.

A space with too much room

A binary tree with six levels has 127 nodes and needs room that doubles at every level. Flat circles grow in a line, 62.8 at radius ten, while hyperbolic ones reach 69,198. Ten flat dimensions leave a 6.3 percent error placing the tree, whereas two curved dimensions with edges of length five reach 4.6.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a space with too much room. To see why anyone wants one, start with a tree.

A tree is made of dots, called nodes, joined by lines, called edges. It has no loop. So between any two nodes there is exactly one path. In a binary tree, every node splits in two. The nodes at the last level are the leaves. The Graph Theory series meets trees in its own episodes. Ours has six levels below the root. That makes one hundred twenty-seven nodes, and sixty-four of them are leaves. Half of all the nodes sit on the last level.

Watch the count. Each level has twice as many nodes as the one before. The room needed doubles at every step.

Now ask the plane how much room it has. Draw a circle of radius one, two, five and ten. The circumferences are six point three, twelve point six, thirty-one point four, and sixty-two point eight. The room grows in a straight line. It does not double.

Now take the hyperbolic plane, with curvature minus one. The same circles have circumferences of seven point four, twenty-two point eight, four hundred sixty-six, and sixty-nine thousand, one hundred ninety-eight. The room grows like a tree. The formula is two pi times the hyperbolic sine of the radius. Once the radius is large, each extra unit of radius multiplies the room by about two point seven.

We draw this plane inside a disc. All of it fits, because the rulers shrink as you approach the edge. From the centre, halfway to the edge is a distance of one point one zero. Nine tenths of the way is two point nine four. Then five point two nine at point nine nine, and seven point six zero at point nine nine nine. The edge itself is infinitely far, like the edge of the cone in episode eight point two.

Shortest paths bend toward the centre. They are arcs of circles that meet the edge at a right angle. This picture is called the Poincaré disc. The same space has other maps, like the Klein disc, where shortest paths are straight and the distances are read differently. In every one of these maps, the distances are the same. Only the picture changes.

Now put two points at radius point nine, a quarter turn apart. Along the shortest path, the distance is five point two zero. Through the centre, it is five point eight nine. The detour costs only point six nine. And it never costs more, however far out the points are. In the flat plane, the same detour would cost forty-one percent more.

That is exactly how you travel in a tree. To go from one leaf to another, you climb to the common ancestor, and you come back down.

So let us try to place our tree. Think of the family photo: every relative must stand as far from the others as the family tree says. We keep its distances: the number of edges between two nodes. And we say how we measure the error. For every pair of nodes, we take the gap between the new distance and the tree distance, divided by the tree distance. Then we average over all pairs.

First, the flat space. We search with an optimiser, from twelve random starts, and keep the best layout found. It is not a proven minimum. In two dimensions, the error is twenty point four percent. In three, fourteen point six. In ten dimensions, six point three.

Now the hyperbolic plane, with a construction by Sarkar. The root sits at the centre. Each node puts its children at equal angles around it, at a distance we choose, the edge length. The result depends on that length. With edges of length one, the error is twenty-two point five percent. With two, eleven point four. With three, seven point six. With five, four point six.

Now compare. Ten flat dimensions give six point three percent. Two curved dimensions, with edges of length three, come close. With edges of length five, they do better. Two curved dimensions can do the work of ten flat ones.

Sarkar proved that the error can be made as small as you like, just by lengthening the edges.

In flat space, more dimensions never quite suffice. For a binary tree, the worst-case error grows with the depth, whatever the number of dimensions. It grows very slowly, like the square root of the logarithm of the depth. This is a result of Bourgain, sharpened by Matoušek, and it is reported here from secondary sources. It concerns the worst pair, not the average we just computed. So the two numbers are different things.

The price of curved space is the precision of numbers. Put a node at the end of a path of ten edges, each of length three. In the disc, it is only two ten-trillionths from the edge. With thirteen edges, the computer writes exactly one. The point sits on the edge, at an infinite distance from everything. Near the edge, one hair's width on the screen is a long walk in the space. So the picture needs very many digits. At fourteen edges, we would need sixty bits. A standard number has fifty-three.

The precision needed grows with the length of the longest path in the tree. It grows very little with the number of branches. For a hierarchy of words, WordNet, Sala and colleagues needed almost five hundred bits. They also showed that you can trade precision for dimensions: ten dimensions bring it down to thirty-two bits.

People have used this. Nickel and Kiela placed hierarchies of words in the disc in twenty seventeen. Krioukov and colleagues argued in twenty ten that large networks live naturally in hyperbolic space.

So here is the card of the hyperbolic distance. The object: points in a hyperbolic plane. The distance: the shortest path, and it is a true distance. What it ignores: which map you draw it in. What it costs: a formula for each pair, and numbers more precise than usual. And the trap: using it on data that do not look like a tree.

Negative curvature means room that grows exponentially, and that is what a tree needs. A hierarchy is a geometry.

To sum up. A tree needs room that doubles at every level. The flat plane gives room that grows in a line. The hyperbolic plane gives room that grows like a tree. And you pay for it in digits.

How tree-like are your data?

Four points have three pairings, and in a tree the two longest sums are equal. The gap between them, divided by the diameter, is the relative delta, zero for a tree. Over 200,000 random quadruplets, a noisy tree reads 0.21, a hyperbolic disc of radius six 0.12, a flat square 0.48 and a circle 0.98. Image features read 0.17 to 0.26.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we found that a tree fits beautifully into curved space. But how do you know that your data are a tree? Not a picture of a tree, not a hunch. A number. And the test that gives it needs nothing but distances, four points at a time.

Take four points. There are three ways to pair them up. The first with the second, and the third with the fourth. The first with the third, and the second with the fourth. The first with the fourth, and the second with the third. For each way, add the two distances. That gives three sums. Picture each sum as a plank: two segments, laid end to end.

Now take a tree, the binary tree of the last episode, with six levels and one hundred twenty-seven nodes. Pick four leaves. Distance is the number of steps along the branches. The three planks measure twenty-two, eighteen and twenty-two. The two longest are equal.

That is no accident. Two of the leaves are cousins, and so are the other two. Pair the cousins together, and you get the short plank. Any other pairing makes both distances cross the middle of the tree, and the same stretch of road is counted twice. So their planks end in exactly the same place. In a tree, the two longest sums are always equal. Buneman proved the converse in nineteen seventy-four: if that holds for every quadruplet, the space can be drawn as a weighted tree.

So the gap between the two longest planks measures what is missing to be a tree. Zero for a tree. In nineteen eighty-seven, Gromov turned this into a definition. A space is hyperbolic up to delta if, for every quadruplet, that gap is at most two delta. The smaller the delta, the closer to a tree. We use this convention, where the largest gap is two delta. The other usual definition, with Gromov products, gives the same value: we checked it on forty points.

An example. The four corners of a unit square. The sides are one, the diagonals root two. The three planks measure two, two, and two point eight three. The gap is zero point eight three. Is that big? It depends on the scale. So we divide by the largest distance of the cloud, here the diagonal, one point four one. That gives zero point five nine, a number between zero and one. Khrulkov and colleagues, in twenty twenty, call it the relative delta: two delta, over the diameter.

Now a whole collection. Each time, two hundred thousand quadruplets drawn at random, and we keep the largest gap over the diameter. Simulated, seed ninety-four. The hyperbolic points are spread evenly over the area of the disc, so most of them sit near the rim, as in the tree.

The tree itself: exactly zero. The same tree, with random noise of zero point three added to every distance: zero point two one. A thousand random points in a hyperbolic disc of radius six: zero point one two. The same disc, radius two: zero point three two. A thousand random points in a square, measured in straight lines: zero point four eight. A city grid of thirty by thirty corners, measured in blocks: zero point seven nine. A thousand points on a sphere, measured along the surface: zero point nine two. And a thousand points on a circle, measured along the arc: zero point nine eight. Four evenly spaced points on the circle reach exactly one.

What about real data? Khrulkov and colleagues took image datasets, and measured the distance between the features that trained networks compute for each image. Averaged over ten samples of one thousand images, they found between zero point one seven and zero point two six. They write that this is significantly closer to zero than to one. Where the line between tree-like and not tree-like lies, the paper does not say.

And here are the traps, all visible in this collection. First, the scale. The disc of radius two reads zero point three two, the disc of radius six reads zero point one two. Same geometry, different size: at small scale, everything is flat. Second, the noise. The tree with noise reads zero point two one, right inside the range of the images. A value near zero point two does not prove a tree.

Third, the worst case. This number is a maximum, and one quadruplet in two hundred thousand decides it. The mean over all quadruplets is much lower: zero point zero three seven for the noisy tree, zero point zero seven seven for the square. But then it is no longer Gromov's quantity. Borassi, Chessa and Caldarelli, in twenty fifteen, found that for networks the worst case is highly influenced by random events, and proposed the average. The average hides what the maximum shows, though. The square and the city grid have the same mean, zero point zero seven seven, and very different maxima. So always say which one you report.

Fourth, a low number says: looks like a tree at large scale. It does not say that the hierarchy means something.

What does the number ignore? Everything except distances: coordinates, rotations, even the number of dimensions. And because we divide by the diameter, it ignores the unit too. Multiply every distance by ten, and the number does not move.

What does the test cost? Every quadruplet. For one hundred points, three point nine million. For one thousand points, forty-one billion. So we draw random quadruplets, and the largest we find is a lower bound on the true maximum. Or we use an exact algorithm: Fournier, Ismail and Vigneron, in twenty fifteen, reach n to the power three point six nine, the best known at the time.

So before you put your data in a hyperbolic disc, compute this number. Say whether it is the worst case or the mean, and at which scale.

PLATE 9.55:48

Subspaces

A plane is not its axes. With twenty channels and three hidden directions, the first components of two recording halves differ by 56 degrees, yet the planes they span differ by only 9.4. Principal angles compare planes, and the root of their squared sum is a distance. Keeping four directions instead of three sends the largest angle to 78 degrees.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, every object we compared was a point. A list of numbers. A signal. A histogram. This time, the object is a plane.

You meet planes all the time. The first three principal components of a recording. The directions along which a population of neurons varies. We keep three axes, and we say: this is where the activity lives. Is it the same few directions in two conditions, on two days, in two animals? That is a question about planes.

But there is a problem. A plane is described by axes, and the axes are arbitrary. Turn them inside the plane, and you describe the very same plane. Think of two pencils stuck into a sheet of cardboard. Turn the pencils inside the sheet, and the sheet has not moved. The pencils are the axes. The sheet is what we care about. So what exactly should we compare?

Let me show it on a simulation. Twenty channels. Three hidden directions, with variances of five, four point eight, and four point six. On top of that, noise of variance one in every channel. Seed ninety-five.

We cut the recording in two halves of four hundred samples each, and compute the principal components of each half.

Take the first component of the first half, and the first component of the second half. They differ by fifty-six degrees, in median over two hundred draws. The second components: sixty-three degrees. The third: fifty-eight. These axes are almost unrelated.

And yet, the plane they span is nearly the same. The three angles between the two planes average nine point four degrees. The largest one is twelve.

The components are unstable. The plane is stable. The three variances are close to each other, so the axes turn freely inside their plane. But they all stand far above the noise, so the plane itself hardly moves. Davis and Kahan, in nineteen seventy, tied the rotation of a subspace to the size of such gaps.

So we should compare planes, without going through their axes.

The tool is the principal angles, due to Jordan, in eighteen seventy-five. Look for one direction in each plane, such that the angle between them is as small as possible. Then look among the directions perpendicular to those two, and do it again.

An example. The floor, and a plane tilted by thirty degrees that shares a line with it. The principal angles are zero, and thirty. Now describe the floor a second time, with axes turned by forty-five degrees. The floor against itself: zero, and zero. The axes changed. The angles did not.

With one dimension, a plane becomes a line through the origin, and there is a single angle. The space of such lines in a plane is a circle: turn a line by a half turn, and you are back where you started. With three dimensions, there are three angles, one for each direction we keep.

The computation is small. Orthonormalise each plane, multiply the two bases together, and take the singular values of that little matrix. They are the cosines of the angles. This is the method of Björck and Golub, from nineteen seventy-three. And these same cosines are the canonical correlations of Hotelling, from nineteen thirty-six. Chapter twelve will use them again.

Now, a distance. Apply Pythagoras to the angles: the root of the sum of the squared angles. For the tilted plane, that is thirty degrees. It is the shortest path in the space of all planes of a given dimension. That space is a curved space of its own, and this distance comes from its geometry, as shown by Edelman, Arias and Smith, in nineteen ninety-eight. On our two halves, it averages seventeen degrees.

Other choices exist. The largest angle alone. Or the sines of the angles, as in Hamm and Lee, two thousand eight. They are different choices, and the literature lists several. We use the first one here, and we do not rank them.

Now, the floor. Take two random planes of three dimensions. In twenty dimensions, their smallest angle is fifty-six degrees on average. In a hundred dimensions: seventy-five. In a thousand: eighty-five. With twenty channels, two planes that have nothing in common are not at ninety degrees. Any angle must be read against this floor. Two thousand simulated draws for each.

There is a second benchmark: two halves of the same recording. Compare like with like. Here, their smallest angle is seven degrees, against fifty-six for planes with nothing in common.

Now the card. The object: a plane. The distance: the root of the squared principal angles. What it ignores: the axes inside each plane, and also the variance that each direction carries. What it costs: one small decomposition per pair. And the trap. Two traps, in fact.

First, the number of dimensions you keep. With three directions, our two halves are twelve degrees apart at most. Keep four, and a direction of pure noise joins the plane. The largest angle jumps to seventy-eight degrees. Keep only two, and it is fifty-five.

Second, the variances. The distance counts every angle the same way. Keep a fifth direction whose variance is barely above the noise, and its angle weighs as much as the angle of the strongest direction. The distance cannot tell a solid direction from a faint one. So we say how many dimensions we kept, and why.

So, remember. Axes are a choice, and a plane does not depend on it. When directions are unstable, compare what they span. A subspace is a point in another curved space, and it has its own distance.

Distances along the data

Straight-line distance fails on a rolled sheet: two points on neighbouring turns are 6.3 apart through the air but 59.9 along the paper. Joining each point to its nearest neighbours and taking the shortest chain of hops recovers the true distance, but with forty neighbours some edges jump across turns, and the correlation falls to 0.72 with no warning.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Remember the sheet of paper from the teaser? Today we roll it up for real.

Two thousand points sit on a sheet that is eighty-nine units long and twenty-one wide. We rolled it like a Swiss roll. Seen from its end, it is a spiral, and the turns are six point three apart.

There is no formula for this space. We cannot say where a point is on the paper. All we have is the cloud of points, and the straight distance between any two of them.

Pick two witness points, on neighbouring turns. The ruler goes straight through the air: six point three. The inchworm goes around the paper: fifty-nine point nine. That is almost ten times more.

Over all the pairs of points, the straight line and the distance along the sheet are correlated at only zero point two seven.

So the distance along the sheet is the one we want. But we cannot unroll the sheet. We do not know how it was rolled. We only have points.

Here is the recipe of Tenenbaum, de Silva and Langford, from the year two thousand. Think of the points as stepping stones.

You may only hop to a stone within reach. So each point is joined to its k nearest neighbours. Each hop has the length of the straight line, because up close, the straight line and the paper agree. And the distance between two stones is the shortest chain of hops.

Dijkstra's algorithm is the usual way to find that chain. The authors computed it with Floyd's algorithm, on a roll of one thousand points, with seven neighbours.

Take eight neighbours. Watch the inchworm go: the chain between the witnesses has fifty-one small hops, each one along the paper. Its length is sixty-three point six. The true distance, along the unrolled sheet, is fifty-nine point nine.

Over all pairs, the correlation with the true distance is one point zero zero zero. The chain zigzags, so it is a little long: five point five percent in the median.

With more points, it gets closer. Bernstein and colleagues proved that, in a Stanford report from around the year two thousand.

Now turn the dial. How far a stone can see is the only setting of this method. If the reach is too short, the hops are tiny, the chain zigzags, and it comes out long.

With three neighbours, the graph breaks into six pieces, and three percent of the pairs are not connected at all. With five, the chain is sixty-six, and a typical pair comes out twelve point eight percent too long. With twelve: sixty-one point one. With twenty: sixty point five.

Longer reach, better chain. So why not reach further?

With forty neighbours: thirty-eight point four.

Something broke. The reach is now long enough to touch the next turn. A hop can jump across the air from one turn to the next, and a jump is a shortcut.

Out of forty-four thousand edges, thirty-five jump. That is enough. The correlation falls to zero point seven two, and twenty-two percent of the pairs are underestimated by more than twenty percent.

It starts earlier. With thirty neighbours, two jumping edges out of thirty-three thousand are already enough to bring the correlation to zero point eight two.

To see it clearly, add one faulty edge by hand, between the two witnesses, with eight neighbours. Their distance falls from sixty-three point six to six point three.

And it is not only them. Thirty percent of all pairs lose more than ten percent of their distance. Sixteen percent lose more than half. The correlation falls from one to zero point six five.

Tenenbaum and colleagues warned about this, in a note: the neighbourhood must be small enough to prevent edges that, in their words, short circuit the true geometry of the manifold. In two thousand and two, Balasubramanian and Schwartz questioned the stability of the method, and the authors replied in the same issue.

Noise does the same thing, all at once. With noise of zero point three on every point, the correlation stays at zero point nine nine nine. With zero point six, still zero point nine nine nine. With one point zero, it falls to zero point three four. The noise is now as large as a hop.

All of this is one draw. So we rolled ten more sheets.

With forty neighbours, the correlation always lands between zero point six two and zero point seven three. But with twenty neighbours, safe on our draw, one sheet in ten already broke, down to zero point eight four. And noise of zero point six broke one sheet in ten, down to zero point seven three. The cliff moves from one draw to the next.

The faulty edge behaved the same on all ten: between twenty-nine and thirty-one percent of pairs lose more than ten percent.

Now the distance card. The object: points with no formula. The distance: the shortest chain of hops, over the k nearest neighbours.

What it ignores: how the sheet is rolled. Any bending that does not stretch is invisible to it.

What it costs: the graph of neighbours, then shortest paths between all pairs. For two thousand points, the paths took six tenths of a second on this machine, and the table of distances takes thirty-two megabytes. With four thousand points: two point seven seconds, and one hundred twenty-eight megabytes. Double the points, and the work is about four times larger.

Its trap: the short circuit. Nothing in the result tells you it happened.

A shortest path is a minimum, and a minimum is decided by its best case. One false edge can shorten thousands of pairs.

So never trust a single setting. Redo the calculation with several values of k, and look at whether the distances hold.

You can do it without knowing the truth. Here, from five to twenty-five neighbours, the witnesses stay between sixty-six and sixty. And each table of distances agrees with the previous one, with a correlation above zero point nine nine eight. Then, from twenty-five to thirty neighbours, the agreement falls to zero point eight two, and the witnesses fall to forty-two.

A plateau is the sign you can trust. A cliff is the sign to stop, and to take the last setting before the cliff, or a smaller one.

The ruler through the air said close. The inchworm along the data says far. And it says it truthfully, only if the stones are well chosen.

So the question to carry away is this: if I change the reach a little, does my answer change a little, or does it fall off a cliff?

Next time: Many paths.

PLATE 9.76:55

Many paths

A shortest path trusts one route, so one wrong edge ruins it. Treating the graph as a circuit of one-ohm resistors uses every path: with three paths between two points the resistance is 0.67 though the shortest path stays 2. A wrong edge is then spread out rather than erased, and on a large dense graph the resistance forgets the geometry.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we found a weak spot. A shortest path trusts one single route, and one wrong edge can ruin it.

This time we stop trusting a single route. Two recipes use every path at once. Both are old and simple, and both come with a catch.

The first recipe turns the neighbour graph into an electric circuit. Every edge is a one-ohm resistor. Connect a battery to two points, and measure how hard it is for the current to get through. That number, the effective resistance, is the distance. Klein and Randić proposed it in 1993.

Here are three tiny circuits, illustrative ones. Two points, joined by one path of two edges. The shortest path is 2, and so is the resistance.

Add a second path. The shortest path is still 2. The resistance is now 1.

Add a third path. The shortest path is still 2, and the resistance is 0.67.

The inchworm sees one route. The current uses them all. The more paths between two points, the closer they are. And this is a true distance: all four rules hold.

It also has a second meaning. A random walker who leaves one point, reaches the other, and comes back, needs on average two times the number of edges times the resistance steps. Chandra and colleagues proved it in 1996. In our three circuits that commute time is 8 every time, because the factor changes with the graph. So the distance is the resistance, not the commute time.

The second recipe keeps the walker. Drop a random walker on each of two points, and let each one take t steps. Then compare the two clouds of places where the walkers might be. If the clouds overlap, the points are close. That is the diffusion distance, from Coifman and Lafon in 2006.

We made three choices, and we say them. Every edge weighs one, so there is no kernel width to tune. The normalisation parameter alpha is zero: the plain random walk. And each place is weighted by one over its long-run probability, as in the paper.

The number of steps t is a scale. With few steps, the walker has only seen its neighbourhood. With many, it has seen the whole sheet.

The authors say this distance is robust to perturbations of the data, unlike the geodesic distance. They say it in 2005 and again in 2006, without a figure. So we measured.

The rolled sheet again. 700 points, 8 neighbours each. One wrong edge, between two points on neighbouring turns. And ten random draws, because a single draw once misled us. The true distance is the one along the unrolled sheet.

First, the shortest path. The distance between the two ends of the wrong edge is divided by 6.8, on average. 21 percent of all pairs lose more than 10 percent of their distance. 3 percent lose more than half. And the correlation with the true distance falls from 0.91 to 0.77.

A detail of the measurement. In five of the ten draws, the graph had already jumped from one turn to the next before we added anything. With 700 points, 8 neighbours are sometimes enough to do that.

Take one pair, in one draw. The inchworm's route is 45 long. With the wrong edge, it is 15. Two thirds of the distance are gone.

Now the resistance, for the same pair. It goes from 1.16 to 0.79, a loss of a third. The wrong edge carries only 41 percent of the current. The rest still goes the long way round. The wrong edge is not erased. It is spread out.

Over the ten draws, 22 percent of pairs lose more than 10 percent. That is about the same as the shortest path. But no pair loses half. And the correlation falls from 0.96 to 0.92, not to 0.77.

If each resistor is as large as the edge is long, 10 percent of pairs move by more than 10 percent. The correlation goes from 0.98 to 0.96.

So going from one path to all paths does not remove a wrong edge. It changes the shape of the damage. With one path, a few pairs collapse. With all paths, many pairs move a little. Which one is more robust depends on what you count.

The diffusion distance adds a knob. With 8 steps, no pair moves by more than 10 percent. But the distance hardly follows the sheet: its correlation with the true distance is only 0.45.

With 128 steps, the correlation is 0.92. And 12 percent of pairs move. With 256 steps, 53 percent of pairs move.

Short, it is solid and local. Long, it sees the whole sheet, and the wrong edge too. So say which t you took, and show what the result becomes with another.

What do they cost? The resistance needs the inverse of a matrix as large as the cloud. The diffusion needs only a few eigenvectors of it. We timed both on this machine. With 700 points, each takes under 10 milliseconds. With 3000 points, the inverse takes 0.36 seconds, and ten eigenvectors take 0.027.

The Graph Theory series treats diffusion on a graph in one of its chapters.

Now the trap of the resistance. Von Luxburg, Radl and Hein studied what happens when the graph grows, and the number of neighbours grows with it. The resistance between two points ends up depending only on how many neighbours each has: one over the first degree, plus one over the second. No geometry is left.

We tested it. Two groups of points in ten dimensions, 20 neighbours each, five draws. The correlation between the resistance and that degree formula is 0.32 with 200 points. It is 0.68 with 1000, and 0.94 with 3000.

With 3000 points, the average resistance is 0.0772 inside a group, and 0.0870 between the two groups. The groups have almost disappeared.

On the rolled sheet, the same correlation is 0.08. There, the trap does not bite. The paper says convergence is slow in low dimension.

One caution. No paper we read tests a single wrong edge on a small graph. So our measurement neither confirms nor contradicts the literature. It is ours.

Remember three things. All the paths do not delete a wrong edge, they spread it. The diffusion distance has a knob, so say which one you took. And on a large dense graph, the resistance forgets the geometry.

Is your space flat?

Established results are kept apart from untested suggestions, with one question between them: is the curvature in the data, or in the method? Through an eight-neighbour graph, the rolled sheet's straight-line correlation falls to 0.28, yet a flat strip scores the same quadruplet numbers as the roll, so a low value says tree-like at this scale, not hierarchical.

TRANSCRIPTEN

This chapter kept asking one question. In which space do your data live?

Today is the honest version. What is established, what is only worth trying, and how do you know the answer is not an artefact of your own method?

Start with the established column.

In 2016, Margulies and colleagues ran a diffusion embedding on one hour of resting-state scans from eight hundred and twenty adults. The principal gradient runs from sensory and motor regions to the default-mode network. It orders regions along one axis. That is all it claims.

Brain networks have also been placed in hyperbolic space. Allard and Serrano, in 2020, found that it gives almost perfectly navigable maps of structural connectomes across species. Whi and colleagues, in 2022, embedded resting-state functional connectomes in a hyperbolic disc.

Zhou, Smith and Sharpee, in 2018, described odour space with a three-dimensional hyperbolic space. Zhang and colleagues, in 2023, reported a hyperbolic geometry in the hippocampus of rats running a forty-eight metre track.

Chaudhuri and colleagues, in 2019, recorded head-direction cells in seven mice. Without assuming it, they found the activity sits on a one-dimensional ring. A ring, not a hyperbolic space.

Gallego and colleagues, in 2018, studied the motor cortex of three monkeys. Their subspaces had twelve dimensions, and the leading principal angles between tasks were well below what chance gives.

Now the right column. These are suggestions, tried here only on simulated clouds. None has a validated threshold.

And the question between the columns. Is the curvature in the data, or in the method?

A flat cloud passed through a neighbour graph also returns numbers. So we pass a cloud of known shape through the same chain.

The chain is this. Link each point to its eight nearest neighbours, take shortest paths along the graph, and read numbers off the result. Our test cloud is the rolled sheet of episode 9.6, two thousand points, simulated, seed 98. The control is the same sheet, unrolled flat.

Idea one. Correlate the straight line with the graph distance. On the roll, it is 0.28. The straight line fails. On the flat control, it is 1.00. Close to 1 means the space is flat enough, and the straight line suffices.

So the number separates them. But look at the graph itself. Even on the flat control, graph paths are about 6 percent longer than the truth.

And the number says the straight line fails. It does not say the space is curved. The roll is flat in its own geometry.

Idea two. The quadruplet number of episode 9.4, worst case, then mean. On the roll: 0.21 and 0.03. On the flat strip: 0.21 and 0.03. The same.

A flat strip is almost a line, and a line looks like a tree. A flat square gives 0.48 and 0.08. The noisy tree of episode 9.4 gives 0.21 and 0.04. The flat strip scores just as low.

So a low number says tree-like at this scale. It does not say there is a hierarchy.

Idea three. Change the number of neighbours. With five, eight, twelve and twenty, the roll's graph distances stay above 0.998 correlated with the truth. With forty, they fall to 0.68, and 27 percent of pairs come out more than a fifth too short.

Some edges now jump to the next turn of the roll, and each one short-circuits many paths. The flat control, with forty neighbours, stays at 1.00. The drop comes from the roll, at that scale, and not from the chain alone.

Idea four. Compare two subspaces by their principal angles. Pick a direction in the first, find the closest direction in the second, and measure the angle. Then repeat, at right angles to the first pair. Read it against two floors. Simulated: twenty channels, a three-dimensional subspace, and a second condition where one axis is tilted by thirty degrees.

Two halves of the same condition: the largest angle is 7 degrees. Two random subspaces: 85. The two conditions: 31.

Without both floors, you could not read the 31. The low floor says how much two measurements of the same thing differ. The high floor says how far apart unrelated subspaces are.

Idea five. Place a hierarchy in a hyperbolic disc, but only after the quadruplet test. The tree of 127 nodes gives a low number, but so does the flat strip. So read that test against a cloud whose shape you know, and say which of the two numbers you report.

So the screen has two columns. On the left, what has been shown. On the right, what is worth trying. Between them, one question. Is the curvature in the data, or in the method?

In which space do your data live? It is a question you can measure, with circles, triangles, quadruplets and paths.

The answer depends on the scale at which you look. So give that scale with the answer.
