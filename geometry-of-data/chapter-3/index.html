What two sets share

Two short texts become sets of five and six words, with four shared among seven in the union: a Jaccard index of four sevenths, distance three sevenths. Jaccard is a true distance, but one minus Dice breaks the triangle rule even though both rank pairs identically. The trap: ten elements inside a set of one thousand give only zero point zero one, though fully contained.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the typesetter's shop. Today, the objects on the bench are sets.

A set is a bag of things with no order and no repeats. The words of a text. The electrodes that are active. The voxels of a region somebody drew by hand.

Take two short texts. The first one says: the cat sat on the mat. The second says: the cat ate the fish on the mat.

Turn each text into a set. The order goes. The repeats go. The first text becomes five words: cat, mat, on, sat, the. The second becomes six: ate, cat, fish, mat, on, the.

Draw each set as a circle, and let the circles overlap. Four words sit in the overlap: cat, mat, on, and the. Three words sit outside it: sat, ate, and fish.

Now count. Four words are shared. Seven words belong to at least one of the two sets. That is the union.

Shared, divided by the union: four sevenths. About zero point five seven. This is the Jaccard index. Paul Jaccard, a botanist, used it in nineteen oh one to compare which plants grow in which parts of the Alps.

An index is a similarity. One means identical. Zero means nothing in common. To get a distance, subtract it from one. Three sevenths, about zero point four three. That is the Jaccard distance.

Does it deserve the name? Check the four lights. It is never negative. It is zero only when the two sets are the same. It is the same in both directions. And there is no shortcut: going through a third set never beats going direct. Levandowsky and Winter proved it in nineteen seventy-one, and Kosub gave a short proof in twenty nineteen. All four lights are on.

The shortcut rule holds, but only just. Take three sets. The set with only a. The set with only b. And the set with a and b together. Direct from the first to the second: one. From the first to the pair: one half. From the second to the pair: one half. Going around costs one half plus one half, which is exactly one. The detour ties with the direct road. It never wins.

There is a close cousin. Count the shared part twice, and divide by the two sizes added up. This is the Dice coefficient, published by Dice in nineteen forty-five and by Sorensen in nineteen forty-eight. Same idea, a different weight on the overlap.

Subtract it from one, and look at the same three sets. From the first to the pair: one third. From the second to the pair: one third. From the first to the second: still one. Around the corner now costs only two thirds. The detour is shorter than the direct road. The shortcut rule falls. One minus Dice is not a distance.

Here is the odd part. Dice and Jaccard always put pairs in the same order. Dice equals two J over one plus J, and that function only goes up. In four hundred random pairs, the order never differed. Same ranking, different rules. To rank neighbours, either one will do. To use the triangle rule, as the k-means saving of episode one point two did, only one of them is safe.

What does Jaccard ignore? Let's look at a row of changes. A set has no time and no scale, so the row is not the one for signals. Take five elements. Rename every one of them: the distance to the original is one, because nothing is left in common. List each element twice: zero. Add one element: one sixth. Reorder them: zero. Swap one element for another: one third.

Notice what the row says. Order and repeats are invisible. And if you rename both sets in the same way, the distance between them does not move. Only the overlap counts.

One more thing it ignores: everything absent from both sets. Add a thousand words that neither text uses, and the distance stays three sevenths. Absence is not agreement.

What does it cost? One pass over both sets. If each set is kept sorted, or in a hash table, the time grows with the sizes of the two sets. That is cheap for one pair. It is too slow for millions of documents, where every pair would need its own pass.

In nineteen ninety-seven, Andrei Broder found a way around it. Shuffle the whole universe of elements at random, with the same shuffle for both sets. Then look at which element comes first in each set. The two firsts are the same element exactly when the very first element of the union is a shared one. So the chance of a match is the Jaccard index itself.

Repeat with many shuffles and count the matches. In my simulation, two sets of six hundred elements shared three hundred. The true index is one third. Sixteen shuffles gave zero point five zero. One hundred twenty-eight gave zero point four one. A thousand twenty-four gave zero point three five. Each document keeps only a short list of first elements.

Now, the trap. A set of ten elements sits entirely inside a set of one thousand. The Jaccard index is ten over one thousand: zero point zero one. Nearly nothing in common, says the number, though the small set is completely inside the big one.

Jaccard answers a precise question: what share of everything we hold between us do we hold in common? It does not answer: is one of us inside the other? For that question, divide the shared part by the size of the small set. Ten over ten is one. Broder defined both measures, and they are different questions.

One last point, for your own data. A set often comes from a threshold. An electrode is called active when its power passes a level. I simulated twenty-four channels in two conditions. At level zero point five, fifteen channels are active in at least one condition, seven in both: an index of zero point four seven. At level one: zero point three eight. At level one and a half: zero point four zero. At level two, the second condition has no active channel at all, and the index is zero.

The threshold decided the distance. So the threshold needs a justification, just like the distance does. The same is true of a region drawn by hand: where the pen goes decides what is in the set.

Let's recap. The object is a set. The distance is one minus the shared part over the union. It ignores order, repeats, and everything absent from both sets. It costs one pass over both sets, or a few random shuffles. Its trap is a small set inside a big one.

Counting the differences

Eight cells of zeros and ones, two strips laid over each other: the Hamming distance counts the lamps where they disagree, four here. On a cube of three-cell words, two codewords three edges apart let one flipped cell be corrected, cutting errors from about ten in a hundred to about three. The trap: an alternating word and its one-cell slide sit at the maximum, eight.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time the objects were sets: no order, no repeats. This time the objects are words of a fixed length.

Eight cells, and every cell holds a zero or a one.

Think of a strip of punched tape. At each position there is either a hole, or no hole. Eight positions make one word.

It could also be eight electrodes, each one active or not. A word like that is a set written as a row of switches.

Now take two strips, and lay one over the other.

Look at the positions one by one. Where both strips agree, nothing happens. Where they disagree, a lamp lights.

Count the lamps. Here there are four.

That count is the Hamming distance: the number of cells that differ. There are no weights, no exponent to choose, no scale. It is a plain count of disagreements.

For two sets written as switches, it counts the elements that belong to exactly one of them. Here too, the answer is four.

Richard Hamming defined it in nineteen fifty, in the Bell System Technical Journal.

He worked at Bell Labs, on a relay computer that ran alone over the weekend. When it met one wrong bit, it dropped the job. Hamming wanted a machine that could find the wrong bit, and put it right.

To think about that, he needed a picture.

Take words of three cells. There are exactly eight of them. Put each one on a corner of a cube. Two words that differ in one cell are joined by an edge.

So the distance between two words is the number of edges you walk to get from one to the other.

From zero, zero, zero to one, one, one takes three edges. Every shortest route takes three.

If this feels familiar, it should. On the chessboard of episode two point two, city blocks counted steps along the streets. Hamming's distance is that same Manhattan distance, on coordinates that can only be zero or one.

Now comes the idea that made it famous.

Allow only two words: zero, zero, zero, and one, one, one. They are three edges apart.

Send one of them down a noisy wire. The noise flips one cell. Say zero, zero, zero arrives as zero, zero, one.

Which allowed word is it closest to? One edge from the first. Two edges from the second. So you answer: it was zero, zero, zero. And you are right. The error is corrected.

That works for any single flip, because the two allowed words are at least three apart. A word with one wrong cell stays closer to the right word than to any other.

With two flips, the guess goes wrong. That is the price of a distance of three: it fixes one error, and no more.

We can measure it. In a simulation with seed thirty-two, each cell is flipped one time in ten. Sending the bit alone, it arrives wrong about ten times in a hundred. Sent as three cells and decoded this way, it arrives wrong about three times in a hundred.

Now the four lights.

Never negative. Zero only for a word and itself. The same in both directions.

And no shortcut. A cell that differs between the first word and the last must differ from the middle word on one side or the other. So a detour never costs less than the direct way.

All four lights are on. Hamming said it himself, in his paper: a distance, or, as it is usually called, a metric.

We also checked it by brute force. There are two hundred and fifty-six words of eight cells, which makes more than sixteen million triples. No triple breaks the rule.

What does it ignore?

First, where the differences are. One cell wrong at the very start, or one cell wrong at the very end: both are at distance one.

Second, between which symbols. Take a strand of letters. Change an A into a C, into a G, or into a T. Each costs exactly one. Hamming asks only whether two cells differ. Never by how much, and never into what.

Here is the row of changes for a word.

Slide it along by one. Flip every cell. Swap two neighbours. Shuffle the cells. Flip a single cell.

This distance sees all of them. It reports six, eight, two, two, and one.

Unlike the distances of the last chapter, nothing here stays at zero. What it cannot tell you is the kind of change. A distance of two could be a swap, or two flips.

What does it cost? One comparison per cell. Twice as long a word, twice the work. Computers do it with one exclusive-or and a count of the ones, so scanning millions of words is routine.

Now the trap.

Here is a word that alternates: one, zero, one, zero, all the way along. And here is the word that alternates the other way, starting with zero.

Compare them cell by cell. Every single cell differs. The distance is eight. The largest it can be.

Yet look at them. The second word is the first, slid along by one cell. Remove the first symbol, add one at the end, and you have it. Two small edits.

Hamming says these words are as far apart as two words of eight cells can be.

Why? Because Hamming compares cell by cell. It assumes that cell five of one word answers to cell five of the other.

For flips, that is true. A flipped cell stays where it was.

But when a symbol is lost, or a symbol is inserted, everything after it slides. Every cell is now compared with the wrong partner.

For that, we need a distance that looks for the correspondence by itself.

So remember this. Hamming counts the cells that differ, and trusts the cells to line up.

The fewest edits

Turning kitten into sitting takes three repairs: replace k with s, replace e with i, insert g. A table filled cell by cell, taking the cheapest of three neighbours, finds the Levenshtein distance and the matching itself, at one cell per pair of symbols. Adding a swap repair broke the triangle rule once in twenty thousand triples, and free repairs hide changes.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the typesetter's shop. Today the objects on the bench are sequences of symbols, and they can have any length.

A word is one. A strand of DNA is one. So is a night of sleep, written as one letter per stage, or the list of rooms a person walked through.

Last time, Hamming compared two words box by box. That works only when box five of one word means box five of the other. Lose one symbol, and everything slides. We need a distance that finds the matching by itself.

Here is the idea. Take two words, kitten and sitting. Count the fewest small repairs that turn the first into the second.

Three repairs are allowed. Insert a symbol. Delete a symbol. Replace one symbol by another. Each one costs one.

Kitten becomes sitten: replace the k with an s. Sitten becomes sittin: replace the e with an i. Sittin becomes sitting: insert a g at the end. Three repairs.

Could two be enough? No. And in a moment, the table will show why.

This number is the edit distance. Vladimir Levenshtein defined it in nineteen sixty-five, in a paper about codes that survive lost and inserted symbols. So it is also called the Levenshtein distance.

Our old friends from episode one come back. Cat and car: one replacement, distance one. Cat and kitten: replace the c, replace the a, then insert three symbols. Distance five.

Now, how do we find the minimum without trying every route? We fill a table.

Write the first word down the side, one row per symbol. Write the second word across the top, one column per symbol. Add an empty symbol at the start of each.

Each cell answers a small question. How many repairs does it take to turn the first few symbols of the first word into the first few symbols of the second?

The first row is easy. To turn nothing into three symbols, insert three times. Zero, one, two, three. The first column is the same, with deletions.

Now take any other cell. Three neighbours are already filled in: the one above, the one to the left, and the one on the diagonal.

Coming from above means a deletion: take the cell above, and add one. Coming from the left means an insertion: take the cell on the left, and add one. Coming from the diagonal means a match or a replacement: take the diagonal cell, and add nothing if the two symbols are the same, or one if they are different. Keep the smallest.

That is the whole rule. A snail can follow it. One cell at a time, row after row, from the top-left corner.

Watch the numbers grow, and watch them stay small along the diagonal, where the two words agree. When the snail reaches the bottom-right corner, it finds a three. The fewest repairs between kitten and sitting is three. Nothing cheaper exists, because every cell holds the best answer for its own small question.

But the table holds more than a number. Walk back from the corner, always toward the neighbour that gave the minimum. The path you draw is the matching.

A diagonal step pairs two symbols. A step down means a symbol of the first word was deleted. A step right means a symbol was inserted.

Here, the path pairs k with s, then i with i, t with t, t with t, e with i, n with n, and ends with an insertion of g. The distance found the correspondence by itself, the one Hamming had to be told.

Robert Wagner and Michael Fischer published this table in nineteen seventy-four. The same idea was found several times, in several fields. In nineteen seventy, Saul Needleman and Christian Wunsch used it to line up the amino acids of two proteins.

Now the four lights. Never negative: it counts repairs. Zero only for identical words: with no repair at all, nothing changed. The same in both directions: an insertion read backwards is a deletion, and a replacement undoes itself. And no shortcut: repair the first word into a second, then that second into a third, and you have built a route from the first to the third. So the cheapest route can never be longer. All four lights are on.

What does it ignore? Look at a row of changes, with kitten as the original.

Rename every letter, and the distance is six. Write every letter twice, and it is six again. Slide the word along by one, dropping the first letter and adding one at the end, and the distance is only two. Shuffle the letters: five. Swap a single letter: one.

Hamming would call that slide a catastrophe. Here it costs two. Every change is seen. It does not care which symbols are involved: replacing a k by an s costs the same as replacing it by a z. What else it ignores is up to us, and that comes at the end.

What does it cost? One cell for every pair of symbols. Two words of ten symbols: a hundred cells. Two sequences of a thousand symbols: a million cells. Double the lengths, and the work is multiplied by four. If you only want the number, you can keep just two rows of the table at a time. But the work stays the same.

Can we do much better? In nineteen eighty, Masek and Paterson shaved off a logarithmic factor, and that is all. In twenty fifteen, Backurs and Indyk showed that a really faster method, one that beats the square by a power of the length, would break a famous conjecture of computer science, the strong exponential time hypothesis. So we have a serious reason to think that it is impossible.

Now the trap. The three repairs, and their prices, are a choice.

Fred Damerau noticed in nineteen sixty-four that most typing slips are one of four kinds. A wrong letter. A missing letter. An extra letter. Or two neighbouring letters swapped. So add a fourth repair, swapping two neighbours, for the price of one.

In the version that is quickest to compute, called optimal string alignment, no part of the word may be repaired twice. Take three words: C, A. Then A, C. Then A, B, C.

From C, A to A, C: one swap. From A, C to A, B, C: one insertion. Going through the middle costs two. But from C, A straight to A, B, C, the cheapest route costs three. The detour is shorter than the direct road. The shortcut rule falls.

How often? Rarely. On twenty thousand random triples of short words over three letters, simulated, seed thirty-three, the plain edit distance never broke the rule. The swap version broke it once.

Rare is not never. One breaking triple is enough to lose the light.

And prices can do more. Say a replacement between two vowels costs nothing. Then kitten and kattan are at distance zero, because the difference is free. Compare them with the plain prices: two. The distance no longer sees the change. But now two different words are at distance zero, and the second light goes out.

An edit distance is defined by a list of allowed repairs, and a price for each. Change the list, and you change the distance, and sometimes its rules. What it ignores can be read in the prices: a free repair is a change the distance cannot see.

PLATE 3.46:21

Two rankings

Two judges rank four films, Pearl, Quill, Rook and Sage, and the Kendall distance counts opposite pairs: four of six, equal to four neighbour swaps. The twenty-four rankings form a solid with thirty-six edges. Spearman's footrule stays between one and two times that. Only order counts, so swapping the top two or the last two both cost one, whatever the scores behind them.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two judges watch the same four films. Each one puts them in order, best first. Do the two judges agree?

So far in this chapter we compared sets, words and strings. Now the object is an order. A ranking says only who comes before whom. No numbers, just places.

Our four films are Pearl, Quill, Rook and Sage. The first judge says: Pearl, Quill, Rook, Sage. The second judge says: Quill, Sage, Rook, Pearl. How far apart are these two lists?

Maurice Kendall gave a way to count the disagreement in 1938. Take every pair of films. Four films make six pairs. For each pair, ask both judges which film they put first. If they give opposite answers, count one.

Pearl and Quill: opposite. Pearl and Rook: opposite. Pearl and Sage: opposite. Rook and Sage: opposite. The other two pairs agree. The Kendall distance is four.

There is a second way to get the same number. Start from the first judge's list and swap two neighbours at a time, until you reach the second judge's list. Pearl and Quill swap. Pearl and Rook swap. Pearl and Sage swap. Then Rook and Sage swap. Four swaps, and no shorter way exists. The number of opposite pairs is the number of neighbour swaps.

Now picture every possible ranking of four films. There are twenty-four of them. Make each ranking a corner. Join two corners when a single neighbour swap turns one ranking into the other. You get a solid with twenty-four corners and thirty-six edges: an octahedron with its corners cut off. From every corner, three edges leave: one for each pair of neighbours in the list.

Walking along the edges is just swapping neighbours. So the Kendall distance is the length of the shortest walk. Our two judges sit at two corners, four edges apart. The farthest corners are six edges apart: a ranking and its exact reverse. Six is the largest distance for four films. For ten films it is forty-five, one for every pair.

Count the corners by their distance from the first judge's list. One corner at distance zero: the list itself. Three at distance one. Five at distance two. Six at distance three. Then five, three, and one. Twenty-four in all. Most rankings sit in the middle, and only one is as far away as it can be.

Another distance is older. Charles Spearman described it in 1906, and called it the footrule. For each film, count how many places it moved between the two lists. Then add. This footrule asks for no pairs at all, only for how far each film travelled. Pearl moved three places, Quill one, Rook none, Sage two. The footrule is six.

The two distances never drift far apart. In 1977, Persi Diaconis and Ronald Graham showed that the footrule is never smaller than the Kendall distance, and never more than twice as big. We checked every ranking of up to seven films. Every one sits between the two lines.

So here is the distance card. The object is a ranking. The distance counts the pairs the two rankings put in opposite order. It is a true distance, so all four lights are on.

What does it ignore? The values. Say each judge first gave scores, and the ranking came from the scores. Add ten to every score. The order is the same, and the distance is zero. Multiply every score by a hundred. Zero. Take the logarithm of every score, which squeezes the big ones. Zero again.

Any change that keeps the order is invisible. Swap two neighbours, and the distance is one. Swap the first film with the last, and it is five. The distance sees only the order, and nothing else.

What does it cost? Counting by looking at every pair is easy to write. For a thousand films, that is four hundred ninety-nine thousand, five hundred pairs. In 1966, William Knight showed how to count the opposite pairs much faster, the way a sorting program works, without ever looking at all the pairs.

For scale, take two judges who rank ten films by pure chance. We simulated ten thousand such pairs of judges, with seed thirty-four. They disagreed on twenty-two point four pairs out of forty-five, on average. Chance sits at about one half of the maximum. Two judges who agree much better than that have something in common.

Now the trap. Swapping the first two films costs one. Swapping the last two films also costs one. Look at the scores behind these rankings. The top film has nine point nine, the second five point zero. The last three are almost tied: five, four point nine, four point eight. Swapping the top two moves almost five points of score. Swapping the bottom two moves a tenth of a point. Kendall counts both as one step.

Two more traps. Ties break the plain count, because a pair is no longer clearly in one order or the other. And if each judge lists only a top ten, the two lists no longer hold the same films. Three researchers at IBM built versions of these distances for top lists, in 2003. You need to pick the adapted version on purpose.

One more question. What is the average ranking of several judges? A natural answer is the ranking closest to all of them. John Kemeny proposed this idea. But finding it is hard. Cynthia Dwork and her colleagues proved in 2001 that it is hard even for four judges. For ten films there are three million, six hundred twenty-eight thousand, eight hundred candidates. For twenty films there are about two point four quintillion.

Here is the point of the episode. A distance between rankings sees only the order. When you do not trust the scale of your values, that is a strength. It is why, in chapter twelve, we will compare two tables of distances by their ranks, as Nili and colleagues recommended in 2014. But when the gap between first and second matters more than the gap between the last two, it is a loss.

Remember: a ranking is compared by its pairs. One swap of neighbours costs one, whatever the scores behind it. Only the order counts.

The worst point

Two outlines of twenty-four and thirty-two clicked points have no pairing. The worst nearest-neighbour distance is 8.8 millimetres one way and 9.3 the other, so keeping the larger gives the Hausdorff distance, a true distance. One stray point jumps it from 9.3 to 108. A ninety-fifth percentile version gives 9.0, while an averaged version loses the triangle rule.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two people trace the same outline on a screen. The first clicks twenty-four points along it. The second clicks thirty-two. Nobody tells you which point of the first goes with which point of the second.

This is the object of this episode: a set of points in space. Two outlines drawn by two people. Two positions of the same sensors, measured on two different days. The sets do not have the same number of points, and no point has a partner. So how far apart are two such sets?

Start small. Take one point of the first outline, and look for its nearest neighbour in the second outline. That gives one distance: how far this point is from the other set.

Now do it for every point of the first outline. You get twenty-four distances. Most are small. Keep only the worst one. On our outlines, the worst point is eight point eight millimetres from the other outline.

Here is a way to picture it. Grow a halo of ink around every point of the second outline. A point of the first outline gets wet when a halo reaches it. The radius at which the very last point gets wet is the worst distance. Eight point eight.

That number is a promise. Every point of the first outline lies within eight point eight millimetres of some point of the second.

But look the other way. Grow halos around the first outline, and watch the second one get wet. The last point is wet at nine point three. It is not the same number.

A tiny example makes the asymmetry plain. On a line, the first set has the points zero and one. The second has zero, one and five. From the first to the second, every point already has its twin: zero. From the second to the first, the point five is four steps from its nearest neighbour: four. One direction says zero. The other says four.

Check this one-way number against the four rules. It is never negative. It is not symmetric. It can be zero for two different sets, since the first set sits inside the second. And the triangle rule holds. Two lights on, two lights off. Not a distance yet.

The repair is the one you may have guessed. Keep the larger of the two. This is the Hausdorff distance. For our outlines: nine point three millimetres. For the line example: four.

Now the four lights all come on. Never negative. Zero only for the same set. The same both ways, by construction. And the triangle rule holds. We also checked it by computation on twenty thousand random triples of small sets: it never broke.

This repairs the symmetry problem from episode 1.3. The history is short. Felix Hausdorff gave this distance in his 1914 book on set theory, and he credited Dimitrie Pompeiu, who had defined a very close distance in his doctoral thesis in 1905. Pompeiu is why some people say Pompeiu and Hausdorff.

What does it ignore? Let us run the row of changes. Shift the whole outline by twelve millimetres: it sees that, twelve exactly. Scale it up by thirty percent: nineteen. Now add twenty-four points on the same outline, in the gaps between the old ones. The distance moves by thirteen: the size of the biggest hole in the old sampling.

So it does not count points. It sees where they fall.

List the very same points in a shuffled order: exactly zero. The order is invisible. And it never builds a pairing. Each point simply looks for its own nearest neighbour.

Last, add one stray point, far away. A hundred and eight millimetres. We will come back to that one.

What does it cost? Here is the table of distances again, with one row for each point of the first set and one column for each point of the second. Take the smallest cell of each row, and keep the largest of those. Take the smallest cell of each column, and keep the largest of those. The larger of the two is the Hausdorff distance.

So at worst, every pair of points is looked at. Twenty-four by thirty-two is seven hundred and sixty-eight cells. Two sets of five thousand points make twenty-five million. In practice, a search tree skips most of them. On this machine, in two dimensions: about seventeen thousandths of a second by brute force, and two thousandths of a second with a tree. Same answer. In many dimensions the tree loses its advantage, the same trap as in episode 2.6.

Now the trap. One stray point, a slip of the mouse, a hundred and fifty millimetres to the right of the outline. The Hausdorff distance jumps from nine point three to a hundred and eight. One point decides everything. Just like the five in the line example.

There are two common repairs. The first replaces the worst point by a rank. Instead of the largest nearest-neighbour distance, keep the ninety-fifth percentile: ignore the worst five percent. Huttenlocher, Klanderman and Rucklidge proposed a rank version in 1993, and medical imaging often reports it as the ninety-fifth percentile Hausdorff distance. With the stray point, it reads nine point zero. The outlier is gone.

The second repair replaces the worst point by an average. Jain and a colleague proposed it in 1994. With the stray point, the average goes from five point three to eight point four. It moves, but it does not explode.

Both repairs have a price. The average version is no longer a distance. On a line, take the sets zero; zero and two; two and four. From the first to the second: one. From the second to the third: one. From the first to the third: three. Three is more than one plus one. The triangle rule falls. We checked it by computation. For the rank version, we did not check, so be careful before you treat it as a distance.

So here is the point of the episode. The Hausdorff distance is a worst-case distance. It guarantees that no point of one set is far from the other set. And for that very reason, it hears only the worst point. Between the worst case and the average, you choose what you want to guarantee.

Three later episodes carry this idea further: with an order of travel in episode 4.4, with mass to move in chapter 6, and with no common frame in chapter 12.

To remember: the object is a set of points. The distance is the worst nearest-neighbour distance, both ways, keeping the larger. It ignores the number of points, their order, and any pairing. It costs every pair, at worst. Its trap is one stray point.

Your data as symbols

Established facts are kept apart from ideas to try. Jaccard is a distance and one minus Dice is not. The sixteen-word Hamming code corrected all one hundred twelve single flips. For a forty-epoch night of sleep, the price list decides the neighbour: the late copy is nearest at four, but pricing N two against N three at one half makes the deeper copy nearest.

Welcome back to the typesetter's shop. This is the last episode of the chapter, so it is about ideas.

Two lists will stand side by side, and they never mix. On the left, what is established: a proof, a calculation, a published result. On the right, what is worth trying: suggestions, not findings.

Start with what is established. First, sets.

Take the set with only a, the set with only b, and the set with a and b together. The Jaccard distances are one, one half, and one half. Going around costs one half plus one half, which ties with the direct road. Levandowsky and Winter showed in nineteen seventy-one that this never fails, and Kosub gave a short proof in twenty nineteen. Jaccard is a distance.

Now one minus Dice, on the same three sets. One third, one third, and one. Going around costs two thirds. That is shorter than the direct road. One minus Dice is not a distance.

Second, words of fixed length. Hamming published his error-correcting codes in nineteen fifty. Take the sixteen codewords of the seven-bit Hamming code. The smallest distance between any two of them is three. So when one bit flips, the received word is still closer to the right codeword than to any other. I checked every single flip: sixteen words, seven positions, one hundred twelve cases. All one hundred twelve are corrected.

Third, sequences, and their price list. Since nineteen eighty-six, when Abbott and Forrest introduced it, social scientists have compared life courses written as strings of states with an edit distance. But an edit distance is only as good as its list of allowed retouches. Allow the swap of two neighbours, in its restricted version, and look at three strings: Y X, X Y, and X Z Y. From Y X to X Y costs one. From X Y to X Z Y costs one. But from Y X to X Z Y costs three. Two is less than three, so the shortcut rule falls. Change the list, and you change the distance.

Fourth, rankings. Spearman's footrule adds up how many places each item moved. It always sits between the Kendall distance and twice that distance. Diaconis and Graham proved it in nineteen seventy-seven. I checked it on all five hundred seventy-six pairs of rankings of four films, and it never failed. Behind this sits a fact worth remembering: a ranking knows only order.

Fifth, comparing two tables of distances. A 2014 toolbox paper on representational similarity analysis, in neuroimaging, recommends a rank correlation. Its reason is that we should not assume a straight-line link between two tables. Here, the second table is the first one raised to the fourth power. Same order, another scale. The ordinary correlation is zero point nine seven. The rank correlation is exactly one. When one table predicts ties, the paper recommends Kendall's tau-a instead.

That is what is established. Now the right-hand column. These are suggestions. I have not run them on your data.

Idea one. Turn a recording into a string of states. Sleep stages, one letter for each thirty-second epoch. Microstates. Behaviours scored frame by frame. Then compare two nights, or two people, with an edit distance, and choose the prices yourself. In two quick searches, I found no published edit distance on sleep stages or on microstates. I did find dynamic time warping used to cluster hypnograms, so a neighbouring idea exists.

Here is a toy night: forty epochs, drawn by hand, so it is illustrative. Take three altered copies. The first starts two epochs late. Epoch by epoch, Hamming counts sixteen differences. The edit distance counts four: two epochs added, two removed. In the second copy, five epochs of N two are replaced by N three. In the third, three epochs trade places between wake and R E M.

Give every substitution the same price, one. The nearest copy is the third, at three. Then the late one, at four. Then the deeper one, at five.

Now say that N two and N three are neighbours in depth, so a swap between them costs one half. The order flips. The deeper copy becomes the nearest, at two and a half. Same nights, two price lists, two different neighbours. Neither list is right. But you must be able to say why N two to N three costs less than wake to R E M.

Idea two. Sets that come from a threshold. Thirty-two channels, two conditions, simulated with seed thirty-six. A channel is active when its power passes a level. At level zero, the Jaccard distance between the two active sets is zero point two nine. At zero point five, zero point five six. At one, zero point seven three. At one point two, zero point nine two. At one point nine, one, because the first condition has no active channel left. One threshold gives one number. So sweep the threshold, and look at the whole curve.

Idea three. Rank the channels by power in each condition, and count the pairs that end up in opposite order. With eight channels, the most it can be is twenty-eight. Here it is nine. Take the logarithm of every power: still nine, because the order has not moved. The ordinary correlation, in the meantime, goes from zero point five nine to zero point seven seven. The footrule is fourteen, between nine and eighteen, as it must be.

Now the climax. Turning a signal into symbols throws information away. That is true of every set of symbols you will ever pick. But it is a choice of what to ignore, like every other choice in this chapter. What you must be able to say is which retouch costs how much, and why. Write the price list down before you look at the result.

Now the two lists, side by side. On the left, five things that are established. On the right, three things to try. Left: Jaccard against Dice, Hamming's distance of three, the price list of an edit distance, the footrule bound, and ranks for comparing tables. Right: an edit distance on states, a threshold sweep, and Kendall on channel rankings. Keep them apart. A habit is not a finding.

Chapter three began with two short texts, and it ends with a night of sleep. Sets, words, strings, rankings, points. Each one came with its own answer to what counts as a difference.
