Blood as a contrast agent · BOLD

Deoxygenated hemoglobin is paramagnetic and shortens T2 star, so it darkens the image. When a region works, blood flow rises far more than oxygen use, washing deoxyhemoglobin away, and the signal climbs by a few percent, like watering the whole garden because one flower is thirsty. BOLD reflects flow, volume and oxygen use together, so it is indirect and relative.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 2. We now know how the scanner makes T2 star weighted images. But what, inside the brain, changes them from one moment to the next? The answer flows through every vessel in your head. Blood.

Red blood cells carry oxygen bound to hemoglobin, a protein with iron at its heart. In 1936, Linus Pauling and Charles Coryell measured its magnetism. With oxygen attached, hemoglobin is diamagnetic. A magnetic field gently pushes it away. Without oxygen, deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic. It is pulled in like a tiny magnet. Almost a century earlier, Michael Faraday had tested dried blood and noted that he should try fresh blood. He apparently never did.

Why does this matter for us? Each deoxyhemoglobin molecule bends the magnetic field around its vessel. Water protons nearby feel slightly different fields, so they precess at slightly different speeds and fall out of step faster. In other words, more deoxyhemoglobin means a shorter T2 star and a darker voxel. Deoxyhemoglobin is a natural contrast agent already flowing in your veins. No injection needed.

Now a region becomes active. Its neurons work harder and burn more oxygen. So you might expect more deoxyhemoglobin and a darker voxel. But something else happens. The local arteries widen, and blood flow increases a lot. Far more fresh blood arrives than the extra oxygen requires.

In 1986, Peter Fox and Marcus Raichle measured both with positron emission tomography. During touch stimulation, blood flow rose by about 29%, while oxygen use rose by only about 5%. Supply wins by far over demand.

So the fresh, oxygen-rich blood washes deoxyhemoglobin out of the capillaries and the veins downstream. Less deoxyhemoglobin means fewer bumps in the field, a longer T2 star, and a slightly brighter voxel. The signal goes up. That is the positive, BOLD response. It is a little like watering the whole garden, just because one flower is thirsty.

How big is the change? Small. At 3 Tesla, a typical task changes the signal by a few percent at most, and often by about 1% in the cortex. That is far smaller than the differences between tissues or the drifts of the scanner. This is why we repeat trials many times, average them, and lean on statistics.

Keep in mind that BOLD is not one single thing. It depends on blood flow, on blood volume, and on oxygen use all at the same time. More flow pushes the signal up. More blood volume and more oxygen use push it down. What we measure is the balance of the three. This is why BOLD is an indirect and relative measure, with no absolute unit. We always compare a task against rest or one condition against another.

If you watched our fNIRS series, this will sound familiar. It is the same physiology, oxygenated hemoglobin rising, deoxygenated hemoglobin falling. fNIRS sees it with light through color. MRI sees it through magnetism, and mostly through the deoxygenated form alone.

So to sum up, deoxyhemoglobin is magnetic and darkens the T2 star image. Activity brings far more fresh blood than it needs, so deoxyhemoglobin is washed away and the signal rises by a few percent. That rise is BOLD, indirect, relative, and slow. How slow exactly? That is our next episode, the hemodynamic response function.

PLATE 2.23:37

The hemodynamic response function

The hemodynamic response function is the slow shape of the BOLD signal after a brief burst of activity. It barely moves for a second or two, peaks around five to six seconds, undershoots below baseline and returns after twenty seconds or more. Responses roughly add up, which allows linear modeling, but the shape varies across regions, people and age, so models add flexibility.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we saw that activity brings fresh blood and that the BOLD signal rises. But not right away. The blood takes its time. Today we draw the exact shape of that delay. It is called the hemodynamic response function or HRF and it shapes every analysis you will ever run.

Picture a lock on our canal. You open the sluice gate for just a moment, a quick flick, and the water level in the lock starts to rise. Not instantly. Slowly. That is what happens to the BOLD signal after a brief burst of neural activity, lasting only a fraction of a second.

In the first second or two, almost nothing happens. Some studies report a tiny initial dip, a brief drop before the rise, but it is small, hard to see, and still debated. Then the signal climbs and reaches its peak about five to six seconds after the event. It then falls back and usually sinks a little below baseline for a while. This is called the post-stimulus undershoot. Only after 20 seconds or more is everything calm again.

So a neural event that lasts a fraction of a second is smeared over 20 seconds of signal. The BOLD signal is a slow, blurry echo of brain activity. If two events happen half a second apart, BOLD alone cannot tell you which came first with much confidence.

Now the good news. To a good approximation, responses add up. Two events close together give two overlapping humps, and the signal is roughly their sum. A long block of activity gives a plateau, which is simply the sum of many overlapping responses. In 1996, Jeffrey Boynton and colleagues tested this linear model in the human visual cortex, and found that it held up well. It is called the linear time-invariant model, and the whole general linear model in Chapter 5 stands on it.

But it is only an approximation. When events come very close together, a second or two apart, the second response is often a little smaller than expected. Very short or very intense stimuli can bend the rule too. Keep this in mind when you design fast event-related experiments.

And the shape is not the same everywhere. It varies between brain regions, between people, and with age. In 2004, Daniel Handwerker and colleagues measured responses in several regions in 20 people. They found more variation across people than across regions, and they showed that getting the timing wrong by about a second could already bias the estimates.

So analysts give their models some flexibility. The standard choice is a canonical HRF, the classic shape with its peak and its undershoot. Add its temporal derivative and the model can slide the peak a little earlier or later. Add a dispersion derivative and the model can make it a little wider or narrower. Or drop the fixed shape altogether and estimate the response point by point with a finite impulse response model, a row of small boxes, one for each time step. More flexibility fits the data better but it costs statistical power and it makes results harder to summarize.

So to sum up, the HRF is slow, a peak around 5 to 6 seconds and undershoot and a return to baseline after 20 seconds or more. Responses roughly add up, which lets us model them with linear tools. But the shape varies across people and regions, so a little flexibility in the model is wise. Next time we ask a deeper question, what does BOLD actually measure?

PLATE 2.33:34

What does BOLD actually measure?

BOLD behaves like the water bill of a neighborhood, following the energy cost of synaptic input and local processing more closely than the spikes a region sends out, as simultaneous recordings and scans in monkeys showed. It cannot separate excitation from inhibition, and draining veins smear it in space. It is not a spike counter or a direct readout of thought.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The BOLD signal rises when a region gets busy. But busy doing what? Firing spikes? Receiving messages? Calming its neighbors down? Today, we open the black box between neurons and the signal. And here is a helpful picture. Think of BOLD as the water bill of a neighborhood.

The brain makes up only about 2% of your body mass, yet it uses around 20% of your energy at rest. Most of that energy pays for signaling, and a large share of it is spent at synapses, pumping ions back after messages arrive. So the blood supply follows the cost of synaptic work, rather than a simple count of spikes sent out.

In 2001, Nikos Logothetis and his colleagues managed something very hard. They recorded neurons with fine electrodes inside the visual cortex of monkeys, while scanning them with fMRI at the very same time. They compared two kinds of electrical activity, spikes the output messages that neurons send away, and local field potentials slower waves that mostly reflect the input arriving in a region and the processing inside it. The result, the BOLD signal was better predicted by the local field potentials than by the spikes.

So BOLD tells us more about what a region receives and works on than about what it sends out. Most of the time, input and output rise and fall together, and the difference does not matter much. But when they come apart, BOLD tends to follow the input.

The same team noticed something else. The BOLD signal was much more variable than the neural signals. So an area that stays dark in your map is not necessarily idle. In imaging, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

What about inhibition? Inhibitory neurons are busy cells too, and they spend energy. Inhibiting a region can raise its BOLD signal or lower it depending on the circuit. So a BOLD increase does not mean excitation, and a decrease does not simply mean inhibition. Decreases called negative BOLD do exist, and their meaning has to be argued case by case.

Between neurons and vessels, there are messengers. Neurons and their support cells called astrocytes release chemical signals that relax the small arteries nearby. This chain is called neurovascular coupling, and it can change with age, disease, medication, or anything that affects the vessels. So a weak or BOLD response in a patient group could mean different neurons or simply different vessels. Always keep both readings in mind.

Now the plumbing. Fresh blood arrives through small arteries and capillaries right where the work happens. But the deoxyhemoglobin is washed downstream into veins that drain larger and larger areas all the way to the big veins on the surface of the brain. Standard BOLD imaging is very sensitive to these draining veins, so the signal can be strongest a few millimeters away from the active tissue. It is like reading the water meter on the main pipe at the end of the street instead of at each house.

So what does BOLD measure? A slow, vascular echo of the energy cost of neural work, mostly synaptic input and local processing, smeared in space by the veins and in time by the hemodynamic response. It is not a spike counter, it is not a simple sign of excitation, and it is not a direct readout of thought. Next time, we look at everything else that hides in your signal.

PLATE 2.43:38

Everything else in your signal

A voxel's time series is like a water gauge disturbed by many things besides the response of interest. Thermal noise, slow scanner drift, head motion, heartbeat, breathing and carbon dioxide changes all add to it, often as large as the task effect of about one percent. Sampling every two seconds also folds fast rhythms such as the heartbeat down into slow waves, called aliasing.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Picture a water gauge, a tall post with markings standing in our canal. We want to read one thing, how much the water rises when the lock opens. That is our BOLD response. But the water level never sits still. The tide comes and goes. Barges pass and leave wakes. The wind ruffles the surface. And once in a while, someone bumps into the post. A voxel's time series is exactly like this gauge. Today, we meet everything else in your signal.

First, thermal noise. Tiny random fluctuations from the electronics and from the body's own warmth. They are fast, unpredictable, and present in every single image. Like the little ripples of the wind on the water. They average out over many images, but they never go away.

Second, drift. The signal slowly wanders up or down over minutes because the scanner warms up and the field shifts a little. Like a tide, it is very slow below about one hundredth of a hertz, and it can be larger than the effect you are looking for. That is why every analysis includes a high-pass filter or drift terms in the model.

Third motion. When the head moves, even by a fraction of a millimeter, voxels near an edge suddenly see a different tissue. The signal jumps. And motion also disturbs the spins themselves, leaving traces that last for several images. Like someone kicking the post, we will spend a whole episode on motion in Chapter 4.

Fourth, the body itself. The heart beats about once per second. Each beat pushes blood through the vessels and makes the brain pulse a tiny bit. Breathing, about once every 3-5 seconds, moves the chest, which shifts the magnetic field in the head. Those are the barges in their wakes. And there is a slower and sneakier effect. When breathing gets deeper or shallower, the carbon dioxide in the blood changes, and that changes blood flow everywhere. In 2006, Rasmus Birn and colleagues showed that these slow breathing changes, around three hundredths of a hertz, sit right in the range of resting state signals, and are not removed by the usual corrections.

At three Tesla, this physiological noise is not small. Gunnar Krüger and Gary Glover showed that it can exceed the thermal and scanner noise. And it grows with field strength.

Now a trap. We sample one image every two seconds. So we can only see rhythms slower than one cycle every four seconds. Faster rhythms like the heartbeat do not disappear. They are folded down and come back disguised as slow waves. This is called aliasing. You have seen it in films where a spinning wagon wheel seems to turn slowly or even backwards. The camera samples too slowly for the wheel, and the heartbeat plays the same trick on fMRI.

So how big is our real signal compared to all this? A task response of about 1% inside a time series where drift, motion, and physiology can each be as large or larger. This is why we record pulse and breathing during scanning whenever we can, why we model drifts in motion, and why we use statistics that respect the noise.

To sum up, a voxel signal is a sum. Thermal ripples, slow drift, motion jumps, heartbeat and breathing, slow changes in carbon dioxide, and somewhere in the middle, a small, BOLD response. With a slow sampling rate, fast rhythms fold down into slow ones. Next time, we close Chapter 2 and ask where all of this lives. Voxels, surfaces, and standard spaces.

PLATE 2.54:19

Voxels, surfaces and standard spaces

A voxel is a crate of brain, a few millimeters on each side, holding millions of neurons yet yielding one number per image, so fMRI sees crowds. The folded cortical sheet means voxels mix tissues, and surface reconstruction follows the ribbon instead. Because every brain differs, each is stretched into a standard template such as MNI 152, which gives every point shared coordinates.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have followed the signal from the magnet to the blood and through all its noise. Now one last question for this chapter. Where does the signal live? Picture a long barge on our canal, loaded with cargo crates all the same size in neat rows. Each crate is one voxel. Today we meet voxels, surfaces and standard spaces.

A voxel is a volume pixel, a tiny box of brain, often 2 or 3 millimeters on each side in a typical study. For every box, the scanner gives us one number per image and one time series per run. That is all we get. Nothing smaller.

So what is inside one crate? In a 2008 review, Nikos Logothetis estimated that a typical voxel of about 55 cubic millimeters holds around 5.5 million neurons, tens of billions of synapses, and kilometers of dendrites and axons, plus glial cells and a dense web of tiny blood vessels. One number for all of that, fMRI does not see neurons. It sees crowds.

And crates are square, but the brain is not. The cortex where most of our signal comes from is a thin, folded sheet, only about 2 to 4 millimeters thick. So a crate often straddles a boundary. Half gray matter, half white matter, a bit of fluid. This is called the partial volume effect. The signal of that voxel is a mix of everything inside it.

Let us place fMRI on a map. On one axis how fine in space. On the other how fine in time. EEG and MEG are fast in milliseconds but blurry in space. fNIRS sits in between around a centimeter and a tenth of a second. fMRI is sharp in space around a millimeter or a few but slow because blood takes seconds to answer. No method wins on both axes. Each one answers a different kind of question.

Now the sheet. Imagine the cortex as a towpath ribbon folded again and again to fit inside the skull. Two points can be close in the volume on opposite banks of a fold and yet far apart along the ribbon. If we blur the volume, we mix them. So some pipelines like FreeSurfer reconstruct the cortical sheet as a surface, a mesh of points. The surface can be inflated, like a balloon, or flattened like a map. Then smoothing and comparisons follow the ribbon, not the crates.

And finally, every brain is different. Bigger, smaller, longer, with folds in slightly different places. To compare people or to pool a group, we need a common place. In 1879, France fixed a standard size for canal locks, the Freycinet gauge. Barges were then built to fit 38.5 meters long, 5.05 meters wide. Brain imaging does the reverse. We keep one standard brain, and we stretch each person's brain to fit it.

The first standard was the atlas of Jean Talairach and Pierre Tournoux in 1988, drawn from the brain of a single 60-year-old woman. Later, the Montreal Neurological Institute built averages from many young adults. First 305 brains, then the template-most software uses today, MNI 152, an average of 152 brains. For surfaces, there are average surfaces too, like fsaverage.

In a standard space, every point has an address. Three coordinates, X, Y, and Z in millimeters, from an origin near a small bundle of fibers called the anterior commissure. X goes left to right, Y goes back to front, Z goes bottom to top. So when a paper reports a peak at minus 40, minus 20, 50, you can find it in your own data. One warning, Talairach and MNI coordinates are not the same, and the same numbers do not point at quite the same place.

To sum up, a voxel is a crate holding millions of neurons and one number. Crates straddle the folded sheet which mixes tissues. Surfaces follow the ribbon. Standard spaces give every brain the same addresses so studies can talk to each other. That closes chapter 2. Next time we open chapter 3 before you scan with choosing your acquisition.
