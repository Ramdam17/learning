PLATE 10.16:21

Parts of a whole

Shares of a whole add up to one, so they are not free. When the second of three bands doubles, the other two fall from 33 to 25 percent though nothing happened to them. Independent parts correlate at minus one over the number of parts minus one, and the straight-line distance rises in 14,113 of 20,000 pairs when a part is dropped.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Some data arrive with a rule, before you have measured anything.

Think of a garden with a sundial. Its day is split into hours, and the hours always add up to one day. If one hour gets longer, another must get shorter. The rule comes first, and the numbers obey it.

Your data have such rules too. This chapter is about four of them. We start with the simplest: parts of a whole.

Take the power of a brain signal in three frequency bands. A band is just a range of frequencies, for example from 8 to 12 hertz. Episode 4.5 split a spectrum into bars, and episode 4.1 of the EEG series draws the map of these bands. Here we add up the power inside each band. Say we get 10, 10 and 10. Each band holds a third of the total.

Now the power of the second band doubles. The other two do not move at all. The powers are 10, 20 and 10, and the total is 40. So the shares become 25 percent, 50 percent and 25 percent.

Look at the first and the third band. Their share went from 33 percent down to 25 percent. Yet nothing happened to them. They only look smaller, because the second band got bigger.

That is the rule of parts. Shares are not free. When one goes up, the others go down.

In 1897, Karl Pearson warned about a close cousin of this trap. He showed that ratios which share a denominator can be correlated, even when the numbers behind them have nothing to do with each other.

Let us measure it. We simulate 20,000 recordings. In each one, every band gets a power drawn at random, from the same recipe, and independently of the others. The seed is 101. The powers are unrelated by construction.

Now turn the powers into shares. Then take two bands, and correlate their shares across the recordings, one point per recording.

With 3 bands, the correlation is minus 0.50. With 5 bands, minus 0.25. With 10 bands, minus 0.11.

This is not luck of the draw. When all the parts play the same role, their correlation is exactly minus one, divided by the number of parts minus one. The reason is simple. The shares always add up to one, so their total never varies. For a total that never varies, the parts must compensate each other.

The sign is not a discovery about your signal. It is the rule, speaking.

It gets worse. Take four unrelated quantities. The fourth one is large, and it varies a lot from one recording to the next. Compute the shares, and compare the first two. Their shares are correlated at plus 0.70. Both are divided by the same big, wobbling total, so they rise and fall together.

Now drop the fourth quantity, and compute the shares again from the remaining three. The correlation of the same two parts is now minus 0.50. And on the quantities themselves, before any sharing, it is 0.01. That is zero, up to the noise of the simulation.

Same data, three answers: plus 0.70, minus 0.50, and nothing. The only thing that changed was the denominator.

Now the straight line. Ruly takes two compositions, and measures the distance between them, as always.

Here are two pairs, with three parts each. In the first pair, one part goes from 1 percent to 2 percent. In the second, it goes from 30 percent to 31 percent. In both pairs, the second part pays back that one percent, and the third part stays put. The straight line gives 0.0141 for both.

But the first change is a doubling. The second is a change of three percent. The straight line cannot tell them apart. It counts points of percentage, and not ratios.

There is a second defect. The distance depends on which parts you kept.

Remember the two histograms of episode 5.1: 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and 0.2, 0.3, 0.5. Their straight-line distance is 0.424.

Now remove the middle part, and rescale what is left so that it adds up to one again. The two compositions become 0.71 and 0.29, against 0.29 and 0.71. The distance climbs to 0.606.

Looking at fewer parts made the gap look bigger.

Is that a quirk of one example? We test it. We draw 20,000 random pairs of compositions, with five parts each. In each pair, we drop one part at random and rescale. The straight-line distance goes up in 14,113 of them. It goes down in the rest. It never stays the same.

So the straight line has no stable opinion. Add a part, remove a part, and the distance moves.

This is not a corner case. Relative band power, a favourite of EEG papers, is a share. The percentage of a night spent in each sleep stage is a share. Any table whose rows are divided by their total is a table of shares. And once a row is divided by its total, every number in it knows about all the others.

These data are everywhere. The stages of a night of sleep. The time spent in each state. The proportions of cell types in a tissue. A timetable, where the hours of the day are shared among activities.

So what does a part tell you, on its own? Nothing. A share of 25 percent is not information about that band. It only says how that band compares to all the others.

The information is in the ratios between parts. This is the idea John Aitchison put forward in 1982, and it is where the next episode starts.

To sum up. Parts of a whole obey a rule: they add up to one. The straight line ignores that rule, and pays for it. It creates correlations that no one measured, it treats a doubling like a small change, and it changes its mind when you remove a part.

Ratios, then logs

Aitchison's distance takes the logarithm of each part, subtracts their average, then uses the straight line. A step from one percent to two percent measures 0.575 against 0.060 from thirty to thirty-one, where the straight line gave 0.014 for both. It ignores the total, but a zero is a trap: replacing it with one hundredth or one millionth gives 2.64 or 10.16.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the sundial garden. Last time, parts of a whole broke the straight line. A step from one percent to two percent counted the same as a step from thirty to thirty-one. Today, the distance that mends it.

First, a question. What is a step worth, when the data are parts? A part that goes from one percent to two percent has doubled. A part that goes from thirty to thirty-one has grown by a thirtieth. So the step with a meaning is a ratio.

Here is a ruler built for ratios. Its ticks are one, two, four, eight, sixteen. They are not evenly spaced in value. They are evenly spaced in doublings. Each tick is twice the one before. A ruler like this is a logarithm. Equal steps on it are equal ratios.

Now the recipe. It comes from the work of John Aitchison, in nineteen eighty-two. It has three moves.

One: take the logarithm of every part. Two: subtract the average of these logarithms. Three: take the ordinary straight line.

Try it on the first shelf of episode five point one. Zero point five, zero point three, zero point two. The logarithms: minus zero point six nine, minus one point two, minus one point six one. Their average is minus one point one seven. Subtract it. We get zero point four eight, minus zero point zero four, and minus zero point four four.

Why subtract the average? Suppose every part is multiplied by the same number. Every logarithm moves by the same amount, and so does their average. The subtraction takes it away. The total cannot matter any more.

There is another way to read the recipe. Take two parts, and look at their ratio. Compare its logarithm in one composition and in the other. Do this for every pair of parts. Add up the squared changes, and divide by the number of parts. That is the same number. The recipe compares all the ratios at once.

Now the two small steps. Three parts. The third stays at fifty percent. The first part gains one point, and the second part gives it up. From one percent to two percent: the distance is zero point five seven five. From thirty percent to thirty-one percent: zero point zero six zero. The straight line said zero point zero one four for both. Here, the first step is almost ten times bigger. The doubling is seen.

And the two shelves of episode five point one, zero point five, zero point three, zero point two, against zero point two, zero point three, zero point five. This distance gives one point two nine six.

So the same two shelves have three distances now. The straight line, zero point four two four. The angle of episode five point two, on the doubled scale, zero point seven three nine. And this one, one point two nine six.

Time for the distance card. The object is a composition: parts of a whole, all positive. The distance is the logarithm of each part, minus their average, then the straight line. The four rules hold, because it is a straight line between ordinary vectors, as long as every part is above zero.

What does it ignore? Now the row of changes, on the same two shelves.

Absolute units. Say these are powers in watts, forty for the first recording and ten for the second, instead of parts. One point two nine six. The total is gone.

Now give every part its own gain, three, one half and one point two, the same for both recordings. The straight line moves, from zero point four two four to zero point four seven three. This distance stays at one point two nine six.

Reorder the parts, the same way for both. One point two nine six. The names of the parts do not matter.

Noisy: ten percent of relative noise on each power, simulated with seed one hundred and two. One point four two. This one it sees.

Last, remove a part. Take out the middle one, equal on both shelves: one point two nine six, unchanged. Last time the straight line rose to zero point six zero six. Take out the first part instead: zero point six four eight. Lower.

Is that always so? We drew twenty thousand pairs of random compositions with four parts, and removed one part at random. The distance went down every time. It never went up. So this distance ignores the total and a gain for each part. If the total matters, keep it as a separate measurement.

Next, the mean. The straight line has an average, and so does this distance. Take two compositions: zero point nine, zero point zero nine, zero point zero one, and zero point nine, zero point zero one, zero point zero nine. The ordinary mean: zero point nine, zero point zero five, zero point zero five. The mean that goes with this distance multiplies instead of adding. Take the square root of the product, part by part. For the small parts: zero point zero three. It sits a factor of three from each. Then make the parts add up to one again. Zero point nine three eight, zero point zero three one, zero point zero three one.

With this distance, the space of compositions is flat. It was shown in two thousand and one, by Billheimer, Guttorp and Fagan, and by Pawlowsky-Glahn and Egozcue. Two years later, Egozcue and colleagues gave it ordinary coordinates. Three parts need two numbers. In those coordinates, the distance is again a plain straight line, one point two nine six, and every tool of chapter two applies. It is the idea of episode eight point two, measure in ratios, on a simpler object.

What does it cost? A logarithm for every part, before the straight line.

And the trap. The zero. A part that is zero has no logarithm. It sits infinitely far from everything. So people replace it with a small value. Take the first shelf, against zero point six, zero point four, and zero. Replace the zero with one hundredth: the distance is two point six four. With one thousandth: four point five two. One ten-thousandth: six point four zero. One millionth: ten point one six. The distance depends on the number you chose. There are thoughtful ways to choose it, described by Martín-Fernández and colleagues in two thousand and three. It is still a choice.

The logarithm also magnifies the noise of small parts. Move a part by half a point, taken from another part. Near one percent: zero point three four. Near thirty percent: zero point zero three.

Is this recipe for everywhere? It is debated. Aitchison treated this behaviour when a part is removed as essential. In twenty twenty-three, Greenacre and colleagues argued that it only needs to hold approximately. They argue that simpler transformations, which accept zeros, often do the job. And that the coordinates of two thousand and three are not a prerequisite for good practice. This episode does not settle it. Read both sides before you choose.

What to remember. On parts, the step that has meaning is a ratio. The edge, a part at zero, goes off to infinity, like the edge of the cone in episode eight point two. So ask your data one question. Can a zero happen here, and what does it mean?

Angles

Angles live on a circle, so ordinary averaging of 350 and 10 degrees gives 180. The shorter arc gives 20 degrees, and the mean arrow points at 0. The length of that arrow, the phase locking value, has a chance floor of 0.28 with ten observations and 0.028 with a thousand, so an index of 0.2 means nothing without its count.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two compass headings: 350 degrees, and 10 degrees. Picture two runners on a round track, one just before the finish line and one just after. They are only 20 degrees apart, almost shoulder to shoulder. Now average them the ordinary way. You get 180 degrees: the opposite direction.

Two bedtimes, 11 pm and 1 am. The ordinary average is 12. Noon. Nobody goes to bed at noon.

Both averages go wrong for the same reason. An angle is not a number on a line. It is a point on a circle, and on a circle, 360 degrees is the same place as 0 degrees. That is the rule these data carry before anyone measures anything.

So what is the distance between two angles? Walk along the circle, and take the shorter way round. The inchworm does it. Between 350 and 10, the shorter arc is 20 degrees, never 340. It can never exceed 180.

Or cut straight across, like the ruler. On a circle of radius 1, the chord between them is 0.347. Both respect the four rules: never negative, zero only for the same angle, the same in both directions, and no shortcut. We checked both on 20,000 random triples of angles, and the shortcut rule never failed.

Now the mean. Put each angle on the circle as an arrow of length 1. Average the arrows, and read the direction of the result. For 350 and 10, the mean arrow points at 0 degrees. For the bedtimes, at midnight.

The length of that mean arrow is useful too. For 350 and 10, it is 0.985. It is 1 when all the angles are identical, and 0 when they are spread all the way around. So the length says how grouped the angles are.

Angles also come from signals. The phase of an oscillation says where it is in its cycle, from 0 to 360 degrees. A calculation called the Hilbert transform reads it at every instant. The EEG series shows how.

When one signal follows the other with a steady lag, that difference is nearly the same every time. This is what we call locking. Take two simulated 10 hertz signals, recorded over 30 trials. At the same instant of every trial, the difference between their phases is an angle. That makes 30 arrows. Their mean arrow has length 0.50. This length is the phase locking value, from Lachaux and colleagues, in 1999. It is the length of the mean arrow of the phase differences.

Episode 9.2 had two means on the sphere. They are here too. Take three directions: two of them at 0 degrees, and one at 90. Yes, 0 counts twice: two observations out of three agree.

The mean of the arrows points at 26.57 degrees. The direction that makes the sum of squared arcs as small as possible, the definition of episode 1.6, is at 30.00 degrees. The costs: 1.645 squared radians at 30, and 1.656 at 26.57. Both are fair answers. They agree when the angles are grouped, and separate when they spread.

Now two opposite directions, 0 and 180 degrees. The arrows cancel. The mean arrow has length 0. There is no mean direction. The squared arcs are equally small at 90 and at 270 degrees: 4.93 squared radians each, against 9.87 at 0. Two means, tied. When the length is near 0, the direction is not worth reading.

And the length sets a trap of its own. Draw angles with no link at all, spread evenly around the circle. The mean arrow still has a length, because chance never cancels perfectly.

Averaged over 20,000 draws, that length is 0.28 with 10 observations. It is 0.16 with 30, 0.089 with 100, and 0.028 with 1,000. This is the floor. It falls roughly like one over the square root of the count: 100 times more observations, 10 times lower.

Why does it fall like that? Each angle is one step of length 1, in a random direction. After many steps, you have wandered off a distance about the square root of their number. Divide by the count, and you get the floor.

So an index of 0.2 says nothing without its count. With 10 observations, 0.2 is below the average of pure chance. With 30, nine draws in ten of pure chance reach at most 0.31, so 0.2 is nothing. With 1,000 observations, 0.2 is seven times the floor.

The measured length is always too large on average, and the more so when observations are few. Vinck and colleagues, in 2010, and Aydore, Pantazis and Leahy, in 2013, work this out. Vinck and colleagues propose a bias-free replacement, the pairwise phase consistency, which estimates the square of the true length.

Two variants. First, orientations. A fault line, a fiber, a bar on a screen: these are lines, not arrows. A line at 10 degrees and another at 170 are 20 degrees apart, not 160. The usual remedy is to double the angles first. We get 20 and 340. Take the mean arrow, then halve its direction. The mean orientation is 0 degrees, where the ordinary mean says 90. Doubling is a convention, not a theorem.

Second, a link between an angle and an ordinary measure. Simulate a measure equal to the cosine of the angle, plus noise. The link is strong. The ordinary correlation with the angle says minus 0.04, or plus 0.12, depending on where you cut the circle. The correlation made for this case says 0.93. Its usual formula goes back to Mardia in 1976 and to Johnson and Wehrly in 1977.

Now the card, for the angle distance. What does the angle distance ignore? Complete turns. What does it cost? Nothing. And its trap is that cut. Take phases between minus 180 and plus 180, and feed them to an ordinary regression. On the circle, 170 and minus 170 are neighbours, 20 degrees apart. On the line, they are 340 apart: the farthest pair. Two neighbours become strangers, and the answer changes with the place of the cut.

An angle is not a number. It is a point on a circle. Before you average it, correlate it, or subtract it, put it back on its circle.

Rotations

Orientations are not three flat columns: two quarter turns done in opposite orders end 120 degrees apart. Two roll-pitch-yaw triplets 127.3 degrees apart as vectors are only 1.41 degrees apart as blocks. The distance is the angle of the single relative rotation, a true metric, and the mean minimises squared distances, with two tied answers for opposite orientations.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A head tracker tells you where a head points, many times a second. So does the sensor on a wrist, or on one segment of the body. Every reading is an orientation. How far apart are two orientations?

Orientations carry a rule before any measurement. Take a block. Turn it a quarter turn around one axis, then a quarter turn around another. Now do the same two turns in the opposite order. The block ends in two different places. Measured as an angle, they are one hundred twenty degrees apart.

Order matters. Add numbers in any order and you get the same sum. Rotations do not behave like that. So orientations cannot be a flat table of three columns, where any point can be added to any other.

Most of the time, an orientation is written as three angles: roll, pitch and yaw. There are many conventions for them. Twelve orders of the axes, each with fixed or moving axes. So here is ours, and it is on the screen. Roll is a turn around the fixed x axis, then pitch around the fixed y axis, then yaw around the fixed z axis, in degrees.

Take two triplets. Zero, eighty-nine, zero. And ninety, eighty-nine, ninety. Treat each triplet as a vector, and the straight line between them measures one hundred twenty-seven point three degrees. They look far apart.

Now build the two blocks. A pitch of eighty-nine degrees is almost straight up. Roll and yaw then turn the block almost the same way. The two orientations are one point four one degrees apart. Same convention, same triplets, and a ratio of ninety to one. In the other convention, with moving axes, the same two triplets would be almost one hundred eighty degrees apart. A triplet means nothing until you say which one you use.

Slide one triplet toward the other along a straight line. The numbers travel one hundred twenty-seven degrees. The block never leaves a cone of one point four one degrees around where it started.

Angles also close up on themselves. Two headings, plus one hundred seventy and minus one hundred seventy, average to zero as numbers. Zero is the opposite of where both are. They are twenty degrees apart, on either side of one hundred eighty.

So here is the trap. Three angles are not a vector.

Here is the distance that respects the rule. Take the single rotation that carries one orientation onto the other. Whatever the path, it is one turn around one axis. Its angle, at most one hundred eighty degrees, is the distance. It is one of six distances compared by Huynh in 2009.

With matrices, multiply the transpose of the first by the second. The trace of the product gives the angle: its arc cosine, after subtracting one and halving. Check it on the quarter turns. One hundred twenty degrees, a turn around the diagonal of the block.

Does it keep the four rules? It is never negative. It is zero only for the same orientation. It is the same in both directions. And on three thousand random triples, the triangle inequality was never broken. It is the length of the shortest path in the space of rotations, so it is a true metric.

Now the average. Add two rotation matrices entry by entry, and halve. For no turn and a half turn, the determinant of the result is zero, not one. It squashes space flat. For zero and ninety degrees, it is one half. The result is not a rotation.

So the mean is defined as in episode one point six: through the distance. It is the rotation with the smallest sum of squared distances to the others. That is the line of Moakher in 2002, and of Hartley and colleagues in 2013. For zero and ninety degrees, it is forty-five degrees, a real rotation. For no turn and a half turn, two answers tie, a quarter turn one way or the other. As with the angles of the last episode, there is no single middle.

A rotation is also written with four numbers, a quaternion. A quaternion and its opposite describe the same rotation. As lists of numbers they are two point zero apart, the largest possible gap between unit quaternions. Any averaging must not depend on this sign, as Markley and colleagues showed in 2007.

This is the sphere with opposite points glued, from episode nine point two. The distance on the sphere is at most ninety degrees. Double it, and you get the rotation angle, at most one hundred eighty.

One more landmark to read results. Draw two orientations at random, and the angle between them is one hundred twenty-six point five degrees on average. It falls below thirty degrees in only about zero point seven five percent of cases. Here, far is the normal state.

A common use is head motion in functional MRI. From one image to the next, Power and colleagues in 2012 add up three translations and three rotations. They first turn the rotations into millimeters, on a sphere of radius fifty millimeters. One degree is then zero point eight seven three millimeters.

That is a convention, not a geometry. A sphere of radius one hundred millimeters would say one point seven four five. Mixing translations and rotations in one distance always needs a reference length, so it is always a choice.

Here is the card. The object is an orientation. The distance is the angle of the relative rotation. It ignores the frame, as long as both orientations are written in the same one. It costs the product of two small matrices. Its traps are treating three angles as a vector, and mixing millimeters and degrees without saying which radius.

Two rotations do not give the same result in either order. So orientations are not three flat columns. Their distance fits in one angle.

PLATE 10.56:50

Up to a transformation

Ignoring a change such as a delay, a sign or a rotation means keeping the smallest distance over all transformed copies. That costs a search, and the search finds likeness in noise: two unrelated noises of 200 samples correlate 0.056 on average, but 0.209 after the best of 200 delays. Aligning 1,000 noise images on a face makes the face appear, correlation 0.82.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You have already seen the same move four times.

In episode 1.5, a distance ignored a delay: we tried every shift and kept the best. In episode 9.2, a map and its inverted copy counted as one state. In episode 9.5, we stopped caring which axes described a plane, and compared the plane. And in episode 10.4, a rotation had two quaternions, one and its negative, and we kept the closer pair.

Four episodes, one recipe. Today we say it once, and then we ask what it costs.

The recipe has two steps. First, choose the changes you want to ignore. A delay, a sign, a rotation of the axes. Second, make every copy of each object that those changes allow, and keep the smallest distance between a copy of one and a copy of the other.

So the distance now says: how far apart are these two objects, once I am allowed to change them in this way. If one object is just a transformed copy of the other, the answer is zero.

When is the result a true distance? When the changes form a group. That means you can undo any change, and you can chain two changes into a third. And no change may alter the distance you started from. Delays of a circular signal qualify. So do rotations. Then the smallest distance over copies obeys the shortcut rule. This comes from metric geometry, in the book of Burago, Burago and Ivanov, from 2001.

There is one reserve. Two different objects can still end up at distance zero. It happens when one is the limit of copies of the other, without being a copy itself. The copies get closer and closer, and never arrive. You then have a pseudo-distance. If the set of copies of each object is closed, the problem disappears.

Let us test the easy case. Take the straight line, with every delay of a signal of 120 samples: a full circle. We draw 3,000 triples of unrelated signals, and check the shortcut rule on each one. Result: no violation, not one. In episode 1.5 we allowed only thirty samples either way. That is not a full group, so we left the light dashed. With the full circle, it is a group.

Now a change that does not fit. Allow a gain, on one object only. Take two points on a page: the first has coordinates 2 and 2, the second has coordinates 1 and 0. Scale the second as you like, and look for the closest copy. The best gain is 2, and the distance is 2.0. Now scale the first and keep the second. The best gain is one quarter, and the distance is 0.707. Two answers for one pair. The distance is no longer symmetric.

Why? A gain changes the distance you started from. It is not a delay, and it is not a rotation. The recipe needs changes that leave the distance alone. The remedy is to bring both objects to the same length first, one, and only then search over the rest. Here, the two points are then 0.765 apart, in both directions.

Now a case from EEG. Independent component analysis cuts a recording into components. They come out in no particular order, and with no particular sign. Comon described this in 1994. To compare two sets of components, we must pair them. So we look for the pairing, with order and sign, that gives the best total match. This is a classic problem of assignment. The Hungarian method of Kuhn, from 1955, solves it. Its cost grows with the cube of the number of components, for every pair of recordings.

And here is the trap. Every freedom you give the search, it uses.

Take two unrelated noises of 200 samples. Their correlation, with no delay, is on average 0.056 in absolute value. Now allow all 200 delays, and keep the best one: 0.209. Nothing was there. We only looked in 200 places. With 1,000 samples, 0.026 and 0.109. More data lowers the floor, and never removes it.

Now allow any reordering of the samples at all. Sort both signals, and the correlation is 0.993. A near perfect match between two noises. That is the end of the road: you ignored everything, and everything matches.

The same happens with components. Two sets of unrelated components, taken in their own order: 0.058. After the best pairing of order and sign: 0.101 for 5 components, 0.141 for 20, and 0.172 for 64. The more components, the more pairings to choose from, and the better the best pairing looks.

The most famous case comes from microscopy. To see a molecule, researchers align thousands of noisy images, then average them. The alignment is a search over positions. In 2009, Shatsky and colleagues showed what it can do. Take 1,000 images of pure noise. Align each one on a photograph of Einstein, by cross-correlation, and average. Einstein appears. In 2013, Henderson reproduced the figure to warn of this pitfall, known as Einstein from noise.

Here, a simulated stand-in: a drawn face, not a photograph. One image of noise shows nothing. Average 1,000 of them without alignment, and you get grey. Now align each one on the face first, and average. The face comes out. Its correlation with the stand-in is 0.82. There was no face in the data. It was in the template.

Now the card. The object is whatever you compare, together with the changes you allow. The distance is the smallest distance among all the transformed copies. What it ignores is exactly the changes you chose. What it costs is one search per pair: 120 delays here, an assignment there.

The four rules. Never negative: yes. The same in both directions: yes, if you brought both objects to the same size first. Zero only for the same object: no, a copy sits at zero by design. And the shortcut rule: yes, when the changes form a group.

The last line is the trap. The more you allow, the lower the distance between two things that have nothing in common.

So an invariance is bought with a minimum. And a minimum over many options always finds something. The floor must be computed after the same search, on data that have nothing in common. Then you read your distance against it.

Shapes

Shape is what remains after removing position, size and orientation. Two triangles, one moved, turned and doubled, are 9.59 apart as coordinates and zero apart as shapes. The recipe centres, scales and rotates, then reads an angle on a sphere. Traps are mismatched labels, one displaced point spread over the others, and a noise floor of about 5 degrees for ten points.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A face. A structure of the brain. A hand. A cap of electrodes.

Put a dot on every landmark you can find again and again: the tip of the nose, the corner of an eye, a knuckle, the electrode over the vertex. Now each object is a short list of coordinates. Two objects, two lists. How far apart are they?

Let us try with the smallest object that has a shape: a triangle.

Take a right triangle, with two legs of length one. Make a copy. Slide the copy away, turn it by a quarter turn, and make it twice as big. It is the same triangle, just drawn somewhere else.

Now compare the two lists of coordinates with the straight line, the ruler of episode 2.1. Six numbers against six numbers. The distance is 9.59.

The ruler says: far apart. But look at what it measured. Where the triangle sits. How big it is. Which way it faces. None of this is its shape.

The shape is what is left when you remove position, size and orientation. That is the definition David Kendall gave in the nineteen seventies, and he built a whole space for it in 1984.

The recipe has three steps. Each step removes one thing.

First, centre. Slide each object so that the average of its points sits at the origin. Position is gone.

Second, scale. Divide each object by its size, the square root of the sum of its squared distances to the centre. Now every object has size one. Size is gone.

Third, turn. Rotate one object until it lies as close as possible to the other. Orientation is gone. And you do not search for this turn by trial and error. It has a direct answer, from the singular value decomposition of a small matrix. That was shown by Schönemann in 1966.

What remains is a small gap between the two objects. We read it as an angle, and here is exactly which one. After centring and scaling, each object is a point on a sphere. The angle is the arc between the two points along that sphere. It is the full Procrustes distance, written as an angle. It is the arccosine of the sum of the singular values.

For our two triangles, the angle is zero. Nine point five nine for the coordinates. Zero for the shapes.

Let us watch it on a real list. Here are eight landmarks, made up for the picture, and a second reading of the same object. The second one is 1.7 times bigger, turned, and shifted, and each landmark carries a little error. Centre both. Scale both to size one. Turn one onto the other. The landmarks do not match exactly, and the angle that remains is 5.3 degrees. That is the real difference between the two readings: here, only the small errors.

This is called a Procrustes analysis, after the bandit of the Greek myth who made every guest fit his bed. Here the fitting is mild: a shift, a rescaling and a turn, and nothing else.

Why an angle, and not the length of a chord? Because the angle is a distance along a curved space, the kind of space chapter 9 taught us to walk on. The straight chord would cut through the inside of the sphere, where no shape lives.

Now some triangles. The equilateral triangle against the right isosceles triangle: 15 degrees. The equilateral triangle against three points in a row: 45 degrees. The equilateral triangle against its own image in a mirror: 90 degrees.

Or zero, if you decide that the mirror does not count. That is one more choice on the list of what you ignore. In this episode, the mirror counts.

These angles live on a sphere, and you have seen one before. In episode 9.2 we pushed pins into a globe. Here each pin is a whole triangle.

Kendall showed that the shapes of triangles form a sphere of radius one half, so that arcs read directly as the angles we just used. The equilateral triangle sits at the north pole. Its mirror image sits at the south pole, 90 degrees away. Every triangle squashed flat, with its three points in a row, lies on the equator, 45 degrees from each pole. The right isosceles triangle sits 15 degrees from the north pole. To change a shape is to walk on this sphere.

With more points, the space of shapes grows. In the plane, count two numbers for each point, and take away four: two for the position, one for the size, one for the orientation. A triangle gives two, the surface of a sphere. Ten points give sixteen. In space, count three numbers for each point and take away seven: three for the position, one for the size, three for the rotation. Ten points give twenty three dimensions.

Now three traps.

The first is the labels. The points must correspond from one object to the other. Take our right triangle, and name its corners in another order, by cycling them. It is now 25.7 degrees away from itself. The recipe cannot know that the nose of one object is the nose of the other. You must tell it.

The second is the Pinocchio effect. Ten points on a circle. Push a single one outwards, by half a radius. The other nine do not move. Now fit. After the fit, the pushed point carries only 71 percent of the gap. The nine points that did not move share the other 29 percent, and the most loaded of them carries 6. The fit scales and turns the whole object to shrink the total gap, so a local change is spread over everything. It has been shown for errors in landmark positions by von Cramon-Taubadel, Frazier and Lahr, in 2007.

The third is the floor. Take the same shape, read twice. Give each point a pose error of 5 percent of the radius. The two readings are still not at zero. Over 10,000 simulated pairs, with seed 106, the mean angle is 2.9 degrees for 3 points, 5.0 degrees for 10 points, and 5.6 degrees for 50 points. Below this floor, a difference is just noise.

What if you have many objects, not two? You fit all of them to their average shape, and you recompute that average at the same time. This is generalized Procrustes analysis, by Gower, in 1975. On five simulated copies of one shape, each ends between 3.1 and 4.8 degrees from the average.

Now the card of this distance. The object: the points of a landmark list. The distance: an angle after centring, scaling and turning. What it ignores: position, size, orientation, and the mirror, if you decide so. What it costs: the decomposition of a small matrix, once for each pair. Its trap: the labels, the spreading of a local change, and the floor.

Chapter 12 will take Procrustes again, for two representations that have no common frame.

So, here is the idea to keep. A shape is what is left when you have removed what you decided not to look at. Write that list down. And if size matters, keep it apart, the way we kept the total apart in episode 10.2.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Six episodes, one habit. Before you choose a distance, ask what rule your data already carry.

This episode is the honest version. What is established, what is only worth trying, and one question that tells you which is which.

First, a reminder of the rules.

Shares have a total. Let one band's power double, and the shares of the other two fall from a third to a quarter, though nothing happened to them. Ratios and logarithms respect that total. For the two histograms of episode 5.1, the straight line says zero point four two four, and the Aitchison distance says one point two nine six.

Angles have a full turn. Three hundred and fifty degrees and ten degrees are twenty degrees apart, not three hundred and forty. Rotations have an order. Two quarter turns, done in the two possible orders, end a hundred and twenty degrees apart.

A minimum over transformations buys invariance, and it finds friends in pure noise. And shapes forget position, size and orientation: a triangle and its moved, turned, doubled copy are nine point five nine apart as lists of coordinates, and zero apart as shapes.

Now the left column. Six entries. Each has the same three parts: the data, the tie between its numbers, and the source.

Microbiome counts. A sequencing run returns an arbitrary total, set by the instrument, so only relative abundances can be read. The tie: the counts add to a total the instrument chose. Gloor and colleagues, in twenty seventeen, argue that these data can and should be treated as compositions at every stage.

Time use. Sleep, sitting, light activity and vigorous activity add up to one day. The tie: the parts add to twenty-four hours. Chastin and colleagues, in twenty fifteen, analysed one thousand nine hundred and thirty-seven adults this way. Dumuid and colleagues, in twenty eighteen, laid out the regression method, with children's body fat as the example.

Sleep stages. Le and colleagues, in twenty twenty-five, treated the stages of a night as parts of a whole. They followed ninety-six healthy young adults for fifteen consecutive days, with a portable EEG device, and related the stages to mood. The tie: the stages add to the night.

Phases. The tie: three hundred and sixty degrees is zero. Circular statistics is the ordinary tool. Lachaux and colleagues, in nineteen ninety-nine, built phase locking statistics for brain signals. Berens, in two thousand nine, published a toolbox for circular statistics, with a neurophysiology example.

Head motion in MRI. Power and colleagues, in twenty twelve, summed each frame's three translations and three rotations into one displacement. The rotations became millimetres on a sphere of radius fifty millimetres. That is a convention, not a geometry. The tie: a rotation is an angle and a shift is a length, and adding them needs a reference radius.

Shapes. The tie: position, size and orientation say nothing about the form. Dryden and Mardia wrote the full theory of landmark shape analysis, in its second edition in twenty sixteen. Bookstein, in nineteen ninety-seven, published a method for outlines, shown on the corpus callosum of twenty-five people. We say nothing about its result here, only about the method.

Now the right column. These are tracks, not results. None has been tried on real data in this series.

Track one. Treat relative band powers as a composition, and compare two states by their band ratios. A ratio between two bands, once you take its logarithm, already is a composition coordinate.

Here is what it would look at. Simulated, seed one hundred and seven: two states, three bands, powers that vary from epoch to epoch. In the second state, only the middle band doubles. The share of the first band falls from thirty-three percent to twenty-five. It did not change. The log ratio of the middle band to the first moves by one bit. The log ratio of the third band to the first moves by zero point zero three.

Track two. Do the same for the time spent in each microstate. A recording spends its time in a handful of states, so the times add to the length of the recording. Decide first what to do with the zeros.

Track three. Between two people who interact, measure the angle between their head orientations over time, with the distance of episode 10.4, not with differences of angles. A difference across the wrap says three hundred and forty degrees when the heads are twenty apart.

Track four. Compare postures or gaze paths as shapes, free of position and size. A posture is a set of landmarks, and the same posture is the same shape anywhere in the room.

Track five. Match the components of two people, and report the floor you get from the same matching on components that have nothing in common. Components come out in any order and sign, and the best matching always finds some likeness. The floor is the number to beat.

No solid paper that treats relative band powers as a composition turned up in our search. That is an absence in one search, not proof of absence.

One question separates the two columns. Which of the numbers in my table are free to vary on their own?

When something ties them, a total, a full turn, a fixed length, the straight line is wrong somewhere. When nothing does, the straight line is fine.

That was chapter nine's curved spaces, found again inside ordinary tables.

So before you choose a distance, write down the rule your data already carry. It tells you which space they live in, and half the choice is made.
