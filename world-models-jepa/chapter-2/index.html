Vectors and the dot product

A vector is a necklace of numbers, and each number is a coordinate. Multiplying two necklaces bead by bead and adding the products gives the dot product: a vote on whether they agree, large when alike, zero when unrelated, negative when opposite. Dividing by the two lengths turns it into the cosine, the similarity compass of the series.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the map room. On a map, a place is just a list of numbers: how far east, how far north. Today we take that idea and run with it. A list of numbers is called a vector. By the end of this video, you will know the one operation that tells you whether two vectors look alike.

Picture a recording from four electrodes, frozen at a single instant. Four voltages, one per electrode. Let's thread them on a string. Each bead is one number. A pink bead is positive, a blue bead is negative, and the bigger the bead, the bigger the value. This is a necklace, and it is how we will draw vectors for the whole series. Four beads, four dimensions.

Each bead is also a coordinate. Keep only the first two electrodes for a moment. The first value says how far to walk along one axis, the second says how far to walk along the other. Walk both, and you land on a point. Draw an arrow from the origin to that point. This is the arrow from the EEG series, episode 2.2. A two-bead necklace and an arrow on a plane are the same object, seen two ways.

Now take two necklaces, one above the other. Pair the beads: first with first, second with second, and so on. Multiply each pair. Then add up the four products. That single number is the dot product. With our two recordings, three times two is six. Minus one times minus one is plus one. Two times three is six. One times zero is zero. Six, plus one, plus six, plus zero: thirteen. In NumPy, the whole recipe is a single symbol, the at sign.

Watch what each product does. When two paired beads have the same color, both pink or both blue, their product is positive, and it adds to the total. When the colors disagree, the product is negative, and it takes away. Big beads that agree push the total up fast. So the dot product is a vote, bead by bead, on whether two necklaces agree.

That gives us the big idea. Two necklaces that look alike give a large positive number: thirteen, for our pair. Now take a necklace that has nothing to do with the first one. Its agreements and its disagreements cancel exactly, and the dot product is zero. Finally, flip every bead of the first necklace, pink to blue and blue to pink. Every pair now disagrees, and we get minus fifteen. Alike, unrelated, opposite: large, zero, negative.

Back to the arrows. Two arrows pointing the same way give a large dot product. At a right angle, zero. Pointing in opposite directions, a negative number. In fact, the dot product is the length of the first arrow, times the length of the second, times the cosine of the angle between them. With four beads, we can no longer draw the arrows. But the angle still exists, and the recipe still works. It works the same with four hundred beads.

There is one catch. Double every bead of a necklace, as if the whole recording got louder. Its pattern has not changed, yet its dot product with the original jumps from fifteen to thirty. Length gets mixed up with resemblance. The fix is to divide by the two lengths. What is left is the cosine of the angle, always between minus one and one. One means the same direction, zero means unrelated, minus one means opposite. Our similar pair scores zero point nine. The louder copy scores exactly one.

If you followed the EEG series, episode 2.3 used this same product to measure how much two signals resemble each other. The Quantum series calls it the inner product, in episode 1.2. In this series, the cosine will be our compass. In chapter 4, it finds which images look alike. In chapter 7, it tells matching pairs from strangers.

To sum up. A vector is a necklace of numbers, and each number is a coordinate. The dot product multiplies the beads pair by pair and adds them up: it measures how much two necklaces agree. Divided by their lengths, it becomes the cosine. Next time: matrices, the machines that take one necklace and give back another.

PLATE 2.25:34

Matrices, machines that move vectors

A matrix is a machine that takes a necklace in and returns another: each row is a station of dials, and each output bead is that row's dot product with the input. Matrices rotate, stretch, squash and change the number of beads, as a linear layer does. The order of matrices matters, since rotation then stretch differs from stretch then rotation.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the map room. Last time we met the necklace and the dot product. Today we build a machine around them. A matrix is a machine that takes one necklace in, and hands another necklace back.

Here it is. A necklace goes in at the top, and each bead rides down its own wire. Inside, the machine has stations, one for each row of the matrix. At every crossing between a wire and a station sits a dial. Each dial is a weight, a number that the passing bead gets multiplied by.

Let's feed it the recording from last time: three, minus one, two, one. The first station holds the weights of our old friend, the second necklace from last time: two, minus one, three, zero. Each bead meets its dial and gets multiplied. Six, one, six, zero. The station adds everything up and sends out thirteen. That is exactly the dot product we computed before.

The second station has other dials: one, one, minus one, zero. Three, minus one, minus two, zero. The sum is zero. So the machine hands back a new necklace of two beads: thirteen and zero.

That is the whole secret. Each row of a matrix is a necklace of weights, and each output bead is the dot product of that row with the input. In NumPy, the machine runs with the same at sign as before. The matrix goes on the left, the necklace on the right. Notice what the machine needs: four dials at every station, because the input has four beads. A matrix with two rows and four columns eats four beads and returns two.

Now make it bigger. A recording from sixty-four electrodes is a necklace of sixty-four beads. Take a matrix with eight rows and sixty-four columns. That is a machine with eight stations, and each station has sixty-four dials, five hundred and twelve dials in total. Sixty-four numbers go in, and eight numbers come out. Every output is one weighted mix of all the electrodes. Because each dial is a different number, each station listens to the electrodes in its own way.

If you followed the EEG series, you have already met this machine. The spatial filters of episode 3.14 are matrices of this kind: each row is a pattern of weights that listens to some electrodes more than others. We will not need any particular filter here. Only the idea matters: a spatial filter is a matrix.

Now for the big moment. What can such a machine do to a vector? To see it, shrink the necklace to two beads. Two beads are a point on a plane, and a matrix moves every point at once. So we draw a grid, and watch what the machine does to it.

First, a rotation. The matrix zero, minus one, one, zero turns the whole grid by a quarter turn. The arrow two, one lands on minus one, two. It has the same length, only another direction. Look at the columns of the matrix. The first column, zero, one, is where the first axis of the grid lands. The second column, minus one, zero, is where the second axis lands.

Second, a stretch. The matrix two, zero, zero, one pulls the grid wider, and leaves its height alone. The arrow two, one becomes four, one. The unit square, which has area one, becomes a rectangle of area two.

Third, a squash. The matrix one, zero, zero, zero flattens the whole plane onto a single line. The arrow two, one becomes two, zero. The second bead is gone, and no machine can bring it back. The square collapses onto a line, so its area is zero.

Fourth, a change of size. A matrix with two rows and three columns takes a necklace of three beads and returns two. Two, one, three goes in, and five, four comes out. The shape of the matrix tells you everything: the rows are the outputs, and the columns are the inputs. And the shapes must fit: a machine with two rows and three columns can only take a necklace of three beads.

Rotate, stretch, squash, change the number of beads. This is exactly what one linear layer of a neural network does. It multiplies the incoming necklace by a matrix, then adds a constant shift called the bias. In PyTorch, the weight of a linear layer has one row for each output. Its dials are the machine's dials, and training turns them. We will meet the artificial neuron in episode 3.1, later in this series.

One last thing, and it is a trap. The order of the machines matters. Take the rotation, and the stretch. Send the arrow one, one through the stretch first, then the rotation: it lands on minus one, two. Now send it through the rotation first, then the stretch: it lands on minus two, one. Different machines in a different order give a different result. The reason is simple: the second machine works on whatever the first machine produced, so swapping them feeds each machine a different necklace. Multiplying the two matrices in one order gives zero, minus one, two, zero. In the other order, it gives zero, minus two, one, zero. Rotation times stretch is not stretch times rotation. It helps to read a product from right to left: the matrix on the right acts first.

To sum up. A matrix is a machine: each row is a necklace of weights, and each output bead is its dot product with the input. It can rotate, stretch, squash, and change the number of beads. A linear layer is exactly such a machine, and the order of products matters. Next time: derivatives and the gradient.

PLATE 2.34:12

Derivatives and the gradient

On a loss landscape, a marble in fog feels only the ground beneath it. The derivative is the slope along one knob, rise over run as the step shrinks. The gradient is the necklace of all partial slopes, an arrow pointing steepest uphill, perpendicular to contour lines, and stepping against it lowers the loss using only local information.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the map room. Last time, a point on a map was a list of numbers. Today the map holds a landscape, with a marble resting on it. By the end of this video, you will know how a slope turns into an arrow that points the way down.

Each point on this map is one setting of two knobs. The height at that point is the loss, the error we want to make small. We want the marble at the bottom of the valley. Here is the catch. In real life, nobody hands you this map. You are standing in fog, and all you can feel is the ground under your feet.

So let's study what you can feel: the slope. Start with a single knob, called w. For this toy example, the loss is w squared. Stand at w equals one. The loss there is one. How steep is the curve right here?

Take a step to the right and watch how much the loss rises. Step by one, and the loss goes from one to four. Rise three, run one: the slope looks like three. Now step by a tenth. The loss goes from one to one point two one. Rise over run: two point one. Step by a thousandth, and the answer is two point zero zero one.

As the step shrinks, the answer settles on two. That number is the derivative of the loss at w equals one. It is rise over run, when the run becomes tiny. Written d L by d w, it says: nudge the knob a little, and the loss moves by about two times your nudge. A positive slope means the loss goes up with the knob. A negative slope means it goes down. A slope of zero means flat ground.

Now two knobs, theta one and theta two. Our marble sits at theta one equals one point five, and theta two equals three. Its loss is four point five. Feeling the ground, you can ask two questions.

Question one. If I walk east, changing only theta one, how does the loss change? Imagine a small spirit level lying on the ground, turned east to west. The bubble reads the slope: minus four. Every step east lowers the loss. Question two. If I walk north, changing only theta two? Turn the level a quarter turn. The slope is plus one.

Each answer is called a partial derivative: the slope along one knob, with the other knob held still. Now put the two answers on one necklace. Minus four, and plus one. This list of slopes is the gradient. On the map, it is an arrow that starts at the marble: four steps back along theta one, one step forward along theta two.

Why does this arrow point uphill? Remember the dot product. Pick any direction to walk. The slope in that direction is the dot product of the gradient with that direction. It is largest when the two line up. It is zero at a right angle, and that is exactly what a contour line is: level ground. So the gradient crosses every contour line at a right angle, and points up the steepest way. Its length, four point one, says how steep.

Flip the arrow, and you get the steepest way down. Take a small step that way, from one point five and three to one point nine and two point nine. The loss falls from four point five to about three. One slope reading, one step, and the marble is lower.

Notice what we used: only the ground under the marble. We never looked at the whole map. That is the big idea. A real model has far more than two knobs. The GPT three model, in a paper from twenty twenty, had one hundred seventy five billion. You cannot draw a map with that many axes. But the gradient is still a necklace, one slope per knob. The fog does not matter. The local slope is enough to know which way is down.

One honest limit. A slope only describes the ground right under the marble. It says nothing about the valley far away, and on flat ground it gives no direction at all.

To sum up. The derivative is the slope along one knob. The gradient is the necklace of all the slopes, an arrow that points straight uphill. Minus the gradient points downhill, and we need only the ground under our feet. Next time: gradient descent, where the marble takes that step again and again.

PLATE 2.44:06

Gradient descent

Gradient descent measures the gradient, steps in the opposite direction and repeats, with the learning rate as the step length. Three marbles in one valley show the trade-off: tiny steps arrive slowly, medium steps settle, huge steps leap across the valley and diverge. Using a random mini-batch gives a noisy but far cheaper gradient, which is how large models are trained.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the map room. Last time, we learned to read the slope under the marble: the gradient, an arrow that points to the steepest climb. Today we put it to work. One rule, one line of code, and a marble finds its own way to the bottom of a valley. We will also meet the one knob that can ruin everything.

Here is the whole idea. Stand where you are. Measure the gradient. Take a step in the opposite direction. Then measure again, and step again. That is gradient descent. The idea is old: Cauchy wrote it down in eighteen forty-seven, as a way to solve systems of equations. In code, it is a single line. The weights get the weights, minus the learning rate, times the gradient. The weights are the knobs of chapter one. The gradient is our arrow. And the learning rate is the length of each step.

That last number is where the story gets interesting. Put three marbles at the same starting point, in the same valley, following the same rule. Only their step length differs.

The first marble takes tiny steps. It heads the right way, every time, but after twenty steps it is still on the slope. It will get there, eventually, if we are patient.

The second marble takes medium steps. It slides down, slows as the slope flattens, and settles at the bottom in about ten steps.

The third marble takes huge steps. Its first leap carries it clean over the valley. The slope on the other side points back, so it leaps again, and again. It jumps from side to side, farther each time. Its loss climbs.

So the learning rate is a knob that the machine does not learn. We choose it ourselves. Too small, and we never arrive. Too big, and we jump over the answer. People try several values, and often make the step smaller as training goes on.

In a real model, we cannot draw the map. A model with millions of knobs has a landscape with millions of directions. What we watch instead is the loss curve: the loss, step after step. Tiny steps give a slow, gentle slope. Medium steps give a fast drop that flattens out. Huge steps give a curve that shoots up. You can read the learning rate off the shape of the curve.

There is a second problem. To measure the gradient exactly, we need every example in the data. Imagine a million examples, an illustrative number. One step would mean a million small computations. So we cheat, and the cheat works. We take a random handful of examples, a mini-batch, say sixty-four, and measure the gradient on those alone. That is about fifteen thousand times cheaper.

The price is a little noise. Each batch has its own slightly different valley, so the arrow is slightly wrong. The marble steps toward the bottom of this batch's valley, then toward the bottom of the next one. It trembles. But on average the arrows point the right way, and each step is so cheap that the marble gets there far sooner, for the same amount of work. This is stochastic gradient descent. Its distant ancestor is a paper by Robbins and Monro, in nineteen fifty-one, about hunting for a target with noisy measurements. Near the bottom, the marble keeps trembling. That is one more reason to shrink the step late in training.

Variants exist. The one you will meet everywhere is called Adam, by Kingma and Ba, from twenty fourteen. It keeps running averages of past gradients and uses them to set the step. Same loop, smarter steps.

If you followed the Quantum series, episode 13.1 uses this very loop: a noisy loss, and a classical computer stepping down it. And one question is left open. Where does the gradient of a million knobs come from? Chapter three answers it, with backpropagation.

To sum up. Gradient descent steps against the gradient, again and again. The learning rate sets the step: too small never arrives, too big jumps the valley. A mini-batch gives a noisy but cheap gradient, and that is how large models are trained. Next time: probabilities, softmax and cross-entropy.

PLATE 2.54:19

Probabilities, softmax and cross-entropy

A softmax turns raw class scores into probabilities by exponentiating each score and dividing by the total, so the best score gets the biggest slice while every class keeps a share. Surprise is minus the log of the probability given to the truth. Cross-entropy is the average of that surprise, so classifying means minimizing it, and a confident mistake costs far more than honest doubt.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the map room. So far, the numbers we computed were free to be anything: positive, negative, huge. Today we meet numbers that must behave. By the end of this video, you will know how a model turns raw scores into probabilities, and the one formula that grades them.

A probability is a number between zero and one. A list of probabilities, one per possible answer, adds up to exactly one, because something has to happen. Picture a loaded die with three faces: cat, dog and bird. Cat comes up most often, dog less, bird rarely. Roll it again and again, and the counts follow the list. That is sampling. The Quantum series does the same with a measurement: in episode 2.3, the Born rule gives the list, and nature rolls the die.

But a model does not hand us probabilities. A classifier ends with raw scores, one per class. Say two for cat, one for dog, and zero point one for bird. Scores can be any number, negative ones included, and they add up to nothing in particular. We need a machine that turns any list of scores into a list of probabilities.

That machine is the softmax. It works in two steps. First, raise e to the power of each score. Every number becomes positive, and the gaps stretch: the biggest score grows the most. We get seven point four for cat, two point seven for dog, one point one for bird. Second, divide each by the total, eleven point two. Now the three numbers add up to one: sixty-six percent cat, twenty-four percent dog, ten percent bird. In NumPy, that is the exponential of z, divided by its sum.

Why soft? A hard maximum would give everything to cat and nothing to the others. The softmax gives the best score the biggest slice, but every class keeps a share. The name goes back to a 1990 chapter by John Bridle.

Now the answer is revealed: it was a cat. How well did the model do? Here we borrow from the Information series, the notion of surprise. An outcome with probability p has surprise minus the log of p. In base two, the unit is the bit. Cat had sixty-six percent, so the surprise is only zero point six bits, a short ribbon. Had the truth been dog, two bits. Had it been bird, three point three bits. The thinner the slice, the longer the ribbon that unrolls.

That number is the cross-entropy loss for one example: minus the log of the probability the model gave to the right answer. Nothing else enters. The model is graded only on the slice it gave to the truth. Over a batch, we average. Four examples cost zero point six, two, one, and zero point one five bits. The average is zero point nine four bits. That average surprise is the cross-entropy. Libraries use the natural log, so zero point six bits shows up as zero point four two. Only the unit changes.

Look at the extremes. A model that splits its bets equally between three classes is surprised one point six bits, whatever happens. A model that is confident and right is almost not surprised at all. And a model that is confident and wrong? Give cat a score of zero point one, dog a four, and bird a half. Cat now gets two percent, and the surprise is five point seven bits. The loss punishes a confident mistake much harder than honest doubt. And the loss is smooth, so the gradient from the last episodes can follow it: training pushes the slice of the truth up, and the ribbon down.

That is the big idea. To classify is to minimize surprise.

This formula will come back in disguise. In chapter 7, the loss called InfoNCE, introduced in 2018 with contrastive predictive coding, is a cross-entropy over a handful of images, where the right class is the twin of the one you are holding. Same softmax, same minus log.

To sum up. The softmax turns raw scores into probabilities. Surprise is minus the log of the probability. Cross-entropy is the average surprise of the right answer, and classifying means making it small. Next time: the artificial neuron.
