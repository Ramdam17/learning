Block averaging · The honest first look

Cutting the recording into epochs around each block, aligning them on onset and subtracting a baseline lets the response survive while noise averages out, shrinking with the square root of the trial count. The average needs an uncertainty band, both hemoglobins and a window chosen in advance. It hides variability, overlap, change over time and unequal trial counts, so single epochs should be examined too.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 5, Making Sense of It All. The signal is clean. Now we want answers. And the first answer is the simplest one you can compute — an average.

Here is one clean channel from a finger tapping run. 20 blocks of tapping, each one followed by rest. On a single block the response is hard to see. The brain is there but so is everything we couldn't remove.

So we cut the recording into epochs. Each epoch starts a few seconds before a block begins and ends long after it stops, so the whole response, including its return to baseline, fits inside. Then we line them up on the onset, like sheets of riso paper on a light table.

Before stacking, one small step, baseline correction. For each epoch, take the average of the seconds before the onset and subtract it. Now every epoch starts from zero and slow offsets between trials disappear. year.

Now stack an average. Watch what happens. The response is the same in every epoch so it survives. The noise is different every time so it cancels out. With 4 epochs the noise shrinks by half. With 16 by a factor of 4. The noise falls with the square root of the number of trials. That's why trial counts matter so much. Doubling the precision costs 4 times the trials.

And don't show the average alone. Draw its uncertainty, a band of one standard error or a confidence interval around the mean. And always plot both hemoglobins. Oxygenated rising, deoxygenated falling, a little smaller and a little later. If they rise together, remember episode 2.2 and suspect the body, not the brain.

Then you need a number. Here, decide before you look. Will you take the mean over a fixed time window, say from 5 to 15 seconds after onset or the peak value. A window is more stable. The peak is noisier and if you choose it by looking at your data it's biased. Write your window into your analysis plan.

From each participant you take that number per condition, per channel, or per region. Then you test them across participants. That's the group level and we'll come back to it in episode 5.3.

Now, what the average doesn't tell you. First, variability. Behind one smooth curve, the trials may be wildly different. Some participants respond strongly, some not at all. Plot the single epochs in light ink behind the mean and look at them.

Second, overlap. If trials come every few seconds, the responses pile on top of each other, because each one lasts 15 to 20 seconds. The average of overlapping responses is a blur. you can't tell which trial caused what. Your baseline is contaminated too, because the previous response hasn't finished. Block averaging works when trials are well separated. For fast, jittered designs, you need a model that can untangle the overlap. That's the general linear model next episode.

Third change over time. People habituate, get tired, or learn. An average over the whole session hides that. If it matters for your question, compare the first and second half.

And fourth trial counts. If you rejected more trials in one condition than in another, the two averages don't have the same noise level. Report how many trials went into every average.

Block averaging is the honest first look. It makes no assumption about the shape of the response. It shows you what is actually in your data before any model tells you what it expects. Even if your final statistics use a model, always look at the averages first.

So cut into epochs, correct the baseline stack in average. Show the uncertainty and both hemoglobins. Choose your window in advance and remember what the mean hides. Next time, the general linear model built by hand.

PLATE 5.24:53

The GLM, built by hand

The general linear model is built column by column: data equal a design matrix times betas plus noise. Each condition's onsets are convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response, and drifts and short channels join as nuisance regressors. Least squares fits the betas and contrasts give t-statistics, but autocorrelated fNIRS noise and motion outliers inflate false positives, which prewhitening with robust regression corrects.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, averaging hit a wall. When responses overlap, you can't cut them apart. Today, we build the tool that can. The general linear model. And we build it by hand, one column at a time.

The idea fits in one line. The data equals a design matrix times a set of weights called betas plus noise. The design matrix is your prediction, what the signal should look like if the brain did exactly what you expect. The betas tell you how much of each prediction is really in the data.

Column 1. Start from the timing of your task, a mark at every onset of condition A held for as long as the stimulus lasts. Now we ask, what would the blood do? We convolve these marks with a canonical hemodynamic response, a standard shape borrowed from fMRI that rises for a few seconds, peaks, and returns. Each onset launches its own little response, and where they overlap they simply add up. That's the key assumption, the system is linear. And it's exactly what lets the model untangle overlapping trials.

Column 2, the same for condition B with its own onsets. Each condition gets its own column and therefore its own beta.

Then come the columns you don't care about but must include. A constant for the mean level. Slow drifts modeled with a few smooth curves instead of filtering them out. And from episode 4.4, your short channels and auxiliary signals as nuisance regressors. Their job is to soak up everything that isn't brain.

Now the fit. Ordinary least squares searches for the betas that make the weighted sum of columns as close as possible to the data. Think of each beta as a slider. Push the slider for condition A, and its predicted response grows. Move the drift slider and the baseline bends. The best fit is where the leftover, the residual, is as small as it can be.

What comes out is one beta per column. Beta A is the amplitude of the response to condition A in the units of your data. Then you ask a question with a contrast. Is beta greater than 0? Is beta greater than beta B? The answer is a T statistic, the contrast divided by its standard error.

A word on that canonical shape. If the real response is slower or delayed, a fixed shape will underestimate it. There's an alternative, deconvolution or finite impulse response models. Instead of one column per condition, you use many, one for each time bin after onset. The model then estimates the whole response shape, without assuming it. It's more flexible but costs more parameters, so it needs more data. Block averaging is actually a special case of this, when trials don't overlap.

And now the catch. That standard error assumes the noise is independent from one sample to the next. In fNIRS it isn't. Slow physiology like Mayer waves and breathing is sampled 10 times per second, so neighboring points are strongly correlated. You think you have 6,000 independent samples, but you have far fewer. The standard error comes out too small, the t value too big, and false positives pile up. On top of that, motion adds rare, huge outliers, far heavier tails than a normal distribution.

The fix has a name, AR- IRLS, from Barker, Aarabi, and Huppert in 2013. First, an autoregressive model learns how each noise sample depends on the previous ones. It's used to whiten both the data and the design matrix, so that what remains is closer to independent noise. Second, iteratively reweighted least squares, a robust regression that gives outliers less and less weight at each pass, a cousin of the trick behind TDDR. In their experimental data, ordinary least squares reached false positive rates as high as 37% at a nominal 5%. With the new method, they came down to between 5% and 9%.

A later comparison by Santosa and colleagues found that the best performing combination was exactly what we've built today. Short channels as nuisance regressors inside a robust, prewhitened general linear model. Tools like the NIRS Brain AnalyzIR Toolbox and MNE-NIRS offer autoregressive noise models. Use them and please look at the fit. Plot the model on top of the data and plot the residuals. If the residuals still show your task, your model missed something.

So, columns for your conditions, convolved with a response shape. Columns for drifts, short channels, and everything else. Fit read the betas, test a contrast, and never trust ordinary least squares with fNIRS noise. Next time, from one brain to a group.

PLATE 5.34:20

From one brain to a group

Group analysis runs the general linear model per participant, then tests the betas across people with a t-test or a mixed-effects model with a random intercept. Regions of interest must be chosen before seeing the group map to avoid double dipping. Multiple comparisons call for Bonferroni or false discovery rate correction, and reports should include effect sizes, individual data points and exclusions.

TRANSCRIPTEN

So far, every answer came from one person. But nobody publishes one brain. Today we go from one brain to a group and we meet the two big traps waiting there, choosing where to look and testing too many things at once.

Here's the classic two-level approach. Level 1. For each participant, run the general linear model and keep their betas, one per channel, per hemoglobin, per condition. Level 2, put those betas side by side and ask whether across people, the effect is reliably different from zero or different between conditions.

The simplest version is a t-test across participants, on one beta per person. It works, but it treats every participant as equally reliable, and it needs everyone to have every value.

A linear mixed effects model is more flexible. It models the effect of condition and adds a random intercept for each participant. Everyone gets their own baseline level, and the model estimates the effect shared across people. It copes with missing channels, unequal numbers of trials, several sessions per person, and covariates like age. In MNE-NIRS, the group tutorial does exactly this, with a mixed model fitted channel by channel.

Now where do you look? There are two ways. Channel by channel, test every channel separately. You keep spatial detail, but each channel is noisy, and the same channel number doesn't land on exactly the same piece of cortex in every head. Or regions of interest, average a few channels covering one area and test the region. You gain signal, you lose detail, and you run far fewer tests.

But here's the rule. The region must be chosen before you see the group result. From an atlas from a previous study, from your hypothesis, or from an independent localizer. What you must not do is look at the group map, pick the brightest channels, and then test those same channels. That's double dipping. It's circular, the selection already guarantees the result.

The second trap, multiple comparisons. Imagine a montage with 40 channels and a brain that does absolutely nothing. Test each channel at 5% and you expect two false positives. The chance of at least one is about 87%. And you probably tested both hemoglobins and several contrasts. Your map will light up by chance alone.

So you correct. The Bonferroni correction divides your threshold by the number of tests. It controls the chance of even one false positive, but it's very strict and you lose real effects. The false discovery rate from Benjamini and Hochberg controls something more forgiving, the expected proportion of false discoveries among the channels you call significant. Sort the p-values from smallest to largest, compare each one to a rising threshold, and keep everything below the last crossing. It's the most common choice in fNIRS.

Watch the map. Before correction, channels everywhere. After correction, a handful remain clustered where the motor cortex is, which is a good sign. Report how you corrected and over how many tests, channels hemoglobins and contrasts.

And significance is not the end of the story. A p-value tells you whether an effect is distinguishable from zero, not how big it is. So report effect sizes, the beta itself in micromolar with its confidence interval and a standardized measure like Cohen's D. Remember that small samples tend to overestimate effects, especially the ones that just crossed the threshold. And show the spread, one dot per participant, not just a bar.

A few last habits. Report how many participants you recruited, how many you excluded, and why. Report how many channels survived quality control in each person, because a group map built on missing channels is thinner than it looks. And plot the individual results next to the group, if one person drives everything you want to know.

So, betas from each person, a mixed model across people, regions chosen in advance, a correction for every test you ran, and effect sizes with their spread. Next time, oxygenated, deoxygenated, or both.

PLATE 5.44:39

HbO, HbR, or both?

Four scenarios show how to read oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin together: the classic mirror pattern earns high confidence, oxygenated alone or both rising call for caution about scalp and body physiology. Papers favor oxygenated hemoglobin because it is larger, yet deoxygenated is often cleaner and tracks BOLD closely. Report both chromophores, count both in corrections, or declare a primary one in advance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

fNIRS gives you two signals for the price of one, oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin. And yet many papers only show one. Today, what each one tells you how to read them together, and why you should report both.

Let's look at four scenarios from one channel after a task. For each one we'll ask, what does it suggest and how confident can we be?

Scenario 1, the classic. Oxygenated hemoglobin rises. Deoxygenated hemoglobin falls smaller and a little later. This is the signature of neurovascular coupling from episode 2.2, more fresh blood than the neurons need, washing the deoxygenated blood away. It's the pattern you hope for and the one you can trust the most.

Scenario 2. Oxygenated rises, deoxygenated barely moves. It might be real activation, with a deoxygenated response too small to see above the noise. It is usually much smaller than the oxygenated one. But it might also be the scalp or the body, because systemic physiology affects oxygenated hemoglobin more strongly. So this one deserves medium confidence. Check your short channels and check the other channels. Is the same thing happening everywhere?

Scenario 3 Both rise together. More blood, but not the oxygenation pattern of activation. Remember the clue behind CBSI? In the brain, they move in opposite directions, and when they move together, think of motion, blood pressure, or skin blood flow. Low confidence for a neural interpretation, until you rule those out.

Scenario 4 The Rest Sometimes the pattern is inverted. Oxygenated falls, deoxygenated rises. That can be a genuine decrease in activity like a deactivation, but systemic changes can do it too. And sometimes nothing happens at all. That's not proof that the region was silent. With too few trials or participants, a real effect can hide in the noise. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

So why do so many papers show only oxygenated hemoglobin? Because it's bigger. Larger amplitude, better contrast to noise, more significant results. A systematic review of 660 fNIRS papers, from 2015, 2018, and 2021, found that reporting oxygenated hemoglobin alone was the most common practice in every year reviewed. In 2015, about half the papers reported only that, and fewer than half reported both.

But bigger doesn't mean more specific. Studies that recorded fNIRS inside an MRI scanner tell an interesting story. Strangman and colleagues in 2002 found oxygenated hemoglobin correlated best with the BOLD signal, and noted this may reflect its better contrast to noise. Other studies like Huppert and colleagues in 2006 found deoxygenated hemoglobin matched BOLD best, both in time and in space. And physically, the BOLD signal is driven by deoxygenated hemoglobin. Add the fact that the scalp contaminates oxygenated hemoglobin more, and you get the picture? Oxygenated is the louder signal, deoxygenated is often the cleaner one.

That's why the community's best practice guidelines from Yücel and colleagues in 2021 recommend showing both chromophores with the statistics for both. And if you leave one out, say why.

There are also combinations. Total hemoglobin the sum follows blood volume. The difference oxygenated minus deoxygenated boosts the opposite pattern. And CBSI from episode 4.3 forces them to be mirror images. These can be useful, but they hide the individual chromophores, so report those as well.

Finally, testing both chromophores doubles your number of tests. So, as we saw in episode 5.3, include them in your correction. or decide in advance which one is primary, write it in your pre-registration and treat the other as supporting evidence. What you must not do is test both and then report whichever one worked.

So read the two curves together. Classic mirror high confidence. Oxygenated alone check the body. Both rising suspected. Nothing not proof of silence. Report both decide in advance and correct for what you test. Next time, connectivity when everything correlates with everything.

PLATE 5.54:52

Connectivity · When everything correlates with everything

Functional connectivity in fNIRS asks which regions fluctuate together, but everything correlates with everything. Global physiology, autocorrelation and motion create spurious links, which short-channel regression, prewhitening and robust statistics reduce. Wavelet coherence maps relatedness across frequency and time, needing a null distribution and a band chosen in advance, and a connection remains a statistical dependency, not a cable.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, we asked, where does the brain respond? Today, a different question, which regions fluctuate together? That's functional connectivity. And in fNIRS, it comes with a warning label, everything correlates with everything.

Start with a resting state recording. No task, the participant sits quietly for several minutes. The signals still drift up and down, slowly and spontaneously. In fMRI, it was discovered that these slow fluctuations are synchronized between distant regions, forming networks. fNIRS and diffuse optical tomography have found similar networks .

The simplest measure is the correlation between two channels. Pick a seed, a channel in the motor cortex, and correlate it with every other channel. Or compute every pair, and you get a correlation matrix. Keep the strongest links and you get a graph.

Now let's run it on our recording. The graph lights up everywhere. Nearly every channel is linked to nearly every other one. Beautiful and almost entirely misleading.

Trap number one, global physiology. The heartbeat, breathing, Mayer waves, blood pressure, and skin blood flow reach every channel at once. Two channels on opposite sides of the head will correlate, not because their neurons talk, but because they share the same blood supply. So we regress out the short channels and the global components. Watch the graph have the links evaporate. A simulation study by Lanka and colleagues found that removing components extracted from short channels inside a partial correlation model was the most effective way to reduce these spurious links.

Trap number two, autocorrelation, our old friend from episode 5.2. fNIRS signals are slow but sampled fast, so neighboring samples are not independent. Correlate two completely independent slow signals, and you will often find a correlation that looks significant simply because you overestimated how much data you really have. Santosa and colleagues showed that this alone produces high false discovery rates in fNIRS connectivity, even without any global physiology. Their fix will sound familiar, prewhiten the signals before correlating them.

Trap number three, motion. When the participant moves, many channels jump at the same time, and a single movement can create a huge correlation. Robust correlation methods downweight these outliers, and the same paper recommends them.

So, a statistically valid connectivity pipeline combines three things, prewhitening, robust statistics, and a correction for global physiology, ideally with short channels.

Next frequency. A single correlation coefficient mixes all frequencies together. Wavelet coherence splits it up. It shows how strongly two signals are related at each frequency and at each moment in time as a map. Bright where they move together, dark where they don't. It also gives the phase which signal leads and by how much. But read the map with care. Coherence around 0.1 Hz is mostly Mayer waves. Coherence at the heartbeat is the heartbeat. Focus on the frequency band that matches your question and decide that band in advance.

And always ask compared to what. Two unrelated slow signals still show some coherence by chance. So build a null distribution. Shuffle segments, randomize phases, or pair data that should not be related, and compare your real connectivity against it. We'll meet this idea again with pseudo pairs when we get to hyperscanning.

One more trap during tasks. If two regions both respond to the same stimuli, they will correlate, simply because of the shared task. If you want connectivity beyond the task, model and remove the evoked responses first.

Finally, what does an fNIRS connection actually mean? It's a statistical dependency between slow hemodynamic fluctuations. It is not a cable. A correlation has no direction, and even methods that estimate direction like Granger causality are fragile with hemodynamic signals, because blood vessels in different regions can respond with different delays. So describe your links for what they are, shared fluctuations after removing what you could.

So, connectivity asks what fluctuates together. Remove global physiology with short channels. Prewhiten, use robust statistics and compare against a proper null. Choose your frequency band in advance and describe a link for what it is. That closes chapter 5. Next chapter 6 going further staying honest. We start by turning channels into images.
