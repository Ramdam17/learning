Entropy vs entropy rate

A weather almanac records how often days are sunny or rainy, while a forecast uses today to predict tomorrow. The entropy describes the values and is blind to time, so shuffling the days leaves it unchanged; the entropy rate is the uncertainty of the present once the past is known. For a simulated alpha rhythm the gap between them is 2.66 nats.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to The Weather Station. This is Chapter 7, Information Dynamics.

Until now, every measure in this series looked at a bag of values. Time never entered the formulas. Today it does.

The question of this episode is simple. If you shuffle a signal in time, what changes and what does not?

Here is the intuition. A weather station keeps two kinds of records.

The first is the climate almanac. Over many years, half of the days in this town were sunny and half were rainy. That table of proportions is a distribution, and its entropy is one bit. If you know nothing else, guessing tomorrow's weather costs one bit.

The second record is the forecast. Weather is sticky. In our illustrative town, a sunny day is followed by another sunny day nine times out of ten, and a rainy day by another rainy day nine times out of ten.

Now, if you know today's weather, guessing tomorrow costs much less. The uncertainty that remains is the entropy of a 90-10 coin, about 0.47 bits.

The almanac still says 50-50. Nothing in the table of proportions changed. What changed is that we use the past. The forecaster did not learn anything about the climate. She learned something about the sequence.

Now shuffle the days of the year, as if someone dropped the diary and picked up the pages in random order. The almanac is identical, the same number of sunny days, the same number of rainy days. But the forecast is ruined. Today no longer says anything about tomorrow, and guessing tomorrow costs a full bit again.

That gap has a name. The entropy of the almanac is the entropy of the values. The cost of the forecast once the whole past is used is the entropy rate.

Here is the definition. For a stationary process, the entropy rate is the limit of the conditional entropy of the present, given more and more of the past. Cover and Thomas show that, for a stationary process, this limit exists, and that it equals the joint entropy of a long stretch divided by its length. In words, the entropy rate is the new uncertainty that each sample brings once everything before it is known.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate an alpha rhythm. It is an autoregressive process of order 2, sampled at 250 Hz with a spectral peak at 10 Hz. It is labelled simulated because it is. Because the process is Gaussian, every quantity in this picture has an exact formula, and every value is in nats.

On the left the signal and its histogram. On the right, the same samples shuffled in time. The trace becomes white noise. The histogram does not move by a single count. Same mean, same variance, same entropy, 4.08 nats in the arbitrary units of the simulation.

Now we compute the conditional entropy of the present, given one past sample, then two, then up to eight.

For the alpha rhythm, the curve drops fast. Given one past sample, the uncertainty falls to 2.69 nats. Given two past samples, it falls to 1.42 nats, and then it stays there. Adding more past changes nothing, because this process only remembers two steps. That plateau is the entropy rate. The exact value is 1.419 nats, the entropy of the random kick that drives the process at each step.

For the shuffled signal, the curve is flat. Every point sits at 4.08 nats. The past says nothing, so the entropy rate equals the entropy.

The gap between the two curves about 2.66 nats is what the past tells us about the present. Keep that number, in episode 7.3 it gets its own name, information storage.

So the entropy measures how spread the values are. The entropy rate measures how unpredictable the sequence is. The first is blind to time. The second is made of time.

What is the entropy rate sensitive to?

First, how much past you use. The curve only reaches the plateau if you give it enough history. With too little past, you overestimate the unpredictability. That choice is the whole topic of the next episode.

Second, the sampling rate. Sample the same alpha rhythm 10 times faster, and consecutive samples become almost copies. Each new sample brings less news, so the entropy rate per sample drops even though the brain did nothing different. In the notebook, upsampling our simulated alpha rhythm by a factor of 10 pushes the conditional entropy below zero nats with the same oscillation and the same histogram. Entropy rates per sample are only comparable at the same sampling rate.

Third, filtering. A low-pass filter makes consecutive samples more alike and lowers the entropy rate. Noise added after the fact raises it. Both are properties of the pipeline, not of the brain.

Fourth, for continuous signals, the units. The entropy rate of a continuous process is a differential entropy, so it carries every trap of episode 2.7. It shifts when you change units. The gap between entropy and entropy rate does not shift because it is a mutual information.

And fifth, stationarity. The definition assumes that the statistics do not drift. A recording that slides from rest into sleep has no single entropy rate.

Now the classic pitfall.

Someone wants to show that a signal is more regular in one condition than in another. They compute the Shannon entropy of the amplitude values in each condition, and they find no difference. They conclude that the dynamics are the same.

But the Shannon entropy of the values cannot see the dynamics. A perfectly regular sine wave and a shuffled copy of it have exactly the same histogram, and therefore exactly the same entropy. A signal can become much more predictable while its distribution of values stays identical.

The opposite trap exists too. Two conditions can differ in entropy only because the amplitude changed, while the predictability stayed the same.

If the question is about regularity or predictability, the quantity is the entropy rate, or one of its estimators. Approximate entropy and sample entropy in Chapter 10 are rough estimators of exactly this.

The notebook for this episode simulates the sticky weather chain and the alpha rhythm. It shuffles both, checks that the histograms are identical, and computes the conditional entropy for growing history lengths. You will see the plateau appear, and you can change the sampling rate to watch the entropy rate per sample move.

Let's recap.

The entropy of a signal describes its values. It does not change when you shuffle the signal in time.

The entropy rate is the uncertainty of the present once the past is known. It is the limit of the conditional entropy as the history grows.

For our simulated alpha rhythm, the entropy is 4.08 nats and the entropy rate is 1.42 nats. Shuffling brings the rate back up to the entropy.

The entropy rate depends on history length, sampling rate, filtering, and units, and it assumes stationarity.

How much past? · Embedding, delays, history length

A delay embedding turns one signal into vectors of past samples, set by a delay and a dimension, like a second pen drawing the same pressure late. Takens showed such vectors can reconstruct a deterministic system, but for noisy brain signals history length is chosen by prediction. On held-out data the score is U-shaped: too little past, or too little data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Part 1

How much past? — Part 1, the shape of the past.

Last time, we saw that the entropy rate is the uncertainty of the present once the past is known. But the past of a signal is infinite. In practice, we keep a few past samples spaced in some way. This episode is about that choice. Part 1 is about the geometry, and part 2 is about how to choose.

Here is the intuition. Picture an old barograph at the weather station. A pen draws air pressure on a slowly turning drum.

Now add a second pen that draws the same pressure, but three hours late. Plot one pen against the other, and the line on the drum becomes a shape on a page. Rising pressure, falling pressure, a storm passing, each kind of weather draws its own loop.

That is a delay embedding. From one signal, we build vectors. The value now, the value one delay ago, the value two delays ago, and so on. Each vector is a point. Over time, the points trace a trajectory.

Why would that work? Here is the remarkable result. In 1981, Floris Takens proved that for a deterministic system, delay vectors of a single measured variable can reconstruct the system's state space up to a smooth change of coordinates as long as enough delays are used. Sauer, Yorke, and Casdagli later showed that a dimension greater than twice the box-counting dimension of the attractor is enough, in a generic sense.

In words, one thermometer, read at several moments, can stand in for the whole weather system. Not a copy of it but something with the same shape.

Two numbers define an embedding. The delay tau, which is the spacing between the samples, and the dimension m, which is how many samples go into each vector. The history length the span of time covered is about m minus 1 times tau.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We take our simulated alpha rhythm from episode 7.1 sampled at 250 hertz with a peak at 10 hertz. It is labelled simulated. We plot each sample against the sample tau steps earlier.

With a delay of one sample 4 milliseconds, the points crowd onto the diagonal. Consecutive samples are almost copies. The trajectory is squashed, and the second coordinate adds almost nothing.

With a delay of 6 samples, about a quarter of the alpha period, the points open into a loop. The two coordinates now carry different information, like sine and cosine.

With a delay of 12 samples, about half a period, the loop collapses again, this time onto the other diagonal. The second coordinate is nearly a mirror of the first.

So the delay matters, and there are classic recipes for it. One is the first zero crossing of the autocorrelation. Another, from Fraser and Swinney in 1986, is the first minimum of the auto-mutual information between the signal and its delayed copy. That second recipe can see nonlinear dependence, which the autocorrelation misses.

For our Gaussian alpha rhythm, the two recipes agree. The autocorrelation crosses zero between 6 and 7 samples, and the auto-mutual information reaches its first minimum at 7 samples, 28 milliseconds. For a Gaussian signal, this agreement is expected because the auto-mutual information is a function of the autocorrelation alone.

Now the dimension. For deterministic systems, a classic method is false nearest neighbours, from Kennel, Brown, and Abarbanel in 1992. You add one dimension at a time. Points that were neighbours only because the trajectory was squashed fly apart when the new dimension appears. When almost no neighbours are false anymore, the dimension is enough.

What is the embedding sensitive to?

First, sampling rate. The good delay is a fixed span of time, not a fixed number of samples. Resample the signal, and the delay in samples must change with it.

Second, filtering. A narrow band-pass filter makes any signal look like a clean oscillation, and its embedding becomes a tidy loop, whatever the brain was doing. The geometry then describes the filter.

Third, noise. Measurement noise blurs the trajectory, and false nearest neighbours never fully disappears. Real EEG is never the deterministic system that Takens assumed.

And that is the classic pitfall of this part.

Takens' theorem is a statement about deterministic systems with no noise and with unlimited data. EEG is noisy, high-dimensional, and finite. A beautiful loop in a delay plot is not evidence of a low-dimensional attractor in the brain. Many claims of low-dimensional chaos in the EEG during the 1980s did not survive tests against stochastic surrogates, a re-examination published by Theiler and Rapp in 1996.

So for brain signals, we use the delay embedding differently. We do not ask whether we recovered the true attractor. We ask a more modest question, how much of the past helps predict the present. That turns the choice of embedding into a prediction problem, and prediction problems can be scored on data.

The notebook for this part builds delay embeddings of the simulated alpha rhythm with delays of 1, 6, and 12 samples, computes the autocorrelation and the auto-mutual information, and finds the first minimum.

Let's recap part 1.

A delay embedding turns one signal into a trajectory of vectors, defined by a delay and a dimension.

Takens' theorem says this can reconstruct a deterministic system's state space, given enough dimensions.

For our simulated alpha rhythm, the auto-mutual information reaches its first minimum at seven samples, about a quarter period, where the loop opens.

For noisy brain signals, the embedding is chosen for prediction, not for geometry.

In part two, we score history lengths on data, and the curve has the shape of a U.

Part 2

How much past? — Part 2, the U-curve.

In Part 1, we built delay vectors from one signal. Now we choose how many past samples to keep by asking how well each choice predicts.

Here is the intuition. The forecaster at our weather station keeps a logbook. To forecast tomorrow, she can read the last page only or the last two pages or the last 30.

Read too few pages, and she misses patterns that unfold over several days. Her forecast is worse than it could be, and she thinks the weather is more unpredictable than it is.

Read too many pages, and a different problem appears. To use 30 days of history, she must find past stretches of 30 days that look like the last 30. In a logbook of a few years, there are almost none. Her forecast rests on one or two old matches, and it becomes noisy.

So the quality of the forecast as a function of history length has the shape of a U. Too little past on one side, too little data on the other.

There are two well-known ways to score this.

The first is the Ragwitz criterion, from Ragwitz and Kantz in 2002. For each candidate dimension and delay, you predict each next value as the average of what happened after its nearest neighbors in the embedding space. You keep the setting with the smallest prediction error. This criterion is built for stochastic processes with finite memory, which is a better description of brain signals than a deterministic attractor. TRENTOOL, a MATLAB toolbox for transfer entropy, uses it to choose embeddings.

The second is the approach of David Darmon, which Paul Rapp's notes on information dynamics build on. You estimate the full predictive density of the next value given the past, with kernels, and you score each history length by its negative log predictive likelihood on data held out from the fit. In his 2016 paper, Darmon chooses the order by block cross-validation of that score. The score is in nats, and on held out data it estimates an entropy rate plus the cost of the model's mistakes.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate a noisy Hénon map written in delay form. Each value depends on the two previous values plus a small random kick. So the true memory is exactly two samples. It is labelled simulated because it is.

We train the Ragwitz predictor on 300 points, then on 3000, then on 30000, and we test it on 2000 new points. We average over 10 repetitions. The error is the root mean square error divided by the standard deviation of the signal.

With one past sample the error is about 0.3, whatever the amount of data. One sample is not enough to know where the system is.

With two past samples the error drops sharply. 0.14 with 300 points, 0.09 with 3000, 0.06 with 30000. Two is the true memory.

Then the curves rise again. With 300 points and 10 past samples, the error reaches 0.67, worse than using a single sample. The neighbours in 10 dimensions are no longer close, so the forecast is built from strangers. With 30,000 points, the rise is much gentler, and 10 samples only bring the error to 0.13.

The left side of the U is set by the system. The right side is set by the data.

The Darmon score tells the same story. With 300 training points, the negative log predictive likelihood is lowest at 2 past samples, and it climbs steeply after 5. With 3,000 points, the bottom flattens between 2 and 5 past samples. More data makes longer histories affordable, and the fixed kernel width of our simple version also plays a role.

What is this choice sensitive to?

First, the number of samples as the curves just showed. A history length chosen on a long recording may be too long for a short one.

Second, the sampling rate. Upsample the signal and the same span of time needs more samples, which pushes the right side of the U earlier. Choosing the delay first in units of time helps.

Third, the estimator. The best history length for a nearest neighbour predictor is not necessarily the best for a kernel density or a linear model. Report which one made the choice.

And fourth, the condition. If two conditions get different history lengths, a difference in entropy rate may come from the embedding, not from the brain.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study picks a history length once from a habit or a paper and uses it for every participant in every condition. With short recordings, that history may sit far up the right side of the U. The estimate then mixes the brain's unpredictability with the estimator's lack of data.

The opposite pitfall is just as common, one past sample because it is quick, that sits on the left side of the U and it overestimates the entropy rate, and it underestimates every quantity built on the past, such as information storage and transfer entropy.

The fix is to score the history length on held out data, report the curve, and when comparing conditions, either use the same setting everywhere or show that the result survives a range of settings.

The notebook for this part simulates the noisy Hénon map, computes the Ragwitz prediction error for 1 to 10 past samples at 3 sample sizes, and computes a simple Darmon-style score with a kernel predictive density on held-out data.

Let's recap.

The quality of a forecast as a function of history length has the shape of a U, too little past on the left, too little data on the right.

The Ragwitz criterion scores embeddings by nearest neighbour prediction error. The Darmon approach scores them by negative log predictive likelihood on held out data.

On our simulated Hénon map, whose true memory is two samples, both scores find two with small data, and longer histories become affordable only with much more data.

Report the history length, the criterion that chose it and the amount of data it was chosen on.

Information storage · AIS and predictive information

Information storage measures how much of the present was already in the past: large for a regular tide clock, small for wind gusts. Active storage links the recent past to the next value, excess entropy links the whole past to the whole future, and sharper resonances store more. Storage is not complexity, since band-pass filtering white noise manufactured 8.55 nats without computing anything.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Information storage — How much of the present was already in the past?

In Episode 7.1 we saw a gap between two curves, the entropy of a signal and its entropy rate. For our simulated alpha rhythm, that gap was about 2.66 nats. Today that gap gets its name.

Here is the intuition. On the wall of the weather station hangs a tide clock. It has one hand and it points to high tide, low tide, and everything in between.

The tide clock works because the sea is regular. If you know where the water was over the last few hours, you can say almost exactly where it will be in the next minute. Almost all of the present is already contained in the past.

Now compare with the wind gusts outside. You can watch the last few hours of gusts as carefully as you like. The next gust still comes as a surprise. Very little of the present was in the past.

The amount of the present that the past already contains is called information storage. The tide stores a lot. The gusts store very little.

There are two classic ways to measure it.

The first is the active information storage, introduced by Joseph Lizier, Mikhail Prokopenko, and Albert Zomaya in 2012. It is the mutual information between the recent past of a signal, its last k values, and its next value. In words, how much does the past tell us about the next sample?

The second is older and broader. The excess entropy, also called predictive information when it is computed between a long past and a long future. Crutchfield and Feldman, and Bialek, Nemenman and Tishby studied it around 2001 to 2003. It asks how much the whole past tells us about the whole future, not just the next step.

Lizier and colleagues describe the active storage as the part of the stored information that is in use for computing the next state. The excess entropy is the total.

There is a simple link with the previous episodes. When the history is long enough, the active information storage equals the entropy of the signal minus its entropy rate. Everything the values could have surprised us with, minus what still surprises us once we know the past.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate four rhythms all at 250 Hz, all built around the same 10 Hz resonance, labelled simulated. They differ in one thing, how sharp the resonance is. The softest one has no real peak at all, and barely looks like an oscillation. The second has a broad bump. The third is the alpha rhythm of episode 7.1, with a peak about 1.6 Hz wide. The sharpest has a peak less than half a Hz wide and looks almost like a pure sine.

Because the processes are Gaussian, we can compute everything exactly with 4 past samples in nats.

For the softest rhythm, the active storage is 0.49 nats. For the broad bump, 1.85 nats. For the rhythm of episode 7.1, 2.66 nats, the same gap we saw then. And for the sharpest rhythm, 3.35 nats.

The excess entropy computed between 8 past and 8 future samples is larger each time, from 0.52 up to 5.31 nats. The total storage always contains the part in use.

And for white noise, the storage is zero. The past says nothing about the present.

As a cross-check, a KSG estimate on 12,000 samples of the 7.1 rhythm with two past samples gives 2.48 nats against 2.59 for the exact Gaussian value, close and slightly below, as expected for a nearest neighbour estimator at this level of dependence.

So the more regular the rhythm, the more information it stores. A sharp alpha peak means the next sample is largely fixed by the last few. That is the first strong point of today. A very regular alpha rhythm stores a lot of information.

What is the storage sensitive to?

First, the history length. Too few past samples and storage is underestimated. This is the left side of the U-curve from episode 7.2.

Second, the sampling rate. Sample faster and consecutive samples become nearly copies, so the storage per sample climbs. Compare storage only at the same sampling rate.

Third, the filters. A narrow band pass filter makes any signal more regular, and therefore increases its storage. We will come back to that in a moment.

Fourth, the units. Good news here, storage is a mutual information, so it does not change when you rescale the signal, convert microvolts to volts, or apply any invertible transformation to the values. All the traps of differential entropy from episode 2.7 cancel out.

And fifth, the estimator. For non-Gaussian data, a Gaussian estimator only sees the linear part of the storage. A nearest neighbour estimator sees more at the price of bias.

Now the classic pitfall. It is the second strong point of today. Storage is not complexity.

Take pure white noise. Its storage is exactly zero. Now pass it through a narrow band pass filter between 8 and 12 hertz, the kind of filter used to extract alpha. Compute the active storage again with four past samples.

It jumps to 8.55 nats, more than three times the storage of our simulated alpha rhythm. And with eight past samples, it climbs to 16.3 nats. A narrow band signal is almost perfectly predictable from its recent past, whatever produced it.

Nothing in that filtered noise computes anything. The storage was manufactured by the filter. The same is true of the tide clock, enormous storage, no computation at all.

So when a study reports that one brain region or one condition has higher information storage, ask two questions. Was the signal band pass filtered before the measure, and with which filter? And would a simpler explanation, such as a narrower oscillation or a cleaner signal produce the same difference.

High storage can come from a meaningful memory in the dynamics. It can also come from a sine wave, a filter, or a high sampling rate. The measure alone cannot tell them apart.

The notebook for this episode simulates the four rhythms, computes the active storage with a Gaussian estimator, the excess entropy from covariance determinants, and the KSG cross-check. Then it filters white noise and watches the storage appear.

Let's recap.

Active information storage is the mutual information between the recent past of a signal and its next value. Excess entropy is the information shared by the whole past and the whole future.

With enough history, the active storage equals the entropy minus the entropy rate.

In our simulations, the sharper the resonance, the larger the storage: from 0.49 to 3.35 nats.

Storage is not complexity. Band-pass filtered white noise stored 8.55 nats, more than the alpha rhythm without computing anything.

PLATE 7.48:48

Trip meter vs speedometer · Local measures

A trip meter gives one value for a whole trip, a speedometer one value per moment. Removing the averaging from information measures gives local values that can be negative, misinformative moments when the past pointed the wrong way, yet their average recovers the global measure exactly. A sliding window yields only a series of trip meters, not a speedometer.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Trip meter vs speedometer — Information moment by moment.

So far in this chapter, every measure gave one number for a whole recording — one entropy rate, one storage. Today, we split that number into moments.

Here is the intuition, and it comes from Paul Rapp, who uses it in his teaching. Picture the dashboard of a car.

The trip meter tells you that you drove 120 km in 2 hours — 60 km per hour on average. It is a summary of the whole window.

The speedometer tells you how fast you are going right now. 50 at the traffic light, 110 on the highway, 0 in the queue.

From the speedometer, you can always rebuild the trip meter. Average the speed over the trip. From the trip meter, you can never rebuild the speedometer. 60 on average says nothing about when you stopped and when you sped.

Information measures have both kinds. Everything we computed until now was a trip meter. Local measures are the speedometer.

Here is the trick that makes them possible. Most information quantities are averages. Entropy is the average surprise. Mutual information is the average of a log ratio. Active storage and transfer entropy are averages too.

If you remove the averaging, you get a value for each moment. For the mutual information, the local value at one moment is the log of the probability of what happened given the other variable, divided by its probability without it. Joseph Lizier, Mikhail Prokopenko, and Albert Zomaya formalised local measures of information dynamics, local transfer entropy in 2008, and local storage in 2012.

And the defining property is the one of the dashboard. Average the local values over time and you recover the global measure exactly.

Now the surprising part. A local value can be negative.

Go back to our sticky weather town from episode 7.1. A sunny day is followed by another sunny day 9 times out of 10. The almanac says 50-50.

Today is sunny and tomorrow is sunny again. Knowing today raised the probability of what happened from one-half to nine-tenths. The local storage is the log in base 2 of nine-tenths over one-half, about plus 0.85 bits. The past helped.

Today is sunny and tomorrow is rainy. Knowing today lowered the probability of what happened from one-half to one-tenth. The local storage is the log of one-tenth over one-half, about minus 2.32 bits. The past pointed the wrong way. Lizier and colleagues call such a moment misinformative.

Average them with their frequencies 9 tenths and 1 tenth, and you get about 0.53 bits. That is exactly 1 bit, the entropy of the almanac, minus 0.47 bits, the entropy rate. The global storage rebuilt from its moments.

The average can never be negative because it is a mutual information. Its moments can.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate a signal that switches regime every two seconds. It alternates between a regular alpha rhythm with a sharp 10 Hz peak and a looser one with a much softer resonance. 60 seconds at 250 Hz, labelled simulated.

We compute the active storage with four past samples with a Gaussian model fitted once to the whole recording. The global value of the trip meter is 2.24 nats.

Then we compute the local storage at every sample, under that same model. That is the speedometer. It is a jagged trace, sample by sample.

Average it over the full 60 seconds, and it returns 2.24 nats to the last digit.

Average it over the regular blocks only, and it gives 2.79 nats. Over the looser blocks, 1.70 nats. The trip meter could not have told us that the storage rises and falls with the regime. The speedometer does.

And the local values go negative. About 2.6% of the samples have negative local storage overall. In the looser blocks about 4%. In the regular blocks only about 1%. A few are as low as minus 12 nats. Moments where the recent past made the next value look very unlikely, and it happened anyway.

There is one subtlety here. The probabilities behind every local value were estimated on the whole recording. A local value says how one moment fares under the global model. It is not a new estimate made from that moment alone, which would be impossible from one sample.

What are local measures sensitive to?

First, everything the global measure is sensitive to, history length, sampling rate, filters, estimator. The local values inherit all of it.

Second, the model. Change the estimator or the window used to fit the probabilities and the local values change, sometimes more than their average does. Two estimators that agree on the trip meter can disagree on the speedometer.

Third, noise. A single sample is noisy by nature. The local trace is far noisier than any average, so a peak at one sample is weak evidence. It becomes interesting when local values are pooled over many moments of the same kind, such as all the regular blocks or all the samples after a stimulus.

And fourth, the sign convention. Negative local values are real and informative. Truncating them at zero or taking their absolute value breaks the link with the global measure. The average of truncated values is no longer the mutual information.

Now the classic pitfall.

Someone wants a time-resolved measure, so they slide a window of one second along the recording, and they estimate the global storage in each window. They call the result local storage.

But a sliding window gives a series of trip meters, not a speedometer. Each window is a fresh estimate with very few samples, and so with all the bias problems of Chapter 5. On pure white noise where the true storage is zero, one-second windows give a small positive storage on every window, about 0.008 nats on average. That bias is small here only because the Gaussian estimator has few parameters. Binned or nearest neighbour estimators in short windows do much worse. On our switching signal, the windows scatter widely, with a standard deviation of 0.87 nats, and their average 1.83 nats is not the global value of 2.24. Each window refits its own model so the windows do not add up to the trip meter, and the values from overlapping windows are not independent.

The local measure of Lizier and colleagues works the other way around. The probabilities are estimated from all the data, which keeps the bias low, and only the evaluation is done moment by moment. Both approaches can be useful, but they are not the same thing and the methods section should say which one was used.

The second half of the pitfall is the one of the dashboard itself. A study reports only a global value and then discusses when the information happened. The global value cannot say. Only local values or a design that compares conditions can.

The notebook for this episode builds the sticky weather chain and checks the local values plus 0.85 and minus 2.32 bits. Then it simulates the switching signal, computes the local storage, verifies that its average equals the global value and compares the regular and loose blocks.

Let's recap.

A global measure is a trip meter, one value for a whole window. A local measure is a speedometer, one value for each moment.

The average of the local values recovers the global measure exactly. The global value never recovers the local values.

Local values can be negative, misinformative moments where the past pointed the wrong way.

On our switching simulation, the global storage was 2.24 nats, 2.79 in the regular blocks and 1.70 in the loose ones.

A sliding window is a series of trip meters, not a speedometer.

PLATE 7.511:33

Local vs specific entropy rate · Surprise vs forecast

The local entropy rate looks back and measures how surprising an observed value was, while the specific entropy rate looks forward and measures how uncertain the next value is. For a particle hopping between two dips, both rise near transitions, but the largest local spikes come from isolated surprises while the specific rate stays low. One spike is one surprise, not a change of state.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Part 1

Surprise vs forecast. Part 1, two speedometers.

In the last episode, we split information into moments. Today we meet two moment-by-moment versions of the entropy rate. They sound alike. They answer opposite questions. Part 1 sets them side-by-side on a simulation. Part 2 is about estimating them and about how they mislead.

Here is the intuition. Every evening the weather station does two things.

Before midnight, it issues a forecast for tomorrow. Sometimes the forecast is confident, clear skies almost certainly. Sometimes it is wide open, a front is arriving it could go either way. The width of that forecast is the uncertainty about a future that has not happened yet.

The next evening the station checks the rain gauge and asks a different question. How surprising was what actually fell, given what we knew yesterday? A downpour on a day forecast as clear is a big surprise. The same downpour on a stormy day is not.

The first question looks forward. The second looks back. One is about uncertainty before the fact. The other is about surprise after the fact.

These are the two quantities of today. Both come with a time stamp, like the speedometer of the last episode.

The local entropy rate looks back. At each moment, it is minus the log of the predictive density of the value that was actually observed, given the specific past just before it. It measures how surprising this observation was. It is the local measure of Lizier and colleagues, applied to the entropy rate.

The specific entropy rate looks forward. At each moment, it is the entropy of the whole predictive distribution, given that specific past. It measures how uncertain the next value is, before we see it. David Darmon defined and estimated it in 2016.

In words, the local rate grades the observation. The specific rate grades the state.

And here is the link between them. Averaged over time, both of them return the same number, the entropy rate of chapter 7.1. Two different speedometers, one trip meter.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate a particle in a double well. Picture a valley with two dips separated by a low hill. The particle jiggles in one dip, and now and then the noise kicks it over the hill into the other dip. That is a transition. The system is labelled simulated, and we sample at every half time unit 3,000 samples with 38 transitions.

Because we built the system, we can compute both rates exactly by simulation. From each possible state, we launch 4,000 copies of the particle, let them run for one sample, and look at where they land. That cloud is the predictive distribution. Its entropy gives the specific rate for that state. Its density at the value that actually came next gives the local rate.

From the bottom of a dip, the copies stay close together. The forecast is sharp, its entropy is about zero nats.

From the top of the hill, the copies split. Some fall left, some fall right. The forecast is wide, even bimodal. Its entropy is about 0.56 nats, the highest of any state.

Now we follow the real trajectory and draw both rates under it.

The specific entropy rate is calm while the particle sits in a dip, and it rises each time the particle climbs towards the hill. Near the 38 transitions, it averages 0.19 nats. Away from them, 0.06. The forecast widens before and during every crossing.

The local entropy rate is spikier. Near transitions, it averages 0.39 nats because the particle often lands somewhere the forecast found unlikely. Away from transitions, it averages only 0.03.

But look at the biggest spikes. Of the top 1% of local values, about half happen far from any transition, while the particle sits quietly in a dip. At those moments the specific rate is low, around 0.05 nats. The forecast was confident, and an unusually large kick of noise broke it. A single surprising observation, not a change of state.

So near a transition, both rates rise. Between transitions, the local rate fires on isolated observations, while the specific rate stays low. That is the strong point of today. The local rate tells you that something unexpected happened. The specific rate tells you that the system was in a state where anything could happen.

And as promised, the two averages agree. 0.081 nats for the local rate, 0.077 for the specific rate, equal up to the Monte Carlo error of our simulation. One entropy rate, two ways of spending it over time.

Let's recap part one.

The local entropy rate looks back, how surprising was the observed value given its specific past.

The specific entropy rate looks forward, how uncertain is the next value given that past.

Both averaged to the entropy rate.

In our double well simulation, both rose near transitions. Between transitions, the largest local spikes came from isolated surprising observations, while the specific rate stayed low.

In part 2, we estimate both rates from data and see how they can mislead.

Part 2

Surprise vs forecast. Part 2. Estimating and Misreading.

In Part 1, we computed the local and specific entropy rates exactly, because we had built the system and could launch thousands of copies from every state. With a brain recording, you get one trajectory once. Everything has to be estimated.

Here is the intuition. At the end of the season, the weather station grades its forecasters on a scorecard.

To grade a forecast after the fact, you need a full forecast to grade against not just a single number. A forecaster who said about 12 millimetres cannot be graded on surprise. One who said most likely 12 but anywhere from 0 to 40 can. So both of today's rates need the full predictive distribution, not just a best guess.

And to build that distribution from one season of records, you have to find, for today's sky, the past days that looked most similar and pool what happened next. That is a nearest neighbour or kernel method in the embedding space of episode 7.2.

There are two families of estimators.

Darmon, in 2016, estimates the predictive density with kernels and chooses the history length and the kernel widths by cross-validation of the negative log likelihood. The specific rate is then the entropy of that estimated density at each state.

Gilpin, Darmon, Siwy, and Martens in 2018 used nearest neighbour estimators for both rates on a model of a conical nanopore, a tiny hole in a membrane whose ionic current flips between levels on its own. They report that both the local and the specific entropy rates increase around the fluctuations of the nanopore current. A different system, the same picture as our double well. Darmon also released a Python package for these measures, called sidpy.

Now, what are these rates sensitive to?

First, the history length. With too short a past, the state is ambiguous, and the specific rate is inflated everywhere. With too long a past, there are not enough neighbours, and the estimated forecasts become noisy. The U-curve of episode 7.2 applies to every value of both rates.

Second, the number of samples. The specific rate is an entropy estimated at every single state. In states the system rarely visits such as the top of the hill, there are few neighbours. That is precisely where the specific rate is most interesting and least reliable. Report how many neighbours supported each value or at least the total amount of data.

Third, the estimator and its bias. Nearest neighbour entropy estimates can go negative and differential entropies can be negative anyway, as episode 2.7 showed. A negative specific rate is not a bug. Comparing it across recordings with different units or scaling is.

And fourth, filtering and causality. If the predictive density is built from neighbours drawn from the whole recording, including the future, the result cannot be used in real time. That is a theme of the next episode.

Now the classic pitfalls. There are three.

The first, reading a local spike as a change of state. A single large local value says that one observation was surprising. In our double well, half of the largest spikes happened while the system sat quietly in a dip. A change of state shows up as a rise of the specific rate, sustained over several samples, or as a cluster of local spikes. One spike is one surprise.

The second, confusing the two rates in the methods section. The words local and specific are not interchangeable. One grades the observation after the fact, the other the state before it. A paper that says the time-resolved entropy rate rose before the event must say which one, because only the specific rate can rise before anything has been observed.

The third, treating either rate as a measure of the brain alone. Both depend on the model of the past that the estimator built. A different embedding, a different number of neighbours, or a different filter changes every value. When two conditions are compared, the estimator settings must be identical, and the comparison should survive a range of settings.

There is a positive message under these pitfalls. Used carefully, the pair is powerful. A rise of the specific rate flags states from which the future is open, which is exactly what one wants to detect before a transition, a lapse of attention, or the onset of a seizure. Whether brain signals show such warnings in a given paradigm is an empirical question to be tested against surrogates not assumed.

It checks that their averages agree, compares their values near and far from transitions, and lists where the largest local spikes occur.

Let's recap.

Both rates need a full predictive distribution, estimated from the past of a single trajectory with kernels or nearest neighbours.

Gilpin and colleagues found both rates rising around current fluctuations in a nanopore model, as in our double well.

The specific rate is least reliable exactly where it matters most, in rare states with few neighbours.

One local spike is one surprise, not a change of state. Say which rate you used. Keep the estimator settings identical across conditions.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Dynamical Neighbours — Cleaning a Signal with Its Own Past and Its Own Future.

In this chapter, Neighbours in state space helped us measure. They built forecasts, entropy rates, and storage. Today, the same neighbours help us clean a noisy signal. And they come with a catch about time.

Here is the intuition. Weather forecasters have an old trick called the analogue method. To guess tonight's weather, find the days in the archive whose weather maps looked most like today's, and look at what happened on those days. Edward Lorenz famously studied how well such natural analogues predict the atmosphere in 1969.

Now use the same trick to clean a record. Suppose the thermometer at the station is noisy. One reading looks odd. You could average it with the readings just before and just after it. Those are its neighbours in time.

Or you could find the moments in the archive when the weather was in the same situation, the same shape of the last few hours and average what the thermometer said at those moments. Those are its neighbours in state space, its dynamical neighbours.

The difference matters. Neighbours in time smear anything that changes fast. A sudden drop in temperature gets blurred into its surroundings. Dynamical neighbours share the same situation, so they share the same sudden drop, and averaging them keeps it while the noise cancels.

That is the idea behind simple nonlinear noise reduction from Thomas Schreiber in 1993. Build delay vectors as in episode 7.2. For each vector, find its nearest neighbours in the embedding space. Replace the central value by the average of the neighbours' central values. Schreiber used all neighbours within a fixed radius. Here we use a fixed number of neighbours instead.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulate the Lorenz system, a classic chaotic flow, and keep its first coordinate 6000 samples rescaled to unit variance. We add measurement noise with a standard deviation of 0.3. It is labelled simulated, and because we built it, we know the clean signal and can score every filter against it.

The raw noisy signal is off from the clean one by 0.30, in root mean square error.

First, neighbours in time, a moving average over 7 samples the same span as our delay vectors. The error gets worse, 0.47. The Lorenz signal turns quickly, and the moving average flattens every turn.

Second, dynamical neighbours. Delay vectors of seven samples and for each one the average over its nearest neighbours in state space, excluding its own neighbours in time. With 32 neighbours, the error falls to 0.20. The turns are kept and the noise is reduced by about a third.

How many neighbours should we use? Too few and the average is still noisy. Too many and the neighbours are no longer in the same situation and the average starts to blur the dynamics. Another U-curve.

In this simulation, we can read the best number from the truth. 128 neighbours give the lowest error, 0.18. With 512, the error climbs back to 0.23.

But with real data, there is no clean signal to score against. So we need a criterion that uses only the noisy data. Paul Rapp's notes choose the number of neighbours by splitting the data into two halves. Here is a version of that idea.

Take the first half as the archive and the second half as the test. For each test vector, hide its central value, find its neighbours in the archive using the other coordinates only, and predict the hidden value from the neighbours. Score the prediction against the noisy value that was hidden. The noise in that value cannot be predicted by anyone, so it adds the same amount to every choice and the best choice still wins.

On our simulation, this split-half criterion picks 32 neighbours. The truth would have picked 128. The criterion lands on the flat bottom of the U, with an error of 0.20 against a best possible 0.18. Close without ever seeing the clean signal.

Now the catch and the strong point of today.

Look again at where the dynamical neighbours come from. They come from anywhere in the recording, before the current moment and after it. And the delay vector around the central value contains samples that come after it.

So this filter uses the future. It is acausal. For offline analysis that is fine. For anything in real time such as neurofeedback, a brain-computer interface, or closed-loop stimulation, it is forbidden. At the moment you need the clean value, the future does not exist yet.

There is a causal version. Use delay vectors that end at the current sample. Search for neighbours only among vectors that ended before now. Replace the current value by the average of what came at the end of those neighbours.

But a causal filter starts with an empty archive. In our simulation, with the same 32 neighbours, the causal filter is worse than doing nothing during the first 250 samples, with an error of 0.51. Between 250 and 500 samples, it reaches 0.27. After 2000 samples, it settles at 0.21, close to the acausal filter.

That is the price of causality, a running-in period during which the filter has not yet seen enough of the system to find good neighbours. A real-time pipeline must either accept it or record a calibration period first and say so.

What is this filter sensitive to?

First, the embedding. The delay and dimension of episode 7.2 decide what counts as the same situation.

Second, the number of neighbours, which we chose with the split-half criterion. Report the criterion and the value it picked.

Third, the amount of data. The archive must have visited the current situation before. Rare states get poor neighbours, as with the specific entropy rate.

Fourth, the type of noise. The method assumes measurement noise on top of structured dynamics. If the brain signal is itself mostly stochastic, there is little structure for the neighbours to share, and the filter mostly smooths.

And fifth, what comes after. A filter that borrows from the future can create dependence on the future. If the cleaned signal is then fed into transfer entropy or any directed measure, the direction of time has been blurred before the measure even starts. Episode 6.8 showed a zero-phase filter doing exactly that.

Now the classic pitfall.

A pipeline is designed offline on recorded data with a zero-phase filter or a state-space filter, and it works beautifully. Then the same pipeline is moved to a real-time neurofeedback session. Either it cannot run because the future is missing, or it is quietly replaced by a causal version whose first minutes are worse than the raw signal and nobody reports the difference.

The fix is to decide at the design stage whether the analysis is offline or real time, to use causal filters for anything that will run live, to report the running in period, and to validate the causal version on recorded data before using it on a participant.

Let's recap.

Neighbours in time smear fast changes. Dynamical neighbours share the same situation, so averaging them keeps the dynamics and cancels the noise.

On our simulated Lorenz signal, the moving average made the error worse, from 0.30 to 0.47, while the state space filter reduced it to 0.20.

The number of neighbours can be chosen without the clean signal by a split-half criterion. Here it picked 32. Dynamical neighbours come from the future too. The filter is acausal and forbidden in real time. The causal version needs a running in period.
