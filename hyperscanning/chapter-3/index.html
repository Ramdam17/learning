A map of paradigms

Paradigms sit on a map with two axes: controlled to natural, and acting together to taking turns. Cooperation games, trust games, guitar duets, conversation and classrooms each occupy a different spot. The more natural a paradigm, the more sources of synchrony it mixes; the more controlled, the cleaner the conclusion but the further from real life.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the board-game café. Every table here hosts a different way of putting two brains in one study. Before we visit them one by one, we need a map.

Look at the floor from above. Five tables, five paradigms. Where would you put each one? Two questions are enough. The first is about control. Does the experimenter decide everything that happens, or do the two people do what they would do anyway? The second is about time. Do the two people act at the same moment, or do they take turns?

So the map has two axes. From left to right, controlled to natural. From top to bottom, acting together to taking turns.

First table, top left. Two people play a cooperation game on a computer, side by side. In a 2012 fNIRS study, Xu Cui and colleagues compared cooperation with competition. Coherence in the right superior frontal cortex rose during cooperation, and not during competition. The experimenter controls the screen and the task, and both players act at once.

Second table, bottom left. A trust game. One player sends money, the other decides how much to send back, and they take turns. In 2005, King-Casas and colleagues scanned both players at the same time, in two fMRI scanners, and published it in Science. The brain response linked to the intention to trust came earlier and earlier as the players' reputations developed. The rules are strict, and the turns are fixed.

Third table, in the middle. Two guitarists play a short melody together. In 2009, Lindenberger and colleagues recorded EEG from eight pairs. Phase synchronization between brains rose when the players set the tempo with a metronome, and again when they started to play together. The task is still set by the experimenters, but the music is real.

Fourth table, on the right, low on the map. Two people talk face to face. In 2012, Jiang and colleagues used fNIRS and found more synchronization in the left inferior frontal cortex during face-to-face dialogue. They did not find it when the two people talked back to back, or when each one spoke alone. Conversation has turns, so this table sits low. And the conditions keep a little control.

Last table, top right. A real classroom. In 2017, Suzanne Dikker and colleagues recorded EEG from twelve high-school students, over eleven classes in a semester. The more synchronized the group was, the more engaged the class was. Nothing is staged, and everyone acts at once.

Now light up the compass on each table. Remember the four sources. Stimulus, rhythm, prediction, body. This is an illustrative reading, not a measurement. At the controlled end, the experimenter has fixed the stimulus and the movements, so few routes stay open. At the natural end, everyone hears the same teacher, moves, breathes in the same room, and anticipates the others. All four routes stay open at once.

That is the idea of this chapter. The more natural a paradigm is, the more sources of synchrony it mixes together. The more controlled it is, the easier it is to conclude, but the further it is from real life. Choosing a paradigm is a trade between ecological validity and the power to conclude. There is no free table.

Neither end is the right one. A natural paradigm asks a richer question. A controlled paradigm gives a cleaner answer. The skill is to pick your table knowing what it costs, and to plan the control that will pay for it.

So here is the habit. For any study you read, find its table on the map. Then ask which routes it leaves open.

Next time, we sit at the first table. Cooperation and competition. Two players, one button, and a very good control.

PLATE 3.24:37

Cooperation and competition

In cooperation tasks, players press a key close together; in the competition version, the same lamp and buttons require pressing faster than the partner. Prefrontal coherence rose during cooperation only. Simulation shows the catch: faster, steadier duel presses can make competition look more synchronized with no social effect, so movements must be matched.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the board-game café. Today we sit at the first table. Two players, two buzzers, and one lamp in the middle.

Start on the team side of the table. When the lamp turns on, both players press their buzzer. The goal is to press together. The closer the two presses, the more points the team wins.

In 2012, Xu Cui, Daniel Bryant and Allan Reiss ran this game with fNIRS. Eleven pairs sat side by side and watched a screen. After a random delay, a go signal appeared, and each player pressed a key. In the cooperation task, the pair won a point when their two response times were close.

In 2011, Funane and colleagues used a cooperative timing task, also with fNIRS. Each player counted ten seconds, then pressed. They tried to press as close together as they could. When the activity of the two prefrontal cortices covaried more, the two presses were closer in time.

Now flip the table to the duel side. Same lamp. Same buzzers. Same go signal. But the goal changes. Press faster than the other player. In the 2012 study, that was the competition task.

Here is what they found. Coherence between the right superior frontal cortices of the two players rose during cooperation, and not during competition. And pairs with higher coherence cooperated better.

Why is this comparison elegant? Because both sides share almost everything. The lamp is the same, so source one, stimulus, is the same. The button press is the same, so source two, rhythm and movement, is similar. What changes is the goal. On the team side, each player has a reason to guess when the other will press. That is source three, mutual prediction. This is an illustrative reading, not a measurement.

But it only works if the movements are equivalent. In a duel, people press faster, and a faster press can drive a bigger brain response. The authors raised a related worry. The players sat side by side, so some of the coherence may come from reacting to the other's movement. In most cases, they covered the players' hands with a cloth, to limit that.

Let us test the worry in a simulation. Forty simulated pairs play both sides. In the first case, the presses are equally fast on both sides, and we plant no social effect. Team minus duel is minus zero point zero two, about zero.

In the second case, the presses are still equal, and we plant a small effect on the team side. Team minus duel is plus zero point zero nine. The contrast finds it.

In the third case, we plant nothing. But the duel presses are faster and steadier. Now team minus duel is minus zero point one seven. The duel looks more synchronized, and nobody influenced anybody. The movements did it.

Now look at the shelf. Two small game boxes. The first is the trust game. One player sends money, and the other decides how much to send back, round after round. In 2005, King-Casas and colleagues scanned both players at once, with two fMRI scanners. The peak of the brain response linked to the intention to trust moved earlier, by fourteen seconds, as reputations developed. This game favours source three, mutual prediction. The term hyperscanning itself comes from Montague and colleagues in 2002, who linked two fMRI scanners over the internet and showed data from a simple game of deception.

The second box is the prisoner's dilemma. Each player chooses, at the same time, to cooperate or to defect. In 2010, De Vico Fallani and colleagues recorded EEG from twenty-six pairs playing the repeated version. Pairs of two defectors had fewer links between their brains than the other pairs. Again, source three is at work.

What changes with the modality? With fNIRS, two people can sit side by side and press real buttons, as at the first table. With EEG, you follow the decision as it happens, millisecond by millisecond. With fMRI, the scanners are linked but apart, and the blood signal is slow, so the game is built in turns.

So here is the habit. When you read a cooperation study, ask three questions. Is there a competition control? Are the movements the same? And which of the four sources does the game leave open?

Next time, we move to the conversation table. Conversation and turn-taking. Who is speaking, and who is listening?

PLATE 3.35:04

Conversation and turn-taking

Conversation leaves stimulus, rhythm and prediction all open, since the listener hears the voice, speech moves jaw and breath, and sentences can be anticipated. A simulated listener trailing the speaker by three seconds shows a clear correlation peak at that lag. Pooling both roles blurs it into two humps, and a shared speech artifact adds zero-lag correlation, so roles must be labelled.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the board-game café. At this table, nobody is pressing a button. Two people are simply talking. Talking is the oldest way of putting two brains in step, and the hardest one to analyse honestly.

Here is the game. Scrabble, played out loud. One player says the letters of a word, and the other has to hear them and find the tiles. Each time a letter is spoken, it lights up in the listener's rack, a beat later. That small delay is the whole story of this episode.

First, what does this table measure? How one person's brain activity relates to the speech of the other. Which of our four sources does it favour? The first one, stimulus, because the listener receives the speaker's voice. And the second one, rhythm and movement, because speech has a rhythm, and the jaw, the lips and the breath move with it. Mutual prediction, the third source, is possible too. The listener can guess where the sentence is going.

Its natural control is a listener who hears the same speech without taking part in the conversation. Then the stimulus is the same, and the interaction is gone.

Now the studies. In 2010, Stephens, Silbert and Hasson recorded fMRI from speakers and listeners during natural verbal communication. They used the speaker's brain activity to model the listener's. The speaker's activity was coupled to the listener's, in space and in time. This coupling vanished when the participants failed to communicate. On average, the listener's brain mirrored the speaker's with a delay. But some areas showed anticipatory responses, and the more anticipation, the better the understanding.

A word of care. Speaker–listener coupling is the authors' own term, for a statistical link between two brains. It is not the body coupling of our fourth source.

In 2014, Silbert and colleagues had speakers tell a fifteen-minute narrative. They found that the networks for producing speech and for understanding it overlap extensively. And they found areas where activity is coupled across the speaker's and the listener's brains.

Other modalities, other windows. In 2012, Kuhlen, Allefeld and Haynes recorded EEG from speakers telling stories and from listeners watching the recordings. The listeners' EEG correlated with the speaker's EEG, peaking at a delay of about twelve and a half seconds. So the size of a lag depends on the signal and on the analysis, and not only on the brains.

Also in 2012, Jiang and colleagues used fNIRS. They found synchrony in the left inferior frontal cortex during face-to-face dialogue, and none during back-to-back dialogue, or during monologue. It came mainly from direct interaction, including turn-taking.

And in 2017, Pérez, Carreiras and Duñabeitia recorded EEG while pairs interacted through speech. Brain oscillations were synchronized between speaker and listener. This was partly explained by speech-to-brain synchronization, a low-level sensory mechanism, but not only. The interaction itself added something.

Let's look at the lag itself, in a simulation. Two simulated people take turns, twelve seconds each. Whoever listens follows the speaker with a delay that we built in: three seconds. We correlate the two signals at many shifts. When we analyse speaker to listener, the correlation peaks at a delay of three seconds, at 0.58. Zero shift gives only 0.10. The peak sits where the delay is.

Here is the moment that matters. In a real conversation, speaking and listening alternate. When A speaks, B follows A. When B speaks, A follows B. Pool both kinds of turns together, and the picture blurs. In the same simulation, we now get two humps, one at minus three seconds, 0.30, and one at plus three seconds, 0.27. The direction of the lag is lost, and the peak is half as high. The pooled number mixes two roles.

Now add a speech artifact. The voice, the jaw and the breath leave the same kind of trace in both recordings. In the simulation, we add one shared component. At zero shift, the pooled correlation jumps from 0.11 to 0.61. That is higher than the true peak. And it sits at no delay at all, so it looks like synchrony, but nobody followed anybody.

We will come back to these artifacts in episode 5.2. For now, the rule is simple. Label who speaks and who listens. Compute the link within each role. And ask what the shared voice is doing to both signals.

Back to the compass. In conversation, stimulus, rhythm and prediction all stay open, and the body can join in through breathing. Only the design, with its control, tells them apart.

PLATE 3.44:06

Music and joint action

Finger tapping is the minimal music paradigm. With a metronome, simulated tappers land together because both follow the clock, and their tap-to-tap intervals are uncorrelated. Without it, only the partner remains to follow, so intervals compensate each other, one longer and the other shorter, with a correction at the next tap, which marks mutual prediction.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The board-game café has a small stage in the corner. Tonight a guitarist and a percussionist are playing together. This table is the paradigm of music and joint action.

Music is a natural way to put two brains in step. The players hold one tempo, answer each other's phrasing, and watch each other's hands. That is a lot of sources at once. The sound is shared input. The beat is shared rhythm and movement. And each player predicts the other. Routes one, two and three, all mixed together.

So the field uses a minimal version. Two people sit side by side and tap a finger at a steady pace. Nothing else. It is a workhorse of the timing literature. In 2013, Repp and Su reviewed recent research on sensorimotor synchronization, from solo tapping to musical ensembles.

Start with a metronome. A pendulum sits on a stool, and MAESTRO looks after it. Both tappers follow the beat. Each tap lands a little early or a little late, and each person corrects on the next tap. The two are together because they follow the same clock. That is route one, the stimulus, shared input. And since both bodies move to the beat, route two is there as well.

Here are simulated tappers. Each makes one hundred and twenty taps, and we simulate two hundred pairs. With the metronome, a tap lands on average fifteen milliseconds from the beat. The two tappers are about twenty-one milliseconds apart. The pair looks tightly synchronized.

Now look at the intervals between taps. Correlate one person's intervals with the other's, and you get zero point zero one. The two are not following each other at all. Each one follows the clock.

Now MAESTRO picks up the pendulum and carries it away. This is the key experimental move. Without the metronome, nothing outside holds the tempo. The only thing left to follow is the partner.

Each tapper now corrects toward the other. A long interval from one is answered by a shorter one from the other, and the correction comes back. In the simulation, the two tappers are sixteen milliseconds apart. They are just as together as before. But the intervals tell a different story. At lag zero the correlation is minus zero point one four: when one interval is longer, the partner's is shorter. At lag one it is plus zero point one three: what one does at this tap shows up in the other at the next tap. The two trails now track each other. These numbers are simulated, and the effects are modest for any single pair.

This is route three, mutual prediction, and it is the logic of real experiments. In 2010, Konvalinka and colleagues had pairs tap while they heard each other, or only a computer. The abstract describes pairs that heard each other as a mutually adapting unit, with intervals shifting in opposite directions from tap to tap, and no leader and follower strategy. The authors propose that coordination depends on predicting the partner's next action and adapting within milliseconds.

In 2014 the same group recorded both brains with EEG. In one condition the partners adjusted to each other. In the other, each followed a computer metronome. Interactive tapping showed stronger suppression of alpha and low beta activity over motor and frontal areas. And in eight of nine pairs, an asymmetry in frontal alpha suppression matched which partner had led.

Let's read the compass. With the metronome, stimulus is open, and rhythm is open too. Without it, stimulus is controlled. Rhythm still needs care, because both people still move a finger in the same way. Prediction becomes the real candidate. In EEG, those finger movements also leave artifacts in both recordings, so the movement should be matched across conditions.

So remember one move. Remove the metronome, and the rhythm no longer comes from outside. The two must adjust to each other.

PLATE 3.55:03

Imitation, gaze and joint attention

Imitation, mutual gaze and joint attention are three ways to act together without words. Gaze acts like a switch, and a framed photo of a face keeps the stimulus but removes the live partner. In simulation, live gaze gives a phase locking value of 0.42 against 0.10 for the photo, while joint attention adds a shared object, itself a stimulus.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the board-game café. One table is playing a game of mimes. Nobody speaks. Two players face each other, and everything passes through the body and the eyes.

This episode follows three things people do together without words. They imitate. They look at each other. And they look at the same thing.

First, imitation. One player makes a gesture, and the other copies it, without being told to. In 2010, Guillaume Dumas and colleagues recorded dual EEG from nine pairs of people who spontaneously imitated each other's hand movements. They found that moments of interactional synchrony went with a network between the two brains, in the alpha-mu band, linking the right centroparietal regions. In the higher frequencies the link was asymmetrical, which the authors suggested could reflect the roles of model and imitator.

Now ask where that synchrony comes from. Imitation is movement, so route two, rhythm and movement, is certainly on the table. Two bodies move alike, and movement leaves its own traces in both recordings. But imitation is also a loop. The model moves, the imitator follows, and each one starts to expect the other. So route three, mutual prediction, is a candidate too.

Now the eyes. Between the two players, imagine a light switch. When they look into each other's eyes, it is on. When they look away, it is off. Mutual gaze is a switch of the interaction. It changes what the two people are doing together, while the task stays the same.

But how do we know the effect comes from a live person, and not just from seeing a face? Put a framed photo where the partner was. The same face is on view, the same eyes, but nobody looks back.

In 2017, Joy Hirsch and colleagues did exactly this with fNIRS. Nineteen pairs either looked into each other's eyes, or looked at a picture of a face. During live eye contact, activity rose in a left frontal region, and coherence between the two brains rose in temporal, parietal and motor areas. The increase was partner specific. It disappeared when partners were randomly reassigned.

Here is what that contrast isolates, in a simulation where we know the truth. In the live condition, looking at each other switches on a weak mutual coupling. In the photo condition, the face is shown, but nothing looks back. Over thirty simulated dyads, the live condition gives a mean phase locking value of 0.42. The photo condition gives 0.10. The only thing we built in is that someone looks back.

So the photo controls route one, the stimulus, and leaves route three, mutual prediction, standing alone. It is not a perfect control. A photo does not blink or move.

Third, joint attention. Both players look at the same object on the table, a die, say, and each knows that the other is looking. Here the object is a shared input, route one, and that is the trap. Two brains that see the same thing will look alike, even if the players never notice each other. What makes it joint is that each player follows the other's gaze to the object.

Two fMRI studies recorded pairs exchanging gaze. In 2010, Saito and colleagues found stronger inter-brain correlation in the right inferior frontal gyrus for real pairs than for non-paired people. In 2016, Koike and colleagues, again with fMRI, reported that inter-brain synchronization in the right inferior frontal gyrus increased once eye contact was established, and that the partners' eye blinks synchronized too.

What changes with the modality? In EEG, eye movements and blinks are themselves artifacts, so gaze conditions need careful cleaning. In fNIRS, two people can sit face to face, which is what Hirsch's live condition needed. In fMRI, both players lie in scanners, a long way from a café table.

One more result, with babies. In 2017, Victoria Leong and colleagues recorded dual EEG from adults and infants, on a screen and live. The adult's directed influence on the infant's brain activity was stronger when the adult looked straight at the infant than when the gaze was indirect. Seventeen infants took part in the screen experiment, and nineteen in the live one. We come back to parents and children in episode 3.7.

So, three games, three questions. Imitation brings in movement. Gaze is a switch, and the photo is the control that isolates a live partner. Joint attention brings in a shared object, which is a stimulus. Body coupling stays open in all three, because in these examples we have no body measure. For each one, ask the compass which routes are still possible.

PLATE 3.64:54

Learning and teaching

Teaching has a built-in asymmetry, with a teacher keeping a model of what the learner knows, so one-to-one tutoring favours mutual prediction while a whole class mostly shares the lesson. In a simulated class of 24, synchrony tracks engagement at 0.86 but test scores at only 0.46. Synchrony with the class marks attention, not learning, so learning must still be measured by a test.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the board-game café, the chalkboard menu has been wiped clean. Today it says: lesson. Under it sits a quiet table with two chairs, and beyond it, a row of small desks. This is the table for learning and teaching.

Teaching is a paradigm with a built-in asymmetry. One person knows, one person is learning, and the knowledge is meant to travel from one head to the other. So what do we measure? The synchrony between a teacher and a learner, and then between a learner and the rest of the class.

Start with the quiet table. Ava teaches, Otto learns, one to one. Which of the four sources is at work here? Route one is present, because both of them look at the same material. Route two is present, because speech has a rhythm. But the source this paradigm is built for is route three, mutual prediction. A good teacher keeps a model of what the learner already knows, and picks the next step from it.

A functional near-infrared spectroscopy study tested exactly this. Zheng and colleagues, in Human Brain Mapping in 2018, scanned teacher and student pairs together. Better teaching outcomes went with higher synchrony between the teacher's right temporal-parietal junction and the student's anterior superior temporal cortex, and only when the teacher's activity came first. The teacher led, and the student followed.

Another near-infrared study, by Pan and colleagues, published in NeuroImage in 2020, asked what the teacher actually does. Brain-to-brain coupling was correlated with learning outcomes. It was driven by scaffolding, such as asking a guiding question or giving a hint. It was absent when the instructor simply explained, with definitions and clarifications.

The natural control follows from the design. Compare the same pair across two ways of teaching, and compare real pairs with shuffled pairs who saw the same lesson. Without that, a shared lesson alone could produce the effect.

Now walk to the row of desks. A whole class, recorded with mobile electroencephalography. Dikker and colleagues did this in 2017, in Current Biology: twelve high school students, eleven classes over a semester. Synchrony across the students predicted how engaged they were. Here the main source is route one, because everyone hears the same teacher at the same time. The natural control is the format of the lesson: the same class, taught by video or by lecture.

That is what Bevilacqua and colleagues did in 2019, in the Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, with twelve students and six classes. Synchrony with the teacher depended on how close the student felt to the teacher, but only during lectures.

In 2023, in Psychological Science, Davidesco and colleagues recorded nine groups, each with four students and a teacher. Alpha-band synchrony, eight to twelve hertz, between students predicted test performance. Synchrony between students and teacher predicted it at a lag of about three hundred milliseconds.

What changes with the modality? In a class, we use wireless electroencephalography, which tolerates a classroom but picks up every movement and every word spoken. In a tutoring session, near-infrared recording is more tolerant of movement, but slower: it follows blood flow, not electrical rhythm.

Here is the key idea, shown on a simulated class of twenty-four students. Each desk holds a slate. When a student is in step with the class, the slate lights up yellow. Engaged students follow the shared lecture, so their slates light together. Disengaged students drift.

Plot class synchrony against engagement, and the points line up. In this simulation the correlation is 0.86. Synchrony with the class follows engagement.

Now plot synchrony against the test score. The cloud is much looser. The correlation is 0.46, so synchrony accounts for about one fifth of the differences in score. The rest comes from what students already knew before the lesson, and from noise.

This is why synchrony does not prove learning. In real data the picture is mixed. In one classroom study, retention correlated with closeness to the teacher, but not with synchrony. In another, synchrony between students did predict test performance. Whatever the result, learning has to be measured by a test, before and after. We come back to this in episode nine point two.

Let's recap. Teaching favours mutual prediction one to one, and the shared lesson in a class. Natural controls are shuffled pairs and different teaching formats. Synchrony with the class follows engagement. It is a marker of attention, not a measure of learning.

Next, the table by the window: parent and child.

PLATE 3.74:54

Parent and child

Parent and child is the paradigm where all four sources of synchrony overlap, as songs are shared, bodies touch, and the parent adapts to the baby. Natural controls are a screen between them or a stranger in place of the parent. Young brains also need their own settings: a child's alpha rhythm sits at another frequency, and in simulation the adult band hides the coupling.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the board-game café. At the low table by the window, a parent sits on the floor with a baby on their lap. A few tables along, an older child works on a puzzle with the same parent. Parent and child is the paradigm people ask for most, and the one that breaks the most habits we learned on adults.

First, the baby. In 2017, Leong and colleagues recorded EEG from an adult and an infant at the same time. The adult sang nursery rhymes, either looking at the infant or looking away. In two experiments, with seventeen and then nineteen infants, the adult's brain activity influenced the infant's more strongly when the gaze was direct.

In 2018, Wass and colleagues recorded twelve-month-old infants and their parents, during solo play and joint play. The parents' theta power tracked the changes in their infant's attention. So at this table, the parent adjusts to the baby all the time. That is route three, mutual prediction.

But it is not the only route. The song is shared, so route one is open. The parent rocks and bounces, so route two is open. And the baby sits against the parent's chest, so route four is open too. All four sources are on this table at once.

Now the puzzle. In 2020, Nguyen and colleagues recorded fNIRS from forty-two mothers and their preschool children. Together, they solved a tangram puzzle. Or each of them solved it alone, with a screen in between. Synchrony was higher in prefrontal and temporo-parietal regions when they worked together, and it predicted success at the puzzle beyond what their behaviour alone explained.

The screen is a natural control. Same puzzle, same pieces, same task. Only the interaction is taken away.

A second control swaps the parent for a stranger. In 2018, Reindl and colleagues had children play a cooperative and a competitive game, once with a parent and once with an adult stranger, while recording fNIRS from both. Synchrony appeared in prefrontal cortex when the child cooperated with the parent. It was not significant in parent–child competition, nor in either game with the stranger.

Same child, same game, a different adult. That isolates the relationship. But a stranger also differs in familiarity, in voice, and in the way they hold and talk to a child. So the stranger fixes the task, and changes the partner. It does not change only one thing.

Here is the same logic in a simulation. Forty parent–child dyads with a real coupling, and forty stranger–child dyads with none. All of them do the same task, and receive the same task-driven input. The phase locking value in the child's band is about 0.48 for parent and child, and 0.05 for the strangers. The task alone did not make them synchronise.

Now the problem of two different heads. Three things change when one brain is young.

First, frequency. Marshall, Bar-Haim and Fox followed twenty-nine children from five months to about four years of age. They found an alpha-range rhythm between six and nine hertz, and its peak frequency rose with age.

The usual adult alpha band runs from eight to twelve hertz. Filter a child in the adult band, and you cut through the middle of their rhythm. In our simulation, the same forty coupled dyads give 0.48 in the child's band, and 0.05 in the adult band. Nothing changed but the filter.

Second, the head. Beauchamp and colleagues measured the distance between scalp and brain in seventy-one children, from newborns to twelve years old. The distance was smallest in the youngest children, and grew with age, up to double the newborn value. Optical signals fade with distance. So in fNIRS, an age difference can be a distance difference, and a probe layout built for adults cannot be assumed to reach the same tissue in a child.

Third, movement. Babies wriggle, reach, and lean into their parent. Movement shakes caps and optodes. It also creates its own shared rhythm, when the parent rocks the child.

And one practical constraint. A parent consents for the child, the child is asked to assent, and either of them can stop at any time, so the design must survive a session that ends early.

So parent and child is a paradigm where sources are hard to separate, because bodies touch, songs are shared, and one brain follows the other. The screen and the stranger are the two natural controls. And the bands, the optodes and the movement must be chosen for the child, not borrowed from the adult.

PLATE 3.84:45

Clinicians, patients and teams

Therapists, patients and teams cannot be randomized or replayed, so each pair is compared with itself, starting from a baseline neutral session. In simulated dyads, synchrony rose by 0.26 over eight weeks, while stable dyads wobbled about 0.05 between sessions. For crews, a quiet phase of the same flight and mismatched pairs serve as controls.

TRANSCRIPTEN

On the wall of the board-game café, tonight, hangs a long strip of calendar pages. In the middle stands a big table. This is the paradigm of clinicians, patients and teams.

Here the two people are not equals. A therapist sits with a patient. One helps, and one is helped. The stakes are real, and nobody can replay the session with a different partner.

Start with counseling. In 2018, Zhang and colleagues used fNIRS with thirty-four clients. Each client had forty minutes of either counseling, with one of three professional counselors, or casual chatting. Working alliance was stronger during counseling, and so was synchrony over the right temporoparietal junction. That synchrony also tracked the strength of the bond.

In 2020, Ellingsen and colleagues put patients with chronic pain and their clinicians in two separate fMRI scanners, linked by live video. The clinician treated pain evoked in the patient. Pain relief was linked to nonverbal mirroring and to matching brain activity. And the brain coupling appeared only in pairs who already had clinical rapport.

A third route needs no brain recording at all. In 2011, Ramseyer and Tschacher measured body movement in one hundred and four videotaped psychotherapy sessions, from seventy patients. Synchrony was higher in real sessions than in shuffled pseudo-interactions. It was also higher in sessions rated high in relationship quality, and in therapies with greater symptom reduction.

Now the problem. In a clinic you cannot randomize who meets whom. You cannot swap in a stranger as a control, and you cannot play Tuesday again. So the control has to be thought differently.

One answer is to compare each dyad with itself over time. This is the session diary on the café wall. Page zero is grey. It is a baseline session, a neutral conversation before treatment. Then comes one page per week.

Here are twelve simulated dyads, with one baseline and eight weekly sessions. At baseline, the dyads already differ a lot. Synchrony runs from zero point one two to zero point seven four. Comparing one dyad with another would mostly measure who they are. But look at each dyad against its own page zero. By week eight, synchrony is up by zero point two six on average, and it is higher than baseline in all twelve dyads.

A second group of twelve simulated dyads stayed stable. Their change averages minus zero point zero one, and only four of twelve ended above their baseline. From one session to the next, a stable dyad wobbles by about zero point zero five. That wobble is the yardstick for a real change. These numbers are simulated, and a rise alone does not say where it comes from.

Now the team. A big shared table holds a map, and each person has a role badge of a different size. Pilots, a care team, a sports team. In 2016, Toppi and colleagues recorded EEG from airline pilot crews, in a full-motion flight simulator. Six crews flew together. Connections between the two brains were densest in the most cooperative phases, and linked mainly frontal and parietal areas. During cruise, when the pilots worked independently, they fell close to zero.

That gives two controls without any replay. The quiet phase of the same flight is a baseline inside the crew. And formal couples, a captain and a first officer from different crews who never flew together, play the role of pseudo-pairs. Real crews showed higher density than formal couples in the cooperative phases.

For care teams and sports teams, body signals are lighter to wear than scanners. In 2017, Palumbo and colleagues reviewed interpersonal autonomic physiology. Synchrony appeared in new and established relationships, across many contexts. But the literature was fragmented, which makes consistency hard to judge.

Let's read the compass. In a consultation, the two people share a room, so stimulus is rarely controlled. Rhythm and movement, mutual prediction and body coupling may all be present. A baseline session removes who the pair is. It does not remove what changed in the room, like the topic of the week. So the baseline must be matched to the sessions.

So remember one move. When roles are unequal and the stakes are real, compare each pair with itself. A baseline first, then the weeks that follow.

Choosing your control condition

A synchrony value of 0.3 means nothing without a comparison, and the control defines the question. Alone versus together isolates the partner, live versus a picture isolates reciprocity, face to face versus back to back isolates the exchange, cooperation versus competition isolates the goal, and a pseudo-partner isolates the partner from shared timing. No control closes every route, so they are chosen first and combined.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the board-game café, the table is set. Between the menus and the dice sits a tray of small bottles. Each bottle is a control condition. Today we learn which one to pour.

Here is the problem. Ava and Otto play a game together, and we measure a synchrony value between their brains. The number comes out at, say, zero point three. Is that high? Is that low? On its own, it says nothing. A number only means something next to another number.

That other number is the control condition. It is the recipe we compare against. And here is the idea to keep for the whole episode. The control defines the question. Change the control, and you ask a different question about the same data.

Let's open the bottles one at a time. The first is alone versus together. Ava does the same task by herself, and then with Otto next to her. The task, the screen and the movements are the same. What changes is that a partner is there. So this bottle isolates the partner, and tells us what the task alone could not explain.

The second bottle is live versus recorded. Hirsch and colleagues, in NeuroImage in 2017, used near infrared spectroscopy. They compared two people looking at each other's eyes, live, with two people looking at the eyes of a picture of a face. The eyes were the same visual input. The live person could answer back. So this bottle isolates reciprocity: the fact that the other person responds to you.

The third bottle is interaction versus observation. In 2012, Jiang and colleagues, in the Journal of Neuroscience, had pairs talk face to face. They compared this with talking back to back, and with a monologue. Synchrony rose in the left inferior frontal cortex only in the face to face dialogue. Taking away the view of the partner took the effect away. So this bottle isolates the exchange of signals between two people.

The fourth bottle is cooperation versus competition. Cui, Bryant and Reiss, in NeuroImage in 2012, asked pairs to play a computer game side by side, once together and once against each other. Inter-brain coherence in the right superior frontal cortex rose during cooperation, but not during competition. The screen and the button presses are alike. Only the goal changes. So this bottle isolates the shared goal.

The fifth bottle is a real partner versus a pseudo-partner. We keep every person's recording, but we pair it with someone who did the same task at another time. The task timing, the stimulus and the rhythm are all still there. Only the partner is wrong. If the real pair beats the pseudo-pairs, then the shared timing alone cannot be the whole story. This is the null distribution of the series. We come back to it in chapter seven.

Now look at the compass of sources. Each bottle can close some routes. A pseudo-pair closes the routes of shared stimulus and shared rhythm. A live versus picture contrast closes the stimulus too, because the eyes are identical. But no single bottle closes everything. That is why serious designs often pour two or three.

So here is the rule. Choose the control before you choose the paradigm. Ask yourself what you want to say. Want to show that a partner matters? Compare with alone, or with a pseudo-partner. That reciprocity matters? Compare live with a picture or a recording. That the goal matters? Compare cooperation with competition, with matched movements.

If you pick the paradigm first, you may discover too late that it has no honest control. A button game has an easy pseudo-pair. A free conversation has no shared timing to match. You pick the sauce before you cook the dish.

Let's recap. A synchrony value needs something to be compared with. The control condition defines the question. Alone isolates the partner, live versus picture isolates reciprocity, face to face versus back to back isolates the exchange, cooperation versus competition isolates the goal, and a pseudo-partner isolates the partner from the shared timing. No bottle closes every route, so combine them, and choose them first.
