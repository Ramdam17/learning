PLATE 16.15:02

A link that says something

Don Swanson read two piles of papers, on Raynaud's syndrome and on fish oil, and noticed that neither cited the other, yet both pointed to blood viscosity. A fact is a triple: subject, named directed relation, object. RDF, Google's Knowledge Graph and Wikidata follow that shape. Earlier measures describe how such a graph is wired, but not what it means.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the apothecary. Every jar has a label, and every label says what is inside.

In 1986, an information scientist named Don Swanson read two piles of medical papers. One pile was about Raynaud's syndrome, where small arteries spasm and blood flow drops. Its papers kept returning to thick blood, platelets that clump, and vessels that squeeze too hard.

The other pile was about fish oil. Its papers kept reporting thinner blood, less clumping, and calmer vessels.

Swanson noticed that neither pile cited the other. He called it undiscovered public knowledge: facts that were published, and never joined.

Now draw each idea as a dot, and each finding as a line. Fish oil joins three properties of blood. Raynaud's joins the same three. Between them runs a path of length two, and nobody had walked it.

Swanson published the idea. In 1989, a trial gave thirty-two patients either fish oil or olive oil. It found an effect in patients with the primary form of the condition, and not in the secondary form.

Look at those lines again. None of them is a number. Each one is a statement: fish oil lowers blood viscosity.

Take a plain sentence. Aspirin treats migraine.

Cut it in three. The first piece is the subject: aspirin. The last piece is the object: migraine. The piece in the middle is the relation: treats.

Together, the three pieces make a triple.

Subject and object become nodes. The relation becomes an edge, and the edge has a name. It also has a direction, as in episode one point two: aspirin treats migraine, and not the other way round.

Now take real triples, from Wikidata. Ibuprofen, medical condition treated, migraine. Migraine, symptoms and signs, nausea.

Whenever two triples share a word, they share a node. Add a few more, and a graph grows by itself.

Notice the colours. In episode one point four, a node was one of two kinds. Here a node can be a drug, a condition, a symptom, a gene, a person. The name of the edge usually tells you which kinds sit at its two ends.

People wanted a common way to write such triples. In February 2004, the W3C made RDF a recommendation. RDF writes every fact as a triple, and names every thing with a web address, so two sources can talk about the same thing.

In May 2012, Google announced its Knowledge Graph, with a slogan: things, not strings. A string is only letters. A thing is a node that many sentences can share. Google said it already held more than five hundred million objects, and three and a half billion facts.

Wikidata opened in October 2012, as a free graph that anyone can edit. Today it holds about one hundred and twenty-four million items.

Here is what changes. Until now, a link was a number. A distance, a strength, a correlation. You could average two of them: two and four make three.

Now a link is a sentence. What is the average of treats and interacts with? There is no such thing. You cannot average two sentences.

Look at the same graph twice. First, forget the words. Only the shape remains. Migraine has four links, the most. Ibuprofen has three. Every measure from chapters six to nine still works on this shape.

But the shape counts a symptom, a treatment and a warning as the same thing: a line.

Now read the words. Migraine has three symptoms and one drug. Ibuprofen and aspirin both treat pain, and Wikidata also records a significant interaction between them. That is a statement about the two drugs, and no measure on the shape can read it.

Aspirin's own page shows it better. It links to other items one hundred and twenty-five times. Twelve links name a condition it treats. Fifty-nine name a significant drug interaction. Five name a discoverer or inventor. The number one hundred and twenty-five adds them together, and the sentence behind each link is lost.

Back to Swanson. Fish oil lowers blood viscosity, and Raynaud's goes with high blood viscosity. Change one word, so that fish oil raises it. The shape is identical. The path still has length two. The meaning is the opposite.

So the measures of the earlier chapters describe how a graph is wired. Reading a graph that says things needs more.

To sum up. A fact is a triple. Subjects and objects are typed nodes, and relations are named, directed edges. RDF, Google's Knowledge Graph and Wikidata all follow that shape. You can measure the shape, but not the meaning.

Schemas and ontologies

A schema, written before the facts, names the kinds of node and what each relation may join, so aspirin treats Tuesday is refused. An ontology arranges concepts, each pointing to a more general one. In the Gene Ontology, 14,673 of 38,092 terms have two parents or more, so the hierarchy is a directed acyclic graph, not a tree.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended with a question. Before anyone writes a single fact, who decides what may be said?

Here is the problem. Nothing stops a careless writer from adding two more triples to the apothecary's graph. Migraine treats aspirin. Aspirin treats Tuesday.

Both are well-formed triples, with a subject, a relation and an object. Both are nonsense.

So a knowledge graph starts with rules, written before the facts. That list of rules is called a schema.

A schema says which kinds of node exist. In our apothecary there are three: drugs, conditions and symptoms.

It also says what each relation may join. Treats goes from a drug to a condition. Has symptom goes from a condition to a symptom. Interacts with goes from a drug to another drug.

Look at that list again. That list is a graph. Three nodes, three arrows. A small graph that describes the shape of a big one.

Now check the triples against it. Aspirin treats migraine. A drug, then a condition. It fits.

Take the triple migraine treats aspirin. A condition stands where a drug should be. The schema refuses it.

The last one was aspirin treats Tuesday. Tuesday is no kind of thing the schema knows. Refused again.

Remember the dictionary of episode two point one. The list of allowed words decided which ladders could exist. The schema does the same job. It decides which facts can exist.

That choice has a price. Go back to Swanson. Fish oil lowers blood viscosity. Try to write it in our schema.

There is no kind for a blood property. No relation is called lowers. The sentence cannot even be written.

To say it, the schema must grow. One new kind, a substance. One more kind, a blood property. One new relation, lowers, from a substance to a blood property. Only then does Swanson's sentence fit.

Choosing the schema is choosing what you will be able to say.

A schema lists kinds. An ontology goes one step further. It arranges the meanings, and says which concept sits under which.

Here is a real one, the Human Phenotype Ontology. Robinson and colleagues published it in 2008, to describe the features of hereditary diseases. The release read here, from September 2026, holds 19,894 terms.

Each term points to a more general term. Migraine points to headache. Headache points to abnormal nervous system physiology. Then comes abnormality of the nervous system, then phenotypic abnormality, and at the very top a term named simply All.

That is five steps from migraine to the top.

Every arrow goes up, and no climb ever returns to where it started. That is a directed acyclic graph, as in episode five point two. Because there is no cycle, more general has a clear meaning.

Two other ontologies show the range. In 2000, the Gene Ontology Consortium published the Gene Ontology, a shared vocabulary for what genes do. Its July 2026 release holds 38,092 terms.

In 2011, Poldrack and colleagues published the Cognitive Atlas. It organizes the concepts of cognitive neuroscience, and links each concept to the tasks that measure it. Today it lists 918 concepts and 859 tasks.

Now the question that matters. Is such a hierarchy a tree?

In a tree, as episode two point five said, every node hangs by one thread, and two nodes are joined by exactly one path.

Count the parents in the Gene Ontology. Of its 38,092 terms, 23,416 have one parent. Three are the roots, and have none. The other 14,673 have two parents or more. That is thirty-eight and a half percent. One term has nine.

Take visual learning. It is a kind of visual behavior. It is also a kind of associative learning. Two parents.

Now climb from it, by every road. There are four ways to the top. They take four steps, six steps, six steps, and eight steps. A tree would offer a single road.

The other two ontologies do the same. In the Human Phenotype Ontology, 3,846 terms have two parents or more, which is 19.3 percent. In the Cognitive Atlas, declarative memory is a kind of memory, and also a kind of long-term memory.

This is the family tree of episode two point five all over again. The name says tree. The graph has several roads to the top.

I checked the whole Gene Ontology file. Start from any term, follow the arrows, and you never come back. The longest climb in it has sixteen steps.

To sum up. A schema names the kinds of node, and what each relation may join. An ontology also arranges concepts, each pointing to a more general one. That arrangement is a directed acyclic graph, not a tree. And whatever the schema leaves out cannot be said.

PLATE 16.35:25

Asking questions

A question for a graph is a small graph with holes, and its answers are every place it fits, listed as a table. From the known node nausea, two lookups answer the toy question instead of forty-nine placements. On Wikidata, SPARQL returned 251 fits. Finding patterns is hard in general, but small patterns anchored on a known node and named links stay fast.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the apothecary. Last time ended with a question. How do you ask a graph for something you do not know yet?

Start with a plain question. Which drugs treat a condition that has nausea as a symptom?

Look at what the question holds. One thing is known: nausea. Two things are not: the drug, and the condition.

Now draw it. What you know becomes a node, with its name written on it. What you do not know becomes an empty node. Call it a hole. Between the nodes, draw the relations the question names.

A drug treats a condition. The condition has the symptom nausea. Two arrows, one known node, and two holes.

That drawing is the question. A question for a graph is itself a small graph, with holes in it.

To answer it, look for every place in the big graph where the small one fits. Each hole must land on a node. Each arrow must land on a link with the same name. Every fit is an answer.

Try it on the seven real items of episode sixteen point one. Start from the node you know. Nausea has one link named has symptom pointing at it, and that link comes from migraine. So the first hole is migraine.

Next, look for a link named treats that points at migraine. Ibuprofen has one. So the second hole is ibuprofen. One fit, one answer.

Answers come out as a table, with one row per fit and one column per hole. Picture a departures board. Each time a fit is found, a new row flips into place.

I counted the work. Starting from nausea took two lookups. Trying every possible placement blindly would take forty-nine, because two holes over seven nodes make seven squared: every pair.

Now the real thing. Wikidata holds about 124 million items. I ran the same question on its public query service, on the 26th of November 2026. It took about fourteen seconds, and returned 251 fits. They involve 215 different drugs and 47 different conditions. Migraine alone has 33.

The language that wrote our question is called SPARQL. The W3C made it a recommendation in January 2008. A question mark in front of a word marks a hole.

ISO published a second standard in April 2024. This one is GQL, and it covers graphs whose nodes and links carry labels and properties. You write the pattern as a little drawing in text, with round brackets for nodes and arrows for links. The idea is the same: a pattern with holes.

Now a warning. Placing a pattern is not always easy.

Remember episode four point two. Colouring sat on Karp's list of hard problems. So does clique: do some nodes all link to each other? Now ask for a pattern made of a chosen number of nodes, all linked together. That is the clique question, written as a pattern.

So finding a pattern in a graph is hard in general, in the same way. Nobody knows a method that is fast for every pattern in every graph, and nobody has proved that none exists.

Yet questions like ours are answered every day. Why? Because the pattern is small.

A pattern with a fixed number of holes can be tried by brute force: check every placement. With two holes, that means every pair of items. In Wikidata, that is about fifteen quadrillion pairs. The cost is polynomial, and still far too big.

So an engine does not try every pair. It does what we did on the seven items. It starts from the node you know, and follows only the links with the right name.

In our question, that means looking at the 360 conditions that have nausea as a symptom, then at the drugs that treat each one. That is 611 lookups, instead of fifteen quadrillion.

In episode four point two, a forced move cut the search short. Here, the known node and the names of the links do that job.

Back to Swanson. Fish oil lowers blood viscosity. Raynaud's goes with high blood viscosity. Now write the question. What does fish oil lower, that Raynaud's goes with high?

One node is known on the left. One is known on the right. One hole sits between them.

Here is the graph, drawn for illustration. It holds the three blood properties of episode sixteen point one, beside our seven real items. Twelve nodes in all.

Place the pattern. Start from fish oil. It lowers three things. For each one, ask whether Raynaud's goes with a high value of it. It does, every time. Three fits: blood viscosity, platelet aggregation, and vascular reactivity.

Each answer is a path of length two, from fish oil to Raynaud's. Swanson found his path by reading two piles of papers. In this small graph, the question finds every such path at once.

To sum up. A question is a small graph with holes. Answering means finding every place it fits, and the answers form a table. SPARQL and GQL write such patterns. Finding a pattern is hard in general, but small patterns, anchored on a known node and on named links, stay fast.

PLATE 16.44:49

Deducing new facts

A rule is a pattern plus a link to add, and a reasoner applies it until nothing new appears. On the Human Phenotype Ontology, one rule grows 24,436 links into 203,185. Rules can also reveal contradictions. A missing fact is not a false fact: under the open world assumption, the 119 silent pairs among twelve nodes are unknown, not no.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the apothecary. Last time ended with a question. Some links are written nowhere. Can a graph work them out for itself?

It can, if it is given rules. Start with one you already use.

The Human Phenotype Ontology says that migraine is a kind of headache. It also says that headache is a kind of abnormal nervous system physiology.

Nobody wrote that migraine is a kind of abnormal nervous system physiology. Yet you accept it at once.

Here is the rule behind that step, written once for every case. If A is a kind of B, and B is a kind of C, then A is a kind of C.

A rule has two halves. The first half is a pattern, like the questions of episode sixteen point three: two links, one after the other. The second half is a link to add.

Picture the rule on tracing paper. Lay the sheet over the graph. Wherever the pattern fits, a pencil draws the new link, in dashes, so you can tell it from the written ones.

A program that does this is called a reasoner. It slides the rule over the graph, adds every link the rule gives, and slides it again, until nothing new appears.

Run it on the whole ontology, in the release of September 2026. It has 19,894 terms and writes 24,436 links. Once the rule has run to the end, it holds 203,185.

Migraine is a small case. It had one written link going up, to headache. It now has five. Four of them were never written.

Rules may name relations too. The relation has symptom has a mirror: is a symptom of. Logicians call it the inverse.

Write the rule. If a condition has a symptom, then that symptom is a symptom of the condition.

Try it on the seven real items of episode sixteen point one. Migraine has three symptoms: headache, photophobia and nausea. The rule adds three links, pointing back at migraine. Seven written links, three deduced.

In February 2004, the W3C made a language for writing such rules a recommendation. It is called OWL. It builds on RDF, and it has words for the rules we just used: a kind of, transitive, and inverse.

A second version, OWL two, followed in October 2009.

Rules can also forbid. Suppose one list in the apothecary says that a drug is recommended for a condition. Another list says that the same drug is forbidden for the same condition.

Each list is well-formed. Together they cannot both be true.

So write a rule about the two relations. No drug and condition may be joined by both. OWL two calls such relations disjoint.

Now the reasoner finds a pair with both links. It does not guess which list is right. It reports a contradiction, and a person has to look. The drug and the condition here are illustrative.

Now the question that matters most. What does a graph conclude from a link that is not there?

Ask an ordinary database. Does fish oil treat Raynaud's? It finds no such row, and answers no. A database assumes that whatever it does not hold is false. That is called the closed world assumption, and Raymond Reiter studied it in the late nineteen-seventies.

A knowledge graph, in general, does not assume that. A missing fact may simply be unknown. That is the open world assumption, and OWL follows it. The OWL two primer says that a fact may be missing, but possibly true.

Take the twelve nodes of the last episode. Between twelve nodes, there are one hundred and thirty-two ordered pairs. The graph writes thirteen links. That leaves one hundred and nineteen pairs with nothing written.

Read them as a closed world, and all one hundred and nineteen mean no. Now read them as an open world, and all one hundred and nineteen mean not known.

Fish oil and Raynaud's is one of those pairs. Swanson's path was written nowhere. Nothing in the graph said that fish oil helps Raynaud's, and nothing said that it does not.

That changes what a reasoner may conclude. In a closed world, silence gives a no. In an open world, only a written rule gives a no or a contradiction. The OWL primer's own example needs a disjointness rule to deduce that Mary is not a man.

To sum up. A rule is a pattern and a link to add. A reasoner applies it until nothing new appears. Some rules reveal contradictions. And a missing fact is not a false fact.

PLATE 16.55:53

Predicting a missing link

TransE gives each node a point and each relation a move, and a fact fits when start plus move lands near the end. On Hetionet, with 151 treats links hidden, 17.2 percent land in the first ten candidates, yet a rule counting known treatments does as well. Proposals carry the habits of the literature: a candidate is a hypothesis, not a discovery.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the apothecary. Last time ended with a question. Some silent pairs look likelier than others. How could a graph tell which?

Here is one answer. Give every node a place, as a point on a plane. Give every relation a move, with a direction and a length.

Now take a written fact. Fish oil lowers blood viscosity. Start at the point of fish oil. Make the move named lowers. You land on blood viscosity.

That is the whole idea. A fact fits when its start, plus its move, lands near its end. The distance between the landing spot and the end tells how badly it misses.

In 2013, Antoine Bordes and four colleagues gave this method a name, in a paper for the NIPS conference. It is called TransE, short for translating embeddings. A relation translates one point into another.

Nobody places the points by hand. Start with random points. Take a written fact, and nudge the points so that its landing spot moves toward its end. Then make a false fact, by swapping its end for a random node, and nudge so that its landing spot moves away. Repeat for every fact, many times.

I ran it on the twelve nodes of the last episode, in two dimensions, with seed 165. At the start, the thirteen written facts miss by 0.80 on average. After 3,000 rounds, they miss by 0.11.

Now ask it something it was never told. Does fish oil treat Raynaud's? Take the move named treats, starting from fish oil. Measure the distance to Raynaud's.

Count every fact that twelve nodes and five relations could write. There are 660. Thirteen are written. The other 647 are silent. Ranked by distance, fish oil treats Raynaud's comes 456th.

Look at the closest silent facts. One of them says that Raynaud's treats fish oil. The model has never been told that a disease cannot treat a drug. A point only knows the facts it appears in.

So try it where there is more to learn from. Hetionet is a knowledge graph of biology and medicine, built by Daniel Himmelstein and colleagues, and published in eLife in 2017. It has 47,031 nodes of 11 types, among them genes, compounds, diseases and side effects. It has 2,250,197 links of 24 types.

It was built to find new uses for known drugs. Of its links, 755 say that a compound treats a disease.

Their own predictions counted paths between drugs and diseases. TransE is my own demonstration, run on their graph.

Here is the test. Hide 151 of the 755 treats links, one in five. Train on all the other links, in 50 dimensions, for 60 rounds, with seed 165.

Then take a hidden link. Keep its disease, and line up all the compounds by distance, leaving out those already known to treat it. Where does the hidden compound land?

Guessing blindly, about one of the 151 hidden compounds would land among the first ten. TransE puts 26 of them there. That is 17.2 percent of the hidden links.

But what does a candidate look like? Take migraine. Rank the compounds, skipping those already listed as treating it. First comes naratriptan. Hetionet does list it, under another relation: naratriptan palliates migraine. That means it relieves symptoms, and does not change the disease. Most likely, the model carried that link over to treats.

Then come vincristine, dacarbazine and thiotepa. Five of the ten are cancer drugs.

Why cancer drugs? Look at who gets proposed. Give each of the 137 diseases its ten best compounds. That makes 1,370 places. Only 115 compounds out of 1,552 ever appear. The ten most proposed fill 47.7 percent of the places.

Thiotepa alone is proposed for 113 diseases, though in training it is known to treat only four. Yet overall, the more diseases a compound is known to treat, the more often it is proposed. The rank correlation is 0.41.

A rule that ignores all geometry, and ranks compounds only by how many diseases they already treat, does just as well in the first ten. It gets 17.9 percent.

And the model cannot see what is new to it. Of the hidden links, 68 belonged to a compound with no other treatment in training. Not one reached the first ten.

So a candidate is a pair that looks like the pairs already written. It carries the habits of the literature that wrote them. Of the 1,370 proposed places, 26 are treatments we had hidden. The other 1,344 are candidates. Some may be true, and some false. Nothing in the graph can tell.

Swanson's path was a candidate too. In 1989, a trial on thirty-two patients turned it into evidence. That step is still the one that counts. A candidate says where to look. Only an experiment says what is there.

To sum up. Give each node a point and each relation a move. A written fact is one where the start, plus the move, lands near the end. The silent pairs that land close become candidates. But a candidate is a hypothesis, not a discovery.
