What synchrony shows

Reading the results board of published studies, synchrony goes along with outcomes: cooperation, closeness in couples, classroom engagement, and teaching and learning, where a pooled correlation of 0.44 is reported. A simulation shows that such a correlation lets the more synchronous of two random crews do better only about two times in three. These are associations between dyads, within one paradigm and modality, not explanations.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the rowing club. In the boathouse hangs a results board, with one line for each crew. Today we read it. When two people's brain signals move together, what has been shown to go along with it?

A boat is a good picture. Two rowers share one hull. When their strokes match, the boat often runs smooth and fast. Often, but not always. And a fast boat does not prove that the rowers understand each other. Keep that in mind while we read the board.

Line one, cooperation. In 2012, Cui, Bryant and Reiss recorded eleven pairs of adults with fNIRS while they played a computer game together. Over the right superior frontal cortex, the two partners' signals became more coherent when they cooperated, and not when they competed. The paper says that increased coherence was associated with better cooperation performance. It gives no correlation coefficient for that link, so the board leaves the effect size blank. Eleven pairs is a small study.

Line two, closeness. In 2017, Kinreich and colleagues recorded EEG from romantic couples and from strangers, while each pair talked. In the gamma band, couples were more synchronous than strangers. The test gave a t value of minus three point oh two, with a p value of point oh oh four. That compares twenty-four couples with twenty-five pairs of strangers. Among the strangers, something else showed up. The more time a pair spent in positive affect, the higher their gamma synchrony. The correlation was point five six six, across twenty-five pairs.

Twenty-five pairs is not many. With a sample that size, a correlation of point five six six could plausibly lie anywhere from about point two two to point seven nine. That interval is our own arithmetic, and it assumes an ordinary correlation.

Line three, the classroom. In 2017, Dikker and colleagues recorded EEG from twelve high school students, over eleven classes. They report that how well brain activity is synchronised across the students predicts class engagement. We could not read the coefficients in the paper directly, so the board shows the design and leaves the number off.

Line four sums up many lines. In 2022, Zhang and colleagues pooled sixteen studies of teaching and learning, twenty-three samples in all. Across them, synchrony between teacher and learner went with learning outcomes. The pooled correlation was point four four, with a ninety-five percent interval from point three four to point five four.

Now read the board the way a coach would. Every line here is a comparison between dyads. Some crews are more synchronous, and those crews tend to do better.

How much better? Let's simulate thirty crews with a correlation of exactly point four four. Each dot is one crew. The cloud rises to the right, but it is wide. Pick two crews at random, and the more synchronous one does better in about two cases out of three. One time in three, it is the other way round. A correlation of point four four accounts for about one fifth of the differences between crews.

Notice, too, what each line is. One paradigm, one measure, one modality. A cooperation game recorded with fNIRS does not tell us about a conversation recorded with EEG. And most of these studies are small.

So the board says this. In that paradigm, and with that modality, more synchronous dyads tend to do better. It does not say why. That question is for the next episode.

Let's recap. The board is made of correlations between dyads. They are often moderate, and often based on few dyads. They hold in one paradigm and one modality. They describe an association, not an explanation.

Next, a fast boat with two rowers who never speak, and what synchrony does not show.

PLATE 9.25:36

What synchrony does not show

In the rowing boat, two rowers who never influence each other still synchronise because both follow the cox, and speed correlates with synchrony at 0.8; remove the cox's call and the link vanishes. A real link between rowers gives the same average synchrony. Explanation is a ladder of correlation, specificity, mechanism and causality, and reverse inference from synchrony to understanding is unjustified.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the rowing club. A crew of two slides past the boathouse, and it is fast. The two rowers have not said a word to each other. They are listening to someone else.

That someone is the cox. The cox sits in the boat, faces the rowers, and calls the stroke. Both rowers pull on the same call. So their strokes match, their brain signals move together, and the boat flies.

Last time, the results board said that more synchronous crews tend to do better. Today's question is what that synchrony tells us about the rowers.

Let's put numbers on this boat. Forty crews, simulated. In each crew the two rowers never influence each other. Each follows the cox, some crews more closely than others. The more closely they follow, the faster the boat goes.

Across the forty crews, synchrony and boat speed correlate at zero point eight. Now take the cox's call out of each rower's signal. The synchrony between the two rowers falls to zero. Compare crews that follow the cox equally closely, and the link with speed is gone as well. The strokes lined up because of the cox. Nothing passed between the two rowers.

We have met this trap before. Episode seven point three showed how a shared stimulus creates synchrony, and episode two point six how a design rules a source out. Today's question is what to conclude once the cox is gone.

A second set of forty crews, also simulated, has no cox. One rower's signal drives the other's, with a strength that varies from crew to crew. The average synchrony is zero point three six. In the cox crews, it was zero point three five. The same number comes from two different mechanisms, one with a link between the rowers and one without. A synchrony value does not say which one you are looking at.

So think of an explanation as a ladder with four rungs. This is the framing of this series, not a framework taken from a paper.

The first rung is correlation. Do more synchronous dyads do better? Episode nine point one showed that often they do.

The second rung is specificity. Does the link survive when the obvious alternatives are controlled? The shared stimulus, the shared movement, the shared effort, the behaviour alone. In our boat, it did not.

The third rung is mechanism. Suppose the link survives. How does it arise? Is it a shared cause, or does something pass from one brain to the other?

The fourth rung is causality. If you change the synchrony, or the interaction behind it, does the outcome change? Only a manipulation can answer, and episode nine point five comes back to this.

Most published results sit on the first rung, and some reach the second. A headline often jumps to the top.

That jump has a name. It is reverse inference. You see synchrony, and you conclude that the two people understand each other. In 2006, Russell Poldrack, in Trends in Cognitive Sciences, examined this move for brain imaging. He wrote that inferring a cognitive process from the activation of a brain region is not deductively valid. It can give some information, but only as much as the activation is selective for that process. Replace the brain region with synchrony, and the same warning applies. Many things produce synchrony, so it says little about which one was at work.

Synchrony does not show that two minds understand each other. It does not show that information passes from one brain to the other. It shows that two signals vary together. The rest has to be argued, or tested.

The field itself has said so in recent years.

In 2021, Antonia Hamilton, in Neuron, wrote that hyperscanning can be hard to interpret. She argued that adding behavioural data and models of mutual prediction can lead to progress.

Also in 2021, Giacomo Novembre and Gian Domenico Iannetti, in Trends in Cognitive Sciences, asked whether interbrain synchrony causally helps social interaction. Their answer was that recording several brains cannot settle it. It takes causal protocols that stimulate the brains together.

Later that year, in the same journal, Quentin Moreau and Guillaume Dumas replied. They did not deny the value of that approach. They argued that causal explanation can also come from other experimental designs and from computational models.

And in 2022, Clay Holroyd, in Trends in Neurosciences, named three problems. The field has no widely accepted definition of interbrain synchrony. It lacks theories to guide experiments. And the variety of tasks and methods leaves too much flexibility when testing the idea.

The authors do not agree on everything. But a synchrony value is where an argument starts.

So what can you write? Compare two sentences. One says that synchrony reveals that partners understand each other. The other says that, in this paradigm and with this measure, more synchronous dyads tended to perform better, and that the sources we controlled did not explain it. The second one is smaller, and it is the one that survives.

Let's recap. A fast boat does not prove that the rowers understand each other. The same synchrony can come from a shared cox or from a real link. Ask how far up the ladder your result goes. Be wary of reverse inference.

PLATE 9.36:36

Synchrony as a predictor

A synchrony model that fits thirty simulated crews well, with correlation 0.8, drops to chance on leave-one-dyad-out cross-validation. Random splits of windows or members leak information, choices must happen inside folds, and with few dyads the error bar is wide. A predictor should be compared with behaviour alone, where synchrony here added little, and prediction says what will happen, not why.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the rowing club. The regatta is on Saturday, and the coach has one question. Which crew will win? She has the brain recordings of every crew's training sessions. Can synchrony help her predict the result?

Episode nine point one asked whether synchrony and results go together. Prediction asks something stricter. Given a crew the model has never seen, can it say how that crew will do?

Many articles say that synchrony predicts an outcome. Take one example. In 2021, Diego Reinero, Suzanne Dikker and Jay Van Bavel, in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, recorded EEG from groups of four people solving problems together. They reported that inter-brain synchrony predicted how well the groups performed. The test they report is a mixed model, fitted on all the groups at once. That is an association, measured in the same data that built the model. We read the full text, and found no test on held-out groups. This is not a criticism of one paper. It is how most of the field writes.

In 2020, in JAMA Psychiatry, Russell Poldrack, Grace Huckins and Gaël Varoquaux reviewed prediction in neuroimaging. They wrote that a significant association is not enough to claim a prediction, and that in-sample fit should not be reported as evidence of predictive accuracy. A prediction must be tested on data that were not used to fit the model.

How big can the gap be? Here are thirty crews, simulated. For each crew, we have twenty synchrony values, one per channel pair, and one regatta score. In this simulation, each value carries only a weak trace of how well the crew rows in training.

The coach fits a regression of the regatta score on the twenty values, using all thirty crews. It fits well. The correlation between its predictions and the true scores is zero point eight.

Now she covers one crew's result with a flap. She fits the model on the other twenty-nine crews, and predicts the covered crew. Then she does it again for the next crew, thirty times in all. This is leave-one-dyad-out cross-validation. The correlation between these predictions and the true scores is minus zero point zero seven. On the data it was fitted on, the model looks excellent. On a crew it has not seen, it is no better than guessing.

That was one cohort. We drew a thousand. Fitted and tested on the same crews, the median correlation was zero point eight seven. Leave-one-dyad-out, it was zero point one three. Nine cohorts out of ten fell between minus zero point two seven and zero point five, and three out of ten were below zero.

With thirty crews, a cross-validated score has a wide error bar. Gaël Varoquaux showed this in NeuroImage in 2018, for the sample sizes typical of neuroimaging. Poldrack and colleagues add that prediction analyses should not be run on fewer than several hundred observations. So a number from thirty dyads is a first estimate, not a verdict.

The second trap is leakage. A dyad is not one row of data. It has windows, and two members. Cut each crew's recording into forty windows, and ask a random forest to predict the crew's result from each window. If the windows are split at random, some windows of a crew land in training and others in the test. The model recognises the crew by its signature, and returns the result it learned for that crew.

In this simulation, synchrony has no link with the result at all. Even so, a random split of windows gives a median correlation of zero point seven five. Keep whole crews together, and it falls to minus zero point zero seven. These are medians over twenty repetitions.

The two members of a dyad leak in the same way. Treat each rower as a row, and split at random: the correlation is zero point two seven. Split by dyad: zero point zero three. The two rowers share a boat, a session and a room, and whatever is shared leaks. Saeb and colleagues, in GigaScience in 2017, showed the same effect for people: splitting records at random can strongly underestimate the prediction error, compared with splitting by person. In hyperscanning, the unit is the dyad, as in episode eight point one. Now it is the unit of the split, too.

Every choice goes inside the fold too. Pick the best channel pairs using all the crews before the split, and the held-out crew has already been seen.

The third point is the comparison. A prediction is only worth reporting against what simpler information predicts. The coach also records behaviour: how steadily each crew holds its stroke. And she knows how fit each rower is.

Here are five predictors, all tested leave-one-dyad-out, as medians over a thousand cohorts. We measure the typical error, in standard deviations of the regatta score. Guessing the average gives zero point eight. Individual fitness gives zero point seven eight. One synchrony summary, averaged over the twenty channel pairs and fixed in advance, gives zero point seven four. Behaviour alone gives zero point six nine. Behaviour plus synchrony gives zero point seven zero.

In this simulation, synchrony predicts a little, but only because it mirrors behaviour. Adding it to behaviour lowered the error in two cohorts out of ten. That is how we built the simulated crews: the ones that train well also look synchronous. In real data, synchrony may add something, or not. The comparison is how you find out.

So how should the coach report her result? Keep whole dyads together in every split. Do every choice inside the folds. Report the error on held-out dyads, with an uncertainty range, not the fit on the data. And compare with behaviour and with individual characteristics, not only with zero.

One last caution. A good out-of-sample prediction does not say why. A shared cause can predict as well as a real link. Prediction tells what will happen, not what would change it. Episode nine point five comes back to that.

Let's recap. A correlation in the sample is not a prediction. Hold out whole dyads, never windows or members. With few dyads, expect a wide error bar. And always compare with behaviour alone.

Dynamics, not averages

Two simulated crews share an average synchrony near 0.7 yet differ completely: one is flat, the other breaks and repairs four times, and a sliding window shows the lead changing hands between rowers. An average hides this film, and more synchrony is not always better. Report how often a dyad breaks, how long repair takes, and how the lead moves.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the rowing club. Today we film the whole race and hang the film on the boathouse wall. One frame for every ten seconds of rowing, twelve frames in all. In some frames the two blades dip together. In others they do not.

An average would squash this film into one picture. Today's idea is simple. The film matters more than its average.

Here are two crews, both simulated, both filmed for one hundred and twenty seconds. We slide a window of six seconds along each film, one second at a time, and measure the synchrony of the strokes inside each window. As before in the series, the window length is a choice. Here it covers three strokes.

The first crew is flat. Its synchrony wanders a little around zero point six nine, and never drops below zero point four five. The second crew falls into step, drifts apart, and falls back into step, four times. Its average is zero point six seven. Two crews with almost the same average, and two very different races.

Let us count what the average hides. Call it a break when synchrony falls below zero point four. Call it repaired when it climbs back above zero point seven. The flat crew has no break. The other crew has four. It takes them nine, ten, nine and fourteen seconds to get back in step, a median of nine and a half seconds. Its spread is zero point three two, against zero point one zero for the flat crew.

Take forty simulated crews. Fourteen of them have an average between zero point seven and zero point eight. Among those fourteen, the number of breaks goes from two to four, and the average repair time of a crew goes from six point three to twenty seconds. The average cannot tell them apart.

Real interactions move in and out of step too. In 1989, Tronick and Cohn, in the journal Child Development, filmed fifty-four mother and infant pairs at three, six and nine months. The pairs became more coordinated with age, but the share of time they spent coordinated was small. The authors suggested describing an interaction by how it moves between coordinated and miscoordinated states, and not only by its degree of coordination.

In 2020, Mayo and Gordon, in the journal Psychophysiology, proposed a model with two tendencies that exist together. One pulls toward synchronising with others. The other pulls toward moving out of synchrony and acting independently. They pointed out that more synchrony is not always more beneficial, and that moving in and out of synchrony has rarely been studied as an adaptive state. An adaptive pair, they suggested, is a flexible one, and meta-stability may be a marker of it.

One limit. We built these crews and gave them no race result, so this simulation cannot say that repair helps. That needs real data.

Now look at who leads. In each window, we also slide one rower's stroke signal in time against the other's, and keep the delay that matches best. If the second rower repeats the first one a little later, the first one is leading. This is the lagged correlation of episode six point two, applied window by window.

In the crew that breaks and repairs, the delay is about a tenth of a second, whenever they are in step. But its sign changes. First Ava leads, then Otto, then Ava, then Otto, then Ava again. The lead changed hands four times in two minutes. An average delay would have hidden all of it.

Wass and colleagues, in Trends in Cognitive Sciences in 2020, separated two ways of measuring this coupling in early social interaction. Concurrent entrainment is when A is high, B is high. Sequential entrainment is when changes in A forward-predict changes in B. Our delay belongs to the second kind.

A leader is not always an advantage. In 2011, Noy and colleagues, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, had expert actors and musicians play the mirror game, mirroring each other's hand movements. With no designated leader, the players moved together to within forty milliseconds. When one player was designated the leader, synchrony went down.

Put the two measures together, and the film becomes a few states that come back. Drifting apart. In step, with Ava leading. In step, with Otto leading. In step, with no clear leader. Over the two minutes, this crew spends twenty-eight percent of the time drifting, twenty-nine percent with Ava leading, thirty-two percent with Otto leading, and eleven percent with no clear leader. It enters the drifting state four times.

So is more synchrony always better? Mayo and Gordon say no. Tronick and Cohn found pairs coordinated only a small part of the time. And in the mirror game, naming a leader made things worse. A crew locked together at every moment would have no room to adapt.

What should you report? Not only the mean. Report how often the dyad breaks, how long it takes to repair, and how the lead moves. And remember that the sliding window is a choice. Say which one you used.

Let's recap. An average hides the film. Two crews can share an average and still differ in breaks and repairs. The lead can change hands. And more synchrony is not always better.

PLATE 9.54:35

Testing causal claims

A correlation cannot separate a hidden shared cause from a real effect, as two simulated worlds with identical scatter plots show. Three interventions help: manipulating the interaction, as with a curtain between partners; manipulating synchrony itself, for instance with brain stimulation; and animal studies. Each concludes something different, so the question is what was changed and what changed along with it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the rowing club, in the training room. Two rowing machines stand side by side, and the coach walks between them with a clipboard. She does not film a race. She changes one thing, and then she looks at what moves.

Today's question is the top rung of our ladder. When synchrony and a good outcome go together, how do we find out what causes what?

A correlation cannot tell us. Here are two simulated worlds, with sixty dyads each. In both, synchrony and the outcome score go together. The correlation is point five five in the first world, and point five seven in the second.

In the first world, a hidden factor drives both. The pair simply works well together, and that raises their synchrony and their score. Synchrony does nothing by itself. In the second world, synchrony itself raises the score. Look only at the scatter plot, and you cannot tell the worlds apart.

So the coach intervenes. There are three ways to do this in our field.

The first way is to manipulate the interaction. The coach pulls a curtain between the two machines. The partners can no longer see each other, and nothing else changes.

Real studies work like this. In 2012, Jiang and colleagues, in the Journal of Neuroscience, measured two brains at once during conversation. Neural synchronization rose in the left inferior frontal cortex during a face-to-face dialog. It did not rise during a back-to-back dialog, a face-to-face monologue, or a back-to-back monologue.

In 2017, Hirsch and colleagues, in NeuroImage, used near infrared spectroscopy. They compared two people looking into each other's eyes with one person looking at a picture of a face. Coherence across the two brains was higher during live eye contact. And it disappeared when the partners were assigned at random.

Our simulation does the same. Thirty dyads keep the view open, thirty have it blocked, and the assignment is random. Synchrony falls from point five five to point three eight. The score slips from forty nine point seven to forty seven.

What can we conclude? That the interaction matters for synchrony, because we changed the interaction ourselves. But the curtain changes much more than synchrony. It also removes gaze, gesture and timing cues. So we cannot say that synchrony did the work.

The second way is to manipulate synchrony itself. Now the coach reaches into the brains. In 2017, Novembre and colleagues, in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, applied transcranial alternating current stimulation over the left motor cortex of two people at the same time. Both tapped a finger. In-phase stimulation at twenty hertz made their taps line up better than anti-phase or sham stimulation did. Stimulation at two or ten hertz showed no such gain.

That is a test of a cause. We come back to it in episodes eleven point five and eleven point six. Today we keep only its logic.

Here is the logic in our simulation. Set the synchrony of thirty dyads low, and of thirty others high, at random. In the first world, the score barely moves. It is one point two higher. In the second world, it rises by seven point six. Same correlation, different answers. Only the intervention could separate them.

The third way is to test in animals. In episode one point five, we met bats and mice. In 2019, in Cell, Zhang and Yartsev recorded from pairs of bats. The brains were correlated when the bats shared an environment, and little or not at all when they were in separate ones. In the same issue, Kingsbury and colleagues found that the correlation in mice depended on ongoing social interaction. Animals let us look at single cells. But they are other species, and they do not hold a conversation.

So each way concludes something different. Changing the interaction shows that the situation shapes synchrony. Changing synchrony itself can show that it moves behaviour, in this task, with this stimulation. And animals show how cells take part, in another species. None of them says that two minds understand each other.

Let's recap. A correlation cannot separate a shared cause from a real effect. Intervene, and compare with a random or sham control. Ask what you changed, and what you changed along with it.
