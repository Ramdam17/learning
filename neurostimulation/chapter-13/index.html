Robustness I · A study that can fail

A study that can fail has its target painted before the first shot, because choices made after seeing the data inflate false positives. Pre-registration files the hypothesis, primary outcome, control, sample size, exclusion rule and blinding check in advance, and Registered Reports add a promise to publish whatever happens. Registration does not guarantee quality, but it makes choices and deviations visible.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a study that cannot fail. It stimulates, it measures, and whatever comes out, the authors find something worth reporting. It sounds comfortable. It is also worthless, because a study that cannot fail cannot tell you anything. This episode is about building a study that can fail, and about showing, afterwards, that it could.

Picture an archery range. The first archer shoots, and paints the target afterwards. She walks up to her arrows, draws a bullseye around the tightest cluster, and announces a perfect score. The second archer works the other way round. She paints the target first. She seals a copy of it in a dated envelope, in front of the whole court. Only then does she shoot. Her arrows can miss. And that is exactly why a hit means something.

In research, the target painted afterwards has a name. Researcher degrees of freedom. In 2011, Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed, with simulations and two experiments, that flexibility in data collection, analysis and reporting dramatically increases the false-positive rate, far above the nominal five percent. How many people? Which of them to drop? Which measure, which time window, which comparison? Every choice made after seeing the data moves the bullseye toward the arrows. The next episode calls this the garden of forking paths.

The fix is to paint the target before the first shot. That is pre-registration. Nosek and colleagues defined it in 2018: committing to analytic steps without advance knowledge of the research outcomes, in a registry that keeps the plan and makes it findable. Its purpose, they wrote, is not to favour prediction over postdiction. It is to make clear which is which.

What goes on the target? Six rings, all filed before the first session.

Ring one, the hypothesis. Not "stimulation changes something", but a direction and a measure: active stimulation raises the response more than the control does.

Ring two, the primary outcome. One task, one measure, one time point, one way of computing it, as episode ten point one asked. Everything else is secondary, and is labelled as secondary.

Ring three, the control condition. Sham, or an active control, with its parameters, and the result that would count as specific. That was episodes nine point one and nine point two.

Ring four, the sample size, with the calculation behind it. Minarik and colleagues did this in 2016, in a preregistered tDCS study. They assumed an effect of point six and eighty percent power. That called for at least seventy-two participants, and they tested seventy-five. Episode nine point six showed why this number is not decoration.

Ring five, the exclusion rule. Who is dropped, and why, decided before anyone has seen their data. A participant who cannot tolerate the stimulation. A recording that fails a quality threshold. A rule written afterwards tends to remove the inconvenient arrows.

Ring six, the blinding check. At the end of the last session, ask each participant, and each rater, which condition they think they had. Report the answers, whatever they are. That was episode nine point one. Next to the rings goes the analysis itself, which the next episode covers.

Does it work? Medicine has the clearest evidence. In 2015, Kaplan and Irvin looked at large trials funded by the American National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, testing drugs or supplements for heart disease, between 1970 and 2012. Before the year 2000, seventeen of thirty trials showed a significant benefit on the primary outcome. After 2000, two of twenty-five. In those same years, prospective registration became a requirement. In 2005, the journal editors' committee, the I-C-M-J-E, made registration before enrolment a condition for publication. In 2007, American law required registration of applicable trials. The authors point to registration as the likeliest explanation, and say plainly that they cannot show it was the cause. These were drug trials, not stimulation trials. And episode ten point one has already shown a similar contrast for Registered Reports in psychology.

Registered Reports go one step further. Chambers proposed them at the journal Cortex in 2013. They have two stages. At stage one, the introduction, the methods and the analysis plan are reviewed before any data exist. If the plan is sound, the journal gives an in-principle acceptance. It will publish the paper whatever the result, as long as the plan is followed. At stage two, reviewers check that the study did what it promised. A null result is as publishable as a positive one. By 2021, close to three hundred journals offered the format, according to Chambers and Tzavella. Brain stimulation has examples. In 2024, Willmot and colleagues published, in Cortex, a double-blind, sham-controlled Registered Report asking whether the effect of tDCS repeats, in the same person, from one session to the next.

Now the key point. Pre-registration does not guarantee quality. A registered plan can be a weak plan, with a small sample or a sham that leaks. And a plan can be broken. In 2021, Claesen and colleagues examined twenty-seven studies that carried the preregistered badge in Psychological Science, between 2015 and 2017. Only two had no deviation from their plan. Nine disclosed none of their deviations. The usual ones concerned sample size, exclusion criteria and the statistical analysis.

And the broader evidence is mixed. In 2023, van den Akker and colleagues compared a hundred and ninety-three preregistered psychology studies with a hundred and ninety-three that were not. They did not find fewer positive results among the preregistered ones. They found that preregistered studies more often contained a power analysis, and had larger samples. But they found no robust evidence that preregistration prevents p-hacking.

So what is it for? Nosek and colleagues put it this way: preregistration does not eliminate poor statistical practices, but it makes them detectable. Deviations are allowed, because the world is untidy. What matters is that each one is reported with its reason, so that the reader can judge whether an arrow was moved. Pre-registration does not make a study good. It makes the choices visible.

Chapter nine, as a checklist, before the first session. Sham: does it leak, and will the blinding be tested? Active control: was it chosen from the question? Dose card: is every line filled in, including what the participant does during stimulation? Time course: when will the effect be measured, and for how long? Responders: defined in advance, with repeated measures? Power: is the sample sized for a realistic effect, not a hoped-for one? Each line is a place where a choice can be made early, in the open, or late, in the dark.

To sum up. A study that can fail has its target painted first: the hypothesis, the primary outcome, the control, the sample size, the exclusion rule and the blinding check, filed before the first session. A Registered Report adds a promise to publish, whatever happens. Registration does not guarantee quality. It shows what was chosen, and when.

PLATE 13.26:06

Robustness II · Statistics that hold up

Three analysis habits trap real stimulation studies, shown by simulation. Dividing after by before values inflates false alarms, calling one group significant and the other not is not a test of their difference, and many measures and time windows make a false positive likely. Models that match the design, the interaction against the control, and correction for the number of tests hold up.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A stimulation study is a trial in the courtroom of science. The data are the evidence. The statistics are the cross-examination. And a weak analysis can make a harmless result look guilty. This episode walks down a path with three trapdoors, three analysis habits that catch real studies. Then it builds the handrail. Everything we simulate here is labelled simulated, with the settings shown on screen.

Trap one. The ratio. A study records the M-E-P before stimulation, and again after. To make people comparable, the analyst divides each after value by its before value, then tests whether the average ratio differs from one. It feels natural. But it is a ratio of two noisy numbers.

Here is a simulation. Twenty people, and no stimulation effect at all. Each block averages ten pulses, and single responses are skewed, as M-E-Pees are. We repeat the experiment two thousand times. The average ratio comes out at 1.15, not one. When the before value happens to be small, the ratio explodes. When it is large, the ratio can only shrink toward zero. Tested against one, this analysis finds an effect in 9.6 percent of experiments, instead of five. Take the logarithm of each ratio, and the average sits at zero, with 5.5 percent false alarms.

The damage is not only false alarms. In 2001, Vickers simulated trials and found that percentage change from baseline had the lowest statistical power of the methods he compared. He recommended using the baseline as a covariate instead. In 2016, Lahr and colleagues pooled nine paired associative stimulation studies, 190 healthy volunteers, and found that quotients systematically indicated stronger effects than differences or log quotients. The way you build the number changes the result.

Trap two. One group significant, the other not. A paper reports that active stimulation improved the score, with p below point oh five, while sham did not. The authors conclude that stimulation beats sham. But nobody tested that. Gelman and Stern said it plainly in 2006: the difference between significant and not significant is not itself statistically significant.

Simulate it. Fifteen people per group, and exactly the same true effect in both groups, sized so that each group has about a fifty percent chance of reaching significance. We repeat it four thousand times. Exactly one group is significant in 49 percent of the experiments. Yet the test that compares the groups fires in only 4 percent, which is what it should do, since the groups are identical. In one of our experiments, the active group has p equals 0.028, the sham group 0.27, and the difference between them, 0.54.

This is not a rare slip. Nieuwenhuis, Forstmann and Wagenmakers read 513 papers from five leading neuroscience journals, published in 2009 and 2010. In 157 of them, the design called for comparing two effects. In 79, the authors made this error. Chapter nine showed why sham matters. Here is how to use it: the test that counts is the interaction with the control condition.

Trap three. Many measures, many windows. A stimulation study can look at the response amplitude, its latency, the silent period, inhibition, and more, at several times after the session. Say five measures, in four time windows. That is twenty tests. When nothing is going on, each one has a five percent chance to come out positive. The chance that at least one does is 1 minus 0.95 to the power twenty, about 64 percent. Our simulation of four thousand empty experiments gives 65 percent. A paper that reports only the lucky cell shows one test and hides nineteen.

It does not take bad faith. Gelman and Loken called it the garden of forking paths. If the choice of outcome or window depends on what the data looked like, the comparisons that were not run still count. The fix starts before the data, and episode 13.1 covers it.

So what holds up? Three habits. First, model what you measured. Ten pulses from one person are not ten independent people. A mixed model keeps every trial, takes the log of the single response, and gives each participant their own baseline. Barr and colleagues argued that the random effects should follow the design.

Second, test the right contrast. The question is not whether the active condition changed. It is whether the active condition changed more than the control did. That is the condition by time interaction. Whenever possible, measure both conditions in the same participants, so that each person is their own control. In one simulated crossover of sixteen people, active stimulation raised the log response by 0.45 and sham by 0.12. The difference, 0.34, has p equals 0.002.

Third, correct for the number of tests, and count them honestly. Bonferroni, or the false discovery rate of Benjamini and Hochberg, or cluster permutation tests for maps, as Maris and Oostenveld proposed. In our empty experiments, Bonferroni and the false discovery rate both bring the false alarms back to about five percent.

To sum up. Do not divide, or at least take logarithms. Do not compare p-values, test the interaction against the control. Do not hide the tests you ran, count them and correct them. A result that survives these three habits is ready for the next witnesses.

Next: replications and meta-analyses.

PLATE 13.37:54

Replications and meta-analyses

One study is a lead, a replication asks again with a new sample, and a meta-analysis stacks studies like transparencies on a light table, with an interval that often settles around a smaller effect. Motor cortex tDCS shrank, depression tDCS split depending on the study, and high-frequency rTMS for depression held with a modest effect. Pooling has limits: weak studies, mixed protocols and small-study inflation.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A courtroom does not convict on one witness. Even a vivid, confident witness can be wrong, or lucky. So the court calls a second witness, and a third, and then it puts all their testimony on one table.

In science, the second witness is a replication: the same question, asked again, with new people. The table is the meta-analysis. It pools the studies into one estimate, with an interval around it. Our picture is a light table. Each study is a transparent sheet, with its dots drawn on it. One sheet shows a bold shape, but part of that shape is noise. Stack many sheets, and the noise washes out. What stays is what the sheets share.

Three stories follow. One effect shrank. One split, depending on the study. One held. Episode 10.1 already showed pooled results for single-session tDCS and cognition. Episode 9.6 explained why small studies overestimate effects. Here we follow whole stories, from the first witness to the last.

Story one. The motor cortex. In 2000, Nitsche and Paulus passed a weak direct current through the scalp, and measured the response to T-M-S. They reported excitability changes of up to 40 percent, lasting several minutes after the current stopped. Anodal stimulation excited, cathodal inhibited. It became a foundation of tDCS research.

Then the other witnesses spoke. In 2015, Horvath, Forte and Carter pooled every neurophysiological outcome in healthy adults that at least two research groups had reported. There were thirty. Only one showed a reliable effect: the amplitude of the M-E-P. And the size of that effect had been decreasing, significantly, over the previous fourteen years.

The same year, Héroux, Taylor and Gandevia surveyed 47 T-M-S researchers. Across protocols, 45 to 60 percent found results similar to the originals. The others reproduced them only sometimes, or not at all. In 2017, a similar survey of 154 researchers covered electrical brain stimulation. For anodal and cathodal stimulation, 45 to 50 percent said they could routinely reproduce published results. Those are experiences, not measurements.

A measurement came in 2021. Jonker and colleagues ran a pre-registered, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 62 healthy people: anodal tDCS, two milliamps, twenty minutes. They found no main effect on excitability, and no evidence for responders. Their conclusion was careful: this protocol may not reliably change excitability. It is a different dose from the original, so it does not erase the first witness. But it says the effect is less general than the first sheet suggested.

Story two. Depression. In 2016, Brunoni and colleagues pooled the data of individual patients from six sham-controlled trials of tDCS, 289 people. Response was 34 percent with active stimulation and 19 percent with sham. The odds ratio was 2.44, with an interval from 1.38 to 4.32.

Then new witnesses arrived. In 2017, the ELECT-TDCS trial randomised 245 adults. On the Hamilton scale, scores fell by 9.0 points with tDCS and 5.8 with placebo. The difference, 3.2 points, had an interval from 0.7 to 5.5. But escitalopram lowered scores by 11.3, and tDCS could not be shown to be as good as the drug. In 2018, an international trial of 130 people, 2.5 milliamps, twenty sessions, found no antidepressant difference between active and sham, in unipolar or bipolar depression. Its authors note that its sham delivered a small current that may have been biologically active. So: a modest effect in the pool, a smaller one in a large trial, none in another. Depending on the study.

Story three, an effect that held. In 2014, Berlim and colleagues pooled 29 sham-controlled trials of high-frequency rTMS for depression, 1,371 people. After about thirteen sessions, 29.3 percent had responded and 18.6 percent had remitted, against 10.4 and 5 percent with sham. The odds ratio was 3.3, for both. Heterogeneity between the trials was not statistically significant. This is what a surviving effect looks like: many independent trials, pointing the same way, with a size that clinicians can weigh. It is also modest. Most people did not respond within that course.

Because these are clinical uses, here are the status stamps. As of November 2026. For rTMS, in the United States, the F-D-A first authorised a system for depression in 2008, and cleared a theta burst system on August fourteenth, 2018, for adults with major depression who have not improved on antidepressant medication in the current episode. In Canada, Health Canada approved rTMS for depression in 2002; the public licence list names Class III devices but not their indications. In Europe, the first C-E mark for depression dates from 2011.

For tDCS, in the United States, the F-D-A approved a home-use device in December 2025, for adults with moderate to severe major depression, alone or added to treatment, not for treatment-resistant depression. In Canada, the licence list shows an active class two licence for tDCS stimulators, but no indication. In Europe, one manufacturer states a C-E mark, class two A. Evidence levels are not regulatory status, so they stay outside the stamps. This is not medical advice.

Now, how does the table work? Each study gives an estimate and an interval. Precise studies weigh more. The pooled estimate is a weighted average, drawn as a diamond. As sheets pile up, the diamond narrows. And often it settles lower than the first, striking sheet. This picture is illustrative, not data. Two cautions. A narrow interval is precision, not truth: it can tighten around a biased estimate. And a random-effects model widens the interval when the studies differ among themselves, which is honest.

A meta-analysis has its own limits. First, garbage in, garbage out. Pooling many weak studies does not make a strong one. Second, heterogeneity. Different doses, montages, tasks and populations are stacked under one name. Horvath and colleagues noted that cognitive paradigms in tDCS are extremely variable. Third, small studies tend to show larger effects, and the pool inherits that. Whether missing studies are hiding is the question of the next episode, with funnel plots. And a pool can be overtaken: the 2016 depression pool was followed by trials with mixed results.

To sum up. One witness is a lead, not a verdict. A replication asks again, with a new sample. A meta-analysis lays all the sheets on one table, and its interval narrows, often around a smaller effect. Motor cortex tDCS shrank. Depression tDCS split. High-frequency rTMS held. None of this is a blacklist. It is how testimony is weighed.

Next: publication bias and the placebo effect.

PLATE 13.47:51

Publication bias and the placebo effect

Publication bias hangs only the striking photographs on the wall: in a simulation, publishing mostly significant results made a true effect look nearly half as large again, and a funnel plot reveals the missing corner. A sham arm also carries expectation and nocebo, with large sham responses and sham adverse events, so expectation and blinding guesses are reported by group.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A trial can be run perfectly and still mislead us. Two things can go wrong before the verdict. The first is which studies reach the court at all. The second is what the participants expect when the stimulation starts. This episode is about both.

Here is our picture. A curator hangs a gallery wall. The photographs that reach the wall are the striking ones. The blurry ones stay in a drawer. Nobody lies, and every photograph on the wall is real. But a visitor who only sees the wall will think the photographer is better than she is. That is publication bias.

In stimulation, the drawer fills fast, because many studies are small. A small study measures its effect with a lot of noise. By chance, some find a big effect, and some find none. If the big ones get published and the empty ones stay in the drawer, the wall tilts.

There is a plot that shows the tilt. It is called a funnel plot. Each dot is one study. Sideways, the effect it found. Upwards, how precise it was, so big studies sit at the top and small studies at the bottom. Precise studies land close together. Small ones scatter widely. Without bias, the dots make a symmetric funnel, centred on the true effect.

We simulated one. This is not real data. We drew three hundred studies of a stimulation whose true effect is small, a standardised difference of zero point two. Then we applied a rule. A study with a significant positive result is always published. Any other study is published about one time in seven.

Look at the result. Only one hundred and three studies reach the wall. The bottom left corner is almost empty. That is where small studies with no effect, or a negative one, would sit. Averaging all three hundred gives about zero point two two. Averaging only the published ones gives about zero point three two. The wall makes the effect look nearly half as large again.

Can we detect the missing corner? Egger and colleagues proposed a test in 1997. It checks whether the small studies report larger effects than the big ones. In our simulation it flags the published set. A second tool is trim and fill, from Duval and Tweedie. It guesses how many studies are missing and adds their mirror images. Here it adds sixteen, and pulls the estimate down to about zero point two nine. Better, but not back to the truth.

Sterne and colleagues gave a warning in 2011. An asymmetric funnel is not proof of publication bias. Real differences between studies can tilt it too, and so can chance. It is a smoke alarm, not a verdict.

What does this look like in our field? In 2016, Mancuso and colleagues reviewed thirty-one articles on anodal tDCS and working memory in healthy people. For stimulation of the left prefrontal cortex alone, the effect was reduced to nonsignificance after correction for publication bias. Correction methods can disagree, so we read this as a warning, not a measurement.

In 2017, Héroux and colleagues surveyed researchers in electrical brain stimulation. One hundred and fifty-four completed the survey. Forty-one percent knew colleagues who had reported only some of their outcomes. And in an audit of one hundred published studies, only six reported a power calculation.

The remedies sit elsewhere in this chapter. A registered report is accepted before the results exist, so an empty result still gets published. Replications and meta-analyses come next door, in episode thirteen point three. And episode nine point six showed how small studies that pass the filter overestimate their effect.

Now the second question. What does the participant expect? A frame changes how a photograph looks. An ornate golden frame makes any picture look more valuable. In medicine, the ornate frame is the impressive device and the ritual around it. And a sham arm gets the frame too.

In 2006, Kaptchuk and colleagues compared two placebos in two hundred and seventy adults with arm pain. One group received a sham device, the other an inert pill. The sham device lowered self-reported pain faster: about zero point three three points a week on a ten point scale, against zero point one five for the pill. Grip strength did not differ. The ritual changed what people said they felt.

How big is the sham response in stimulation trials? Depression is where it has been counted. In 2009, Brunoni and colleagues pooled twenty-nine rTMS trials. The change in depression scores inside the sham groups was large: about zero point eight, with a range from zero point six to one. In 2018, Razza and colleagues pooled sixty-one trials and found the same, zero point eight. For tDCS, De Smet and colleagues pooled twenty-three studies in 2021 and found one point one. A sham with a ramp at the start and end gave a lower response than other shams.

Careful with these numbers. They measure change inside the sham group, from before to after. That includes expectation, but also natural recovery and the visits themselves. They also depend on the study. In Razza's analysis, the sham response was larger in more recent trials, and smaller in treatment-resistant patients. This episode is about sham arms. It makes no claim about what is approved anywhere.

Does expectation itself act? The evidence is mixed. Rabipour and colleagues, in 2019, told young adults that tDCS would either help or not. Across two experiments, with one hundred and twenty-one people, reaction times and accuracy were largely unaffected by expectation or by stimulation. So the effect is not automatic. It looks stronger for what people report about themselves. In 2022, Burke and colleagues combined thirty-four imaging studies of placebo effects. One placebo-related region, in the left prefrontal cortex, overlaps with a TMS target for depression. So a sham arm may not be a neutral baseline.

The frame can also darken. This is the nocebo effect: expecting harm makes harm appear. In 2020, Zis and colleagues pooled ninety-three placebo-controlled TMS trials. In the sham arms, thirteen point six percent of participants reported at least one adverse event. Headache was the most common. Active stimulation did cause more: the odds were two point six times higher. But the sham arm shows that part of the side effects comes from the situation, not from the field.

So here is the key point. In stimulation trials, the sham response can be large, especially for what people report about themselves. We cannot remove it. We can measure it. Ask each participant, before the first session, how much improvement they expect. Ask them afterwards which condition they think they had, and how sure they are. Report both, by group. Check whether expectation changes the result. This is a result in its own right, not an accessory.

That makes a checklist. Which studies are missing, and does the funnel tilt? What did participants expect, and what did they guess? How large was the sham response? Which adverse events appeared in each arm?

To sum up. A wall of published studies can flatter an effect, so we look at the funnel and we ask about the drawer. A sham arm gets the frame, the ritual and the expectation. So we measure expectation and blinding, and we report them.

Next: report it so others can redo it.

PLATE 13.55:34

Report it so others can redo it

A study report must stand alone, like a file sent to a second court that must rerun the trial from it alone. Each technique has its checklist, for TMS, electrical stimulation and ultrasound, and the file needs the device and coil, exact montage, modelled field, sham, blinding test, all adverse effects, data and code. Every blank becomes a guess where a replication may differ.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The last episode of this chapter starts from a happy ending. Suppose your study did not fail. It worked. The effect is there, the statistics hold. One question is left. Can anyone else do it again?

In the courtroom, one person writes everything down. The clerk. Our clerk is called CHECK, and has one rule. The file must stand alone. A second court, in another city, will receive the file and nothing else. No phone call to the first court. If the second court cannot rerun the trial from the file alone, the file is incomplete.

Here is a thin file. It says: stimulation at one hundred and ten percent of threshold. The second court reads it, and has questions. Threshold of what, measured how? Which coil? Which machine, with which pulse shape? Placed where, and held how? Episode nine point three showed why an intensity needs its reference. This episode is about everything around that line.

In 2012, Peterchev and colleagues set the principle. Dose is defined by every parameter of the device that shapes the field in the body. The size, shape and position of the coil or electrode. The pulse shape, amplitude, width and polarity. The repetition frequency, the trains, the number of pulses, the number of sessions. Their test is reproducibility. Report enough that someone else could repeat the stimulation.

Each community then turned this principle into a checklist. For TMS, the classic is Chipchase and colleagues, in 2012. It came from a two-round survey of international experts. It has twenty-seven items for single-pulse studies, and thirty for paired-pulse studies. Eight items concern the participants. Twenty-one concern the methods, such as coil type and stimulus intensity. Two concern the analysis.

In 2026, a newer tool appeared. The T-M-S reporting assessment tool, from Székely and colleagues. Every item comes with a definition and examples. It has seventy-two items, and a subset of fifty that raters agreed on most reliably. Its authors tested it on three hundred and thirty-three studies of one TMS protocol only, and say it is not yet validated beyond that field.

For electrical stimulation, the checklist is called RATES. Report Approval for Transcranial Electrical Stimulation. Nejati and colleagues published it in Nature Protocols, in October 2025. Thirty-eight experts took part, over three rounds. Sixty-six items were kept, in five groups: participants, device, electrodes, current and procedure. A short version keeps twenty-six essential items.

For transcranial ultrasound, the checklist comes from the ITRUSST consortium. Martin and colleagues published it in Brain Stimulation, in 2024. It has six parts. The transducer and its drive system. The drive settings. The acoustic parameters in free field. The pulse timing. Estimates of the exposure inside the brain itself. And the intensity parameters. It covers the ultrasound only. The EEG, the imaging and the behaviour are yours to report.

And for TMS combined with EEG? No standalone reporting checklist could be verified for this episode. What exists is a 2023 expert review by Hernandez-Pavon and colleagues, in Brain Stimulation. It gives recommendations for recording and analysis, and lists the open issues. It is a guide, not a checklist. So on our clerk's file, that tab stays marked: not verified.

Trials in general have a framework too. CONSORT, from 2010, by Schulz, Altman and Moher. Twenty-five items, and a flow diagram that follows every participant. The stimulation checklists add what CONSORT cannot know: the coil, the electrode, the field.

Whatever the technique, the clerk's file needs the same tabs. First, the device. The coil or transducer by model, and the machine, not just a percentage. Second, the exact montage, with the position and how it was found. Third, the field. Modelled, with the tool and the head model named, or measured, and in whom. Peterchev's paper says that knowing the field is necessary, though not sufficient.

Fourth, the sham. What was done to make it look real, as in episode nine point one. Fifth, the blinding test. Ask participants, and the raters, to guess the condition. Report the answers, whatever they were. Sixth, the adverse effects. All of them, in every group. Seventh, the data and the code.

The file also has a first page and a last page. The first page is the pre-registration, from episode thirteen point one. The last is the statistics, from thirteen point two.

Now the retrial. The second court takes the file, and only the file. Every tab that is filled, it can rebuild. Every tab left blank, it must guess. Each guess is a place where the second trial may differ from the first, and nobody will know why.

To sum up. The file must stand alone. Report the machine and the coil, not just a percentage of threshold. Report the exact montage and the modelled field. Report the sham, and test the blinding. Report every adverse effect, and share the data and the code. Use the checklist of your technique, and say so when none exists.
