Close according to whom?

Cat is closest to car by edit distance, one change, but closest to kitten by meaning. The same holds for signals: a bump and its late copy are far apart point by point, yet at zero once sliding is allowed. Changing the distance changes the table of neighbours, groups and outliers. Ask whenever two things are called close: close according to whom?

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here are three words: cat, car, and kitten. Which one is closest to cat?

Let us count letters. An edit is one small change to a word: add a letter, remove a letter, or replace one letter by another. Cat and car differ by one edit. Replace the t with an r, and you are there.

Cat and kitten are much farther apart. Replace the c with a k. Replace the a with an i. Then add a t, an e, and an n. That is five edits. So by letters, the neighbour of cat is car.

That count has a name: the edit distance. Vladimir Levenshtein defined it in 1965, in a paper about codes that correct errors. It is a perfectly good distance.

But now ask a child what a cat is close to. Nobody says car. A kitten is a small cat. By meaning, the neighbour of cat is kitten.

Same three words. Two neighbours. And neither answer is wrong. Each one comes from a question. Letters ask: how many edits? Meaning asks: what kind of thing is it? The words did not choose between these questions. We did.

Could meaning be measured with a number? Yes, but only after we describe each word by a list of numbers. That is a new object, and it lives in a new space. We will meet such spaces later. For now, keep the lesson: the same words gave two neighbours, because we asked two questions.

Surveyors know this well. The distance across a valley and the distance along the road are two different numbers. Both are correct. Before you give a number, you have to say how you measured.

Words are easy to argue about. So let us try something closer to your data.

Here are three signals. They are simulated, so we know exactly how they were made. Signal A is a bump. Signal A-prime is the same bump, arriving a little later. And signal B is a different curve: two smaller bumps, one on each side of the first, so it overlaps the bump.

Which signal is closest to A?

Here is a way to picture it. Each signal has one hundred and twenty samples. So each signal is a single point, in a space with one hundred and twenty axes. Comparing signals is comparing points. And a distance is a ruler laid between two of them.

First, compare them point by point. At every moment, take the gap between the two curves. Square the gaps, add them up, and take the square root. This is the straight line, in a space with one axis for every moment of the recording. It is the ruler most people reach for first. It is also the default of many tools. Scikit-learn's nearest neighbour classifier, for one, uses it unless you say otherwise.

Between A and B, the distance is two point seven. Between A and its own late copy, it is four point two. The copy is farther away. By this distance, A is closer to a different curve than to itself, a little late.

That feels wrong. A-prime is A. It only arrived later.

The reason is simple. Point by point, a bump at the wrong moment is a mistake twice: once where the bump should be, and once where it is. The straight line has no way to say that the shape is right and only the timing is off.

So change the question. Let one signal slide in time, by up to a quarter of the recording, and keep the best fit. Between A and A-prime, the distance falls to zero point three. That is only the noise we added. Between A and B, it barely moves: two point one.

Now the neighbour of A is its own copy.

In the picture of points, this ruler is different. It does not lie between two fixed points. It is the shortest of sixty-one rulers, one for each delay we allow.

Notice what this costs. We tried sixty-one delays instead of one. And notice what it ignores: when things happen. If the timing is exactly what you are studying, this distance will not see it. If the timing is only a nuisance, the straight line buries your result under it. Neither distance is better. Each one is right for a different question.

Same three signals. Nothing was moved, nothing was erased. Only the question changed.

Let us put this in a table. The same three signals sit in the rows and in the columns. Each square is one pair. The darker the square, the farther the pair. The diagonal stays blank, because a signal is never far from itself.

Read a row to find the neighbour of one signal: look for the lightest square. Here is the table for the point by point distance. A and B form a pair. A-prime stands alone, far from both. If you were looking for outliers, you would pick A-prime.

And here is the table when sliding is allowed. A and A-prime form a pair, almost touching. Now B is the one that stands alone. The outlier is a different signal.

Same objects. Another table.

A table is not a detail. Every method that comes after reads it. A neighbour is the nearest square in a row. A group is a set of objects that are close to one another. An outlier is an object far from all the others. Change the table, and neighbours, groups, and outliers change with it.

The words had their tables too. Letters made one. Meaning would make another. The three words never changed. The three signals never changed either. Only the distance did.

So here is the idea to keep. Data do not bring their distance with them. Before any analysis, someone decided what close means. Maybe it was you. Maybe it was the default setting of a function. Neighbours, groups, and outliers all follow from that choice.

It is a good habit to ask, whenever a method says two things are close: close according to whom? According to a straight line? According to a count of edits? According to a distance that forgives a delay?

In this series, every distance gets the same short card. What is the object? What is the distance? What does it ignore? What does it cost? And where is its trap? You have just seen the first three questions at work, without the card.

And a second question follows. Is this a distance at all? Not every way of comparing two things deserves the name. There are four rules, and they are short.

Let us look at them.

The four rules of a distance

A distance must keep four promises: never negative, zero only from an object to itself, the same in both directions, and no shortcut through a third place. Three villages at four, three and five kilometres show the triangle rule. It lets k-means skip work: with two thousand points and eight centres, a shortcut cut 288,000 distance computations to 68,000, same groups.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that data do not bring their distance with them. Someone chooses it.

So here is a fair question. When is a way of comparing allowed to be called a distance?

Let's find out on a map. Three villages: Alder, Birch and Cedar. You are the surveyor, and your job is to say how far apart they are.

We start with the simplest measure, the one a crow would use. A straight line, with no roads and no hills. Alder to Birch, four kilometres. Birch to Cedar, three. Alder to Cedar, five.

The map is illustrative, but the arithmetic is real.

Notice that we have not said what a village is. The promises will not care.

Now we ask what a measure like this has to promise before we trust it. There are four promises. Just four. They arrive one at a time.

The first promise. A distance is never negative.

That sounds too obvious to say. But look at what a plain difference does. Put Alder at zero on a line, and Birch at four. Birch minus Alder is four. Alder minus Birch is minus four.

A negative number of kilometres is not a way of being far away. So we keep the size of the difference, and drop its sign. Four, whichever way we look.

Rule one. A distance is greater than or equal to zero.

The second promise is about zero. A distance can be zero, but only from a place to itself.

Watch Cedar walk towards Alder. The reading falls. Five. Three. One. It reaches zero only at the moment when the two villages are in the same place.

If two different places could sit at distance zero, the ruler would not be telling them apart. It would be blind to the difference. Two villages with different names and no distance between them are really one village with two signposts.

Rule two. The distance is zero if, and only if, the two objects are the same.

The third promise. The same in both directions.

From Alder to Birch, the ruler reads four. Turn it around, from Birch to Alder, and it still reads four.

A straight line does this for free. Other things do not. A road that climbs a hill takes longer going up than coming down. We will meet such cases soon. For now, notice that we want one answer to the question, not two.

Rule three. The distance from A to B equals the distance from B to A.

The fourth promise is the clever one. It is about detours.

You want to go from Alder to Cedar. The straight line is five. Or you can stop at Birch on the way. Four, then three. Seven in total.

A detour never beats the direct way. Five is less than seven.

Now move Birch around. The detour changes, but it never drops below five. It reaches five only when Birch sits exactly on the straight line, and then the detour is no detour at all.

Think of a string, pulled tight from Alder to Cedar. No other route can be shorter than the string. That is all the fourth promise says.

Rule four. The direct distance is never longer than the distance through any third place. We call it the triangle rule, because three places make a triangle, and one side is never longer than the other two together.

Here is the whole list on one card. Never negative. Zero only for the same object. The same in both directions. And no shortcut through a third place.

The straight line passes all four tests, so all four lights turn on. This is the card we will fill in for every distance in this series.

The other lines are easy to fill too. The straight line ignores roads and hills. It costs almost nothing, one square root for each pair. And its trap is the valley, where the walk is much longer than the line.

The list has an author. In 1906, the French mathematician Maurice Fréchet wrote his doctoral thesis. He wanted to talk about distance between things that are not points on a map. Curves, for example, or whole functions.

He gave it a French name that means a gap. The name we use today came eight years later. In 1914, Felix Hausdorff published a book on set theory, and he called such a space a metric space.

Notice what the four rules leave out. They do not mention points, or lines, or kilometres. They only talk about a number for each pair of objects.

So anything that keeps the four promises has the right to be called a distance. Between curves. Between words. Between brain maps.

And the reverse is true too. If a promise fails, the tool may still be useful, but we should not call it a distance. Next time, we will break the rules one at a time.

Now, why do we care so much about the fourth rule? Because it lets you say something about two objects you never compared.

Suppose Alder is within one kilometre of Birch, and Birch is within one kilometre of Cedar. Then Alder is within two kilometres of Cedar, and nobody had to measure it. The neighbour of my neighbour is not far.

It also lets a program skip work. Take the k-means method, which groups points around centres. For each point, it asks which centre is nearest. The ordinary version measures the distance to every centre.

Here is one point and two centres. The point is one point one from the first centre. The two centres are three point two apart. That is more than twice one point one.

By the triangle rule, the second centre must be at least as far from the point as the first. We can skip it, without measuring.

In 2003, Charles Elkan built a fast version of k-means on this idea. It gives exactly the same answer as the ordinary version, with fewer distance computations.

We tried the simplest form of the shortcut on simulated data. Two thousand points, eight centres, seed twelve. The ordinary version computes two hundred eighty-eight thousand distances. With the shortcut, sixty-eight thousand, including the distances between centres. The groups are identical.

That only works because the fourth rule holds. A measure that breaks it cannot be trusted to skip.

So, remember four promises. Never negative. Zero only for itself. The same both ways. No shortcut through a third place. Keep them, and you have a distance. And the triangle rule is what lets an algorithm trust it.

When a rule breaks

Breaking a rule is information, not a mistake. A hill with one-way streets takes sixteen minutes up and three down, so closeness has a direction. Comparing signals by their average alone puts two different signals at zero. The squared gap breaks the shortcut rule, and the divergence between coins loses symmetry and shortcut, as zero point five three bits against zero point seven three show.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we met four rules. A distance is never negative. It is zero only from a place to itself. It is the same in both directions. And no detour is shorter than the direct road.

Today we do something rude. We take the rules away, one at a time, and watch what happens. A rule that breaks is not a mistake. It is a message about what the tool can do. Each rule gets its own example, and its own lamp. The lamp is lit while the rule holds. When the rule falls, it is crossed out in pink.

The first rule rarely breaks alone. In the textbook list, it follows from the other three. Go from A to B and back. The shortcut rule says that round trip is at least as long as staying put, and staying put costs nothing. By symmetry, the round trip is twice the distance. So the distance cannot be negative. If you keep the other rules, you get this one for free. So we start with a rule that is easy to break without noticing: the same in both directions.

Picture a hill. Three places stand on it: one at the foot, one halfway up, and one at the top. The streets are one-way. From the foot, a road climbs to the middle. From the middle, another road climbs to the top. And from the top, a short, steep street runs straight back down to the foot. The times, in minutes, are made up for the picture.

From the foot to the top takes sixteen minutes. From the top to the foot takes three.

Same two places. Two different numbers. Here is the table. Each row says where you start, and each column says where you arrive. It is not the same table turned on its side. The foot-to-top square is dark, and the top-to-foot square is almost paper.

So what does "the foot is close to the top" mean? Nothing, until you say which way. Without the symmetry rule, closeness has a direction.

This is not only about hills. Psychologists found the same thing in people's heads. In 1977, Amos Tversky reported that people judge North Korea to be more similar to China than China to be similar to North Korea. He explained it by prominence: the better known country tends to serve as the reference. Judgments of similarity are not exactly distances, but they show how natural an asymmetry can feel. We will keep that in mind, because the next episode is about similarity.

Now the second rule: zero only from a place to itself.

Here are three simulated signals, drawn with seed thirteen. A is a bump. B is a slow wave. C is a bump again, but flatter, and riding higher. They are clearly different curves.

Now suppose we compare signals by one number only: their average. The distance between two signals is the gap between their averages.

A and B have the same average. So their distance is zero. Two different signals, at distance zero. In the table, the square for A and B is paper, just like the diagonal.

This little eye watches what a distance ignores. Here it closes: it cannot tell A from B. That is the price of keeping one number. A summary is cheap, but it throws the shape away.

Without this rule, a distance cannot separate two objects. The practical answer is to treat them as one object. Everything the distance can say about A, it says about B. Group the signals by this distance, and A and B always land together, whatever they look like.

Next, the rule that sounds the most technical: no detour is shorter than the direct road.

Take three points on a line: zero, one, and two. Compare them by the square of their gap. That is a popular choice, because it is cheap and smooth.

From zero to one, the square of the gap is one. From one to two, it is one. But from zero straight to two, it is four.

Go through the middle, and the total is two. Go direct, and it is four. The detour is shorter than the direct road. The shortcut rule has fallen.

Why does it matter? Because the rule is what makes friendship chain. With a true distance, if A is close to B and B is close to C, then A cannot be far from C. With the squared gap, it can. Put ten points in a row, one step apart. Every neighbour is at squared distance one. But the two ends are at eighty-one. With the plain gap, the ends are at nine.

For finding the nearest neighbour, the square changes nothing. It puts the pairs in the same order. What it breaks is the chaining, and every method that skips calculations by relying on the shortcut, like the fast version of k-means that Charles Elkan published in 2003. Those methods need the plain distance, not its square. Take the square root, and the rule comes back.

Last, a word you will hear often: divergence. A divergence is a gap between two distributions that keeps only the first two rules. It is never negative, and it is zero only for identical distributions. But it need not be symmetric, and it need not obey the shortcut rule.

The best known divergence comes from Kullback and Leibler, in 1951. Let us feel it on three coins. The first lands heads nine times in ten. The second is fair. The third lands heads one time in ten. We measure the gap in bits.

Measured from the first coin to the fair one, the divergence is zero point five three one bits. From the fair coin back to the first, it is zero point seven three seven. Not the same in both directions.

And the shortcut? From the heads-heavy coin to the tails-heavy coin, the divergence is two point five three six bits. Through the fair coin, the total is only one point two six eight. The detour is again shorter.

So a divergence is a gap with direction, and with no promise about shortcuts. It is still very useful, and later chapters use it a lot. But you must say which way you are measuring.

Let us sum up in one table. Four ways of comparing, and four rules. Travel time on the hill loses the symmetry. The average-only gap loses the zero rule. The squared gap loses the shortcut. And the divergence loses both the symmetry and the shortcut.

A rule that falls is not a defect. It is information. Without symmetry, "A is close to B" must say which way. Without the shortcut rule, two objects close to a third may be far from each other. Without the zero rule, some different objects cannot be told apart.

From now on, every distance card in this series will say which of the four rules hold.

Similarity is not distance upside down

Turning a similarity into a distance is not just a sign change. One minus the correlation gave 0.29, 0.29 and 1 for three signals, so the detour beats the direct road: seventeen percent of random triples broke the triangle rule. Taking the square root of two times one minus the correlation repairs it. One minus the absolute correlation also fails, in fourteen percent of triples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A similarity is big when two things look alike. A distance is small when two things look alike. So turning one into the other seems to be a matter of sign. Put a minus in front, or subtract from one, and you are done. Correlations and cosines are used everywhere to say that two things look alike. Many methods then ask for a distance. This episode is about why it is not that simple, and how to do it properly.

Take three simulated signals. Call them alpha, beta and gamma. Each one is centred and scaled to length one, so only its shape is left. Alpha and beta look alike. Beta and gamma look alike. Alpha and gamma do not look alike at all. Their correlations say the same. Alpha with beta is zero point seven one. Beta with gamma is zero point seven one. Alpha with gamma is zero. Correlation is a similarity. Big means alike, and it runs from minus one to one. The usual way to turn it into a distance is one minus the correlation.

Let us do it. Alpha to beta becomes zero point two nine. Beta to gamma becomes zero point two nine. Alpha to gamma becomes one. Here is the table. Darker means farther. It looks like a perfectly good table of distances. Now check the fourth rule, the triangle. A detour is never shorter than the direct road. Going from alpha to gamma by way of beta costs zero point two nine plus zero point two nine. That is zero point five eight. Going straight from alpha to gamma costs one. The detour is shorter than the direct road. Picture two rods of length zero point two nine, joined at beta. They cannot reach across a gap of one. A triangle with these three sides cannot be drawn. So one minus the correlation is not a distance. Victor Solo wrote it down in two thousand nineteen. Nor is it a rare accident. Roll the dice. We drew ten thousand random triples of slow signals, simulated, and checked each one. Seventeen percent broke the triangle rule.

The repair is a square root. Here is why it works. Centre each signal and give it length one. Now every signal is an arrow of length one. Alpha points straight ahead. Gamma points at a right angle to it. Beta sits halfway between. The straight-line distance between two such arrows, squared, is two minus two times the correlation. So two times one minus the correlation is a squared distance. It is not a distance. You have met this trap before. In episode one point three, the squared gap between points on a line broke the same rule. Take the square root, and the squared distance becomes a distance. The square root of two times one minus the correlation gives zero point seven seven for alpha to beta, zero point seven seven for beta to gamma, and one point four one for alpha to gamma. Check the triangle. Zero point seven seven plus zero point seven seven is one point five three. That is more than one point four one. The rods reach, and they close a triangle, bent at beta. That triangle is real. It is the triangle of the three arrows, drawn flat. The angle between the arrows is a distance too. It is forty-five degrees, forty-five degrees, and ninety degrees. Same order as before, another scale. In the ten thousand random triples, neither of these two ever broke the rule.

Now fill in the card for the straight line between centred signals. The object is a signal. The distance is the straight line between two signals, once each one is centred and given length one. What does it ignore? Two things. Adding a constant, and multiplying by a positive number. Lift a signal, make it one point six times bigger, and its correlation with the original is still one. The distance is still zero. What does it cost? Very little. Subtract the mean, divide by the length, then one ordinary straight-line distance. And the trap sits on the second rule. Two different recordings that differ only by an offset and a positive gain are at distance zero. On raw signals, this distance cannot tell them apart. That is not a defect. It is the first sign of a choice, and the next episode is entirely about it.

Sometimes the sign should not matter. A signal and its mirror image carry the same pattern. Two sensors on opposite sides of a source can record the same wave upside down. Correlation minus one means opposite. One minus the correlation puts opposites as far apart as possible, at two. So people use one minus the absolute value of the correlation. Mirror images land at zero. It looks like the fix. It is not a distance either. On our three signals the correlations are all positive, so the absolute value changes nothing. Zero point two nine, zero point two nine, one. The triangle fails again. In the dice test, fourteen percent of the triples broke it. Solo shows this one too. The repair is the square root of one minus the correlation squared. It is the size of the sine of the angle between the signals. Van Dongen and Enright, in two thousand twelve, show that a sine of this kind gives a distance on centred data. Zero point seven one, zero point seven one, and one. Zero point seven one plus zero point seven one is one point four one, and that is more than one. In the dice test, never a violation. This one gives zero between a signal and its mirror image. That is what we asked for, and it is a choice, not an accident.

So there are several ways to turn a correlation into a dissimilarity. They do not keep the same rules, and they do not answer the same question. Two of them are not distances at all. Before you hand a table of one minus correlation to a clustering method or a map, check what the transformation kept, and what the method needs. Some methods use the triangle rule to skip work. Give them a table that breaks it, and they can skip the wrong things. A minus sign is not a proof. Check the four rules, then choose.

What a distance ignores

Every distance has a blind spot. Five copies of one signal, each altered in one way, are compared with four distances: the straight line sees everything, the correlation distance ignores shift and gain, a histogram distance ignores timing, and a delay distance ignores delay. Whether ignoring amplitude helps depends on the question. The more a distance ignores, the closer unrelated things get.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A distance is a way of looking. And every way of looking has a blind spot.

Last time we asked whether a similarity is just a distance turned upside down. Today the question is simpler. What does a distance refuse to see?

Here is a signal. It is simulated, with one hundred and twenty samples: a bump riding on a slow wave. Now we make five copies. Each copy changes the signal in exactly one way.

The first copy is shifted. Every sample is moved up by the same amount. The second is amplified. Every sample is multiplied by one point six. The third is delayed. The whole signal arrives twenty-four samples late, and what falls off the end comes back at the start. The fourth is shuffled. The same values, in a random order. The fifth is noisy. A little random noise is added to every sample.

Five copies, five changes. Now we ask four distances to compare each copy with the original. For each one, the question is the same. Does it notice?

A distance notices when its value is not zero. It stays at zero when, for that distance, the copy and the original are the same thing. We call a value zero when it is smaller than one billionth. The rounding errors of these computations are far smaller than that.

First, the straight line, point by point. It is the ruler from the first episode. It compares sample one with sample one, sample two with sample two, and so on. Every copy moves at least one sample, so every copy is seen. Shifted, four point nine. Amplified, one point two. Delayed, two point seven. Shuffled, two point five. Noisy, zero point nine. The straight line sees everything. That sounds like a virtue. Hold on to that thought.

Second, the distance built from correlation. It is the square root of two times one minus r, from the last episode. It is the straight line between the two signals once each has been centred and scaled to the same length. Centring removes a shift. Scaling by a positive number removes a gain. So this distance stays at zero for the shifted copy, and at zero for the amplified copy. But it does see the delay: one point five. It sees the shuffle: one point four. And it sees the noise: zero point four. This distance does not care how high the signal sits, or how big it is. It cares about the shape in time.

Third, a distance between histograms. Count how many samples fall in each of sixteen ranges of amplitude. Then compare the two histograms, by adding up the differences between their bars. The result lies between zero and one. This distance has no idea when anything happens. A delay moves values around in time, but the bars do not change. A shuffle does the same. Delayed, zero. Shuffled, zero. But a shift pushes every value into another bar: zero point eight eight. Amplified, zero point four three. Noisy, zero point two eight.

Fourth, a distance that allows a delay. Try every delay from minus thirty to plus thirty samples. That is sixty-one tries. Take the straight line each time, and keep the smallest. The delayed copy is exactly twenty-four samples late, so one of the tries puts it back on the original. Distance zero. Everything else is still seen. Shifted, four point nine. Amplified, one point two. Shuffled, two point four. Noisy, zero point nine, because for noise the best try is no delay at all.

Now read the grid. No distance stays at zero in front of all five changes, and none of them sees only one. The straight line ignores nothing. The correlation distance ignores a shift and a gain. The histogram distance ignores the order in time. And the delay distance ignores a delay.

That refusal is a choice. Take amplitude. Suppose it changes because an electrode makes poor contact with the skin. The resistance between skin and electrode, its impedance, is known to affect the quality of an EEG recording. Then amplitude is a nuisance, and we want a distance that ignores it.

But suppose amplitude is the effect we are looking for, a response that grows with the dose. Then ignoring it throws the answer away. Same change, opposite decisions. Only the question can say which one is right.

Ignore too little, and the nuisances drown the effect. Ignore too much, and everything looks alike. Here is the signal and its shuffled copy. They are two different signals. One has a bump. The other is a jumble. Yet their histograms are identical, so the histogram distance puts them at zero. If you used it to ask whether a signal differs from a scrambled version of itself, it would say no.

Now we can fill the whole card, for the distance that allows a delay. The object is a signal: one hundred and twenty numbers. The distance is the smallest straight line over the delays. What it ignores is a delay of up to thirty samples. What it costs is sixty-one straight lines instead of one.

Then the four rules. It is never negative. Yes. It is the same in both directions. Yes. Zero only for the same signal? No. A delayed copy is a different signal, and it sits at zero. And the shortcut rule? We do not know. We tried three thousand triples of unrelated signals and found no violation. But a test is not a proof, and a limited range of delays is not the complete set of delays. So that light stays dashed.

The last line of the card is the trap. The more you ignore, the closer unrelated things get. We drew three hundred pairs of unrelated smooth signals. With no delay allowed, their average distance is one point four. Allow thirty samples either way, and it falls to one point one five. Allow sixty, and it falls to one point one.

A signal and its own delayed, slightly noisy copy sit at about zero point three, once the right delay is allowed. Here the gap is wide. But every extra freedom narrows it, and a long enough search can find a match in noise.

So a distance is defined as much by what it refuses to see as by what it measures. Before you choose one, say what should not matter. Then check that the distance ignores that, and only that.

The distance you already chose

Three everyday analyses hide one distance. The mean is the point minimising squared straight-line distances, k-means alternates nearest centre and mean, and principal component analysis fits the line minimising squared distances. Switching to Manhattan distance changed the group of seven of forty points. The episode separates what is established from suggestions, such as redoing an analysis with one other distance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You have used a distance today. You probably did not choose it.

Open any analysis you run every week. Somewhere inside it, there is a ruler. This is the last episode of the chapter, so it is about ideas. We will open up three of them, like watches on a bench. Then we will keep two lists apart: what is established, and what is worth trying.

Here is a small cloud of forty points. They are simulated, with seed sixteen. Each point could be a person, a trial, or a recording. Two columns of numbers, nothing more.

First, the mean. You learned it as add everything up and divide. Here is another way to say the same thing. Put a pin anywhere in the cloud. Measure the straight-line distance from the pin to every point. Square each distance, and add them up. Call that the cost of the pin.

Move the pin, and the cost changes. Far from the cloud, it is huge. In the middle, it is small. There is exactly one place where it is smallest, and that place is the mean. On this cloud, the cost at the mean is two hundred and two. Move the pin one step to the right, and it rises to two hundred and forty-two.

So the mean is not only a formula. It is the answer to a question. Which point is closest to everyone, when closest means the smallest sum of squared straight-line distances? Change the distance, and the question changes. Measure with the Manhattan distance, the sum of absolute differences, and the answer becomes the median, taken column by column.

This way of defining a mean is credited to Maurice Fréchet, in nineteen forty-eight. He asked for nothing but a distance. So the same definition works for curves, for shapes, for any objects you can measure. We call the result a Fréchet mean, and the series will use it again and again.

One more thing the cost tells you. Add a single far-away point, and the mean moves by zero point two three. The median moves by zero point zero two. Squaring gives far points a loud voice. That is a property of the distance, not of the data.

Second, k-means. You ask for two groups. The algorithm starts with two centres, placed anywhere. Then it repeats two moves. First, give each point to its nearest centre, by straight line. Second, move each centre to the mean of its points.

Look at what those moves are made of. The first move uses the straight line. The second is the pin from a minute ago, the point that makes squared straight-line distances smallest. Both moves lean on the same distance.

The total cost can only go down. Here it falls from one hundred and seventy-two to eighty-five, and after three passes nothing moves any more.

The procedure was written by Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs in nineteen fifty-seven, for signal quantisation. It stayed unpublished until nineteen eighty-two. The name k-means comes from James MacQueen, in nineteen sixty-seven.

Now keep the cloud, keep the same two starting centres, and change one thing only: the distance. Use Manhattan distance, and the centres become medians. Seven of the forty points change group. Same data, same algorithm, other groups. Nothing in the data told it which ruler to use.

Third, principal component analysis. You look for the line that fits the cloud best. Best means this: draw the shortest segment from each point to the line, at a right angle. Square those distances and add them up. The best line makes that sum smallest.

Turn the line. When it points across the cloud, the segments are long. When it points along the cloud, they shrink. The best line sits at twenty-five degrees. The sum of squares is eleven there, and one hundred and ninety-one for the line across. That line keeps ninety-four percent of the spread.

Karl Pearson posed this problem in nineteen oh one, in a paper called On lines and planes of closest fit to systems of points in space. It is the same story again. Squared, straight-line distances, chosen before you arrived.

Three analyses, one distance. None of them asked you. That is not a flaw. The straight line is a fine default, and often the right one. The trouble starts only when you do not know that you chose it.

So there is no analysis without a distance. The only choice is between a distance you chose, and a distance you inherited.

Now the two lists. On the left, what is established. The mean is the point that minimises the sum of squared distances, and Fréchet extended that to any space with a distance. K-means alternates between nearest centre and mean, from Lloyd and MacQueen. And Pearson's best line minimises squared distances at a right angle.

On the right, what is worth trying. These are suggestions, not results. One: redo one of your own analyses with a single other distance, and watch what changes. The neighbours, the groups, the outliers. Two: fill in the distance card for the distance you use today. Five lines. If one of them stays blank, you have found something.

Keep the columns apart in your head, too. The left is the literature. The right is a habit I think is worth having. It is not a finding.

This chapter gave you the words: two ways to measure, four rules, what happens when a rule breaks, similarity against distance, and what a distance ignores. The rest of the series is a catalogue to choose from, arranged by the kind of object you want to compare.
