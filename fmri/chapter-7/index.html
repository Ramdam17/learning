PLATE 7.14:11

Patterns, not blobs · MVPA

Two conditions can give the same mean activation yet different voxel patterns, like two books of equal weight with different barcodes. Multivariate pattern analysis trains a classifier on patterns across voxels and tests it on new data. Haxby showed object categories can be read from distributed, overlapping patterns, but decoding shows information is present, not that the brain uses it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 7, Decoding. Our new world is a library. And our first question changes the way we look at fMRI. Until now we asked, where does the brain light up? Now we ask, what information is in this pattern?

Picture two books on the library cart. Same size, same thickness, same weight. If you only weigh them, they look identical. But each book has a barcode on the back. The stripes differ, and a scanner tells the books apart instantly. The information is not in the total. It is in the pattern.

A classic GLM analysis weighs the books. In each voxel, it asks whether the average response is higher in condition A than in condition B. Then often, it averages over a region. Two conditions can give exactly the same mean activation in a region, and the GLM sees nothing. Yet voxel by voxel, one condition may push some voxels up and others down in a consistent arrangement. That arrangement is a barcode.

Multivariate pattern analysis or MVPA reads the barcodes. Instead of one voxel at a time, it treats the response of many voxels together as a pattern. For each trial or block, you get a vector one value per voxel. Then a classifier, for example, a linear support vector machine or logistic regression, learns to separate the patterns of condition A from those of condition B. Finally, you test it on data it has never seen. If it predicts the condition better than chance, the region carries information about it.

The founding study came in 2001. James Haxby and colleagues showed people pictures from eight categories—faces, cats, houses, chairs, scissors, shoes, bottles, and scrambled control images. They split the data in two halves and asked a simple question. Is the pattern in ventral temporal cortex more similar for the same category across the halves than for different categories? In pairwise comparisons, the category was identified correctly 96% of the time.

And the striking part, when they removed the voxels that responded most to a category, the category could still be identified with 94% accuracy. Even weak responses carried information. Representations of faces and objects were distributed and overlapping, not confined to one specialized patch.

Decoding is now everywhere. Perceived orientation in visual cortex, imagined objects, intentions, memories. A few habits come with it. Patterns are fragile, so smoothing is usually kept light or skipped, as we saw in chapter 4. Each trial or block needs its own estimate, for example one beta per trial, and the accuracy must be tested on independent data, which deserves the whole next episode.

Now the most important caution. A successful decoder proves that information is present in the pattern. It does not prove that the brain uses that information. A classifier can pick up anything that differs between conditions. A difference in attention, in eye movements, in difficulty, in head motion, or even in the timing of trials. The librarian reads the barcode, but the author may never have looked at it.

And a decoder that fails does not prove absence. The information may be coded at a scale finer than our voxels, or in a form a linear classifier cannot read.

Finally, decoding accuracy is not the same as effect size, and above chance does not mean large. 55% can be significant with many trials and still tell us little.

To sum up, the GLM weighs the books, MVPA reads the barcodes. A classifier learns patterns across voxels and is tested on new data. Haxby and colleagues showed in 2001 that object categories can be read from distributed overlapping patterns even without the most responsive voxels. Decoding shows that information is present, not that it is used. Next time, cross-validation done right.

PLATE 7.24:17

Cross-validation done right

Cross-validation tests a decoder on data it has never seen, and fMRI needs splits by run or participant because neighboring trials share drifts. Leaks let information from the sealed test set into training: preprocessing on all data, selecting voxels before splitting, tuning on the test set, and neighbors in time. Permutations of the whole pipeline give true chance, and accuracy carries large error bars.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A decoder is only as good as the test it passes. Cross-validation is how we test it. Done right it is honest. Done wrong, it can report 70% accuracy on pure noise.

Back in the library imagine two rooms. The reading room where the classifier studies. In a sealed archive, boxes that have never been opened. The classifier learns in the reading room, then we open a sealed box and see if it guesses correctly. That is the whole idea. Train on one part of the data, test on another part, never seen.

Cross-validation repeats this several times. Split the data into folds. Train on all folds but one, test on the left-out fold, rotate, and average the accuracy. Every sample gets to be a test sample once, and never while it is also in the training set.

In fMRI, how you split matters enormously. Trials close in time share slow drifts, and the hemodynamic response of one trial spills into the next. If a trial in the training set and its neighbor in the test set overlap, the classifier can recognize the time, not the condition. So we split by run. Leave one run out, train on the other runs, test on the held-out run. For decoding across people, leave participants out, never mix trials of the same person into both sets.

Now the leaks. A leak is any path by which information from the sealed archive slips into the reading room. A sticky note from a sealed box tucked into a training book.

Leak 1. Preprocessing on all the data. If you z-score, normalize, or fit any transform using the whole dataset, the training set has peeked at the test set. Fit every step on the training data only, then apply it to the test data.

Leak 2. Selecting voxels before splitting. Suppose you pick the thousand voxels that best separate the conditions, using all trials, and only then cross-validate. The selection already used the test trials. This leak is powerful. On pure noise, it can produce accuracies far above chance. Feature selection belongs inside the loop, repeated within each training fold.

Leak number three, tuning on the test set. If you try many settings and keep the one with the best test score, the test set is no longer sealed. Use nested cross-validation, an inner loop to choose settings and outer loop to test. Or use sensible defaults.

Leak number four, neighbors in time which we just met. Trials from the same run or adjacent events in both sets.

Then chance. With two classes you might think chance is 50%. But with few samples, unbalanced classes or a leak, the real chance level can be higher. The honest way is a permutation test. Shuffle the condition labels respecting the structure of runs, rerun the entire pipeline, including any selection, and record the accuracy. Repeat a thousand times. Where your real accuracy falls in that null distribution gives your p-value.

Finally, how sure is the number itself? In 2018, Gaël Varoquaux showed that with sample sizes typical of neuroimaging, cross-validated accuracy has large error bars, around plus or minus 10% for 100 samples. And the standard error across folds strongly underestimates that uncertainty. In an earlier review, he and colleagues found that leave one out gives unstable estimates and recommended repeated random splits. For example, many splits holding out 20% of the data while respecting runs or participants.

Here is a picture to keep. A score of 70% with features selected on all the data. Move the selection inside the loop and the score falls back to chance. Same data, same classifier. One leak.

To sum up, train in the reading room, test on the sealed archive. Split by run or by participant. Fit every preprocessing and selection step inside the training folds. Tune with nested cross-validation. Check chance with permutations of the whole pipeline. And remember the error bars, with 100 samples about 10% either way. PLATE 7.34:20

Representational similarity analysis

Representational similarity analysis describes how a region arranges many conditions with a distance table between every pair of stimuli, the representational dissimilarity matrix. Like comparing two atlases through their distance tables, it compares brains, models, behavior and species without aligning voxels. Human and monkey inferior temporal cortex share one geometry, and deep networks come close.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Decoding asks, can I tell these conditions apart? Representational similarity analysis asks something richer. How is the whole space of conditions arranged in this brain region?

On a shelf of the library, there is an old road atlas. At the back, there is a table, the distance between every pair of cities. You do not need the maps to read it. Paris and Lyon are close, Paris and Moscow far apart. Now take a second atlas in another language in miles instead of kilometers, drawn with another projection. The maps look nothing alike, but you can still compare the two distance tables cell by cell. If the tables agree, the atlases describe the same geography.

That distance table has a name in neuroscience, the Representational Dissimilarity Matrix or RDM. Here is how to build one. Show many stimuli, for example pictures of faces, animals, tools, and places. For each stimulus, estimate the response pattern across the voxels of a region. Then, for every pair of stimuli, compute how different their patterns are. A common measure is 1 minus the correlation between the two patterns. Fill in the table. It is symmetric with zeros on the diagonal, and it describes the geometry of the representation.

The method was laid out by Nikolaus Kriegeskorte, Marieke Mur, and Peter Bandettini in 2008. Its power is that it is a common language. Once you have an RDM, the voxels have disappeared. What remains is the pattern of distances between conditions, and that can be compared with almost anything.

With a model, for example, a matrix that says animate things are similar to each other and different from inanimate things. With behavior, how often people confuse two images or how similar they judge them. With another brain region, another person, another method, or even another species.

The same year, Kriegeskorte and colleagues showed 92 object images to humans in the scanner, and compared their inferior temporal cortex with recordings from neurons in the same region of monkeys, shown the same images. The two RDMs matched. Both separated animate from inanimate objects and faces and bodies within the animate group. Two species, two very different measurements, one shared geometry, without aligning a single voxel to a single neuron.

Later, in 2014, Seyed Khaligh-Razavi and Kriegeskorte compared many computer vision models with human and monkey inferior temporal cortex. A deep convolutional network trained on more than a million labeled images explain the brain best, although not fully. RSA became a bridge between brains and artificial networks.

How do you compare two RDMs? Usually with a rank correlation like Spearman between their cells because we care about the ordering of distances more than their exact values. Test significance by permuting the condition labels, which reorders rows and columns together. And estimate a noise ceiling, how well do RDMs from different participants agree with each other? No model can be expected to do better than the data agree with themselves.

A few cautions. Noise inflates all distances, so prefer cross-validated distance measures, estimated on independent runs which are not biased upwards. Design matters. If two conditions differ in low-level features like brightness or size, the RDM will reflect that. And as always, a match between a model and a brain RDM shows that they share a geometry, not that they compute the same way.

To sum up, RSA asks how a region arranges many conditions. The RDM is the distance table at the back of the atlas, one cell per pair of conditions. It is a common language that compares brains, models, behavior, and species without aligning voxels. Human and monkey inferior temporal cortex share one geometry, and deep networks come close. Compare RDMs with rank correlations, permutations, and a noise ceiling. Next time, encoding models and naturalistic stimuli.

PLATE 7.44:27

Encoding models and naturalistic stimuli

Encoding goes from stimulus to voxel: describe the stimulus with features, add delayed copies for the slow blood response, and fit one ridge regression per voxel. Each voxel is like a reader whose borrowing card predicts what it will love. Judged on new data, such models mapped semantics across cortex, though correlated features mean good prediction does not prove which feature a voxel uses.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last episode, we compared brains through their distance tables. Today we flip the arrow around. Decoding goes from the brain to the stimulus. Look at a pattern, guess what was seen. Encoding goes the other way. Start from the stimulus and predict how every single voxel will respond.

Picture a small library with a group of regular readers. Each reader has a borrowing card. One loves stories about the sea. Another lights up at numbers and dates. A third only cares about people and their feelings. A good librarian learns these tastes from the cards. Then when a brand new book arrives, she can predict, reader by reader, who will love it and who will yawn.

In an encoding model, each voxel is one of those readers. The borrowing card is a set of weights. And the book is the stimulus, described as a list of numbers.

That description is called the feature space, and choosing it is the real scientific decision. For pictures, features might be edges at different positions and orientations. For speech, they might be the sounds, the words, or the meaning of the words. In the study by Huth and colleagues, published in Nature in 2016, each word was described by how often it appears next to 985 common English words. So every moment of a story becomes a long vector of meaning.

Now, a reader does not react the instant a page is turned. The blood response arrives several seconds later. So the model gets a few delayed copies of every feature, typically shifted by 2 to 8 seconds. The fit then learns its own shape of the delay for each voxel.

Fitting is a regression, one per voxel. With tens of thousands of voxels, that is tens of thousands of regressions. And there are often more weights than time points, so plain least squares would memorize the noise. The usual fix is ridge regression. It gently shrinks all the weights towards zero. How strongly it shrinks is chosen by cross-validation, inside the training data only.

Then comes the moment of truth. The model hears a story it has never heard. It predicts a time course for every voxel, and we correlate that prediction with what the voxel actually did. One number per voxel mapped on the brain. This is a map of where the feature space explains activity, tested on data the model never saw.

Huth and colleagues had seven people listen to more than two hours of stories from the Moth Radio Hour. They held out a 10-minute story for testing. The semantic models predicted responses well across large parts of cortex. Lateral and ventral temporal cortex, lateral and medial parietal cortex, and several regions of prefrontal cortex. The maps were strikingly similar from one person to the next, which let the authors build a semantic atlas.

Here is a lovely bonus. Once you can predict brains, you can also decode them. Kay and colleagues in 2008 built encoding models of early visual cortex. Then they showed 120 new photographs. For each recorded pattern, they predicted the pattern for every candidate photo and picked the closest match. They identified the right image 92% of the time for one participant and 72% for the other. Chance was below 1%.

Why stories and movies rather than tidy blocks? Natural stimuli pack many features into a single session keep people engaged and sit closer to everyday life. But they come with a catch. Features travel together. Words about places often come with words about seeing. So a good prediction does not prove which feature a voxel truly cares about. Careful studies fit competing feature spaces and ask how much each one explains on its own.

Two more cautions. Compare your scores to a noise ceiling because a noisy voxel can never be predicted perfectly. And remember that a model which predicts well says the features carry the right information. It does not prove the brain computes them the same way.

So to recap, encoding goes from stimulus to voxel. Describe the stimulus with features, add delays, fit one ridge regression per voxel, and judge it only on new data.

PLATE 7.54:20

Brains watching the same movie · ISC

Inter-subject correlation treats other brains as the model. People watch the same movie or hear the same story, and a voxel's time course is correlated across them, so stimulus-driven activity remains while private noise does not. Null distributions come from time shifts or resampling, and a high value means a region follows the stimulus, not what it represents.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, an encoding model needed a careful list of features. Today, we drop the features altogether. We use another brain as the model. This is inter-subject correlation.

Picture Story Hour at the Library. A librarian reads aloud to a circle of listeners. Nobody told them when to react. Yet they all gasp at the same page, laugh at the same line, and lean forward when the wolf appears. If you recorded each listener's reactions over time, the curves would rise and fall together. Not because the listeners talk to each other, but because the story drives them all at once.

Inter-subject correlation, or ISC, does exactly this with voxels. Several people watch the same movie or hear the same story in the scanner. Take one voxel at the same place in every brain and correlate its time course across people. If a region is driven by the story, its time courses will rise and fall together. If a region is busy with private business, they will not.

The classic study came from Uri Hasson and colleagues, published in Science in 2004. Five people freely watched 30 minutes of a feature film, The Good, The Bad, and The Ugly. On average, about 29% of the cortical surface showed a highly significant correlation across viewers. And it went far beyond visual and auditory cortex, into association areas. As a control, the same analysis on people resting in the dark for 10 minutes gave negligible correlations.

Why is this so useful? Think about the noise. A heartbeat, a breath, a small head movement, a wandering thought, these are private to each listener. They do not line up across people. Only what the story drives is shared. So ISC filters out much of the idiosyncratic noise without any model of the stimulus.

There are two common ways to compute it. In the pairwise approach, you correlate every possible pair of subjects and average. In the leave one out approach, you correlate each subject with the average of all the others. The average of the others is a cleaner signal, so leave one out values are usually higher. Report which one you used.

Now the statistics need care. Pairs of subjects are not independent, each person appears in many pairs. And fMRI time courses are smooth, so neighboring time points are not independent either. A textbook test on the raw correlation would be far too optimistic. Instead build a null distribution. You can circularly shift each subject's time course or randomize its phases, which breaks the alignment with the story but keeps the smoothness. Or you can bootstrap by resampling subjects. Then correct for the many voxels as in Chapter 5.

ISC can also answer sharper questions by changing the stimulus. Lerner and colleagues in 2011 played a story intact or scrambled at the level of paragraphs, sentences, or words. Early auditory cortex stayed reliable even for scrambled words. Higher regions only synchronized when longer stretches of the story were intact. Each region seems to have its own window of time.

A cousin method is inter-subject functional connectivity. Instead of the same voxel across brains, you correlate region A in one brain with region B in another. Private noise cancels out again, leaving only the network activity driven by the story.

Two cautions. A low ISC does not mean a region is idle. It may respond strongly but differently in each person, for instance with memories or emotions of their own. And a high ISC only says the region follows the stimulus. It does not say what it represents. For that, you need models like the ones from the previous episodes.

So to recap, same stimulus many brains. Correlate each voxel across people. Choose pairwise or leave one out. Build the null by shifting time or resampling subjects. And remember that shared means stimulus-driven, not understood.

This closes the decoding chapter. Next we climb to the observatory, where scanners get stronger, faster and more personal. First stop, 7 Tesla and the layers of cortex.
