Neural coding part 1. How many bits does a spike carry?

Welcome to the last chapter, Applications. Everything we built, entropy, mutual information, estimators, transfer entropy, decompositions, now meets the brain. We start where information theory first met neuroscience, with single neurons and their spikes. How much does a neuron tell us about what it sees?

Here is the intuition. Picture a radio station that broadcasts in clicks like a telegraph. A listener records the clicks. Some of the variety in the clicks reflects the broadcast. Different programmes produce different click patterns. Some of it is noise. The same programme replayed never produces exactly the same clicks twice. The information carried by the clicks is the variety minus the noise. To measure both, you need two recordings, a long one with ever-changing programmes to see the full variety and many replays of the same short programme to see the noise.

That is the direct method of Steven Strong, Roland Koberle, Rob de Ruyter van Steveninck and William Bialek, published in 1998. Divide the spike train into small time bins of width delta tau, each holding a one if a spike occurred and a zero otherwise. Group consecutive bins into words, covering a window of length T. First, during a long, non-repeating stimulus, estimate the entropy of these words, the total entropy, the full variety of the neurons' responses. Second, present the same stimulus segment many times, and at each moment of the segment, estimate the entropy of the words across repeats, the noise entropy, the variety that has nothing to do with the stimulus. Average the noise entropy over the segment. The information is the total entropy minus the noise entropy. Divide by T to get bits per second. Divide again by the firing rate to get bits per spike.

A word concretely. With bins of 2 milliseconds and a window of 24, a word is a string of 12 zeros and ones. For example, 0001, then six zeros, then 10, two spikes at the fourth and the eleventh bin. With bins of 12 milliseconds, the same moment becomes a word of two symbols, one, one, a spike in each half. The fine word knows exactly when, the coarse word only knows how many and roughly where.

The method makes no assumption about what the neuron encodes. It does not need a model of the stimulus features, a decoder, or a tuning curve. That is its strength. Its price is data. The number of possible words grows as two to the power of the number of bins, so the entropies must be estimated with care, and Strong and colleagues extrapolated their estimates to infinite data and to long windows.

They applied it to H1, a motion-sensitive neuron in the fly's visual system, recorded for hours. With bins of 3 milliseconds, the neuron transmitted information about the visual stimulus at rates of up to 90 bits per second, within a factor of two of the limits set by the entropy of the spike train itself.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated a neuron firing about 30 spikes per second, driven by a fast-changing stimulus with a refractory period of 2 milliseconds. 300 seconds of non-repeating stimulus for the total entropy, 300 repeats of a 2-second segment for the noise entropy. Words cover 24 milliseconds. All of it is simulated.

With bins of 12 milliseconds, 2 bins per word, the total entropy is 71 bits per second, the noise entropy 44, and the information 27 bits per second, about 0.9 bits per spike.

With bins of 6 milliseconds, 42 bits per second, 4 ms 53, 3 ms 60, 2 ms 68 bits per second about 2.3 bits per spike.

That is the strong point of today. The same neuron, the same recording, gives different values depending on the time resolution chosen. At coarse resolution we only see how many spikes fall in each window, a rate code. At fine resolution we also see when they fall, a temporal code. Our simulated neuron carries two and a half times more information when we read its spike timing at two milliseconds than when we read its counts at 12. The question, how many bits does a spike carry, has no answer without a time resolution.

Why does timing help this neuron? Because its stimulus changes on a scale of about 10 milliseconds, and its firing probability follows. A spike two milliseconds earlier or later corresponds to a different moment of the stimulus. Coarse bins average those moments away.

It is also useful to compare the information with the total entropy. At 2 milliseconds, the total entropy was 159 bits per second, close to the maximum possible for independent bins at 30 spikes per second, about 160. The information, 68, is 43% of the total. The rest is noise. Strong and colleagues called that ratio the coding efficiency. For H1, the information was within a factor of 2 of the total entropy.

Is the extra information at fine resolution real or an estimation artefact? With 12 bins per word, there are 4,096 possible words, and 300 repeats are not many samples per moment. We checked, in the spirit of Strong and colleagues, by recomputing the noise entropy with 75, 150, and 300 repeats, and extrapolating to infinitely many. At 2 milliseconds, the noise entropy moved from 83 to 91 bits per second as repeats increased, and extrapolated to 93. The information became 67 bits per second instead of 68. At 12 milliseconds, nothing moved. The bias was small here because our neuron is simple and our data plentiful. With real neurons and fewer repeats, the same check can change the answer a lot.

What does the direct method depend on?

First, the time resolution as we just saw. It defines which code you are measuring.

Second, the word length. Short words miss correlations between spikes farther apart than the window. Long words cannot be sampled. Strong and colleagues extrapolated in the window length to recover the information rate of arbitrarily long words.

Third, the amount of data through the bias of Chapter 5. The noise entropy, estimated from a limited number of repeats, is biased downward, which biases the information upward.

Fourth, the stimulus ensemble. The information is about this stimulus distribution. A neuron can carry many bits about naturalistic stimuli and few about white noise or the reverse.

And fifth, stationarity. Repeats must be comparable. Adaptation or slow drifts across repeats inflate the noise entropy.

Sixth, the recording itself. Spike sorting errors, missed spikes or spikes from a neighbouring neuron, add variety to the words. Some of that variety repeats with the stimulus and some does not, so sorting errors can bias the information in either direction.

Finally, a contrast with what comes next. The direct method estimates the information in the spike train without saying how it could be read. A decoder, which we meet in the next episode, does the opposite. It proposes a way to read the spikes and measures how well that reading works. The two answer different questions, and as we will see, the decoder can only ever give a lower bound on what the direct method measures.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study compares two neurons and reports that one carries more information per spike. But the first was analysed with two millisecond bins and the second with 10 millisecond bins. As our simulation showed, resolution alone can change the answer by a factor of 2.5.

The fix report the bin width and the word length compare neurons at the same resolutions, show information as a function of resolution, and check bias by subsampling the repeats.

The direct method, information equals total entropy from a long non-repeating stimulus minus noise entropy from many repeats of the same segment. No model of the code is needed.

For the fly neuron H1, Strong and colleagues found up to 90 bits per second.

For our simulated neuron, the information rose from 27 bits per second at 12 millisecond bins to 68 at 2 milliseconds. Rate code versus temporal code.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Part 1

Neural coding part II — Decoding, Fisher information, populations.

In episode 12.1, the direct method measured how much information a single neuron's spike train carries, without asking how it could be read. Today we look at the other side, reading. How precisely can a population of neurons tell us the stimulus? How does a decoder relate to information? And what happens when neurons share their noise?

Here is the intuition. At the radio station, a transcriber listens to the broadcast through a crackling receiver and writes down what she thinks was said. Her transcript can never contain more about the original programme than the crackling audio she heard. She can lose information through mishearing or careless notes, but she cannot create it. A good transcript tells you that at least this much got through. A poor transcript does not tell you that little got through. It may only mean the transcriber was poor.

First precision. Suppose the stimulus is a single number, such as the orientation of a grating, and each neuron's firing rate depends on it through a tuning curve. Fisher information measures how sharply the population's responses change when the stimulus changes slightly. For neurons with Poisson noise, each neuron contributes the square of the slope of its tuning curve, divided by its mean count. Neurons on the flanks of their tuning curves where the slope is steep contribute most, neurons at their peak where the slope is zero contribute nothing.

The Cramér Rao bound then says that no unbiased decoder can estimate the stimulus with a variance smaller than one over the Fisher information. Fisher information is not a mutual information, it is local about precision near one's stimulus value. But for large populations and fine discrimination, the two are closely linked.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 16 neurons with bell-shaped tuning curves spread over orientation, with Poisson spike counts in half-second windows. All simulated. At an orientation of 45 degrees, the Fisher information is 0.075 per square degree, so the Cramér Rao bound on the standard deviation of any unbiased estimate is 3.66 degrees. A maximum likelihood decoder run on 3,000 simulated trials achieved a standard deviation of 3.72 degrees with no measurable bias, almost exactly at the bound.

Look at which neurons do the work. The neuron whose preferred orientation is exactly 45 degrees contributes nothing at all. At the peak of its tuning curve, a small change of orientation does not change its rate. The largest contributions come from the neurons preferring 22.5 and 67.5 degrees, on the steep flanks, each about 0.014 per square degree. Neurons far away preferring 135 degrees contribute nothing either. They barely fire. The most informative neurons for fine discrimination are not the ones that respond most.

Second, decoding and information. Now take a discrete stimulus, four orientations, equally likely, and two neurons with overlapping tuning. Here everything can be computed exactly. The mutual information between the stimulus and the pair of spike counts is 1.06 bits.

A decoder reads the counts and outputs a guess. The best possible decoder which picks the most probable stimulus given the counts is right 70% of the time, and the mutual information between the stimulus and its guesses is 0.89 bits. A cruder decoder which only checks which neuron fired more keeps 0.71 bits.

That is the strong point of today. By the data processing inequality of chapter 4, a decoder's output is a function of the responses, so the information between the stimulus and the decoder's output can never exceed the information between the stimulus and the responses. A decoder gives a lower bound on the information. It does not measure it. Even the optimal decoder here kept 0.89 of 1.06 bits because a single guess per trial throws away its uncertainty. And a poor decoder only shows that the decoder was poor.

Accuracy alone can be turned into bits, but only as a bound. With four equally likely stimuli and 70% correct, if the errors were spread evenly, the information would be at least 0.64 bits. The actual decoder output carries 0.89 because its errors are not even. It mostly confuses neighbouring orientations. The bound from accuracy is a lower bound on a lower bound.

Part 2

Third, populations. With two neurons, we can ask the question of Chapter 9. Each neuron alone carries about 0.65 bits, together 1.06. The sum of the single neuron information exceeds the joint information by 0.26 bits. The pair is redundancy-dominated, as overlapping tuning curves would suggest.

Now add noise correlations. Real neurons often share fluctuations, a common gain that makes both fire more on some trials and less on others, whatever the stimulus. We gave our two neurons a shared random gain with a coefficient of variation of 40%. Each neuron alone now carries less, about 0.45 bits because its gain noise blurs its tuning. The pair carries 0.90 bits.

Is that shared noise helping or hurting the pair? Compare with the shuffled case. Keep each neuron's responses to each stimulus, but pair trials at random, which removes the noise correlations and keeps everything else. The shuffled pair carries only 0.78 bits. Here, the correlated noise raised the joint information by 0.12 bits. A common gain multiplies both neurons together, so the ratio of their counts, which carries the stimulus, is partly protected from it. In other configurations, noise correlations lower the information instead. The only way to know is to compare with the shuffled case.

What do these analyses depend on?

Fisher information depends on the tuning curves and the noise model. Poisson noise gives the simple formula, other noise models and correlated noise change it.

The Cramér Rao bound applies to unbiased estimators and is reached only asymptotically with many neurons or long windows. Biased estimators can beat it locally.

Decoding depends on the decoder. Linear decoders, nearest neighbour decoders, and Bayesian decoders can give very different accuracies on the same data. And only the optimal decoder for the true distribution approaches the information limit. And decoding accuracy must always be measured on held-out trials. A flexible decoder tested on its own training data can look excellent and generalise to nothing, which would break the lower bound logic, because the reported number would no longer be a property of the data.

Population information depends on noise correlations, which must be estimated from simultaneous recordings, and on the number of trials. Joint distributions of several neurons are hard to sample and the biases of chapter 5 grow quickly.

And everything depends on the stimulus set, 4 orientations, a continuum natural images.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study decodes a stimulus from EEG with 55% accuracy against a chance level of 50% and concludes that the EEG carries very little information about the stimulus. But the decoder was linear, the features were a fixed time window, and accuracy throws away the decoder's confidence. Low decoding accuracy is a lower bound. It does not show that little information is present.

In bits, 55% correct on two balanced classes is at least 0.007 bits per trial. That small number says something about this decoder on these features. It says nothing about how much information the EEG contains about the stimulus.

The fix, report decoding as a lower bound. When possible, also estimate the mutual information directly, try decoders of different flexibility, and convert accuracy to a mutual information or use the decoder's predicted probabilities so that the result is in bits.

Information and decoding. 1.06 bits in the responses. 0.89 through the best decoder. 0.71 through a crude one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Functional and effective connectivity with information.

Connectivity is one of the most common words in neuroscience and one of the most overloaded. Today we build two connectivity maps from the same recordings, both with information theory, an undirected map of mutual information and a directed map of transfer entropy. Same data, two different objects answering two different questions.

The two words have a history. In a widely cited synthesis from 1994, Karl Friston distinguished functional connectivity, statistical dependencies between remote neurophysiological events, from effective connectivity, the influence one neural system exerts over another. Information theory gives a measure for each, with the same unit.

Here is the intuition. At the radio station, there is a switchboard log. One version of the log only records which pairs of studios were busy at the same times. Studios that were often busy together get a thick line between them. Another version records who called whom and whether the call still mattered once you knew about all the other calls. The first log tells you which studios share activity. The second tells you who drives whom. Two studios that both receive orders from the same director will look strongly linked in the first log and not at all in the second.

First map functional connectivity with mutual information. For every pair of signals, estimate their mutual information. A practical estimator for brain data is the Gaussian Copula Mutual Information of Robin Ince and colleagues, from 2017, implemented in the frites toolbox. Each signal is first transformed to a standard normal shape through its ranks, then the Gaussian formula is applied. Because ranks are unchanged by any increasing transformation, the estimate is robust to the shape of each signal's distribution, while remaining a lower bound on the true mutual information. The result is a symmetric matrix, undirected static real valued.

A quick check of what the Copula buys. For sources 4 and 5, the plain Gaussian formula and the Copula version agree, 0.22 bits each, because the simulated signals are already close to Gaussian. Now pass both standardised signals through an exponential, which changes nothing about their dependence but makes them strongly skewed. The plain Gaussian formula drops to 0.15 bits. The Copula version stays at exactly 0.22 because the ranks did not change.

Second map, effective connectivity with transfer entropy. For every target signal, find the set of source signals at specific lags, whose past adds information about the target's future, beyond the target's own past and beyond the other selected sources. This is the multivariate greedy approach of episode 8.8, implemented for example in the IDTxl toolbox. Add the most informative candidate, tested against surrogates, repeat until nothing significant remains, then compute each source's conditional transfer entropy given all the others. The result is an asymmetric matrix, directed, static, and conditioned on the rest of the network.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated six sources with a known wiring, 6,000 samples each. A chain, source 1 drives source 2, through a saturating nonlinearity, and source 2 drives source 3. In a fork, source 4 drives both source 5 at a lag of one sample and source 6 at a lag of two. There is no direct link between sources 5 and 6 and none between 1 and 3. We used copula normalised signals and lags up to two samples. Values are in bits.

The mutual information map, 0.05 bits between 1 and 2, 0.11 between 2 and 3, and 0.005 between 1 and 3. In the fork, 0.22 between 4 and 5, 0.10 between 4 and 6, and 0.19 between 5 and 6. The strongest links in the fork include a pair 5 and 6 that is not connected at all. Their shared driver makes them look as related as a real link.

The transfer entropy map with surrogate tests, exactly four links. 1-2, 0.11 bits. 2-3, 0.23. 4-5, 0.32. 4-6, 0.30. No link between 5 and 6 in either direction and no link from 1 to 3. Once Source 2's past is in the model, Source 1 adds nothing about Source 3.

Here is how the greedy search reached that conclusion for Source 3. Alone, the past of Source 2 adds 0.23 bits about Source 3's future, so it is selected first. Alone, Source 1's past, two samples back adds 0.04 bits, not nothing because its influence travels through Source 2. But once source 2's past is included, source 1 adds 0.0002 bits, below the surrogate threshold of 0.0005. It is rejected. A pairwise transfer entropy would have reported a link from 1 to 3. The conditioning removed it.

That is the strong point of today. Same data, two objects, an undirected dependence and a directed conditional predictive dependence. They answer different questions. The mutual information map says which signals share information, for whatever reason, including common inputs. The transfer entropy map says which signals' pasts help predict which others' futures, given everything else in the model. Neither is wrong. They are different maps, and their numbers are not interchangeable. Both are in bits, but a mutual information of 0.2 bits and a transfer entropy of 0.2 bits measure different things. Shared information at the same moment versus predictive information from one past to another future, conditioned on a model. Comparing their sizes across the two maps has no meaning.

What does each map depend on?

The mutual information map depends on the estimator. The Gaussian Copula estimator captures monotonic dependence well, but it can miss non-monotonic relations like the parabola of chapter 4. It also depends on volume conduction. In sensor space, neighbouring channels share sources and their mutual information can be large without any neural interaction. We mixed 6 completely independent sources into 12 simulated sensors with overlapping sensitivity as volume conduction would. Neighbouring sensors then shared 0.69 bits of mutual information on average and up to 1.22 bits. That is more than any true link in our network from sources that do not interact at all. This is why connectivity in sensor space must be interpreted with care or computed on reconstructed sources.

The transfer entropy map depends on the conditioning set, so on which signals are recorded. A hidden common driver that is not among the recorded signals can still produce a spurious directed link. It also depends on the lags considered, the sampling rate, the surrogate test and its correction for multiple comparisons, and the number of samples as in chapter 8.

Both depend on the choice of nodes, sensors or reconstructed sources and which parcellation.

Now the classic pitfall.

A study computes a mutual information matrix between regions, applies a threshold, and describes the resulting graph as the pathways along which information flows between regions. But mutual information is symmetric and includes shared inputs. In our fork, the link between 5 and 6 was the third strongest, with no pathway at all.

The fix say which question the map answers. For shared information, mutual information is the right tool, and common inputs are part of its answer. For directed, predictive influence, use conditional multivariate transfer entropy with surrogate tests and say which signals were included in the conditioning set.

In whichever map you draw, show the true wiring of a simulation like ours next to it, or at least test the pipeline on one. If a method cannot recover a known fork and a known chain, its graph of real brain data should not be read as a wiring diagram.

The notebook for this episode simulates the six sources, computes the copula mutual information matrix, runs the greedy multivariate transfer entropy with surrogates, and compares the two graphs with the true wiring.

Let's recap.

Functional connectivity with Gaussian copula mutual information, symmetric, undirected, robust to each signal's distribution.

Effective connectivity with multivariate transfer entropy Directed, conditional, tested against surrogates On our six sources, the mutual information map linked 5 and 6 at 0.19 bits through their common driver. The transfer entropy map recovered exactly the four true links.

The conditioning removed the indirect link from 1 to 3. The copula kept the mutual information unchanged under a skewing transformation, and volume conduction alone produced up to 1.22 bits between neighbouring sensors.

Same data, two objects, two questions.

Higher-order interactions in brain networks

Some dependencies exist only among three or more signals and are invisible to any list of pairs: a keyring of three identical keys is redundant, a three-dial combination lock synergistic. The O-information, total correlation minus dual total correlation, separates the two, positive for redundancy and negative for synergy. A value near zero means balance, not absence.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Higher-order interactions in brain networks.

A connectivity matrix is made of pairs. Every cell links two signals and nothing else. But some dependencies only exist among three signals or more, and they are invisible to any list of pairs. Today we look for them with the O-information on simulated multichannel recordings at rest and during a task. Then we look at what has been published in fMRI and at its limits.

Here is the intuition. Picture a locksmith's counter at the radio station. On the left, a keyring with three copies of the same key. Any one of them opens the door, the information is redundant, and each key tells you what the others would. On the right, a combination lock with three dials. No single dial tells you anything, and no pair of dials either. Only the three together open it, the information is synergistic. A list of pairs sees the keyring perfectly well. It does not see the combination lock at all.

The O-information was introduced by Fernando Rosas and colleagues in 2019. It compares two ways of counting shared information in a group of variables. The total correlation, the sum of the individual entropies minus the joint entropy, grows fastest when the variables repeat each other. The dual total correlation, the joint entropy minus what each variable keeps to itself, grows fastest when information needs several variables at once. The O-information is the total correlation minus the dual total correlation. Positive means the group is dominated by redundancy, like the keyring. Negative means it is dominated by synergy, like the lock. For three variables, it is exactly the co-information of chapter 9.

In practice, we compute it with the hoi toolbox published by Matteo Neri, Etienne Combrisson, and colleagues in 2024. It estimates entropies with the Gaussian copula, the same rank trick as in the previous episode, and it scans all multiplets of a chosen size.

Now the calculation in one picture.

We simulated 12 channels, 8,000 samples each, labelled simulated throughout. Three channels 0, 1, and 2 share a common driver, that is our keyring. Four more channels 6 to 9 share a second weaker driver. Channels 10 and 11 form one strong pair. And channel 5 receives the sum of channels 3 and 4 which are independent of each other. That is our combination lock. Values are in bits.

12 channels give 220 triplets. For each the O-information.

At rest the most redundant triplet is 0, 1, 2 at plus 0.12 bits. The most synergistic triplet is 3, 4, 5 at minus 0.03 bits. The second cluster, channel 6, 7, and 8, is mildly redundant at plus 0.06 bits because its driver is weaker. Every other triplet sits close to zero.

Why is the lock triplet synergistic? Our simulated lock is not a perfect one, each dial leaks a little. Channel 3 alone carries 0.115 bits about channel 5. Channel 4 alone carries 0.105 bits. Together, they carry 0.247 bits, more than the sum of the two. The extra about 0.03 bits only exists when both dials are read together. For three variables, that extra is exactly what the negative O-information measures.

During the task, we doubled the weight of the sum feeding channel 5. The redundant triplet barely moves, plus 0.12 bits again. The synergistic triplet goes from minus 0.03 to minus 0.17 bits. The change between rest and task lives in a triplet, and the O-information tracks it.

As a cross-check, the hoi toolbox and a direct implementation of the Gaussian formula give the same extremes to three decimals.

Here is the strong moment. Look at the pairwise map of the same rest data. The strongest pair in the whole recording is 10 and 11 at 0.42 bits. It belongs to no synergistic triplet. A triplet made of 10, 11, and channel 0 has an O-information of 0 to three decimals. The next strongest pairs belong to the keyring, and the most synergistic triplet contains a pair 3 and 4, whose mutual information is 0.0003 bits. Ranked by the sum of its pairwise links, that triplet comes 16th out of 220.

So the most synergistic triplets are not made of the most connected pairs. If you build your hypotheses from the strongest edges of a connectivity matrix, you will walk past the combination lock without seeing it.

What does the O-information depend on?

First on the estimator. The Gaussian copula is fast and robust to the shape of each signal, but it only captures dependencies that a Gaussian model can see. For Gaussian variables, synergy can only arise in particular configurations, like our sum. Non-monotonic dependencies of the kind we met in episode 4.2 can be missed entirely.

Second on the size of the search. 12 channels give 220 triplets. 64 channels give 41,664 triplets, and 635,376 quadruplets. Each one is a test. Surrogates built by shuffling each channel independently give the null distribution and the multiple comparisons must be corrected, as in chapter 8.

Third on what the channels are. Mix neighbouring channels the way volume conduction mixes EEG sensors. We added half of each neighbour to each channel. The triplet 3, 4, 5 synergistic at minus 0.03 bits becomes redundant at about plus 0.1 bits. Shared sources create redundancy. On sensor data, a positive O-information may simply mean that your electrodes see the same sources. Source reconstruction, or at least care, comes first.

Now the published results in fMRI.

In 2023, Thomas Varley, Maria Pope, Joshua Faskowitz, and Olaf Sporns applied the O-information to resting state fMRI of the human cerebral cortex. They reported that synergistic subsystems are widespread. Using simulated annealing to search for maximally synergistic subsystems, they found that such subsystems typically comprise about 10 regions, recruited from several canonical brain systems. And they reported that these subsystems are invisible to pairwise functional connectivity, a kind of shadow structure. That is the same lesson as our simulation, at the scale of the whole cortex.

Their limits are worth stating clearly. The estimator is Gaussian, so the synergy found is synergy that a Gaussian model can express. The search is heuristic. Simulated annealing finds good subsystems with no guarantee that they are the best. The O-information is a net balance. It tells you which of redundancy or synergy dominates, not how much of each there is. And fMRI is slow, so these are dependencies among slow fluctuations, not fast neural interactions.

The classic pitfall, reading an O-information close to zero as the absence of higher order interactions.

Here is why that is wrong. We built four simulated channels with both a keyring and a combination lock inside them. A common driver on some channels and a difference of two channels feeding another. With no lock, the O-information is plus 0.11 bits. As we increase the weight of the lock it falls to plus 0.01 then minus 0.01 bits. Near that point it is essentially zero. Yet there is a strong redundancy and a strong synergy in the same group. They cancel in the balance.

So an O-information near zero means balance not absence. If you need to separate the two use a decomposition. The partial information decomposition of chapter 9 for a target and its sources or the integrated information decomposition for dynamics. And always report the pairwise structure next to the higher order one.

In the notebook, you simulate the 12 channels, compute the O-information of every triplet with hoi and with the direct Gaussian formula, compare rest and task, rank triplets by synergy and by pairwise links, mix the channels to see volume conduction create redundancy, and build the cancelling system yourself.

To recap, the O-information is total correlation minus dual total correlation. Positive means redundancy dominates, negative means synergy dominates. In our simulation, the keyring triplet sat at plus 0.12 bits, and the lock went from minus 0.03 at rest to minus 0.17 during the task. The most synergistic triplet contained an unconnected pair and ranked 16th by pairwise links. Mixing channels turned synergy into redundancy and a zero balance can hide both.

Brain and body — Information between EEG, heart and pupil.

The brain does not work alone. It talks with the heart and it moves the pupil and both send signals back. These signals are recorded in different units, at different rates, with different machines, microvolts at hundreds of samples per second for the EEG, intervals between heartbeats in milliseconds, and pupil diameter in millimetres. Today we use one toolbox, transfer entropy on all three. The answer comes out in a single unit bits. And then we meet the guest nobody invited.

Here is the intuition. Picture the interpreter's booth at the radio station. Three guests speak three languages. The heart speaks in beats. The brain speaks in oscillations. The pupil speaks in widening and narrowing. The interpreter does not care about the language. She only asks one question of each pair. Does knowing what this guest just said help me predict what that guest will say next, beyond what that guest's own past already tells me? Her answer is always in the same currency, bits. So the three conversations can finally be compared on one page.

That question is transfer entropy from chapter 7. It has nothing to do with the physics of the signal. What it needs is a common time grid, a choice of past, and an estimator.

There is real work behind this idea. In 2015, Luca Faes, Daniele Marinazzo, Fabrice Jurysta, and Giandomenico Nollo studied sleep recordings of 10 healthy young subjects. They used the high-frequency component of heart rate variability, a marker of cardiac vagal activity, and the EEG power in the delta, theta, alpha, sigma, and beta bands across the whole night. With both Granger causality and transfer entropy, they found structured networks, with links directed predominantly from the heart rate variability to the EEG waves.

For the pupil in 2016, Siddhartha Joshi, Yin Li, Rishi Kalwani, and Joshua Gold recorded single neurons in monkeys. They found that activity in the locus coeruleus, the brainstem source of noradrenaline, closely tracks moment-to-moment changes in pupil size, and that microstimulation of the locus coeruleus can drive pupil responses. Similar but delayed relationships appeared in the colliculi and the cingulate cortex. The pupil is a window on arousal, not a direct readout of any single brain area.

Now the calculation in one picture.

The fil rouge dataset could not be used here, so everything that follows is simulated and labelled so. 20 simulated participants, five minutes each, on a common grid of four samples per second. Four signals per participant. The heartbeat interval series resampled to that grid an EEG alpha power envelope, pupil diameter, and respiration which we will keep hidden at first.

The true wiring is known. Breathing modulates the heart as in respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and it also modulates the EEG. The heart drives the EEG weakly, with a lag of half a second. The EEG drives the heart even more weakly. A slow, hidden arousal process lowers the EEG alpha power and dilates the pupil, three quarters of a second later. There is no direct link between the pupil and the EEG, and none between the pupil and the heart.

For each participant and each ordered pair, we computed the Gaussian transfer entropy, with one second of past for both target and source. Significance came from 60 circularly shifted surrogates per participant at the 95th percentile. Values are in bits per sample.

Heart to brain 0.066 bits on average, significant in 16 participants out of 20. Brain to heart 0.041 bits, significant in 13. Pupil to brain 0.036 bits, significant in all 20. Brain to pupil 0.024 bits, also significant in all 20. Pupil and heart 0.0045 and 0.0022 bits, significant in 5 and 1 participants. 1 participant out of 20 is what chance alone would give. Five is a little more and it comes from a weak indirect path from arousal through the EEG to the heart.

Here is the strong moment. Three modalities with nothing physical in common, one estimator, one unit. On one page you can read that the heart-to-brain transfer is larger than the reverse and that the pupil-to-brain transfer is in the same range. No conversion factor, no arbitrary scaling. The method does not depend on the modality. That is exactly what information theory promises.

And here is what the page cannot tell you on its own.

Look at the pupil first. The simulation contains no link at all between the pupil and the EEG. Yet the transfer entropy is significant in both directions in every participant. The reason is the hidden arousal process. It drives both signals at different lags, and the past of the pupil carries information about the arousal that will shape the EEG. Transfer entropy measures prediction, not wiring. With the pupil, the honest reading is shared arousal, not a pupil that talks to the cortex.

Now the heart. Let us bring respiration out of hiding and condition on it. One second of respiration's past, added to the conditioning set. Heart to brain falls from 0.066 to 0.018 bits. Brain-to-heart falls from 0.041 to 0.007 bits. Both remain significant in most participants, 19 and 16 out of 20, because the simulated links are real but weak. Most of what we had called brain-heart interaction was breathing, talking to both.

To be sure, we simulated a second world, with no coupling at all between heart and brain, only respiration driving both. Without conditioning, heart-to-brain transfer entropy was 0.044 bits, and 12 participants out of 20 came out significant. Every one of them is a false positive. Conditioning on respiration brought it down to 0.0027 bits, with one participant out of 20, as expected by chance.

What do these numbers depend on?

First on the common time grid. The heart gives events, not samples, so the beat interval series must be interpolated, or the EEG summarized beat by beat, as Faes and colleagues did. Interpolation smooths the series, and smoothness changes how much its own past explains.

Second on the past. One second of past is a choice. Arousal and pupil dynamics are slow, the heart is faster, and the alpha envelope sits in between. Too short a past and some self-prediction leaks into the transfer.

Third on the surrogates. Circular shifts keep each signal's own dynamics. But with an oscillation-like respiration inside the source, a shifted copy can still line up with the target for some shifts, which is why the counts changed when we conditioned. Always look at the effect size together with the count.

The classic pitfall, a significant transfer entropy from the heart to the brain reported as a direct heart-to-brain pathway without measuring breathing.

Breathing modulates heart rate. It is also known to modulate cortical activity. In 2016, Christina Zelano and colleagues showed that nasal respiration entrains oscillations in human limbic areas. A common driver like this one does not appear in the data unless you record it. The fix is cheap. Record respiration with a belt or at least derive it from the ECG and condition on it. For the pupil, control luminance and report the arousal state. And state your conclusion in terms of prediction, given what was measured.

To recap, transfer entropy asks the same question of every pair of signals, whatever their physics and answers in bits. In our simulation, heart-to-brain reads 0.066 bits and pupil-to-brain 0.036 bits on the same page. The pupil links came entirely from a hidden arousal process. Conditioning on respiration cut heart to brain to 0.018 bits and removed the false positives of a world without coupling. One toolbox for all modalities and one rule, measure the drivers you can.

Information about behaviour.

So far in this chapter, information has linked signals to signals. Now we link the brain to the task. How much does the EEG tell us about what the participant is holding in memory, and when? We compute mutual information between the EEG and working memory load at every moment of the trial, and we decide where it is real with permutations.

Here is the intuition. At the radio station, a listener sits in a guessing booth. On each trial, the participant memorises 5, 9, or 13 digits. The listener never sees the digits. She only hears the EEG one instant at a time, and she must guess the load. Before the digits appear, she can do no better than chance. If at some moment her guesses get better, the EEG at that moment carries information about the load. Mutual information measures how much better in bits. With three equally likely loads, the ceiling is the logarithm base 2 of 3, about 1.58 bits.

The tool of choice here is the frites toolbox, by Etienne Combrisson, Andrea Brovelli, and colleagues, described in NeuroImage in 2022. It computes the Gaussian copula mutual information of Robin Ince and colleagues between a brain signal and a task variable, time point by time point, and it provides permutation-based statistics for single participants and for groups.

For a continuous signal and a discrete variable like load, the estimator compares a Gaussian model of the signal within each load to a single Gaussian model across all loads. The more the loads pull the signal apart, the more information.

Now the calculation in one picture.

The fil rouge recording could not be used here, so this participant is simulated and labelled so. 150 trials, 50 per load, one frontal channel, sampled at 50 Hz, from half a second before the digits to 4 seconds after. The simulated signal has two parts. A large evoked response at 200 ms, identical for every load. And a slow negativity during retention starting at exactly one second whose depth grows with the load. On top, smooth noise and trial-to-trial variability.

We compute the mutual information at each of the 225 time points.

Before the digits, the curve sits at zero. The bias-corrected estimator even dips slightly below, minus 0.0045 bits on average, which is normal. At 200 milliseconds, where the evoked response is largest, the information stays at 0.005 bits. The biggest bump in the average carries nothing about the load.

Then during retention, the curve rises. It peaks at 0.22 bits, 1.9 seconds after the digits. That is about one-seventh of the ceiling. The listener guesses better than chance, far from perfectly.

Is it real? We shuffle the load labels across trials 500 times. Each shuffle breaks the link between brain and load, but keeps everything else, the evoked response, the noise, the number of trials. For each shuffle, we recompute the whole curve and keep its maximum over time. The 95th percentile of those maxima is the threshold, 0.074 bits. Any time point above it is significant, with the family-wise error controlled across all time points at once.

Here is the strong moment, one curve of information across the trial, one threshold from the data themselves. 94 time points are above it, all during retention, none before the digits.

With a group of participants, the same logic scales up. The frites framework supports fixed-effect models, which pool the information across participants, and random effect models, which ask whether the effect is consistent enough across participants to generalise to the population. Both come with corrections across time, point by point, or by clusters. The choice changes the question. Fixed effect asks whether the effect exists in these participants, random effect whether it is typical of people like them.

What does this curve depend on?

First on the estimator. The Gaussian copula model sees differences in the level of the signal across loads. If load changed only the variability of the signal or its shape in a nonlinear way, a different estimator would be needed.

Second on the number of trials, mutual information estimates are biased with few trials. The permutations take care of the bias because shuffled data suffer the same bias. That is one reason to never compare raw information values across studies with different trial counts.

Third on the preprocessing. A zero-phase filter spreads a sharp change in both directions in time, so information can appear slightly before it exists. Check your filters before you interpret early time points.

Fourth on what the curve does not say. Mutual information is unsigned. Swap the labels so that 13 digits become the lowest load, and the curve is exactly the same. To know the direction, look at the averages. In our simulation, the late amplitude goes from about 0 microvolts for 5 digits, to minus 1.2 for 9, to minus 2.4 for 13.

The classic pitfall, reading the first significant time point as the moment the brain starts to encode the load.

In our simulation, the effect starts at exactly 1 second. The first significant time point with 50 trials per load is at 1.44 seconds. We repeated the whole simulation 8 times for each design. With 20 trials per load, the first significant point came, on average, at 1.98 seconds. With 50 at 1.57. With 150 at 1.30. The brain did not change. Only our power to detect a ramp that starts small did.

This is the point made by Jona Sassenhagen and Dejan Draschkow in Psychophysiology in 2019 for cluster-based permutation tests. A significant result tells you that there is an effect, not precisely when it starts or where it stops. The same caution applies to our maximum statistic. If latency is your question, test it directly, for example by comparing onsets estimated with a dedicated method across conditions, with resampling.

In the notebook, you simulate the participant, compute the information curve with frites, build the maximum statistic threshold with permutations, swap the labels to see that the curve does not move, and vary the number of trials to watch the first significant point drift.

To recap, mutual information between EEG and load asks how well a listener could guess the load from the signal at each instant, in bits, with a ceiling of 1.58 bits for three loads. In our simulation, the evoked response carried nothing about load, and retention peaked at 0.22 bits. Permutations of the labels gave a threshold of 0.074 bits, corrected across time. The curve is unsigned, so look at the averages for direction. And the first significant point depends on your trial count, not only on the brain.

Part 1

Hyperscanning Part 1 — Information shared between two brains.

Hyperscanning records two brains at the same time. Two people talk, play, or listen to the same story, and we ask whether their brain signals share something. Information theory gives a clean way to ask it — the mutual information between the two brains — in bits. It also gives a clean way to ask the harder question — the one that matters — how much of that shared information comes from the interaction itself.

Here is the intuition. Picture two radio sets on the same shelf at the station. Their needles move together. There are three different reasons why. Both may be tuned to the same broadcast, they share a stimulus. There may be a cable running from one to the other, one drives the other. Or both may be plugged into the same flickering mains which dims and brightens them together, they share a body rhythm. From the needles alone, the three look alike. To tell them apart, you need to know the broadcast, the mains, and what happens when you swap one radio for another.

So this episode starts where we know the truth, a simulator. The series needed hyperscanning data with a known answer, so we wrote a simulator. It is called DyadSim, and its code is published with this series. Each simulated brain has four oscillating sources at 10, 6, 20, and 8 Hz, mixed to 32 sensors through a smooth lead field, the way volume conduction mixes real EEG, plus sensor noise. Switches turn on, separately, a shared stimulus, a direct coupling from brain A to brain B, and a shared breathing rhythm.

The shared stimulus is a slow, speech-like envelope. It modulates the amplitude of the 10 Hz source in both brains. The direct coupling sends brain A's 6 Hz source into brain B's 6 Hz source 40 ms later. The breathing rhythm, at a quarter of a hertz, modulates the amplitude of every source in both brains. Everything that follows is simulated and labelled so.

For each dyad, we extract the amplitude envelope of every sensor in two bands, alpha, 8 to 12 Hz, and theta, 4 to 8 Hz, averaged in bins of 100 ms. 2 minutes give 1,200 values per sensor. For every pair of sensors, one in each brain, we compute the Gaussian-Copula mutual information, and we average over all 1,024 pairs. We simulated 8 dyads per scenario. All of them heard the same story so that the stimulus is identical across dyads, as it would be in an experiment.

One number first: the floor. With no link at all, this estimate sits at about 1.3 thousandths of a bit. That is the small positive bias of an estimator with this much data. Everything is read against it.

Now the calculation in one picture.

Scenario 1: shared stimulus only. In the alpha band, the mutual information between the two brains is 6.2 thousandths of a bit, about 5 times the floor. Something is shared. Now condition on the stimulus envelope. We remove from both brains what the story explains and recompute. The mutual information falls to 1.4 thousandths of a bit, back to the floor.

Here is the strong moment. The raw information between two brains collapses once you condition on the signal they share. In this scenario, nothing remains because nothing else was there. The two radios were simply tuned to the same broadcast.

And the pseudo pairs agree. Pair brain A of one dyad with brain B of another dyad that heard the same story. They never met. Their mutual information is 5.8 thousandths of a bit, almost the same as the real pairs. Shared input, not interaction.

Scenario 2, direct coupling only. In the theta band, the real pairs share 13.4 thousandths of a bit. Conditioning on the stimulus changes nothing, 13.3, because there is no stimulus effect to remove. And the pseudo pairs dropped to 1.2 thousandths, the floor. This time, the information belongs to the interaction. It exists only between partners who were actually connected.

So far, two clean tools agree. Conditioning on the shared signal removes what the shared signal explains. Pseudo pairs remove what any two people in the same situation would share. Part 2 shows when they disagree and why that matters.

Part 2

Hyperscanning Part 2. Continued.

Scenario 3 — Breathing Only. No stimulus effect, no neural coupling. Just two people whose breathing rhythm modulates their brain signals, and who, in this simulation, breathe together.

In the alpha band, the real pairs share 0.026 of a bit. That is 20 times the floor, the largest value so far, and there is no neural link at all. Conditioning on the stimulus does nothing, 26 again. Conditioning on breathing as well brings it down to 2.2 thousandths of a bit, close to the floor.

Now the pseudo pairs. They give 7.6 thousandths of a bit, lower than the real pairs by a factor of three and a half. If pseudo pairs were your only control, you would conclude that real partners share information beyond what any two people share, and you would call it interaction. But the extra information is breathing. Partners breathe together, strangers paired after the fact do not. And the pseudo pairs are not at the floor either, because every simulated person breathes at the same rate, a quarter of a hertz, so even strangers' rhythms partly line up.

So a pseudo pair controls for exactly what pseudo pairs share and nothing more. Anything that partners share because they are partners, breathing, posture, gaze, speech rhythm survives the pseudo pair test. That does not make it neural.

Scenario 4. Everything on. Stimulus, coupling, and breathing together. In the theta band, the real pairs share 41.5 thousandths of a bit. Condition on the stimulus, 41.7 unchanged as expected for theta. Condition on the stimulus and on breathing, 10.4 thousandths. What remains is close to the coupling alone, which gave 13.4. In the alpha band, the same conditioning takes 27.7 thousandths down to 1.9, the floor. Only the band with a real coupling keeps information, once the shared signals are removed.

This is the logic of the whole episode. Raw interbrain information is a sum of reasons. Condition on every shared signal you can measure—the stimulus, the speech envelope, the breathing, the movements. What remains is a candidate for interaction, and still only a candidate.

These concerns are not new. In 2013, Adrian Burgess compared hyperconnectivity measures on simulated data, and on EEG recorded from people individually, then combined into pseudo pairs where no hyper-connection could exist. He found spurious hyper-connections arising from genuine similarities between EEG recorded under the same experimental conditions. In 2021, Antonia Hamilton argued in Neuron that hyperscanning results are hard to interpret, and that integrating behavioural data and models of mutual prediction is the way forward.

What does this depend on?

First on the features, we used amplitude envelopes. Phase-based measures or the raw signals respond to each reason differently. The 6 Hz coupling in DyadSim is a coupling of the oscillation itself, and envelopes only see part of it.

Second on what you can measure. You can only condition on signals you recorded. Record the audio, compute the speech envelope, record breathing, record movement. A shared signal you did not record cannot be removed.

Third on the average. We averaged over all sensor pairs which dilute strong local effects. The maximum over pairs is larger, but it needs its own null distribution built with the same maximum.

The classic pitfall? Reporting that real pairs share more information than pseudo pairs, and calling the difference neural synchrony caused by interaction.

The difference may come from anything partners share because they are together. Breathing, gaze, movement, the rhythm of the conversation. The fix is to measure those signals and condition on them, to compare with pseudo pairs as one control among several, and to write the conclusion at the level the evidence supports, shared information beyond the measured shared signals.

Hyperscanning II — Who predicts whom, and synergy between brains.

Last episode, we asked how much information two brains share, and how much of it survives once the shared signals are removed. Today, two further questions. First direction, in a conversation does the speaker's brain predict the listener's, more than the reverse? Second, the group, can three brains carry information that no pair of them carries?

Here is the intuition. Picture the call-in show at the radio station. A host and a caller take turns. When the host speaks, the caller listens, and a moment later the caller's attention follows. If you only watched their brains, you would find that the host's brain predicts the caller's. But both brains are following the same thing, who has the floor. Now picture a conference call with three lines. A message has been split into three parts, such that any single line or any two lines together sound like pure noise. Only the three lines combined reveal it. That message exists at the level of the group and nowhere else.

First question direction. We use DyadSim again, now with turn taking switched on. Turns alternate between speaker A and speaker B, with random durations around 3 seconds. While A speaks, the amplitude of A's 20 Hz source rises. 200 milliseconds later, the same source rises in B, the listener. There is no neural connection between the two brains in this scenario. Everything is simulated and labelled so.

For each brain, we take one sensor, the one where the 20 Hz source projects most strongly. That is an oracle choice. We know the lead field because we built it. We extract the beta envelope 15 to 25 Hz in bins of 100 ms over 3 minutes for 8 simulated dyads. Then we compute the Gaussian transfer entropy in both directions with half a second of past and test it against 60 circularly shifted surrogates.

Now the calculation in one picture.

From A to B, 0.0098 bits, significant in all 8 dyads. From B to A, 0.0072 bits. So the speaker's brain predicts the listener's a little more than the reverse. A tempting headline, information flows from speaker to listener.

But look at the reverse direction again. 0.0072 bits is not nothing, and there is no path at all from B's brain to A's in this simulation. Why does B's past predict A's future? Because B's beta reflects who had the floor a moment ago, and who had the floor a moment ago predicts who will have it next. Turns last a few seconds. Each brain carries a delayed copy of the same conversation, and each copy predicts the other brain a little. The asymmetry comes from the lag, not from a connection.

Now add the turn signal itself to the conditioning set, who had the floor over the same half second of past. From A to B, the transfer entropy falls to 0.0017 bits, significant in none of the eight dyads. From B to A, the same 0.0017. Both directions collapse to the floor.

Here is the strong moment of this first part. Once you know who was speaking, one brain tells you nothing more about the other. The prediction was real, but it ran through behaviour. Both brains were following the conversation at different lags.

Now switch on a real neural coupling as well. Brain A's 6 Hz source drives brain B's 40 ms later. In the theta envelope from A to B, the transfer entropy is 0.44 bits, and from B to A 0.0023. Condition on the turns, still 0.44 bits significant in all 8 dyads. This part of the prediction does not run through behaviour. It survives because it is there.

So for direction, the rule is the same as for shared information. Align your analysis on the behaviour, the turns, the speech envelope, the gestures, and condition on them. A transfer entropy that survives is a candidate for brain-to-brain influence beyond the measured behaviour. One that disappears was the conversation itself.

Now a second question. Synergy between brains.

Second question, the group. Can three brains carry information that no pair of them carries?

We simulated triads, not with DyadSim this time, but with the simplest possible construction because the point is conceptual. Three simulated signals, A, B, and C, 18,000 samples each. A and B are independent noise. C is noise whose sign is the product of the signs of A and B, with 10% of the signs flipped at random. Then each signal is binarized at its median, above or below. Nothing else connects them.

Why should pairs be blind? Take A alone. Knowing whether A is above or below its median tells you nothing about C, because C's sign also depends on B, which is a fair coin. The same holds for B alone. And A and B are independent of each other by construction. Only when you know A and B together does C become predictable, apart from the 10% of flipped signs.

The pairwise picture first. Mutual information between A and B, zero bits. Between A and C, zero. Between B and C, zero. To four decimals, any connectivity matrix built from pairs is empty.

The O-information of the triad from episode 12.4 computed with a discrete estimator on the binarized signals, minus 0.51 bits. Strongly synergistic. Equivalently, A and B together carry 0.51 bits about C, while each alone carries nothing. With 10% of the signs flipped, the exact value is 1 minus the binary entropy of 0.1, about 0.53 bits. Median binarization costs a little.

Two checks. Shuffle B and C independently 200 times, the O-information stays within 3 ten thousandths of a bit of zero. And build pseudo-triads with C taken from a different triad, minus 0.0001 bits. The synergy belongs to the real group and only to it.

Here is the strong moment of this second part. Information that exists only at the level of the group, invisible in every pair of brains. If such structure exists in real interactions, pairwise hyperscanning measures cannot see it by construction.

And a warning from episode 12.4. The Gaussian copula O-information of the same triad computed on the continuous signals is 0 to 4 decimals. The dependence here is a product of signs, which is non-monotonic. A Gaussian estimator is blind to it. To look for this kind of synergy, the estimator has to be able to see it.

What do these results depend on?

First on the alignment. Turn-taking, speech envelopes, and gestures must be recorded with timestamps on the same clock as the EEG. A behavioural signal that is not time-locked cannot be conditioned on.

Second on the choice of channels. We used an oracle the best sensor for each source. In real data you either reconstruct sources or test many channel pairs and correct for them. The values will be smaller and the multiple comparisons larger.

Third on the estimator for synergy. Gaussian estimators see sums, not products of signs. Discrete estimators see everything that survives binarization, but they need many samples. 18,000 samples and three binary variables is easy. 64 channels in three brains is not.

The classic pitfall, reporting that the speaker's brain predicts the listener's brain and calling it information transfer between brains.

The speaker's brain may predict the listener's because both follow the conversation at different lags. The fix is to align on the turns, to condition on the behaviour that both brains follow, and to state the result as prediction beyond the measured behaviour. And for groups, do not conclude from empty pairwise maps that the group shares nothing.

Measure what you can, condition on it, and name what you could not measure.

Line 7 Multiple Comparisons. Count the tests, time points, channels, frequency bands, pairs, triplets. 64 channels give over 41,000 triplets. Use a correction that matches your claim and remember episode 12.6. A significant cluster or window does not establish when an effect starts.

Line 8. The unit and the scale. Bits or nats, said out loud. Raw or normalized and normalized by what? A value of 0.2 bits can be large for one question and negligible for another. Compare it to the floor, to the ceiling, and to the null. And give its uncertainty, resample trials or participants recompute and report an interval, not only a point. A value without an interval cannot be compared with anything, including your own replication next year.

Line 9, the interpretation. Information is not meaning, episode 2.5. Prediction is not wiring, episodes 8 and 12. Correlation is not information, and zero correlation is not independence, episodes 1.4 and 4.2. An O-information near zero means balance, not absence, episode 12.4. Say which of these traps your claim avoids.

Line 10, the sentence. Write the result the way the evidence allows. Not information flows from A to B, but the past of A carries this many bits about the future of B, beyond the past of B and the measured shared signals, with this estimator against this null corrected for this many tests. It is longer. It is also true.

Here is what that looks like for episode 12.5, written as a result. In 20 simulated participants, the Gaussian transfer entropy from the heartbeat interval series to the EEG alpha envelope, with one second of past, was 0.018 bits per sample after conditioning on respiration, significant against circularly shifted surrogates in 19 of 20 participants. Every clause in that sentence is a line of the sheet.

The classic pitfall of this episode is to treat the checklist as a ritual, to tick the boxes after the analysis, rather than to let them shape it. The lines are in this order for a reason. The question comes first, the sentence comes last, and everything in between is decided before the data can tempt you.

What does the checklist depend on? On honesty about what was not done. A missing control is not a failure if it is reported. It becomes one when it is hidden. The best methods sections in this field list their estimators, their parameters, their nulls, and their simulations, and publish the code so that anyone can rerun the sheet.

To recap the whole series in one breath, bits measure reduced uncertainty. Estimators are biased, and the bias must be checked on data with a known answer. Nulls control for what they preserve. Shared drivers create information without interaction. Many tests need correction. And the final sentence must say no more than the evidence does.

That is the last line of the sheet. The broadcast can go on air. Thank you for following the series and good luck with your bits.
