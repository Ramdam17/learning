Teaser · A flashlight for the brain

Red light from a flashlight passes through fingers, skin and even skull, and what comes back out can reveal the working brain. Try this tonight, press a flashlight against your fingers in the dark. They glow red. Red light goes through skin and tissue, and some of it comes back out.

It can travel through your skull too. Not much of it, but enough. Shine it in, catch what comes back and you can watch the brain at work, while you talk, walk, play, or work with someone else.

PLATE 1.12:27

Why light? · The optical window

Tissue absorbs light unevenly: hemoglobin swallows blue and green, melanin absorbs short wavelengths, and water absorbs beyond about 950 nanometers. Between roughly 650 and 950 nanometers a gap opens where light travels centimeters. Inside it, oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin cross near 800 nanometers, so systems shine two colors, one on each side, to tell them apart.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Why light? And why this particular kind of light? Remember the flashlight behind your hand. Your fingers glowed red. Not blue. Not green. That's a clue.

Tissue is full of molecules that absorb light. Hemoglobin in your blood swallows blue and green light very strongly. That's actually why blood looks red, red is what's left. Melanin in your skin also absorbs a lot at short wavelengths. And water, which makes up most of your body, starts up to the skin. absorbing heavily in the infrared beyond roughly 950 nanometers.

Put those curves together and a gap appears. Between roughly 650 and 950 nanometers, everything absorbs relatively little. Light can travel centimeters instead of millimeters. Researchers call it the optical window or the near-infrared window.

Couldn't we just use brighter light and ignore the window? Not really. Absorbed light turns into heat, and safety limits cap how much power we can shine on skin. So we work with the window, not against it.

Inside that window, light is still scattered like crazy, as you'll see with the banana in episode 1.4. But it isn't swallowed straight away. Enough of it survives to go in, dip into the cortex, and come back out.

And there's a bonus. In the window, hemoglobin is still there to be measured, absorbing a little and differently depending on whether it carries oxygen. Faint enough to let light through. Strong enough to leave a fingerprint. That's the sweet spot fNIRS lives in.

Look closer at that fingerprint. Below about 800 nanometers, hemoglobin without oxygen absorbs more. Above it, hemoglobin with oxygen takes over. The two curves cross around 800 nanometers, at what's called the isosbestic point. So most systems shine two wavelengths, one on each side, for example around 760 and 850 nanometers. Each color sees a different mix of the two, and comparing them lets us tell them apart. We'll do the maths in episode 2.1.

So, blood swallows blue and green, water swallows the far infrared and in between, a window to the brain with two colors on either side of the crossing. Next time how we got here. A short history of shining light on heads.

PLATE 1.22:49

A short history of shining light on heads

The story runs from Stokes showing that blood changes color with oxygen, through the ear oximeter and the pulse oximeter, to Jöbsis shining near-infrared light into a cat's head in 1977. Four groups published the first functional brain studies in 1993. Multichannel maps, high-density tomography, hyperscanning and wearable wireless caps followed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Shining light through the body to check on oxygen is older than you might think. And it starts with a color change.

In 1864, the physicist George Stokes showed that blood changes color with oxygen. Its spectrum shifts when hemoglobin picks up or lets go of oxygen. That shift is the fingerprint this whole story relies on.

In 1935, Karl Matthes in Germany built a device that shone two colors of light through the ear to follow oxygen in the blood. In the 1940s, Glenn Millikan turned it into a practical ear oximeter for pilots, a little clip that warned them when their blood ran low on oxygen at altitude. In 1974, Takuo Aoyagi, an engineer in Japan, presented the trick behind today's pulse oximeter. Use the heartbeat to isolate arterial blood.

Then, in 1977, Frans Jöbsis showed something bolder. Near-infrared light shone into the head, could come back out carrying information about the oxygenation of the brain. Through skin. Through bone. His first experiments were on cats.

In the 1980s, the idea moved into hospitals, first studies in newborn babies and in adult patients. In 1988, David Delpy's team in London measured how much longer light's path really is inside tissue. The path length factor will meet in episode 2.1. And by the end of the decade, came the first commercial cerebral oxygen monitors.

And then the leap. Around 1992, four groups working independently led by Britton Chance, Toshinori Kato, Yoko Hoshi, and Arno Villringer, asked the same question. Does this signal change when the brain does something? It did. Their first functional studies came out in 1993. That's the birth of fNIRS.

From there, things grew fast. One channel became dozens. In 1995, a team at Hitachi led by Atsushi Maki started drawing maps of activity over the scalp. Then dense, overlapping arrays turned fNIRS into true imaging, called high-density diffuse optical tomography, by 2014 its maps could be compared with functional MRI. In the early 2010s, researchers put caps on two people at once to record two brains in interaction, hyperscanning which gets its own chapter in this series. And today, racks and fibers have given way to wearable caps and wireless headbands.

So, from a color change in a test tube to an oximeter on a pilot's ear, to caps that let children play while we watch their brains at work. Next time, let's take the hardware apart.

PLATE 1.33:05

The hardware, exploded

A system has sources (LEDs or laser diodes), detectors, a cap that holds optodes against the scalp, and a controller. A channel is a source-detector pair, about 3 cm apart in adults, and it listens to the banana between them. Switching or flickering sources avoids crosstalk, and short channels, an accelerometer and event markers complete the setup.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Let's take an fNIRS system apart, piece by piece.

First, the sources, small LEDs or laser diodes, each shining near-infrared light into the scalp at two or more wavelengths. The power is low, well within safety limits. LEDs are cheap, light and safe, perfect for wearable systems. Laser diodes give narrower, more powerful beams, usually delivered through optical fibers.

Then, the detectors, very sensitive light sensors, usually photodiodes, sometimes avalanche photodiodes or silicon photomultipliers, built to catch the tiny fraction of light that makes it back, often millions of times weaker than what went in. They're so sensitive that ordinary room light becomes noise. That's why caps are often black and why some labs dim the lights or add a dark overcap.

Sources and detectors sit in optodes, held against the head by a cap, with holes so you can part the hair and get real contact with the skin. In tabletop systems, optical fibers carry the light to and from a box. In wearable systems, the electronics sit right on the head.

Here's the key idea. A measurement doesn't come from one optode. It comes from a pair, one source, and one detector. That pair is a channel, and it listens to the banana between them. By convention, we place each channel at the midpoint between its two optodes.

The distance between them matters. In adults, about 3 centimeters is the classic choice. In infants whose heads are smaller and thinner, a bit less, around 2 to 2.5. Closer and you mostly see the scalp. Further and too few photons make it back. Place 8 sources and 8 detectors well and you get dozens of channels.

In practice, layouts come in three families. Small patches or headbands cover a single region, like the forehead. Whole head caps spread dozens of optodes over the scalp. And high-density arrays pack optodes so tightly that channels overlap at several distances. To make studies comparable, optode positions are usually referenced to the same 10-20 or 10-10 system used to place EEG electrodes. We'll design a montage properly in episode 3.1.

With many sources on at once, how do you know whose light is whose? Mix them up and one channel picks up another's light, that's called crosstalk. So the control unit takes turns, switching sources on one after another, or makes each one flicker at its own frequency. Then it samples every channel many times per second, often around 10, fast enough to catch every heartbeat.

Add a few extras, short channels about 8mm apart to watch the scalp, an accelerometer to catch head movements, and markers that timestamp every event of your experiment.

So, sources, detectors, a cap, a clever controller, and channels made of pairs, about 3cm apart. Next time, we follow a single photon through all of this.

PLATE 1.43:11

A photon's journey · The banana

A photon in tissue scatters constantly and is rarely absorbed, and the few that reach a detector trace a curved band called the banana. Source-detector distance sets its depth: about 8 millimeters sees only scalp, about 3 centimeters reaches the cortex. Even then part of the signal comes from outside the brain, spatial resolution is about a centimeter, and deep structures stay out of reach.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Hold a flashlight against your hand in a dark room. Your fingers glow red. Light, it turns out, goes right through you. Not in a straight line, it wanders. And that wandering is the whole trick behind fNIRS.

Let's slice through the head. Skin. Then skull, about half a centimeter of bone. A thin layer of fluid. And finally, the cortex, the part we actually care about, sitting one to two centimeters below the surface.

Now follow one photon of near-infrared light. In tissue, it almost never travels in a straight line. It bounces off cells, membranes, and fibers, and after about a millimeter, it has completely forgotten where it was going. Scattering is the rule. Absorption is rare, but absorption is what we want to measure because hemoglobin is one of the things that absorbs.

How rare is absorption? In tissue like this, a photon scatters roughly 50 to 100 times for every time it's absorbed. So it zigzags for centimeters before anything catches it.

Most photons get lost. They're absorbed or they pop back out somewhere else. But a tiny fraction come back out right where we placed a detector. Now trace every path that made it from the source to the detector, and a shape appears. A curved band dipping into the head and coming back up. We call it the banana.

The banana's depth depends on one thing you control, the distance between source and detector. Slide the detector close about 8 millimeters and the banana stays shallow. It only sees the scalp. Move it to about 3 centimeters and the banana reaches down into the cortex. Go further still and it digs deeper, but far fewer photons survive the trip. For adults, 3 centimeters is the sweet spot.

But the banana isn't evenly sensitive. A channel feels most strongly near its source and its detector, right where the scalp is and much less at depth. So even at 3 centimeters, a good part of the signal still comes from outside the brain. The banana also sets the spatial resolution of fNIRS. It's a centimeter or so wide, so each channel sees a patch of cortex, not a single fold. And depth has a hard limit. Light reaches the outer cortex, but deep structures like the hippocampus or the amygdala stay out of reach. In newborns, it's a different story. The scalp and skull are much thinner, so light reaches the cortex more easily, which is why infant studies use shorter distances.

Those short, shallow channels aren't useless though. They measure what's happening in the skin, blood flow that has nothing to do with the brain. Later, we'll subtract them from the long channels to clean up the signal.

One last surprise. A photon that comes out three centimeters away has actually traveled much further, often around six times that distance, zigzagging all the way. That extra path length matters. We'll need it to turn light into concentrations.

So, light goes in, scatters, and a lucky few come back, drawing a banana through your head. Next time, how we turn those photons into numbers.

PLATE 1.52:40

Three ways to count photons · CW, FD, TD

Continuous-wave systems measure brightness only, so they are cheap and scalable but give relative changes and rely on an assumed path length. Frequency-domain systems add the phase delay, which reveals path length and gives absolute values. Time-domain systems time individual photons from picosecond pulses, which also separates scalp from brain, at the cost of bulky hardware; continuous wave dominates studies.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every fNIRS system shines light and catches what comes back. But there are three different ways to count those photons.

The simplest is continuous wave, or CW. The light stays on, steady, and the detector measures one thing — how bright it is. Brightness goes down, absorption went up. It's cheap, robust, and easy to scale to many channels. But it can't tell how far the light traveled, so it relies on an assumed path length factor and gives only relative changes. If the light dims by 10%, did more hemoglobin absorb it, or did the light simply take a longer route? CW alone can't tell. One clever variant uses several distances at once and estimates an absolute oxygen saturation from how the light fades with distance. That's how many clinical cerebral oximeters work.

Next, frequency domain. Here, the light's intensity is modulated very fast, around 100 million times per second. The light that comes back is weaker and slightly late. Its wave is shifted in phase. That delay reveals the path length. Measuring both brightness and phase separates absorption from scattering and gives absolute values. That's why frequency domain shows up where absolute numbers matter, like measuring tissue oxygen saturation.

Then time domain. The source fires ultra-short pulses, a few picoseconds long, and the detector clocks the arrival time of individual photons. A sharp pulse goes in. A broad smear comes out because every photon took its own route. Light in tissue covers roughly a fifth of a millimeter per picosecond. So a photon arriving one nanosecond after the pulse has traveled about 20 centimeters. Arrival time is path length. Early photons mostly stayed shallow. Late photons are more likely to have wandered deep. So time domain can help separate the scalp from the brain. Catching single photons with picosecond timing takes special detectors, like single-photon avalanche diodes, which long made these systems bulky and expensive. New chips are shrinking them fast.

The trade-off is simple. CW gives you many channels at low cost. frequency and time domain give you richer absolute information with more complex hardware and usually fewer channels most fNIRS studies today use cw and that's what the rest of this series will focus on so steady light tells you how much flickering light adds how late and pulsed light tells you exactly when next chapter the signal itself
