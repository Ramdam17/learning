PLATE 9.14:30

Agents, states, actions, rewards

A rover on a game board sets up reinforcement learning. The state is what it sees, the action its move, the policy the rule choosing moves, and the reward a token, its only feedback. The goal is the return, the sum of future tokens with distant ones discounted, and the hard part is credit assignment: which earlier move earned a late token.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the game board. A winding path of colored squares, a die, and a small rover called ROVI, who has to find its way along it. This chapter is about acting in the world. Today we set up the vocabulary of reinforcement learning, and we end on its hardest question.

Let's watch ROVI play. At every moment, ROVI stands on one square, and it sees what is around it. That is the state: where ROVI is, and what it sees from there. The state is all that ROVI knows about the game right now.

From that square, ROVI picks a move. Step forward, step aside, stay put. The move it plays is the action. In episode one point one, a model was a function with knobs. The rule that picks the move is such a function too: a state goes in, an action comes out. We call it the policy, and its knobs are what ROVI will learn. A policy can also be a little random, using the probabilities of episode two point five, but today we only need the simple version.

Then the board answers. ROVI lands on a square, and receives a token, or loses one, or gets nothing at all. The token is the reward. It is a single number, and it is the only feedback ROVI ever gets. Nobody tells ROVI which move was right. It only sees tokens.

Put these pieces together and you get a loop. ROVI observes the state and plays an action. The world answers with a reward and a new state. Then it starts again. This agent and environment loop is the standard picture of reinforcement learning. It is set out in chapter three of Sutton and Barto's textbook, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, second edition, published by MIT Press in twenty eighteen.

So what does ROVI want? Not the biggest token right now. It wants the most tokens over the whole game. Add up the tokens it will receive from now on, but count the far ones a little less. A token that arrives k moves later is multiplied by gamma, to the power k. Gamma is a number a little below one. Say gamma is zero point nine. A token one move away counts for zero point nine. A token ten moves away counts for zero point three five. A token thirty moves away counts for only zero point zero four. The discounted sum is called the return. Section three point three of the same book defines it.

Here is a small example. ROVI wins one token two moves later, loses one five moves later, and wins three ten moves later. With gamma at zero point nine, that is zero point eight one, minus zero point five nine, plus one point zero five. The return is about one point three. These tokens are made up, for illustration.

Now the hard part. Picture a long game. ROVI plays thirty moves, and only on the last one does it win a token. Which of those thirty moves deserves the credit? Perhaps the last one. Perhaps a turn taken at the very beginning, dozens of moves earlier, that opened the right road. The token carries no label. This is the credit assignment problem, and it is the central difficulty of reinforcement learning. Marvin Minsky posed it in nineteen sixty-one, in his paper Steps Toward Artificial Intelligence: how do you share out the credit for a success among the many decisions that led to it?

The discount gives a first, crude answer. The nearer a move is to the token, the brighter it shines. But that is bookkeeping, not knowledge. The move that really earned the token may sit far back, where the light is dim. Finding it takes many games, and that is what the next episodes are about.

There is a useful parallel with episode three point three. Backpropagation carries blame backwards through the layers of a network, from the loss to each weight. Credit assignment carries credit backwards in time, from a token to the moves that came before. Take it as an analogy only. In a network, we know the exact path from each weight to the loss. In the game, ROVI must discover which moves counted.

To sum up. The state is the square and what ROVI sees. The action is the move it plays. The reward is the token it receives. ROVI wants the largest return: the sum of future tokens, with distant ones counted a little less. And the hard part is credit assignment, because a token can arrive long after the move that earned it. Next time: policies and value functions.

PLATE 9.26:11

Policies and value functions

A policy is an arrow on every square, and a value is a water level in a tank on every square: the return expected from there when following the policy. The Bellman relation says a value equals the reward on the way plus gamma times the next square's value, so sweeping it until nothing changes fills a small board.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back on the game board. ROVI is chasing a token, and last time we ended on a hard question: which move deserves the credit? Today we give ROVI two things it can carry around. A way to choose, and a way to judge.

The way to choose is the policy. Draw it as an arrow on every square. Here, I go right. Here, I go up. A policy is a map from squares to moves, and ROVI simply follows the arrows. This one is deterministic: one square, one move. A policy can also be stochastic, which means it gives a probability to each move, like a die with weighted faces. Sutton and Barto write a policy with the Greek letter pi, in chapter three of their textbook.

The way to judge is the value. Draw it as a height on every square. From here, I expect this many tokens. More exactly, the value of a square is the return that ROVI expects, with the discount gamma from episode nine point one, if it starts on that square and then follows its policy. The value belongs to a policy. Change the policy, and the heights change.

Here is a way to see it. Each square is a glass tank, and the water level is the value. A tank fed straight from the token is full. A tank far from the token gets its water through a chain of pipes, and every pipe has a valve that lets through only part of the flow. That valve is gamma.

Now a small board to work with. It is four squares by four, and it is illustrative. One square holds a token, in the top right corner. Landing on it pays one token and ends the game. ROVI can step up, down, left or right, and bumping into an edge leaves it where it is. We set gamma to zero point nine.

Now the sentence that the whole episode serves. The value of a square equals the reward you get on the way, plus gamma times the value of the square you land on. That is the Bellman relation. It comes from Richard Bellman's dynamic programming, set out in his book of nineteen fifty-seven, published by Princeton University Press. In the tank picture, the level here is the fresh water poured in here, plus the level next door, after it passes a valve that lets through nine tenths.

Try it. Take the square just left of the token. Stepping right pays one token, so its value is one. One square further back, nothing is paid on the way, so the value is zero plus zero point nine times one: zero point nine. One more square back: zero point nine times zero point nine, which is zero point eight one.

See what the relation did. It turned a sum over a whole future into one look at the next square. We never trace thirty moves ahead. The value of the far tokens has already been poured back, square by square. That is how credit travels backwards.

So how do we fill the whole board? Start with every tank empty. Then sweep. For every square, apply the Bellman relation, using the levels from the sweep before, and keep the best move. After the first sweep, only the two squares beside the token hold anything: one each. After the second sweep, the water has moved one square further back. Each sweep carries it one more step.

After six sweeps, even the far corner is filled, at zero point five nine. The seventh sweep changes nothing. The values have stopped changing, and that is the signal to stop. This is called value iteration. Sutton and Barto present it in chapter four, next to policy evaluation, which is the same sweep for a fixed policy.

Sometimes we want to judge a move, not a square. The action value, written Q of s and a, is the value of playing move a on square s, and then carrying on well. The value of a square alone is written V. Take the second square of the top row. Right gives zero point nine. Up bumps into the wall and gives zero point eight one. Left and down both give zero point seven two nine.

Once you know Q, choosing is easy. Pick the move with the highest Q. This is the greedy policy. Draw it on the board, and every arrow points toward the token. On many squares, two arrows tie: up and right are equally good, because each gets one step closer.

Remember that the value belongs to a policy. Let ROVI wander at random instead, each of the four moves with the same chance. The same relation applies, with an average over the moves in place of the best one. It takes one hundred and thirty seven sweeps to settle, and from the bottom left corner the value is only zero point zero seven, against zero point five nine for the greedy policy. A random walk leaks water everywhere.

Then judging and choosing feed each other. Judge the current policy, make it greedier, then judge again. Most of reinforcement learning is a variation on that loop.

There is a limit, though. This board has sixteen squares, so sixteen numbers do the job. A game seen through a camera has far too many states for one tank each. So the value becomes a function with knobs, a neural network, which guesses the height from what ROVI sees. The sweep also needed the rules of the board: where a move lands, and what it pays. Whether ROVI knows those rules is the question of the next episode.

The Bellman relation is not only textbook material. Dreamer, by Hafner and colleagues, at the International Conference on Learning Representations in twenty twenty, learns a value, and propagates the gradients of learned state values back through trajectories imagined in the compact space of a learned world model. MuZero, by Schrittwieser and colleagues, in Nature in twenty twenty, has a learned model that predicts the reward, the policy and the value. Policy, value, reward: the three things of this episode.

To sum up. A policy is an arrow on every square. A value is a height on every square. The Bellman relation says that the value of a square is the reward on the way, plus gamma times the value of the next square. Sweeping it until nothing changes fills the board. PLATE 9.35:42

Model-free vs model-based

A model-free agent learns only by playing and can need years of game experience, while a model-based agent first learns the rules of the board and then rehearses moves in imagination, needing far fewer real trials. This matters most for a robot, where every trial is real. The catch is that imagined rollouts feed on themselves, so small errors compound unless the model is right.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the game board. Two ROVIs stand at the start of the same game. Both want the same thing: the largest return, the discounted sum of tokens from episode nine point one. But they learn in two very different ways.

The first ROVI has no model. It never asks why a move leads anywhere. It plays a move, sees the token, and nudges its rule for picking moves toward whatever paid off. Then it plays again. And again. And again. This is called model-free learning. It needs no knowledge of the board at all, which is its strength: it can be dropped into any game. Its price is paid in trials.

The second ROVI starts differently. Before it tries to win, it learns the rules of the board. From this square, with this move, where do I land, and which token do I get? That prediction is a model of the world. As in episode one point one, a model is a function with knobs, and the knobs are tuned by gradient descent, from episode two point four, until its predictions match what really happens.

Once ROVI has a model, it can rehearse in its head. It tries a move in imagination, sees where it would land, tries another, and only then plays for real. This is called model-based learning. Its price is paid elsewhere: in the work of building a model that is good enough to trust.

So why does the difference matter? Because of what each one costs. Real moves take real time, so let's count the trials.

In twenty fifteen, Mnih and colleagues at DeepMind published DQN in the journal Nature. It was a model-free agent that learned Atari games from the screen alone. Each game got fifty million frames of training, which the paper puts at about thirty-eight days of game experience. For a single game.

Better algorithms cut that bill. In twenty eighteen, DeepMind's Rainbow agent combined six improvements to DQN. It matched DQN's best performance after seven million frames, instead of two hundred million. That is nearly thirty times less. But seven million frames is still at least thirty-two hours of play, for one game, just to reach what DQN reached.

Later agents asked for more. Agent57, from DeepMind in twenty twenty, finally beat the human baseline on all fifty-seven games of the benchmark. But on one of them, Skiing, it used seventy-eight billion frames. At the game's normal speed of sixty frames a second, that is more than forty years of play. Stack those years as wall calendars, and the pile grows taller than ROVI.

A simulator can run faster than real time, and thousands of copies can run side by side. A robot cannot. It has one body, one clock, and wheels that wear out. For a robot, every trial is a real trial, and the pile of calendars is a real pile.

Now the other side. Kaiser and colleagues point out that human players can learn these games in minutes. So in twenty nineteen, they asked what a model could do with a tiny budget: a hundred thousand moves, which is four hundred thousand frames, about two hours of play. Their method is called SimPLe.

SimPLe works in a loop. The agent plays a little for real. It trains a video model on what it saw. It practises inside that model. Then it goes back and plays for real again, with a better rule, and a better model. The loop runs fifteen times.

On most games it beat the best model-free agents at that budget. On a few, those agents needed over ten times more moves to reach the same score. On some games, a model-free method called PPO needed ten million moves to reach the score SimPLe had after a hundred thousand. That is a hundred times more.

Here is the promise. A world model can replace part of these trials with imagination. An imagined move costs a little computation, not a little of the real world. But only if the model is right.

And here is the failure mode. An imagined rollout feeds on itself. Each step starts from the previous imagined step, so a small mistake early on is carried into every step after it. The errors compound.

Take a toy model that is right ninety-five times out of a hundred at each step. After ten steps, the whole imagined path is still right about sixty percent of the time. After twenty steps, about thirty-six percent. After fifty, about eight. By fourteen steps, the path is already more likely wrong than right. This toy is illustrative, not a measurement.

Kaiser and colleagues write that short rollouts are crucial for exactly this reason. Every fifty steps, they restart the imagined rollout from a state taken from real data, so the model never drifts too far from reality.

So neither approach wins everywhere. When trials are cheap, as in a fast simulator, a model-free agent can simply play. When trials are expensive, as for a robot, a model can save years. But you must be able to trust it, and you pay for that twice: once to build the model, and once to check it. A wrong model can send ROVI confidently in the wrong direction. The series comes back to this when we meet world models, later on.

To sum up. A model-free agent learns by playing, and can need years of experience. A model-based agent learns the rules, then rehearses in imagination, so it needs far fewer real trials, as long as its model is right. And errors in an imagined rollout compound. PLATE 9.47:07

Planning: search and model-predictive control

Planning searches for the sequence of moves that minimizes a cost under a model. The cross-entropy method imagines ghost paths, keeps the elite few and narrows its sampling around them, like a torch beam finding its target. Model-predictive control plays only the first move and replans, correcting model errors, while tree search suits discrete board games.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the game board. ROVI now knows it can try moves in its head, using a model of the board, before it touches a single piece. Imagining is free, but the number of possible futures is enormous. How does ROVI choose which ones to imagine, and which move to actually play? That is planning.

Let's make it concrete with a toy. This one is illustrative. ROVI sits on a plane and wants to reach a goal, three steps east and two steps north. Each move shifts it by at most one step in each direction, and it looks five moves ahead. A plan is a sequence of five moves. For every plan, the model draws the path ROVI would follow, and a cost says how far that path stays from the goal, on average. In chapter 8, energy was a cost: low means compatible. Planning is the same idea, except that we are no longer scoring one answer. We are minimising a cost over a whole sequence of moves.

The simplest recipe is random shooting. ROVI imagines ten random sequences of moves, each one a dotted ghost path, scores them, and keeps the best. That works, a little. But most ghosts wander off, and nothing is learned from the ones that came close.

Here is the better recipe, the cross-entropy method, or CEM. It goes back to Reuven Rubinstein, in 1997, and the standard book is Rubinstein and Kroese, in 2004. Step one: imagine ten sequences, drawing every move from a bell curve centred on zero, with a spread of one. Step two: score them, and keep the best three. We call them the elites. Step three: measure the average of each move among the elites, and the spread. Step four: draw ten new sequences around that average, with that smaller spread. Then repeat.

Watch the ghosts. In the first round, the fan of ghost paths points everywhere. The best cost is two point six seven, and the three elites average two point seven seven. In the second round, the spread has dropped from one to point five four, and the best cost is two point oh three. In the third round, one point eight. In the fourth, one point six seven, with a spread of only point two two. The fan has narrowed like a torch beam finding its target.

Is that really better than random? We repeated the experiment five hundred times. Random shooting, with the same forty imagined sequences, finds an average best cost of one point seven three. The cross-entropy method, with ten sequences over four rounds, reaches one point five oh. The PlaNet paper, which plans in a learned latent space, saw the same trend: refining sequences with CEM beat picking the best of a thousand random ones, on all its tasks.

Three knobs matter. The horizon is how many moves ahead you look. The number of samples is how many ghosts you cast per round. The elite fraction is how many you keep. PlaNet used a horizon of twelve, ten rounds, a thousand samples, and kept a hundred. Look further, and the number of possible sequences explodes. Cast more ghosts, and each one costs a model run. Keep fewer elites, and the fan narrows fast, but it can get stuck. CEM gives no guarantee of finding the best plan. It finds a good one, quickly.

Now the second half of the recipe. ROVI does not play the whole plan. It plays only the first move, looks at where it really is, and plans again from scratch. This is model-predictive control, and because the horizon keeps sliding forward, engineers also call it receding horizon control. A survey by Mayne and colleagues, in Automatica in 2000, is a standard reference. PlaNet does exactly this.

Why bother? Because the model is wrong, as Model-free versus model-based showed. Let's break our toy on purpose. In the real world, a wind pushes ROVI half a step east and half a step south on every move, and the model does not know. If ROVI plays all five moves of its first plan, it ends three point five six from the goal. If it replans after each move, it sees where the wind pushed it, and corrects. In this run it ends one point seven five away. Over five hundred repetitions, the average distance is three point four eight when playing the whole plan, and one point five seven when replanning. Errors cannot pile up over five moves if you start from the truth after every move.

There is a second family of planners, built for discrete moves like a board game. Tree search grows a tree of moves, and spends its time on the most promising branches. AlphaGo, in 2016, combined Monte Carlo tree search with neural networks. MuZero, in 2020, searched inside a model it had learned itself. A robot arm moves continuously, with no short list of moves to branch on. That is why CEM is a natural fit there.

And now, the strong moment. This loop, imagine, choose, take one step, imagine again, is what V-JEPA 2 uses to make a robot act. As of November 2026, here is what the paper reports. The video encoder is trained on over one million hours of video. A predictor that takes actions is then trained on less than sixty-two hours of robot video. The authors call it V-JEPA 2-AC. To act, it encodes a goal image, and runs CEM: it imagines action sequences, measures how far each imagined future is from the goal in representation space, keeps the best, and refits. It executes only the first action, then plans again. Arms in two labs, with no reward and no demonstration of the task, picked up and moved objects.

Speed matters here. The paper compares planning with Cosmos, a video generation model. With eighty samples, Cosmos took four minutes per action. V-JEPA 2-AC used ten times more samples, eight hundred, and needed sixteen seconds. Imagining in a compact latent space is cheaper than imagining every pixel, which is the idea behind this whole series.

LeWorldModel, in March 2026, pushes this further. It has about fifteen million parameters, trains on a single GPU, and plans up to forty-eight times faster than world models built on large pretrained encoders. It also plans with CEM: three hundred samples, thirty elites, a horizon of five steps. We will study both in episode 11.6, From seeing to acting: JEPA world models for robots.

To sum up. Planning means searching for a sequence of moves that minimises a cost, under a model. The cross-entropy method samples sequences, keeps the best, and narrows around them. Model-predictive control plays only the first move and replans, so that model errors get corrected. Tree search is the other family.
