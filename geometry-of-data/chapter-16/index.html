A distance learned from examples

Learned distances come from examples. On a simulated bench with ten columns where only one separates two groups and nine loud ones drown it, the straight line classifies 54 percent right, standardised columns 69, and learned weights 78 percent. Beware overfitting: with sixty-four columns and forty random-label examples, a learned distance scores 88 percent on its own examples and 50 on new points.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, every distance in this series was chosen by hand. We picked it, and we decided what it should ignore. This chapter asks what happens when the distance is learned instead. We give it examples of what should count as the same, and a calculation sets the distance to respect them.

Remember the three people from the tailor's shop. Person A, one point six zero metres and sixty kilograms. B, one point nine zero metres and sixty-one kilograms. C, one point six two metres and sixty-four kilograms. In metres, A's nearest neighbour is B: one point zero four against four. In centimetres, it is C: four point four seven against thirty point zero two. The neighbour flipped, and nobody moved.

Later in that chapter, we set the units from the cloud itself. We turned it, squeezed it and stretched it until it was round, and then measured with the straight line. That was the Mahalanobis distance. But the cloud does not know what the distance is for. It cannot tell a useful column from a noisy one.

So today, we set the units from examples. The examples can be points with a label, like two groups. Or they can be pairs, marked "same" or "different". Think of a perfumer's blotter strips, clipped in pairs, each pair with its verdict.

Here is our test bench. Ten columns, like ten bottles on the shelf. Only one column separates the two groups: their averages sit two standard deviations apart. The other nine carry nothing at all, and they are loud: they vary five times more. Everything here is simulated.

The task: classify each new point like its nearest neighbour among the examples. With two hundred examples, and averaged over ten draws.

First, the straight line. Fifty-four percent. Barely better than a coin. The nine loud columns drown the one that matters. Each of them adds its own big differences to the sum, and the one useful difference is lost among them.

Second, bring every column to the same spread. Sixty-nine percent. Better, but the nine silent columns still get one vote each.

Third, give each column a weight, computed from the labelled examples: how far apart the two group averages are, compared with the spread inside each group. Seventy-eight percent. That is as good as using the useful column alone. And on average, ninety-six percent of the weight lands on that one column. The examples wrote the recipe.

For comparison, the best any rule could do here is eighty-four percent. That number is exact, not simulated.

We can learn more than one weight per column. We can learn a mixture of the columns. This is the distance from the Mahalanobis episode, except that its table is now fitted to the examples, not measured on the cloud. We stretch and turn the space, and then we measure with the straight line. The method here is neighbourhood components analysis, from Goldberger and colleagues in two thousand and four.

It scores seventy-three percent with two hundred examples, and seventy-six with a thousand. Why lower than the simple weights? Its table has fifty-five numbers to set instead of ten. So it needs more examples to catch up. In this draw, its table has not yet found out that only one column matters.

The idea is older than it looks. A survey by Bellet, Habrard and Sebban traces its roots to the nineteen-eighties and nineties, with Short and Fukunaga, and Hastie and Tibshirani. It became a field of its own with Xing and colleagues in two thousand and two, who learned a distance from pairs marked similar and dissimilar. Weinberger, Blitzer and Saul then asked for something more local: keep each point's neighbours of the same label close, and push the others away beyond a margin.

Time to fill the card. The object is a vector. The distance is the straight line, after a stretch and a turn learned from examples.

What does it ignore? Whatever its examples told it to ignore. Here, nine loud columns. A distance learned for one question is not learned for another. Ask a different question of the same people, and the recipe may be wrong.

And watch one light. If the learned transformation crushes a direction flat, two different points can land in the same place, at distance zero. The rule "zero only for the same point" goes out. What is left is a pseudo-distance.

What does it cost? Labelled examples, which are often the expensive part. A training run. And numbers to set: for a full mixture of sixty-four columns, two thousand and eighty of them. That is our own count, the same as for the Mahalanobis table.

Now the trap. A learned distance can learn its examples by heart.

Take sixty-four columns and only forty examples. And let us cheat: the labels are drawn at random, by a coin. There is nothing to learn. Averaged over ten draws again: before training, each example's nearest neighbour has the same label forty-eight percent of the time. Chance. After training, eighty-eight percent. The distance has learned the coin. With two thousand and eighty numbers to set and only forty examples, there is enough freedom to bend the space around any labelling at all. On new points, fifty percent. Chance again.

Now give the groups a real difference, along one column. The learned distance shows ninety-four percent on its own forty examples. On new points, it makes sixty-six percent, against sixty-one for the straight line. To reach seventy-four percent, it needs a thousand examples.

And a smaller recipe does as well. With a thousand examples, a mixture cut down to two directions, one hundred and twenty-eight numbers instead of two thousand and eighty, scores seventy-five percent.

Verma and Branson showed that, with no assumption on the data, the number of examples needed grows with the number of columns. The Statistics series calls this overfitting, and the EEG series comes back to it among the pitfalls of decoding. The cure is always the same: judge a learned distance on examples it never saw.

So here is the card, complete. A learned distance ignores what its examples told it to ignore. With few examples and many columns, it also learns the noise. Eighty-eight percent on its own examples, fifty on the next ones.

PLATE 16.26:46

A space built for the distance

A network can build the whole space, learning from pairs marked same or different, or from altered copies of one object. On two thousand simulated signals, a shift is erased from the distance, dropping from 11.76 to 0.05. The list of alterations is what the distance ignores: adding amplify drops the reading of loudness from ninety-four percent to sixty-six.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the distance learned a weight for each column. The space stayed the same; only its units moved. Today we go one step further. A network builds the whole space, and the distance is measured there.

Picture a cloakroom. You hand in an object at the counter, and the attendant hangs it on a hook. The counter is the network. The hook is where the network places the object: a short list of numbers. People call that list an embedding. Two objects are close when their hooks are close.

Nobody tells the network what to measure. It learns from pairs. Two copies of the same network read the two objects of a pair. If the pair is marked "same", the training pulls their hooks together. If it is marked "different", it pushes them apart.

The idea is old. Bromley and colleagues, in nineteen ninety-three, checked signatures with twin networks they called siamese. Chopra, Hadsell and LeCun trained such pairs on faces in two thousand five, and the following year Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun put the word invariant in their title.

In two thousand fifteen, FaceNet placed each face on a hook of one hundred and twenty-eight numbers. It trained with triplets: a face, another face of the same person, and a face of someone else. Its authors say they took this rule from Weinberger and colleagues.

Now, what if nobody labels the pairs? Then we make them. Take one object, alter it twice, and call the two versions "same". Every other object in the batch is "different". That is the recipe of Chen and colleagues, in twenty twenty. The loss they used comes from van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, in twenty eighteen, who formed their pairs another way: a passage of a signal, and what comes next. The World Models series builds this recipe step by step, in its episode seven point two.

So the training needs one more thing: a list of alterations. The list says which changes do not change the object.

Here is our bench. Two thousand simulated signals, sixty-four samples each. Each one has between three and eight cycles, a loudness between one half and two, a random position in time, and some noise. The network hangs each signal on a hook of eight numbers. Our first list of alterations has two lines: shift in time, and noise drawn again.

How do we read a distance between two hooks? The training compares directions, so we take one minus the cosine. Remember episode two point three: one minus a similarity is not a distance, because it can break the triangle rule. The angle is a distance. We keep one minus the cosine because it is what the training reads, and we say so.

Now the row of changes, first with the straight line on the raw signals, then in the learned space. Each number is a mean over two hundred observations.

The same signal, observed twice with fresh noise. Straight line: three point four zero. Learned: zero point zero four.

Shifted by half a cycle. Straight line: eleven point seven six, the largest of the row. Learned: zero point zero five. The network has stopped seeing the shift.

Twice as loud. Straight line: six point five nine. Learned: zero point two six. Not on the list, so it is still seen.

Four and a half cycles instead of four. Straight line: eight point six eight. Learned: one point two zero.

And two different signals drawn at random. Straight line: nine point seven one. Learned: one point zero zero, the value of two directions at right angles.

Now we add one line to the list: amplify, by any factor between one quarter and four. Same signals, same network, trained again. Twice as loud now gives zero point zero one.

This is the heart of the chapter. The list of alterations is the line "ignores" of the card. It was written by hand, before the training started.

And it has a price. Ask the nearest neighbour, on four hundred new signals, whether each one is loud or soft. Chance is fifty percent. On the raw signals, the straight line gets ninety-one. In the first learned space, ninety-four. In the second, sixty-six. Each of these is a mean over three trainings. The number of cycles is still read at ninety-nine percent in both.

Look at where the network hangs the test signals. Seen along their two main directions, the first space puts loud signals on one side and soft ones on the other. In the second, they are mixed. But not completely: sixty-six is not fifty. The erasure is partial.

Real images show the same thing. Xiao and colleagues, in twenty twenty-one, added rotation to the list. Reading the angle of a rotated image then fell from sixty-one percent to forty-three. Chen and colleagues saw the reverse danger. Without colour changes on the list, two pieces of the same photo match by their colours alone, and the network takes that shortcut. Tian and colleagues drew a rule from this: two versions called "same" should share only what the question needs.

Time to fill the card. The object: a signal of sixty-four samples. The distance: one minus the cosine, where the network places each signal.

What it ignores: what the list declares the same, and only roughly. Nothing forbids two different signals from landing on the same hook.

What it costs: a training, and a new one for each new question. It also costs the shades of far. At four point two cycles, the learned distance is zero point three five. At four and a half, five or seven cycles, it is about one each time. On these signals, the straight line flattens too, near eight point seven. But the training asks only for two things, as Wang and Isola showed: pull the pairs together, and spread everything else evenly. It separates. It does not grade.

The four rules. Never negative: yes. The same both ways: yes. Zero only for the same object: not guaranteed. No shortcut: one minus the cosine can break it, so that light goes out.

And the trap: believing in the progress of the recipes. Musgrave, Belongie and Lim, in twenty twenty, ran the published methods again under equal conditions. The methods published from two thousand six to two thousand nineteen did about as well as one another. Roth and colleagues found the same saturation that year.

So remember the card. In a learned space, the distance ignores what the list of alterations said, written before the training, and only roughly. Add amplify to the list, and loudness falls from ninety-four percent to sixty-six.

PLATE 16.36:48

Reading distances in a learned space

Reading a learned space needs four checks. Retrain: the same signal sits 1.23 from itself across trainings, nearly as far as from a stranger, though 85 percent of ten nearest neighbours agree. Remove the centre: a shift of ten lifts mean cosine to 0.99. Know the training rule, and distrust word arithmetic, which works only if the starting words are forbidden.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a network built a whole space, and the distance was measured there. Today, we learn to read that space. Before a distance read in a learned space means anything, it must pass four checks. Train again. Find the centre. Know the training rule. And distrust word arithmetic.

One word first. The point where a network puts an object is often called its embedding. Here we simply say: where the network places it. The World Models series builds such spaces, in its episodes 4.1 and 4.2.

Check one: train again. We take the network of episode 16.2, with its first list of alterations: shift in time, and fresh noise. We train it three times, on the same two thousand signals. Only the starting point of the training changes. Then each training places the same four hundred new signals, eight numbers each.

Compare the columns first. Column one of the first training, against column one of the second. Their correlation, in absolute value, averages 0.21 over the eight columns. Across the three pairs of trainings, it goes from 0.21 to 0.35. The columns do not correspond.

Now put one signal in both spaces, and measure the straight line between its two places. On average, 1.23. Two different signals, inside one training, sit 1.38 apart. The same signal is almost as far from itself as from a stranger. So never compare coordinates across two trainings.

Compare the tables of distances instead, as in episode 12.3. Inside each training, the distance between every pair of signals. The two tables agree. Their rank correlation lies between 0.79 and 0.87. And on average, 85 percent of a signal's ten nearest neighbours are the same in both trainings. For this signal, eight out of ten.

Why do the tables agree, when the columns do not? Remember the rotation of episode 12.2. Turn one space onto the other, as well as possible. The gap between the two spaces, measured against their size, falls from about 1.3 to about 0.4 or 0.5. And a rotation changes no distance at all. Between trainings, what moved was mostly the frame, not the neighbourhoods.

On words, Wendlandt, Kummerfeld and Mihalcea give an example where two trainings share only four of a word's ten nearest neighbours: the word international. Going the other way, Huh and colleagues argue that networks trained on different data end up placing things alike. They measure it with shared neighbours. How to read their scores is debated, in two papers posted in 2026.

Check two: the centre. A cosine is the angle seen from zero, as in episode 2.3. Take one thousand random points in sixty-four columns. Seen from zero, two of them have a mean cosine of 0.00. Now add the same shift to every column. Shift by one, and the mean cosine climbs to 0.50. By three, 0.90. By ten, 0.99. Nothing changed between the points. Only where zero sits.

Remove the mean point, and the cosine falls back to 0.00. And one loud column is enough. Shift a single column by thirty, and the mean cosine is 0.93. That one column carries practically all of it.

This happens in real spaces. In the last layers of one language model, two words picked at random have a cosine close to one (Ethayarajh, 2019). In such models, one to three columns can dominate the cosine (Timkey and van Schijndel, 2021). The known remedy: remove the mean point, and a few dominant directions (Mu and Viswanath, 2018). So "two unrelated vectors give zero" holds only around the centre.

Check three: the training rule. Take a table of six people and four items, written as the product of two small tables with two columns. Multiply the first column of the items by ten, and divide the first column of the people by ten. Not one of the twenty-four predictions changes. But the cosine between item one and item three goes from 0.470 to 0.955.

Steck, Ekanadham and Kallus showed this in 2024. The freedom exists when training penalises only the product. With a penalty on each of the two tables, the answer is unique, up to a rotation. So ask how a space was trained, before you read a cosine in it.

Check four: word arithmetic (Mikolov, Yih and Zweig, 2013). King, minus man, plus woman, lands near queen. But only if the three starting words are forbidden as answers. Without that rule, the nearest word to the result is the third word, here woman, in 93 percent of cases, and the first answer word, here king, in 5 (Linzen, 2016). The same rule means "man is to doctor as woman is to doctor" can never come out (Nissim, van Noord and van der Goot, 2020).

Here is the card. The object: a signal, placed by a network. The distance: the straight line between two places. What it ignores: the frame. A rotation of the learned space changes no distance, and changes every column. And nothing stops two different objects from landing on the same point: that light stays a question mark. What it costs: at least a second training.

The trap: using it as a graduated ruler. Zhang and colleagues collected 484,000 human judgments: which of two images looks closer to a third? Distances measured in a network agree with people in 67 to 69 percent of cases. Classic measures, in about 63. And two people agree with each other in 74. Better, but not a measurement. The usual score for image generators is the distance of episode 8.6, measured in a network's space. It depends on the number of images, in a way that differs from one generator to the next (Chong and Forsyth, 2020). And it depends on the network chosen (Kynkäänniemi and colleagues, 2023).

So, the columns of a learned space mean nothing. Their frame changes with every training. What you can read are neighbourhoods: after removing the centre, knowing the training rule, and checking that they hold from one training to the next.

Maps in two dimensions

t-SNE and UMAP maps pull neighbours together and push everything else apart, like pressing a bouquet flat. A cloud ten times more spread out takes the same room, a ratio of 1.01 on both maps. Noise forms clumps, the start decides the global order, and eleven equidistant points cannot fit on a page. Only who touches whom survives.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You have seen these maps everywhere. A cloud of coloured dots on a page, where each dot is a cell, a word, or a recording. Neighbours on the page are supposed to be neighbours in the data.

The two most common recipes are t-SNE, from van der Maaten and Hinton in 2008, and UMAP, from McInnes, Healy and Melville in 2018. The paper that describes the UMAP method is a preprint on arXiv; only its software has a journal paper.

Both place every point on the page with the same two moves. Pull together the points that are neighbours in the data. Push apart everything else.

That idea comes from another series, so here is a short reminder. Contrastive learning shows a machine pairs that go together, and pairs that do not. It pulls the first ones close and pushes the others away. Episode 16.2 used it to train a network that builds a whole space. The World Models series presents it in its episode 7.2, with SimCLR.

Damrich and colleagues showed in 2023 that t-SNE and UMAP belong to this family. They are not the same rule. t-SNE can be written as noise-contrastive estimation, and UMAP as negative sampling. But the spirit is the same: only neighbours are pulled.

Think of a flower press. A bouquet has depth. Close the book, and the bouquet becomes flat. Petals that touched still touch. What else survives the press?

To find out, we press data whose geometry we know. Everything here is simulated, five runs per test unless said otherwise.

First test: sizes. Two clouds in ten columns. One is ten times more spread out than the other. On the t-SNE map, the ratio of their sizes is 1.01. On the UMAP map, 1.01 as well. The big cloud and the small cloud take the same room on the page. Why? Each point listens only to its own neighbours, and every neighbourhood is scaled to hold about the same number of points. Seen from inside, a wide cloud and a tight cloud look alike.

Second test: gaps. Three clouds on a line, at 0, 10 and 110. The second gap is ten times the first. On the maps, it is 1.2 times the first for t-SNE, and 1.1 times for UMAP. The far cloud is still the farthest in only 4 runs out of 5 for t-SNE, and 2 out of 5 for UMAP. Between groups, almost nothing is pulled together, so the gaps are set mostly by the push.

Third test: noise. One round cloud, five hundred points, no groups at all. In t-SNE, the size of the neighbourhood is a setting called perplexity: roughly, how many neighbours each point listens to. Van der Maaten and Hinton suggest values between 5 and 50. Set it to 2, and the map shows clumps anyway. Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson warned about this in 2016. We score how well the points form groups, with the silhouette score of chapter 17. In the data, the best score is 0.08. On the t-SNE map, 0.47.

Part of that rise comes from the score itself. An evenly filled square on a page, with no groups either, already scores about 0.4. So the map adds clumps, and the page flatters them.

Fourth test: the start. These methods begin from a first layout, then move the points. Take eight clouds whose centres sit on a line, and run each method ten times.

To compare the distances between clouds, we use a rank correlation, as in episodes 3.4 and 12.3. Replace each distance by its rank. Distances 2.1, 7.5 and 4.0 have ranks 1, 3 and 2. Then correlate the ranks. It is 1 when the map orders the distances like the data, and 0 when the order has nothing to do with it.

From a random start, the order is lost: minus 0.14 for t-SNE, and 0.03 for UMAP. Now start t-SNE from the two main axes, the two directions where the cloud spreads most, like the axes of the ellipse in episode 8.1. And give UMAP its default start, built from its graph of neighbours. Now it is 0.41 and 0.53. Better, but the exact order of the eight clouds is found once in twenty runs of the two structured starts.

Kobak and Linderman made this point in 2021: initialization is critical for preserving global data structure in both t-SNE and UMAP. What people credited to one method over the other came from the start.

Fifth test: what is kept. Ten clouds in fifty columns. For each point, take its ten nearest neighbours in the data, and ask how many are still among its ten nearest on the map. t-SNE keeps 35 percent. UMAP, 24. The two main axes keep only 15.

Now ask for the order of all the distances. The ranking flips. The two main axes reach 0.80. t-SNE, 0.62. UMAP, about 0.3.

On the rolled sheet of episode 9.6, t-SNE keeps 86 percent of the neighbours. But the order of all distances falls to 0.37. The press keeps who touches whom, and loses the rest.

Why can it not keep everything? One picture says it. In ten columns, eleven points can all be at the same distance from each other. On a page, three at most. Van der Maaten and Hinton use this very example. For the eleven points, the best flat map we found gives distances from 0.41 to 1.34 times the true one. Some pairs shrink by more than half, others stretch by a third.

Here is the distance card. The object: points in many columns. The distance: the straight line between two dots on the map.

What it ignores: sizes, densities, and gaps between groups. Everything except who is the neighbour of whom.

What it costs: about 26 seconds for twenty thousand points with t-SNE, as measured when this series was planned. And settings that change the picture, such as the size of the neighbourhood and the start, as Wattenberg and colleagues, then Kobak and Berens in 2019, showed.

The trap: the map is a page. And on a page, all four lights of the card are on. It is never negative, zero only for the same dot, the same both ways, with no shortcut. So we are tempted to measure on it, to group on it, to test on it. But it is not the distance of the data.

The debate is lively. For Chari and Pachter, in 2023, squeezing thousands of columns into two distorts so much that these maps can mislead exploration. For Lause, Berens and Kobak, in 2024, they keep neighbourhoods and clusters well: all two-dimensional maps of such data are wrong, but some are useful. Both sides agree on one point. The distances on the map are not the distances of the data.

So, what survives the press? Who is next to whom. On these maps, the size of a group and the gap between two groups mean nothing. A cloud ten times more spread out than another takes the same room on the page: 1.01.

PLATE 16.56:10

What did your training call the same?

Learned distances are ordered, not found: whatever you declare the same, the space stops seeing. Banville and colleagues learned EEG spaces from time alone, beating a network trained on labels with few labelled examples, though gains over hand-made features were a few points. Large pretrained EEG models remain unsettled, and hyperscanning learned distances are untested leads needing a simple baseline.

TRANSCRIPTEN

What did your training call the same? In this chapter, we stopped choosing distances by hand. We let examples set them. Today we ask where this has been tried on brain data, what has held up, and what is still only a lead.

Start with a picture. A learned distance is not found. It is ordered, like a perfume made to measure. On the order slip, you tick what must count as the same. Everything you tick, the distance will stop seeing. Our little eye closes on each ticked line.

That is what the whole chapter did to the card. In every earlier chapter, we wrote the line of what a distance ignores by hand. Here, labelled examples wrote it first. Then the list of alterations allowed during training wrote it. The card is still there. Only the hand holding the pen has changed.

We sort the claims in two ways. A result that has arrived comes as a sealed parcel, with a tag that says how we read it: the full text, the abstract, or only the catalogue record. A lead stays as a blank order slip. Nobody has filled it in, and nothing on it was tested.

First parcel. Learning a space without labels works on EEG. Banville and colleagues, in 2021, made their pairs from time. Two windows close in time count as the same. Two windows far apart count as different. A second task asks whether three windows are in order, or shuffled. With few labelled examples, a simple classifier on the learned space beat a network trained directly on the labels.

They also checked against simple baselines: features made by hand, and the same network left at random. The learned space came out ahead of both. But against the hand-made features, the gain was a few points at most. We read the full text.

Second parcel. Kostas, Aroca-Ouellette and Rudzicz, in 2021, trained a task of the same kind on massive amounts of EEG, as their title says. We read only the catalogue record.

Third parcel. Schneider, Lee and Mathis, in 2023, learned one space shared by the activity of neurons and by behaviour. Their pairs are shaped by behaviour, or by time alone. The comparisons with other methods are the authors' own.

Fourth parcel. Shen and colleagues, in 2023, counted as the same the responses of two people to the same moment of the same film. Two viewers, one clip, one pair. The goal is to recognise emotions from one person to the next. On their eighty people, positive against negative, they report seventy-two percent, against sixty-five for the same features without the learned space. Their comparison, made by the authors. We read the full text.

The warnings have arrived too. The list of alterations erases what it declares the same. Xiao and colleagues showed it in 2021, and episode 16.2 measured it: adding amplify to the list dropped loudness from ninety-four percent to sixty-six. Much of the progress of these recipes, from 2006 to 2019, was an effect of how they were compared. That is Musgrave, Belongie and Lim, in 2020. A distance learned from few examples learns the noise too: eighty-eight percent on its own examples, fifty on the next ones, in episode 16.1. And the flat maps of episode 16.4 keep only the neighbourhoods.

One parcel is still open on the bench. Large EEG models, trained in advance on many recordings: are they worth it? There is no clear consensus. A recent preprint by Jeon, Cho and Suh, from 2026, finds that the same network left at random does as well for half the models, on sleep and emotion tasks. And no model beat a simple baseline of power in five bands, on emotion or on imagined movement. Other benchmarks disagree. We read only its abstract.

Now the blank slips.

First slip. In hyperscanning, write the list before the training. What counts as the same? Two windows of the same person? Or the two partners at the same instant? Each choice tells the distance what to ignore. The first erases time. The second erases the person. And two partners recorded together also share a room, a session and its noise: write down what else the slip lets through.

A first work exists on the EEG of two people, by Ranjbaran and colleagues, in 2026. They trained on the EEG of single brains, with the shuffled order task of Banville, then tuned the model on pairs. Their test windows came from the same pairs as their training windows, so the authors give their score as an upper bound. We read the full text, and its correction, which fixes the spelling of a name. In fNIRS, the search made for this episode found nothing. That is a limited search, not a proof. See the Hyperscanning series.

Second slip. Train twice, and keep only what holds in both spaces.

Third slip. Compare the learned spaces of two partners through their tables of distances, read between a floor and a ceiling, as in chapter 12.

Fourth slip. Make the maps last. And check in the data everything you thought you read on a map.

Fifth slip. Redo the analysis with a simple baseline: power per band, and the straight line. Then say what the learned space added. That is the test of episode 15.9.

None of these slips has been tested. They are leads.

One question separates the parcels from the slips. What did your training call the same? And against which simple baseline was it compared?

For self-supervised models on brain data, see episode 11.8 of the World Models series, JEPA on brain data.

So here is what to keep. A learned distance is not discovered. It is ordered. What you declared the same, it will no longer see.
