Hitting the wave: phase-triggered stimulation

A TMS pulse can be timed to the phase of the ten-hertz mu rhythm, like pushing a swing at the right moment. Real-time systems must forecast the phase past a distorted filter edge and a machine delay, and the error, often tens of degrees, is measured with circular statistics. Studies disagree on whether the favourable phase changes the effect.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A TMS pulse always lands on a brain that is not quite the same as a moment ago. Some of that difference is the person. Some of it is the moment. This episode is about the moment. Can we choose the instant of an ongoing brain rhythm at which the pulse is sent, and does the choice matter?

Closed loop on the strength of a rhythm, as in adaptive deep brain stimulation, is the subject of episode 6.3. Here the signal is not a strength. It is a phase.

Picture a single swing and a single push. The swing has just reached the back of its arc and starts to move away from you. You push at that moment, and it climbs. Now push at another moment, while the swing is coming back toward you. Your hand meets it, and you only brake it. Same hand, same force. A different moment, a different result.

Keep this image for one thing only: timing. The swing is not a model of the brain. And in the brain, nobody tells us which moment is the good one. That had to be measured.

The rhythm in our story is the mu rhythm. It is an oscillation of about ten hertz, recorded over the sensorimotor cortex. The EEG voltage rises and falls. At every instant the rhythm stands somewhere in its cycle, and that position is its phase. We draw it as a turning arrow, one full turn per cycle. At ten hertz, one cycle lasts a hundred milliseconds.

In 2018, Christoph Zrenner, Ulf Ziemann and colleagues put this to the test in healthy volunteers. A real-time system watched the mu rhythm over the motor cortex. It fired the coil either at the negative peak of the scalp signal, or at the positive peak. Pulses at the negative peak gave larger motor evoked potentials. So the negative peak was a high excitability state, and the positive peak a low one.

Then the key experiment. The team repeated a protocol of two hundred bursts of three pulses, about one burst per second, always at the same peak. At the negative peak, it produced an LTP-like increase of corticospinal excitability. At the positive peak, nothing changed. Same coil, same pattern, a different moment.

In 2020, David Baur and colleagues did the mirror image with one pulse per second. Nine hundred pulses at the positive peak produced an LTD-like decrease. At the negative peak, there was only a trend toward an increase.

So the idea is attractive. But it rests on a hard technical step: you must know the phase at the instant you fire, and the brain does not wait.

The usual way to get a phase is to band-pass filter the signal around ten hertz, and read the phase from the filtered wave. But these filters look at both sides of every sample. At the newest sample, there is nothing on the right side yet. The output is distorted at the edge, and the edge is exactly where you need it. The method that works offline fails at the one moment that counts.

So real-time systems do something different. They filter a window of recent signal and cut off the distorted edge. Then they fit a model to the clean part, typically an autoregressive model, and run it forward to predict where the next peak will fall. In the 2020 study by Zrenner and colleagues, the optimized window was a median of about seven hundred samples, and the trimmed edge a median of sixty-five.

Then comes the delay. The computer takes a millisecond or two. The stimulator adds its own lag between the trigger and the pulse, and that lag has to be measured for each system. So the trigger must leave early, so that the pulse, not the trigger, lands on the peak. This is the swing again. You do not push where the swing is. You push where it will be when your arm arrives. At ten hertz, a delay of just twenty-five milliseconds is a quarter of a cycle: ninety degrees.

A forecast is only as good as the rhythm is clear. In that 2020 study, the team analysed resting EEG from one hundred and thirty-two participants. Even the offline reference estimates, built from the whole signal, disagreed with one another. The typical disagreement was seventeen and a half degrees when the signal-to-noise ratio was zero decibels, and six and a half degrees at twenty decibels. For the real-time estimator, measured against that reference, the median error was about twenty to fifty degrees when the rhythm was in its strongest quarter of amplitude. It was seventy to a hundred degrees in the weakest quarter.

How do you measure a phase error? On a real recording, there is no true phase to compare with. So there are two ways. In a simulation, you bury an artificial ten-hertz sine wave in realistic noise, and the true phase is known. On real data, you compare the real-time estimate with an offline benchmark that uses the whole signal.

Because a phase is an angle, errors are averaged as angles. Three hundred and fifty-nine degrees and one degree are two degrees apart, not three hundred and fifty-eight. The spread is reported as a circular standard deviation, in degrees.

Another way is an accuracy score. Shirinpour and colleagues, in 2020, defined accuracy as one minus the mean absolute error divided by a hundred and eighty degrees. One is perfect. A half is what random timing gives. In real-time recordings of the alpha rhythm, their best method scored seventy percent, an autoregressive method sixty-seven, and a Fourier method sixty-one. By that definition, seventy percent is about fifty-four degrees of mean absolute error.

Schaworonkow and colleagues, in 2018, reported the spread directly, for the mu rhythm. The standard deviation of the phase prediction error was forty-seven degrees with a local spatial filter, and forty-four with a filter tailored to each participant. Fifteen participants were analysed.

A spread of forty-five degrees changes how to read the word peak. When you aim for the negative peak, you hit a blurry sector around it, and sometimes far from it. A good report gives the angle actually reached, its circular spread, and how many trials were rejected.

Now, what do replications say? The results are debated. In 2019, Madsen and colleagues stimulated at four phases of the mu rhythm in fourteen volunteers not selected for a strong rhythm. They found no effect of phase. In 2022, Ozdemir and colleagues found that phase alone did not modulate the responses. The phase effect appeared only when the mu rhythm was strong, and it was reproducible across five visits. The same year, Wischnewski and colleagues, in twenty adults, did find phase-dependent responses, larger at the trough and on the rising phase.

In 2025, Hougland and colleagues applied eight hundred pulses to each of sixty participants. A phase effect was significant in only part of the group, and in a second experiment with ten participants, its test-retest reliability was low. Another 2025 study of thirty participants found no phase effect on motor evoked potentials, but a clear effect of the rhythm's power. So the phase effect may exist, but it is not universal, and it may depend on who is selected, on how strong the rhythm is, and on how well the phase is estimated.

Here is the classic trap. The negative peak is not one fixed thing. It is the negative peak of a particular signal, read through a particular spatial filter. In one participant of the 2018 study, moving the centre of the local filter to a neighbouring electrode shifted the measured phase by fifty-eight and a half degrees in one direction, and by twenty-nine and seven tenths degrees in the other. A target defined on the scalp is a target for that filter. And a trigger that fired is not a target that was hit. Report the error.

So, to sum up. A pulse can be sent at a chosen phase of an ongoing rhythm. To do it, you forecast the phase ahead, past an edge that filters distort and a delay the machine adds. You measure the error with circular statistics, and you report it. And whether hitting the right phase changes the effect is still debated.

PLATE 8.27:34

Neurofeedback meets stimulation

Two loops close around a brain: in neurofeedback the person learns to change their own signal, in closed-loop stimulation a machine reads the signal and acts, like a child watching a bubble level versus a motor under the seesaw. Combined protocols give small, conflicting results, and practice and expectation can mimic learning, so a control must show who learned.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two loops can close around a brain. In the first, a person watches their own brain signal and learns to change it. In the second, a machine reads the signal and acts on the brain. Both are called closed loop. But in a loop, who learns? The brain, the algorithm, or both? That is the question of this episode.

Picture a seesaw in the playground, with a bubble level on the plank. In the first loop, a child sits on the seesaw and watches the bubble. The job is to keep it in the middle. Nobody explains how. The child tries things, and the bubble shows what worked. That is neurofeedback. The bubble is the person's own brain signal, shown on a screen as a bar, a colour or a game.

In the second loop, nobody watches. Under the plank, a small motor reads the tilt and pushes at the right moment. The child does not need to learn anything. That is closed-loop stimulation. Adaptive deep brain stimulation, in episode 6.3, and phase-triggered TMS, in episode 8.1, are of this kind. Keep this picture for one thing only: who is doing the adjusting. The brain is not a seesaw.

Start with the machine acting on the brain's state. A clear example comes from sleep, and it uses sound, not current. In 2013, Ngo and colleagues detected the up-states of the slow oscillation in sleeping volunteers. They played auditory stimulation in phase with them. The slow oscillation grew, spindle activity locked to it, and memory for word pairs improved. Stimulation out of phase with the rhythm remained ineffective.

Later work has been less kind to the memory result. In 2023, Harlow and colleagues pooled twelve studies, with two hundred and six participants. The effect on memory shrank year after year. The predicted effect for 2013 was about one standard deviation in favour of stimulation. For 2021, it was minus zero point four, favouring no stimulation. The authors estimate that about a third of participants are not actually blind, because they hear the sounds. And they call for true placebo controls.

Now the second family: using stimulation to help the learning. The idea is simple. Apply tDCS or tACS before, or during, neurofeedback, and the person learns to regulate the signal faster. The studies found here are small, and they disagree.

In 2023, Sawai and colleagues gave twenty healthy young men a motor imagery task. The feedback was the mu rhythm over the right motor cortex, shown as a bar. Half also received two milliamps of tDCS for ten minutes beforehand. Their rhythm change during training was larger. But there was no sham tDCS group, and the authors call it a feasibility study.

In 2018, Mondini and colleagues ran a sham-controlled, within-subject study with twenty healthy volunteers. Real or sham tDCS, at zero point seven milliamps, was applied over the left motor cortex. Feedback came from the other hemisphere. Cathodal stimulation did not enhance the rhythm change. After anodal stimulation, it was weaker than after sham.

In 2022, Rego and colleagues compared four groups of twenty, in a single-blind trial of nine sessions. Active neurofeedback, sham neurofeedback, active tDCS and sham tDCS. None improved attention more than sham. Everyone got faster. And every group, sham included, rated its own attention as improved.

A preprint from 2026, not yet peer reviewed, tested alpha tACS before alpha neurofeedback in twenty-six people. No difference in reaction times, and no advantage of active feedback over sham. Some of these studies kept the current away from the feedback runs, or put the electrode far from the recording sites, because the current contaminates the EEG near the electrode. So a loop that stimulates and records at once is a technical challenge, not a detail.

Now the key moment. Who learns? In neurofeedback, the hope is that the brain learns. In 2018, Alkoby and colleagues reviewed the problem. Some studies report that a significant proportion of participants does not benefit, and many studies do not report the variation between people. In a 2016 meta-analysis of thirteen randomized trials, Cortese and colleagues found that only four had directly checked whether learning occurred.

In a closed-loop interface, the algorithm learns too. In 2014, Orsborn and colleagues adapted the decoder of a brain-controlled cursor in monkeys, while the animals learned. Brain plasticity and decoder adaptation happened together, with gains in performance. In 2018, Perdikis and colleagues trained two people for the Cybathlon, an international brain-computer interface race, with this mutual learning. In 2020, Perdikis and Millan argued that the field leans heavily toward the machine side. The logic is simple: if both learners change, a better score cannot say which one changed.

So which controls do we need? The same as for a sham. In 2020, the CRED-nf checklist, from Ros and colleagues, set out twenty-three items in seven sections. Using a control group or condition is an essential item. The options include sham feedback, feedback from an unrelated brain signal, and a reversed reward. Blinding is encouraged to be checked, as in episode 9.1: to what extent did participants and experimenters remain blind?

Sorger and colleagues reviewed the options in 2019. Feedback from another participant, or a made-up signal, matches everything except control over the signal. But if participants notice that the bubble does not follow them, frustration and lower motivation become confounds. Other options also have limits. In the seesaw picture: the bubble level of another child's seesaw, a bubble that moves the wrong way, or a bubble that shows something unrelated. Thibault and Raz argued, in 2017, that few experiments isolate the feedback from a specific brain signal. A reply in 2018 disputed that. The debate is open.

For the machine, the matching control is the same pushes, at the wrong moment. That is what out-of-phase stimulation did in sleep. The empty gift box of episode 9.1 comes back here: a sound is a rattle.

Here is the classic trap. Everyone improves. In the four-group trial, even the sham groups got faster, and rated their attention as better. Practice and expectation can look like learning. So report a control, report the learning curve, and test transfer runs without feedback, a point CRED-nf also raises.

To sum up. Neurofeedback asks the brain to learn. Closed-loop stimulation lets a machine act. The combined protocols stimulate by state, or use stimulation to help learning, and the small studies so far disagree. In any loop, ask who learns, and ask for the control that tells you.

Stimulating two brains

Hyperscanning records inter-brain synchrony but cannot show that it causes better coordination, like skipping-rope turners who may simply follow the same song. Hyper-tACS gives two people currents in phase or anti-phase to test this. Early studies were positive, negative and positive, and the limits are sensations, a planned phase that is not measured, and small samples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two swings hang side by side in a playground. Sometimes they move in step. Sometimes they do not. Hyperscanning asks the same question about two brains. It records two people at once, and it looks for moments when their brain rhythms rise and fall together. That is called inter-brain synchrony. The Hyperscanning series shows how it is measured.

Many studies report more synchrony when people cooperate, learn together or move together. But a recording cannot say why. This episode is about the kind of experiment that can: stimulating two brains at once.

Picture two children turning a long skipping rope, and a third child who jumps. The jump works when the two turners are in time. From the side, you notice that every good jump comes with two turners in time. Does the timing make the jump work? Maybe. But maybe a song is playing, and both children simply follow it. Then the timing is a side effect of hearing the same song, and it causes nothing. Keep this picture for one thing only: correlation versus cause. It is not a model of the brain.

To find out, you stop watching and you intervene. You give each turner a beat of their own. Sometimes the two beats are in step. Sometimes they are half a turn apart. Then you see whether the jumper still succeeds.

In 2021, Giacomo Novembre and Gian Domenico Iannetti put this argument in a title: Hyperscanning alone cannot prove causality. Multibrain stimulation can. Their point is that nearly all hyperscanning studies record two brains that receive similar input. Synchrony could be a by-product of that shared input. To test it, synchrony has to be changed from the outside, and its effect on behaviour measured.

With tACS, this takes two stimulators, one for each person. Episode 4.2 explains tACS itself. Both stimulators deliver an alternating current at the same frequency, and the experimenter chooses how the two currents line up. In phase means they rise and fall together. Anti-phase means they are half a cycle apart. This design is called hyper-tACS. The question is whether pairs coordinate better with in-phase current than with anti-phase current, or with no current at all.

The first study came from Novembre and colleagues, in 2017. Sixty adults, thirty pairs, tapped together with the right hand. Each person wore a stimulator over the left motor cortex. The current was one milliamp, peak to peak, at twenty hertz, in the beta range. In-phase stimulation made the first taps of each trial more synchronous than anti-phase stimulation, and more than sham. At two hertz and at ten hertz, there was no such effect. And when real partners were shuffled into artificial pairs, the effect vanished. So it belonged to the pair.

The same year, Szymanski and colleagues had thirty-eight pairs of women drum together over three weekly sessions. The current was one milliamp, at six hertz, over right frontal and parietal sites. They expected that the same current for both partners would help. The result went the other way. Both active conditions made the drumming less synchronous than sham, and the same-current condition was the worse one. Drumming alone, to a metronome, did not change. The authors could not analyse the EEG, so nobody knows whether the two brains moved into step.

In 2021, Yafeng Pan, Novembre and colleagues paired an instructor with a learner who was learning a song. There were twenty-four pairs, all women, from four instructors with six learners each. The current was six hertz over the left inferior frontal cortex. When the two currents were in phase, the pair moved their bodies more in step, and the learner's pitch accuracy was better than with sham. Anti-phase stimulation at six hertz did not do this. Neither did in-phase stimulation at ten hertz. A mediation analysis suggested that the movement synchrony carried part of the gain.

Since then, studies have been slower to come. In 2023, Lu and colleagues stimulated the right inferior frontal gyrus of one hundred and twenty-four adults, in a hyper-tACS group, a hyper-tDCS group and a sham group, while recording fNIRS. They reported that more inter-brain synchrony went with better coordination, and that its effect lasted longer. In 2025, Takeuchi and Terui gave alpha tACS over the somatosensory cortex of sixteen pairs, to test pain relief from a partner's touch. Pain did not differ between the three stimulation conditions. In 2025, Zheng and Lu used direct current over the right temporoparietal junction of eighty-one pairs in a cooperation game. Active dual stimulation raised synchrony and cooperation, compared with sham. As of November 2026, no independent replication of the twenty-hertz tapping result was found.

Now the limits. First, tACS can be felt. The skin and the retina respond. In Novembre's study, nine of sixty people saw flashes of light. Episode 4.2 covered a proposal by Asamoah and colleagues that, for the motor system, tACS effects can come through the nerves of the skin. In a two-person study, there is one more question. Do in-phase and anti-phase currents feel the same? In Pan's study, reported side effects were comparable across conditions. That has to be checked every time, and not assumed.

Second, the phase. The phase in these studies is the phase of the current at the stimulator. It is not measured in the brain. Each brain has its own rhythm, and it differs from person to person. So the result is an average effect of a planned phase relation, over many pairs. It does not show that two brains were in step.

Third, size. These studies had twenty-four to thirty-eight pairs, with several conditions each. Two of them were women only. Novembre's effect was limited to early taps, and the authors listed several competing explanations. Szymanski's result went against its own prediction. Commentaries on the 2021 argument added that stimulation removes the natural interaction that creates synchrony, and that stimulation is not the only way to test causality.

So what should a good two-brain experiment include? A sham condition. Out-of-phase or random-phase stimulation, so that the in-phase relation is compared with the same current, and not only with no current. A frequency control, as in the two and ten hertz conditions. A control where only one partner is stimulated. A check with shuffled pairs, as Novembre did. A blinding check: at the end, ask every participant, and every experimenter, which condition they believe it was, and report the answers. A recording of the brains, to see whether synchrony really changed. And pre-registration, with the sample size set in advance.

To sum up. Hyperscanning shows that two brains can move together. Stimulating both brains lets us ask whether that synchrony matters for coordination. Three early studies gave a positive result, a negative one, and a positive one on learning. The limits are sensations, a planned phase that is not a measured phase, and small samples. And a good control is more than a sham.
