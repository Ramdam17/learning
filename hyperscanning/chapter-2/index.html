PLATE 2.13:56

Four routes to synchrony

Two swings can move together for four reasons: the same wind, pumping to the same song, children anticipating each other, or a chain linking the seats. These are stimulus, shared rhythm and movement, mutual prediction, and the body. Four simulated dyads, one per route, give nearly identical synchrony values, so the design decides which routes remain.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two people talk, and their brain signals start to move together. It is one of the most exciting pictures in social neuroscience. Before we measure anything, this episode asks the question that runs through the whole series. Where does that synchrony come from?

Let's go to the park. Here are two swings, side by side. Sometimes they swing together. There are at least four very different reasons why that can happen.

Route one. The same wind pushes both swings. Nobody on the swings is doing anything special. They simply receive the same input, at the same moment. In the lab, the wind is the stimulus. A film, a sound, a task event that both people see or hear at once. Two brains that respond to the same event will look alike, even if the two people never notice each other.

Route two. The two children pump their legs to the same song. Now the swings move together because the bodies move together. In the lab, this is shared rhythm and shared movement. Clapping, tapping, speaking in turns, nodding. Motor and auditory cortex follow the rhythm in each person. And the movement also leaves artifacts in both recordings, from muscles, from the head, from the voice.

Route three. The children watch each other and anticipate. Each one predicts when the other will push, and adjusts. Here the synchrony comes from the interaction itself. Each brain carries a model of the other, and both models update all the time. This is the route most hyperscanning studies hope to find.

Route four. A chain links the two seats. Pull one, and the other follows. In people, the chain is the body. Heartbeat and breathing can fall into step during conversation, singing or shared effort. And in fNIRS especially, heartbeat and breathing are inside the signal we call brain activity. Two bodies in step can give two brain signals in step.

Now the uncomfortable part. Here are four simulated dyads, one for each route. Each one was built with a single ingredient switched on. Under each pair, the same synchrony measure, computed the same way. The four numbers are almost the same. These curves are simulated, so we know the truth. In a real study, we don't.

So a synchrony value, on its own, cannot tell you which route produced it. A high number opens the question. It does not answer it.

This is not a new worry. In a 2021 article in Neuron, Antonia Hamilton pointed out that many hyperscanning results could simply reflect two people responding in the same way to the same environment. Her suggestion was to bring behaviour and models of mutual prediction into the analysis, rather than reading synchrony alone.

To keep this in mind, the series uses a small compass. Four branches: stimulus, rhythm, prediction, body. Each time we show a result, the compass lights up. Yellow means the route is still possible. Grey with a cross means the design has ruled it out. Pink means this is the route the study is really after.

Let's try it on two quick examples. Two people watch the same video in separate rooms. Their signals correlate. Prediction is ruled out, because they cannot see each other. Stimulus stays wide open. Now two people tap together with no metronome, and we compare them with pairs who tap the same rhythm alone. Stimulus is now controlled. Rhythm still needs care. Prediction becomes a real candidate.

Notice what did the work. The equation stayed the same; the comparison got better. The design decides which routes remain. The analysis can only remove what the design lets it see.

In the next episodes we take each route in turn, then we build the tree that tells them apart. For now, one habit. When you see brain synchrony, ask where it comes from. Wind, song, anticipation, or chain.

PLATE 2.24:29

Route 1 · Same input, same response

Route one is the shared stimulus: two strangers in separate rooms watching the same film show correlated brain signals, called inter-subject correlation. In simulation, real pairs and pseudo-pairs recorded on different days both correlate near 0.69, and shifting one viewer by forty seconds drops it to zero. High inter-subject correlation is a baseline, not a social result.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two people sit in a dark room. They have never met. They watch the same film, each in a different room, and we record both brains. The two signals rise and fall together. Is that a social effect?

Back to the park. This is route one. The same wind pushes both swings. In the lab, the wind is the stimulus: a film, a sound, an event that both people see or hear. The shared input.

Tonight, the park has an open-air cinema. MAESTRO runs the projector. On the screen, one film. On two benches, far apart, two spectators who do not know each other. They never look at each other. They only look at the screen.

This is not just a thought experiment. In 2004, Hasson and colleagues published a study in Science. Five people freely watched half an hour of a popular movie while they were scanned with fMRI. The team used the brain activity of one person to model the activity of another. Voxel by voxel, the signals were synchronized between individuals. Not only in visual and auditory areas, but in association cortex too. The brains, they wrote, tick collectively.

This kind of analysis has a name: inter-subject correlation, or ISC. Take the signal of one viewer. Take the signal of another viewer. Correlate them, moment by moment of the film. Nobody interacts. Everything they share comes from the stimulus. So ISC is the limit case of route one.

Let's simulate it. Each viewer's signal is the film's time course, scaled, plus the viewer's own noise. Nobody interacts. Here are two viewers in separate rooms. The film is in yellow. Their signals correlate at 0.72. Over ten pairs of viewers, the mean correlation is 0.69.

Now a twist. Take viewer A, recorded on one day. Take a viewer B, recorded on another day. They never watched at the same time. They are a pseudo-pair. Aligned to the film, their correlation is 0.68. Over ninety pseudo-pairs, the mean is 0.69 again. Real pairs and pseudo-pairs look the same. The synchrony does not need the two people to be together. It needs the same film.

And the film is the key. Shift one viewer by forty seconds, and the correlation falls to minus 0.04. The two signals were never linked to each other. Each one was linked to the film.

It is not only fMRI. In 2012, Dmochowski and colleagues recorded EEG during short film clips, and extracted components that were maximally correlated across viewers. The correlation peaked at arousing moments of the film. It dropped when people saw the film a second time, and when the scenes were scrambled in time. The same logic works with EEG.

Now the point for hyperscanning. In a conversation, each person is the other's stimulus. What I say, you hear. What you do, I see. In 2012, Hasson and colleagues argued in Trends in Cognitive Sciences that one brain can be coupled to another through a signal that travels through the environment. So part of the synchrony between two interacting people is simply a shared response to what they see and hear. That part is route one. It is real. But it is not mutual prediction.

So we must be able to put a number on it. Here are three ideas, named now and built later. First, compute the same measure for pseudo-pairs who never met, as we just did. Second, model the stimulus, and look at what is left once its locked part is removed. Third, compare conditions where the stimulus is the same but the interaction is different. Pseudo-pairs come back in chapter seven. For ISC itself, Nastase and colleagues wrote a tutorial in 2019, in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, on its logic and its best practices.

On the compass, route one lights up. Two strangers in separate rooms cannot see each other, so prediction is ruled out. The stimulus stays wide open.

One habit to keep. A high inter-subject correlation is not a social result. It is a baseline. Before you read synchrony as an interaction, ask how much two strangers would share, just by watching the same film.

Next time, route two: moving and speaking together.

PLATE 2.33:39

Route 2 · Moving and speaking together

Route two is shared rhythm and movement: two children clapping to one song have brains that follow the beat, with no link between them. In simulation, a beat alone lifts the phase locking value from 0.11 to 0.57, and clapping artifacts in the sensors push it to 0.68. Synchrony may be brains following a beat or sensors recording the same movement.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the park. The two children have left the swings. They stand side by side, and they clap. A song plays, and both of them clap to it.

This is route two of our compass: rhythm and movement. The two brains are not talking to each other. They are following the same beat.

Start with one child. When a beat plays, the brain follows it. In 2011, Nozaradan and colleagues recorded the EEG of eight volunteers listening to a beat. They published it in The Journal of Neuroscience. The EEG showed a steady response at exactly the frequency of the beat, two point four hertz. In our drawing, each brain has two drums, one for hearing the beat and one for moving to it. Both drums light up on every beat.

Now put two children side by side, clapping to the same song. Each brain follows the beat, so the two signals rise and fall at the same rate. Nobody looked at anybody. This synchrony is real. It is in the brain. But it comes from the shared song, not from a link between the two brains.

This is why music studies need care. In 2009, Lindenberger and colleagues recorded the EEG of eight pairs of guitarists playing a melody together. Synchronization between the two brains increased when the players set the tempo and began to play. In their discussion, the authors wrote that stimulus-related and movement-related processes may have contributed.

In 2012, Sänger and colleagues recorded twelve guitar duets. Between-brain connections were stronger when the music demanded more coordination. The authors still noted that the similarities in the motor performance and in what the two players hear remain substantial.

And in 2013, Burgess showed something related. When a task changes the rhythm of the EEG in everyone in the same way, the usual phase measures find synchrony even between people who never met.

Let's see it with numbers. Everything here is simulated. Two people clap at two hertz. There is no coupling between their brains at all. We simulate one hundred pairs, sixty seconds each.

First, brain noise only. The phase locking value is about zero point one one. Now let each brain follow the shared beat, a little. The value rises to zero point five seven. No link between the brains, and the value is still high. That is route two at work.

Now the border. Clapping is a movement. Every clap shakes the head a little, and tightens the muscles. Each recording picks up a bump and a burst of muscle activity, timed by that person's own clap. The two people clap to the same tempo, so the two artifacts line up too.

Add them, and the value rises again, to zero point six eight. It also varies far more from pair to pair. Not a single neuron has changed. It is the same movement, and it gives two kinds of synchrony. One comes from the brain following the beat. The other comes from the body leaking into the sensor.

We will come back to this. Episode five point two looks at the artifacts of speech, and five point three at the artifacts of movement.

On the compass, route two lights up. But the song is also a shared input, so route one is still possible. Telling them apart is a job for the design, and episode two point six builds that tree.

For now, one habit. When the task is rhythmic, ask whether the synchrony is brains following a beat, or sensors recording the same movement.

Next time, route three: predicting each other.

PLATE 2.44:25

Route 3 · Predicting each other

Route three is mutual prediction: like two children playing catch, each brain carries a model of the other and updates it constantly. Only this route predicts a lag that follows who leads, synchrony that grows with predictability, and synchrony that vanishes when the loop is cut. In simulation, cutting the loop drops the phase locking value from 0.79 to 0.16.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Route three. Two children are playing catch in the park. Watch what happens before the ball arrives. The catcher's hands are already moving. She did not wait for the ball. She predicted where it would go.

That prediction rests on a model of the other child. How he throws. How tired he is. How hard he tries. And the thrower carries a model of the catcher too. Each model is updated after every throw. When the ball lands somewhere unexpected, the error teaches the model something new. Two models, each of the other, updating all the time.

Two frameworks turn this picture into theory. The first is coupled dynamics. In 2016, Uri Hasson and Chris Frith described social interaction as two systems whose behaviour and brain activity are coupled, not just mirrored. The second is predictive coding. In 2015, Karl Friston and Chris Frith simulated two birds singing to each other. When both birds share the same model of the song, their states fall into synchrony. The paper is called A duet for one. You do not need the mathematics. You need what both frameworks predict.

This route is special. It is the only one of the four that needs two people who really respond to each other. So it makes predictions that the other routes do not.

First prediction. A time lag. When the same stimulus drives two people, both respond with the same delay. Their signals line up at zero lag. When one person leads and the other follows, the follower's signal is a delayed copy of the leader's. Here are simulated dyads. For the shared stimulus, the peak sits at zero. For the leader and follower, the peak sits at four tenths of a second. When the partners swap roles, the peak flips to minus four tenths. The lag tells you who leads.

In real data, Stephens, Silbert and Hasson scanned a speaker telling a story, and listeners hearing it. This was in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, in 2010. On average, the listener's brain trails the speaker's. But some regions responded ahead of the speaker. And the more of this anticipatory coupling a listener showed, the better the listener understood the story. A response cannot come before its cause. An anticipation can. Still, a lag alone is not proof. A person who only reacts also lags.

Second prediction. The synchrony should grow with how well each person can predict the other. In 2014, in the Journal of Neuroscience, Dikker and colleagues played listeners recordings of sentences that were more or less predictable from their context. When the context made the sentence predictable, the listener's brain activity was more synchronous with the speaker's, in the left posterior superior temporal gyrus.

Third prediction. Break the loop, and the synchrony should disappear. In 2012, in the Journal of Neuroscience, Jing Jiang and colleagues recorded pairs with fNIRS. Pairs held a dialogue face to face, told monologues, or talked back to back. Synchrony in the left inferior frontal cortex rose only during face to face dialogue. Not in the monologue. Not when the partners turned their backs.

Here is the same idea in simulation. Two oscillators, with the same tempo and the same noise. In one set of dyads, each partner is pulled toward the other. In the other set, the pull is cut. Over one hundred simulated dyads, the mean PLV is zero point seven nine with the loop intact. With the loop cut, it is zero point one six. Same noise, same tempo. One change. The loop.

Does this prove route three? No. A shared stimulus or a shared rhythm can mimic one of these signatures, so a good design tests all three. A lag that follows the roles. A synchrony that grows with predictability. A synchrony that vanishes when the loop is cut.

On our compass, this is the pink branch, prediction. It stays possible only if the design allows a real two way loop. It earns the pink only if the signatures appear.

So here is the habit for this route. When you see brain synchrony, ask one question. If one person stopped responding to the other, would it survive?

Next time, route four. Bodies coupling bodies.

PLATE 2.54:13

Route 4 · Bodies coupling bodies

Route four is body coupling: heartbeat and breathing fall into step during singing or shared effort, and those signals also sit inside EEG and fNIRS recordings. In simulation, children whose brains never interact reach an inter-brain phase locking value of 0.48 when singing, because about a quarter of each channel is breathing and heartbeat. Part of what looks like brain synchrony can be body synchrony.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the park. One branch of the compass is left: the chain that links the two swing seats. This is route four, body coupling. Two bodies whose own rhythms fall into step.

Every child carries two clocks. The heart beats about once a second. The breath comes every few seconds. In our drawings, Cardio is the heart, and Puff is the breath. Alone, each child keeps a pace of her own, or his own. The two breaths drift apart, and so do the two heart rates.

Now the children sing together. Watch the chain. Each link stands for twenty seconds of the recording. While the children are alone, the links hang loose. When the song starts, the two breaths begin to pull on each other. The rhythms line up. The links tighten, and they glow yellow.

This is not only a drawing. In 2011, Viktor Müller and Ulman Lindenberger recorded eleven singers and one conductor. In PLOS ONE, they reported that phase synchronization of breathing, and of heart rate variability, increased during singing, compared with rest. It was higher when the choir sang in unison than when it sang in several voices.

Two years later, Vickhoff and colleagues, in Frontiers in Psychology, found that unison singing of regular song structures makes the hearts of the singers accelerate and decelerate at the same time. Singing is guided breathing, and breathing moves the heart.

It is not limited to singing. In a fire-walking ritual in Spain, Ivana Konvalinka and colleagues followed the heart rates of thirty-eight people. Their paper, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2011, showed shared arousal between the fire-walkers and their related spectators. Unrelated spectators did not share it. The authors note that the two groups behaved very differently, so the link may be informational, not just bodies moving alike.

And in 2017, a systematic review by Palumbo and colleagues found physiological synchrony in new and in established relationships, across a range of contexts. The same review called the literature fragmented.

Here is a simulation, built with one rule. Each child breathes at her or his own rate. Heart rate rises and falls with each breath, as it does in real life. When the children sing, each breath pulls on the other. Alone, the phase locking value of their breathing is zero point zero nine. Singing, it is zero point nine nine. The heart rate swings follow, from zero point one one to zero point nine nine. Everything here is simulated.

Now put a cap on each child. The brain channel records the brain, plus a little of the body. Here, about a quarter of each channel is breathing and heartbeat. The two brains do not talk to each other. Their own neural parts are independent noise. And yet the inter-brain phase locking value goes from zero point one two when they are alone, to zero point four eight when they sing.

Across forty simulated dyads, the average goes from zero point one three to zero point four six. We compare with pseudo-pairs, each child matched with a child from another dyad. Their ninety-fifth percentile is zero point four two. Twenty-eight of the forty dyads sit above it. That is brain synchrony, with no neural coupling at all.

In real recordings, this is not a toy worry. In 2016, Ilias Tachtsidis and Felix Scholkmann pointed out that fNIRS can record responses that are not due to neurovascular coupling, and that these can be misread as brain activity. They summarize where such responses come from, and how to avoid and remove them.

So when two bodies fall into step, two brain channels can fall into step with them. Part of what we call brain synchrony can be body synchrony. Chapter ten comes back to this, with the bodies in sync, and with brain and body together.

On the compass, route four lights up. But be careful. A song also gives both children a shared rhythm, so route two is in the picture as well. In life, the routes overlap. In the next episode, we learn to tell them apart.

PLATE 2.64:13

Telling the routes apart

No single analysis separates the four routes; the study design does, as a chalk decision tree with four forks. Pseudo-pairs who never met test whether synchrony needs the real partner, then removing evoked responses, recorded movement and recorded breathing and heartbeat test the other routes. In simulation, only mutual prediction survives all four forks, which makes it a candidate, not proof.

TRANSCRIPTEN

You found synchrony between two brains. The number is clearly above zero. Now comes the hard part. Which of the four routes made it?

Back to the park. This time we are not looking at the swings. We are looking at the ground, where somebody has started to draw a tree in chalk. It is a decision tree. Each fork asks one question, and each answer rules a route in or out.

First, the lesson of the whole chapter. No single analysis can tell the four routes apart. The tool that does it is the design of the study: what you record, and what you compare, before the first participant walks in. The analysis can only use what the design gives it.

Here is the proof, in simulation. We built four kinds of dyads, forty of each, and switched on one ingredient per kind. A shared stimulus. A shared rhythm. Mutual prediction, built as a two-way influence. A shared body signal. The same measure, a plain correlation, gives r equals zero point four five, zero point three seven, zero point four nine, and zero point four five. These data are simulated. You could not tell the four kinds apart from these numbers.

The first fork of the tree. Does the synchrony survive in people who never interacted? Take person A from one dyad and person B from another dyad who did the same task. These are pseudo-pairs. They cannot predict each other, because they never met. If the synchrony stays, it came from something both of them shared in the design.

Look at the simulation. In pseudo-pairs, the stimulus kind still gives zero point four five. The rhythm kind gives zero point three seven. The body kind gives zero point four five. But the mutual prediction kind falls to zero. Prediction is the only route that needs the real partner. Burgess pointed out in 2013 that pseudo-pairs can even show spurious connections, when people in the same condition share features of their EEG. That is why this check belongs in the design, and why it is only a first fork.

The second fork. Is the synchrony just the response to events? Average each person's response to the repeated events, and subtract it. In the simulation, the stimulus kind drops from zero point four five to zero. Nothing else moves much. This fork needs repeated events with known timing. So the design must have them.

The third fork. Is the synchrony carried by shared movement or speech? Record the behaviour, and remove it. Tapping, speaking, head movement. In the simulation, once the rhythm is regressed out, the rhythm kind falls to zero. Here the design must record the behaviour, because you cannot remove what you did not measure. This is the point Hamilton made in 2021: bring behaviour into the analysis, along with models of how people predict each other.

The fourth fork. Is the synchrony carried by the body? Record heartbeat and breathing, and regress them out. In the simulation, the body kind falls to zero. Again the design has to record it.

Now run the four forks together. After removing the evoked response, the rhythm, and the breathing, the three other kinds all end at zero. The mutual prediction kind keeps zero point four nine. What remains is the route we were after.

Two honest limits. In the simulation we knew each reference exactly. In a real study, a reference is noisy, so a removal is never perfect. And what survives is not proof of prediction. It is a candidate. To show that synchrony itself does something, Novembre and Iannetti argued in 2021, recording is not enough. You have to manipulate it.

So, before recording, three questions. What do the two people share besides each other? Did I record it? And what comparison would remove the interaction, and keep everything else? We will come back to them when we design a whole study.
