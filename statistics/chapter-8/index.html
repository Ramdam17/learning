PLATE 8.16:48

Counts against a model: goodness of fit

Chapter eight turns to counts in boxes. Pearson's chi-square squares each gap from the expected count, divides by that count, and adds them up, then reads the total in its curtain of chance. A die thrown 120 times gives 8.2 and p of 0.146. It must be computed on counts, never percentages, and needs about five expected per box.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the polling station. Ballots fall into boxes, and nobody measures them. They are counted. Chapter seven mapped the tests for measures. Chapter eight is about counts in boxes. A model is a statement of what to expect: for a fair die, one sixth of the throws on each face. Today's question: how far from a model is too far?

Here is a simulated die, thrown 120 times. If it is fair, each face comes up 20 times. It gave 16, 16, 29, 15, 19 and 25. Guess before you see: does it look loaded? The gaps from 20 are minus 4, minus 4, plus 9, minus 5, minus 1 and plus 5. They add up to zero.

In 1900, Karl Pearson turned the gaps into one number, in three small moves. Square each gap, because the gaps add to zero and would cancel. Divide each square by the count we expected. Then add everything up. We get 0.80, 0.80, 4.05, 1.25, 0.05 and 1.25. The total is 8.2. This is the chi-square.

Why divide? A gap of 5 is not the same event everywhere. Throw a die 120 times, and a face typically strays about 4 from its 20. Throw it 3,000 times, and a face expected 500 times strays about 20. So a gap of 5 is surprising at 20, and nothing at 500. Divided by the expected count, it adds 1.25 in the first case, and 0.05 in the second.

Is 8.2 big? Ask NIL, whose die is fair. We throw it 120 times, compute the chi-square, and drop it as a tile. We do this 10,000 times. The pile leans left and stretches right, centred on 5.0. Guess before you see: how many of the 10,000 land at least as far out as our flag? About 150, 1,500 or 5,000? It is 1,459. Only the right side counts, since a large chi-square means a poor fit. The p-value is 0.146. Lumpy, but ordinary.

Now the formula. Over the pile, the chi-square curve with 5 degrees of freedom. Why 5, with six faces? The six counts must add up to 120. Once five are known, the sixth is fixed, so only five are free. With 4 or 6, the curve misses the pile. With 5 it matches, and it gives a p-value of 0.146 too.

Now the moment that matters. The chi-square is computed on counts, never on percentages. Same proportions, but 1,200 throws instead of 120: every count is multiplied by ten. On counts, the chi-square goes from 8.2 to 82.0. Fed percentages, both give 6.83. Percentages cannot know how many throws stand behind them.

We simulate 10,000 fair dice at each size, with the usual line at 11.07. On percentages, a fair die rings 2.2 percent of the time at 120 throws, and never at 1,200. Now load the die: the six comes up one time in four. At 1,200 throws, the chi-square on counts rings in all 10,000 studies. On percentages, 0.1 percent. Averages fail the same way: a count is a number of units, and a mean is not. So a paper should always report its counts.

Four more settings, the same five steps: model, expected counts, gaps, chi-square, curtain.

One. Mendel reported in 1866 on peas, round or wrinkled, yellow or green, with a model of nine to three to three to one. Of 556 seeds, he counted 315, 108, 101 and 32. The model expects 312.75, 104.25, 104.25 and 34.75. The chi-square is 0.470, with 3 degrees of freedom: four boxes, minus one. Of 10,000 simulated studies, 9,281 go at least as far, so p is 0.928. A small chi-square is no alarm, and a good fit does not prove the model.

Two. Blood types, against a reference population: 46 percent O, 42 percent A, 9 percent B and 3 percent AB. A sample of 200 donors, illustrative: 70, 98, 23 and 9. We expect 92, 84, 18 and 6. The chi-square is 10.48, with 3 degrees of freedom. Of 10,000 simulated studies, 165 go as far: p is 0.0165. This sample does not look like the reference.

Three. Births by month, 600 of them, illustrative. If every day is equally likely, each month expects a share by its days: 51.0 in January, 46.0 in February. August looks busy, with 65. The chi-square is 12.87, with 11 degrees of freedom, twelve boxes minus one. The curtain is centred on 10.9, and p is 0.297. Ordinary.

Four. Vehicles at a junction, 700 in a week, illustrative. The model: every day alike, 100 each. We counted 124, 115, 97, 93, 109, 77 and 85. The chi-square is 16.94, with 6 degrees of freedom, and p is 0.0094. It says the model fits poorly, not where. Read its terms: Monday gives 5.76, Saturday 5.29.

A caution, not a lesson. The curve is a smooth approximation, and it needs enough expected counts. Throw the die only 12 times, 2 expected per face. The 10,000 simulated studies give just 23 different values, and the curve's 5 percent line is crossed 3.3 percent of the time. A common rule of thumb asks for about five expected in each box.

Remember: the chi-square measures the distance between a table of counts and what a model predicts. It is computed on counts, never on percentages, and read in its curtain of chance. Next time: two variables at once. Are they linked?

PLATE 8.27:11

Are two variables linked? The test of independence

To ask whether two answers on the same slip are linked, build the expected table that independence would give from the row and column totals, then compute the chi-square on its gaps. The test says whether a gap is surprising; risk difference, risk ratio and odds ratio say whether it is large. Count each person once: counting visits rings falsely 42.1 percent of the time.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the polling station. Each voter fills in one slip with two boxes. Last time we held counts against a model that someone handed us. Today there is no model. There are two answers on the same slip, and the question is: are they linked?

Here is a simulated clinic, 200 patients, illustrative. 120 got the new treatment, and 80 got the control. 100 improved, and 100 did not. Of the 120 treated, 72 improved. Of the 80 controls, 28 did.

Draw the table as a window. Each column is as wide as its group. Each pane is as tall as its share who improved. If treatment and improvement were unrelated, every group would improve at the same rate: half. The crossbars would line up, straight across.

So what would the table be? Half of the 120 treated is 60. Half of the 80 controls is 40. In general: row total, times column total, divided by the grand total. Why? This is the product rule for independent events. 0.60 of patients are treated, 0.50 improve, so 0.30 are both. Of 200, that is 60. The table of 60, 60, 40 and 40 is what independence looks like, with these margins. It is not a forecast. It is built from the margins alone, and it asks nothing of the cells.

Now last time's idea. Each gap from the expected table is 12: plus 12 in two cells, minus 12 in the other two, because the margins force the gaps to cancel. Square it, divide by the expected count, and add the four cells. We get 2.4, 2.4, 3.6 and 3.6. The chi-square is 12.0.

Is that surprising? Ask Nil, whose world has no link. We keep the 200 patients, and we keep the margins: 120 treated, 100 improved. We shuffle the improved and not-improved labels among the patients, rebuild the table, and compute its chi-square. We do this 10,000 times. Guess before you see: how many shuffles go as far as 12.0? About 1, 10 or 300? It is 9. So p is 0.0009. The curve with 1 degree of freedom gives 0.0005. Both say: very surprising. Notice that the pile is lumpy. The margins fix so much that shuffling gives only 15 different values, and its centre is 1.0, as the curve says.

Why 1 degree of freedom? With the margins fixed, once one cell is known, the other three follow. In general, the degrees of freedom are rows minus one, times columns minus one. Fisher showed this in 1922. Pearson had counted too many.

But surprising is not large. Here are three ways to say how large, from the same table. First, the risk difference. 60 percent of the treated improved, and 35 percent of the controls. The difference is 25 percentage points: 25 more patients in every 100 improve.

Second, the risk ratio. 60 over 35: the treated are 1.71 times as likely to improve. A ratio says how many times more, whatever the starting rate.

Third, the odds ratio. The odds are improved to not improved. 72 to 48 is 1.50. 28 to 52 is 0.54. The ratio is 2.79. That is harder to feel, but it is the one that regressions will give us later. Odds are not risks. When the outcome is rare, they agree: 2 in 100 against 1 in 100 gives a risk ratio of 2.00, and an odds ratio of 2.02. When it is common, the odds ratio overshoots.

Now surprise against size. Guess before you see: can a result be very surprising and still small? A web test, illustrative: 100,000 visitors on each version. 51.0 percent click on one, 50.0 percent on the other. The chi-square is 20.0, and p is below 1 in 100,000. Very surprising. But the difference is 1 point, and the risk ratio is 1.02.

Now a pilot trial, illustrative: 10 patients per arm. 7 improve with the treatment, against 3. The difference is 40 points, the risk ratio is 2.33, and the odds ratio is 5.44. The chi-square is 3.2, and p is 0.074. Not surprising. Too few people to tell. Shuffling gives 0.18, a reminder that the curve is rough with so few. That is for later. The test says whether the gap is surprising. The ratios say whether it is large. Report both.

One rule above all: each person counts once. A clinic has 100 patients, and each came between 1 and 8 times. Their treatment and their outcome are coin flips, unrelated, simulated. Count the patients, and the chi-square rings the alarm in 5.6 percent of 10,000 studies. Close to 5. Count the visits, and it rings in 42.1 percent. In one study, 100 patients made 431 visits. By patients, p is 0.35. By visits, 0.0006. A person who comes eight times lands eight times in the same cell, and the table believes it has eight people. A person who comes eight times is not eight people.

Two more tables, larger than two by two. Nests of birds, in three habitats, 240 nests, illustrative. Lost: 20 of 100 in forest, 28 of 80 in meadow, 27 of 60 in wetland. That is 20, 35 and 45 percent. We expect 31.25, 25 and 18.75 lost. The chi-square is 11.69, with 2 degrees of freedom: rows minus one is 1, columns minus one is 2. Shuffling gives p of 0.0032. Nests are lost more in the wetland.

Then commuting: car, bike or transit, in three age groups, 300 people, illustrative. Rows minus one is 2, columns minus one is 2, so 4 degrees of freedom. The chi-square is 1.58, and p is 0.82. Ordinary. Older commuters bike a little less, 17.5 percent against 25 for the young, but that is the size of gap that chance makes on its own. This table shows no sign of a link.

Remember: the expected table is what independence looks like with these margins. The test says whether the gap is surprising, and the ratios say whether it is large. Count each person once. Next time: what to do when the counts are small.

PLATE 8.37:06

Small counts: Fisher's exact test

With small counts the chi-square curve is too eager. Fisher's exact test keeps the margins fixed, lists every possible table, and ranks yours, like the tea cups. For ten patients per drug with four side effects, the curve said 0.025 while the exact count says 0.087. It is a permutation test you need not simulate, and it is conservative.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the polling station. Last time, with only a few people, the chi-square curve looked rough. Today we ask what to do when the counts are small.

A simulated trial, illustrative. A rare side effect. Ten patients take a new drug, and ten take the old one. Four patients in all have the side effect, and all four took the new drug.

Read the table as we did last time. We expect 2 side effects in each group, and 8 without. The chi-square is 5.0, and the curve with 1 degree of freedom says p is 0.025. That looks convincing.

But two expected patients is below the usual rule of thumb, about 5 in each box. That rule is a convention, so let us not lean on it. Let us ask the curtain.

We keep the 20 patients and the margins, and shuffle the labels new and old. Rebuild the table, compute the chi-square, and do it 10,000 times. Guess before you see: how many shuffles go as far as 5.0? The curve predicts about 250.

It is 845. So p is 0.0845, more than three times the curve's answer. And the pile has only three lumps, at 0, 1.25 and 5. The curve is smooth, and the table is not.

So let us stop simulating and count. We keep the margins: 10 and 10 patients, 4 side effects. Only one number is free: how many of the four side effects are in the new group. It can be 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4. Five tables, and that is all of them.

Count the ways, as with the tea cups. Which 4 of the 20 patients have the side effect? There are 4,845 ways to choose them. All four in the new group: 210 ways. Three in the new group and one in the old: 120 times 10, which is 1,200. Two and two: 45 times 45, which is 2,025. By symmetry, 1,200 and 210 for the others. They add up to 4,845.

Now rank. Our table is one of the two rarest. The one-sided p adds the tables as extreme as ours, in our direction. That is ours alone: 210 out of 4,845, so 0.043. The two-sided p adds the other end too: 420 out of 4,845, so 0.087. The simulation said 0.0845, the same up to the luck of one run. This is the rule of R and of SciPy: add every table no more likely than ours.

This is Fisher's exact test. Exact, because there is no curve: only counting. The curve said 0.025, and the count says 0.087. The curve was too eager.

Now the strong point. Shuffling the labels, with the margins fixed, picks one of those 4,845 arrangements, each as likely as any other. So the shuffles must land on the five tables with exactly these shares. Here are our 10,000 shuffles beside the exact shares: 4.4 against 4.3 percent, 25.3 against 24.8, 41.2 against 41.8. The exact test is a permutation test. It redistributes the labels, keeps the margins, and lists every redistribution instead of sampling some. Chapter 14 is built on this idea, and this is its first appearance.

Last time's pilot trial: 7 of 10 improved, against 3 of 10. The curve said 0.074, and shuffling said 0.18. That 0.18 was this test, by simulation: the exact p is 0.179. The curve was too eager again.

And the tea cups of 6.1. She named four cups of eight, and her table is 4, 0, 0, 4. There are 70 ways to choose, and one is perfect. Sixteen share exactly three cups, so three or more is 17 in 70, as before. The perfect score alone is 1 in 70, 1.4 percent: a one-sided question. If doing as badly would also have counted, we add the mirror table, all four wrong: 2 in 70, 2.9 percent. Same table, two questions. The curve says 0.005.

Is the curve always too eager? Let us measure. Studies with no real effect: both groups have a 20 percent chance of the side effect, 10 patients per group. We run 20,000 studies, and count how often each test rings the alarm at 0.05. The promise is 5 percent.

The curve rings in 2.8 percent. Fisher's exact test, in 0.7. So here the curve is not too eager overall: it is too cautious. Now 25 patients per group, with a 10 percent chance. The curve rings in 5.1 percent, the exact test in 0.8. The exact test never goes above 5, because it adds exact chances. But the tables are few, so it often stops far short. A test that rings less than it promises is conservative.

In 1934, Yates proposed shrinking each gap by one half before squaring. With this correction the curve rings in 0.7 percent, then 0.5.

Now a larger table: 100 patients per group, a 30 percent chance, no effect. The curve rings in 5.1 percent, and the exact test in 3.5. Closer, and still cautious. On last time's clinic of 200 patients, the curve gave 0.0005, and the exact test gives 0.0008. Both say: very surprising. With many people, the curve and the exact test tell the same story.

Two more tables, illustrative. A rare disease in two villages: 6 cases among 25 people in village A, 4 among 75 in village B. The curve says 0.007. The exact one-sided p is 0.014, and so is the two-sided: no table at the other end is as rare as ours. So the two-sided p is not always double.

A classroom. Six students study with music, and eight in silence. Five of the music group pass, against three of the others. The one-sided p is 0.12. Doubling would give 0.24, but the software rule gives 0.14. The curve says 0.086.

Remember: when counts are small, do not trust the curve. List every table with the same margins, and rank yours. That is Fisher's exact test, and it is a shuffle you do not need to simulate.

PLATE 8.47:14

Paired yes/no: McNemar

When the same people answer yes or no twice, only those who changed carry information. McNemar's test treats the people who moved as coin flips: 17 of 22 changers going one way gives an exact p of 0.0169. The tempting before-against-after table counts everyone twice and is timid. Much churn with no net move rightly shows nothing.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at the polling station, where the same voters are asked the same question twice. In 7.3 we learned that pairing changes the unit. Today the answers are yes or no, and the question is what to count.

A simulated study, illustrative. A hundred people are asked whether they support a measure, before a debate and after it. Before, 35 say yes. After, 47 say yes.

The tempting table has two rows, before and after, and two columns, yes and no. Each row holds 100 answers. Run last time's chi-square of independence on it, and p is 0.084. Nothing to see. But this table counts every person twice, as if 200 different people had answered.

Let us redraw it with people as the unit. Each person has a pair of answers: yes then yes, yes then no, no then yes, or no then no. 30 people said yes twice, and 48 said no twice. 5 went from yes to no. Call that cell b. And 17 went from no to yes. Call that cell c.

Guess before you see: who carries the information about a change? The people who stayed put, or the people who moved? Only the 22 who moved. If the debate did nothing, a person who says yes twice would say yes twice anyway. Those cells are the same under no change, whatever happens. To see it, multiply both unmoved cells by ten, and keep the same 5 and 17. The wrong table's p climbs from 0.084 to 0.54, only because we added people who did not change. The right answer does not move at all.

So the question is a coin flip. If the debate did nothing, each of the 22 changers is as likely to go one way as the other. How often would 17 or more of 22 coins land on yes? Count, as in chapter 6. 17 or more has a chance of 0.0085. Five or fewer is as extreme the other way, and adds the same, so p is 0.0169. This is the exact McNemar test, from 1947: a binomial test of b out of b plus c.

Now the formula in most books. Take b minus c, which is minus 12, and square it: 144. Divide by b plus c, which is 22. We get 6.55, and the curve with 1 degree of freedom gives p of 0.0105. The exact test said 0.0169. They differ because the curve is smooth and 22 coins are not. A continuity correction shrinks the gap by one before squaring: 11 squared over 22 is 5.5, and p is 0.019, closer to the exact one. Which one does your software run? R applies the correction by default, so it says 0.019. The Python library statsmodels runs the exact test by default, so it says 0.0169.

Now the shuffle view, as in chapter 14. If nothing changed, each changer's direction is a coin flip. So NIL flips the direction of each of the 22 changers, and we recompute c minus b. We do this 10,000 times. The pile is centred on zero, with a spread of 4.7. 161 of the 10,000 flips go as far out as our 12, so p is 0.0161, the exact answer up to the luck of one run. One more table is tempting: independence on the people table, before against after. It gives a chi-square of 32.4, and p below one in a million. Of course: people who said yes before mostly say yes after. That asks whether answers are stable, not whether they changed.

Now the mistake, measured. 10,000 simulated studies of 100 people, and the debate does nothing. Opinions are stable, so before and after agree, with a correlation of 0.50. The promise is 5 percent. The wrong table rings in 0.6 percent. McNemar's chi-square form rings in 5.8, and the exact test in 3.0. So the wrong test is timid, as in 7.3 when we forgot the pairing. With only 40 people, the chi-square form rings 4.4 percent and the exact test 2.2: the exact test is cautious when few people change, as Fisher's was.

Now a real change: after the debate, the yes share is 50 percent instead of 40. The wrong table detects it in 23.3 percent of the studies, McNemar's chi-square form in 52.3, and the exact test in 43.0. Two cases show what McNemar looks at. First, 45 say yes before and 47 after: the share hardly moves. But 52 people changed, 25 one way and 27 the other. The exact p is 0.89. Much churn and no net move: McNemar sees nothing, and rightly, because the question was whether the share moved. Second, 40 say yes before and 48 after, and only 8 people changed, all 8 in the same direction. The wrong table says 0.25. The exact test says 0.0078: eight coins all landing the same way is 2 chances in 256.

The same idea in five more settings, illustrative, and from now on I quote the exact p. A symptom in 40 patients, before and after a treatment: 14 go from yes to no, 3 from no to yes, and p is 0.013. Two raters read the same 60 X-rays. One calls abnormal what the other calls normal 12 times, and the other way 4 times. The chi-square form says 0.046 and the exact test 0.077, on either side of 0.05: borderline. McNemar asks whether the raters call abnormal equally often. How well they agree is another question, with its own statistic.

Two diagnostic tests on 80 patients who have the disease. Test A alone is positive 3 times, test B alone 12 times, and p is 0.035. Fifty pupils take a test before and after a course: 2 pass then fail, 14 fail then pass, and p is 0.004. Matched pairs: 100 pairs, each a case and a similar control. In 30 pairs only the case was exposed, in 14 only the control, and p is 0.023. The ratio of the two counts, 30 to 14, is 2.14: the odds ratio for matched pairs.

Remember: when the same people answer twice, count the people who changed, not the answers. Only the pairs that disagree carry information, and under no change they are a coin flip. Next time: chapter nine, a line through a cloud.
