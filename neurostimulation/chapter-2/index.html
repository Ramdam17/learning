PLATE 2.14:50

Current in a wet, layered head

Head models stack scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, grey matter and white matter, each with a conductivity, and current density equals conductivity times field. Like traffic, current takes the wide scalp avenue and the fluid motorway, while the skull is a narrow pass, so most never reaches the cortex. What arrives depends on each head: milliamps describe the device, not the dose.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every technique in this series puts an electric field into a head. So before we meet any machine, one question. Where does that current actually go?

To answer it, physicists build the head like a scale model, as a stack of layers. From the outside in: the scalp, the skull, the cerebrospinal fluid, or CSF, then grey matter, then white matter. Each layer is a different material. For our purposes, each is described by one number: how easily it lets current through. That number is the conductivity, in siemens per metre.

One line ties it all together. Current density equals conductivity times electric field. Current density is how much current flows through each square metre. So under the same field, a layer that conducts well carries a lot of current, and a layer that conducts badly carries almost none. At the slow frequencies of stimulation, tissue behaves like a plain resistor, so this line is enough.

Here are typical values used in head models. The scalp: about zero point four seven. The skull: about zero point zero one. The cerebrospinal fluid: about one point six five. Grey matter: about zero point two eight. White matter: about zero point one three. The cerebrospinal fluid conducts roughly a hundred and sixty times better than the skull, and the scalp more than forty times better.

Think of a city map, where the width of a road is its conductivity. The current is rush-hour traffic leaving a stadium: the electrode on the scalp. Traffic does not care where you want it to go. It takes the wide avenues.

The scalp is a wide avenue. Much of the current spreads along it, from one electrode to the other, and never goes in. The skull is a narrow mountain pass, and it is the bottleneck on the way in. The few cars that get through reach the cerebrospinal fluid, a motorway, and spread out sideways. Only a trickle ends up in the grey matter.

How big is the trickle? Depending on the study. In a model of a spherical head, about half of the current injected during tDCS was shunted through the scalp, depending on the size and position of the electrodes. Measurements in rats and in human cadavers found that about three quarters of the current applied to the scalp was attenuated by soft tissue and skull. Either way, most of it never reaches the cortex.

In living people, Huang and colleagues recorded inside the heads of ten patients with epilepsy. With two milliamps, the strongest fields in the cortex reached about zero point eight volts per metre. Over more extended areas, about a quarter of a volt per metre. Remember these numbers: a few tenths of a volt per metre. The next episode asks what they do to a neuron.

A word of caution. The conductivities in a model are not constants of nature. Reported scalp values range from about zero point two five to zero point four four, and the skull values also differ significantly, depending on the study and the method. When Huang's team calibrated their models against the recordings, the skull came out about three times more conductive than the textbook value, and the scalp lower. But the proportions between layers stayed roughly the same. So the picture holds: the numbers do not.

Now the moment that matters. What reaches the brain depends on the shape of the head as much as on the device. In models built from the scans of twenty-four men, the same montage gave fields in the hand area with a spread of about twenty percent of the mean, from head to head. The thickness of the cerebrospinal fluid alone explained about half of that: the thicker the layer, the weaker the field. In models of two other heads, the thickness of the skull and of the fluid, the depth of the fold, and the distance to the electrodes together explained up to half of the variation of the field across the cortex. Same box, same settings, different head, different dose.

Which gives us the classic trap. Milliamps describe what leaves the device. They do not describe what arrives at the target. Two participants with identical settings can receive different fields. Chapter seven turns this into a calculation, with a model built from each person's own scan. Magnetic stimulation takes a different route into the head, and episode two point four returns to it.

To sum up. Current injected at the scalp follows the easiest roads: along the scalp, and sideways through the cerebrospinal fluid. The skull is the bottleneck, and most of the current never reaches the cortex. What does arrive depends on the layers of each head, and on conductivities that are still debated.

Next: a field has arrived. What does it take to make a neuron fire?

PLATE 2.24:51

What actually fires a neuron

A neuron fires when an external field pushes its membrane past threshold, and the push lands where the field changes along the axon, at bends, branches and terminals, not along a straight stretch. Strong pulses such as TMS and implanted electrodes can fire neurons. Weak transcranial currents shift the membrane by a fraction of a millivolt, so they tilt neurons rather than trigger them.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every stimulator in this series ends up in the same place. A thin membrane, around a single neuron. Coils, sponges and implanted wires all try to change the voltage across that membrane. So before we meet any machine, one question. What actually makes a neuron fire?

Meet our neuron, and its gauge. The gauge shows the voltage across the membrane. At rest, a neuron typically sits ten to twenty millivolts below its firing threshold. Push it past that line, and it fires an action potential. Move it a little, without crossing, and nothing fires. Keep these two cases in mind. They come back in every chapter.

Now let's add an electric field. Picture it as a steady wind, blowing along a long, straight axon. The wind pushes charges inside the axon and outside it. You might expect the whole axon to light up. It doesn't. Along a straight stretch, the charge that arrives at each point is matched by the charge that leaves it. Nothing piles up, so the membrane barely notices.

Things change where the axon bends, or where it ends. There, the charge pushed by the field has nowhere to go. It piles up against the membrane. Where the field drives charge into the bend, the membrane is depolarized. At the far end, where charge is pulled away, it is hyperpolarized. If the push is strong enough, the gauge crosses the line, and the spike starts right there.

Frank Rattay gave this a name in 1986: the activating function. It tells you where the membrane is pushed, all along the fibre. Formally, it is the second spatial derivative of the potential outside the axon. Said simply, it is how quickly the field along the axon changes as you move along it. A flat field gives a flat line. A bend, a branch or a terminal gives a sharp peak. What matters is not how strong the field is, but how much it changes along the axon.

Experiments agree. In the early nineties, Maccabee, Amassian and colleagues placed nerves in a bath and stimulated them with magnetic pulses. The fibres fired first at bends and at cut ends. The sharper the bend, the lower the threshold.

Inside the brain, the same logic applies. Models of realistic neurons suggest that TMS fires cells at their axon terminals inside the cortex, mostly at the crowns of the folds, not at the cell body. With deep brain stimulation, models show the axons near the electrode following each pulse, even when the cell body itself is silenced. The spike usually begins in the axon, not in the soma.

So how strong does the field have to be? Here the techniques split into two families. With TMS, the field in the cortex at motor threshold is roughly fifty to a hundred volts per metre, depending on the study. That is enough to fire axons, and implanted electrodes, close to their targets, can do it too. With transcranial direct or alternating current at two milliamps, recordings inside the human head show fields of at most about one volt per metre, typically a few tenths.

What does a few tenths of a volt per metre do to the membrane? In brain slices, each volt per metre shifts the membrane by about a tenth of a millivolt, and up to half a millivolt in the most sensitive cells. So the gauge moves by a fraction of a millivolt, while the gap to threshold is ten to twenty. Drawn to scale, the shift is barely a line on the gauge. Weak currents do not fire resting neurons. They tilt them.

A tilt can still matter. A neuron already close to threshold, in an active network, can fire a little earlier or a little more often. In ferret brain slices, fields of about half a volt per metre nudged an ongoing rhythm when they followed a natural waveform. In living rats, about one volt per metre was needed to change spiking. Whether usual human doses reach that level is still debated. Chapter four takes that debate on.

Here is the classic trap. A colourful field map shows where the field is strong. It does not show where neurons fire. Firing depends on how the field changes along each axon, on the axon's shape and thickness, and on its direction. That last point gets its own episode.

To sum up. A neuron fires when its membrane crosses threshold. An external field pushes the membrane where the field changes along the axon: at bends, branches and terminals. Strong pulses, like TMS and implanted electrodes, can fire neurons. Weak transcranial currents shift the membrane by a fraction of a millivolt. They modulate. They do not trigger.

Next: orientation is everything. Why the same electrode can excite one neuron and inhibit its neighbour.

PLATE 2.35:20

Orientation is everything

A neuron feels only the part of the field along its own axis, like a wind-farm turbine facing the wind, so the effect follows a cosine of the angle and flips when the field reverses. In a folded cortex, one electrode depolarizes one wall of a groove and hyperpolarizes the opposite wall. Anodal excites, cathodal inhibits is a rule of thumb, not a law.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw that a field fires a neuron where it changes along the axon. This time, a different question. Take one electrode, and one field. Why can it excite one neuron, and inhibit its neighbour? The answer is direction.

Welcome to the wind farm. The wind blows from the left, and every turbine has an axle. A turbine facing the wind spins forwards, at full speed. A turbine turned sideways hardly moves. A turbine facing the other way spins backwards. A turbine feels only one thing: the part of the wind that blows along its axle.

Cortical neurons behave like these turbines. A pyramidal neuron is a long cell. Its apical dendrite reaches towards the cortical surface, and its cell body sits deeper. That line is its axle, and in the cortex it stands perpendicular to the surface. A field shifts the membrane in proportion to its part along this axle. Pointing from the dendrite towards the cell body, it depolarizes the soma, and hyperpolarizes the far tip of the dendrite. Reverse the field, and the pattern reverses. Seen from the side, the cell body feels almost nothing.

In numbers, this is a cosine. A field along the axle has its full effect. Tilted by sixty degrees, half the effect. Side-on, at ninety degrees, none. Beyond ninety degrees, the sign flips.

Now the catch. The cortex is not a flat sheet. It is folded, and by the usual estimate, about two thirds of it is hidden inside the grooves. On the crest of a fold, neurons stand upright, pointing towards the scalp. On the wall of a groove, they lie sideways, pointing into the groove. The two walls of a groove face each other. So their neurons point in opposite directions.

Now put an anode on the scalp. Under it, current runs down into the head, along the neurons on the crest, from dendrite to soma. Their cell bodies are depolarized. But the current does not only go down. It spreads sideways, and it has to cross grooves. One wall sees the field pointing towards its cell bodies. They are depolarized. The opposite wall sees the same field pointing away from its cell bodies. They are hyperpolarized. Same electrode, same current, opposite effects.

Is this real, or just a drawing? In 2023, Aberra and colleagues simulated tDCS over the motor cortex in a realistic head model, at one point eight milliamps. Beneath the anode, they found regions of depolarization and regions of hyperpolarization, side by side. The part of the field normal to the cortex predicted the shift of the cell bodies very well. And the axon terminals were pushed harder than the cell bodies: just under one millivolt at the peak, against less than a tenth of a millivolt for the soma.

There is a second surprise. Under a typical tDCS electrode, the current does not mostly go straight down. Rahman and colleagues found that it runs mostly sideways, along the cortical surface, four to twelve times more than straight down. In rat brain slices, sideways current did not act on the cell body. It acted on the axon terminals, and changed synaptic strength.

Does direction matter in people? Rawji and colleagues placed electrodes across the central sulcus, or along it. Only the montage whose current crossed the sulcus changed the motor responses to magnetic pulses.

Magnetic stimulation shows the same sensitivity. In 1992, Brasil-Neto and colleagues found that in the hand area of the motor cortex, the best current direction is roughly perpendicular to the central sulcus, flowing from back to front. In 2022, Souza and colleagues turned the field electronically, in three degree steps, in sixteen volunteers. The motor response followed a smooth curve of the angle.

But the cosine is only a first picture. In realistic models of strong magnetic pulses, firing is driven by the size of the field, not just by its part normal to the cortex. And the simple cosine model misses differences between cell types.

Here is the classic trap. In 2000, Nitsche and Paulus showed that weak direct current over the motor cortex changed motor responses by up to forty percent: up under the anode, down under the cathode. Anodal excites, cathodal inhibits became a rule of thumb. But a label on an electrode is not a label on neurons. A meta-analysis found that in cognitive studies, the anode often helps, but the cathode rarely hinders. And at two milliamps, Batsikadze and colleagues found that a cathode raised motor responses too, while one milliamp lowered them. Direction, folding and intensity all enter.

To sum up. A neuron feels the part of the field along its own axis. Depolarizing or hyperpolarizing depends on the angle, and flips when the field reverses. In a folded cortex, one electrode does both, on neighbouring walls. So anodal excites, cathodal inhibits is a rule of thumb from the motor cortex, not a law.

Next: three ways to push a field into the brain. Electric, magnetic and acoustic.

PLATE 2.47:01

Three ways to push: electric, magnetic, acoustic

Stimulation reaches the brain by electric, magnetic or acoustic means, and a magnetic pulse works by inducing an electric field. From outside the head, fields cannot peak in the interior, and a deeper reach means a wider spread, as fifty simulated coil designs show. Only focused ultrasound, at the price of the skull, and implants, at the price of surgery, escape this trade-off.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every stimulator in this series has the same job. It must change the voltage around neurons that sit under a skull we cannot open. There are only three ways in. This episode lays them side by side, then asks one question of all of them. How far down does the push reach, and how wide is it?

Door one is electric. Place two electrodes, push current between them, and the tissue in between carries it. The electric field in the tissue is the current density divided by the conductivity, the relation from episode 2.1. Electrodes on the scalp give transcranial electrical stimulation. Electrodes on a wire inside the brain give deep brain stimulation. Either way, we push charge directly.

Door two is magnetic. A coil above the scalp carries a brief, very strong current. That makes a magnetic field that changes fast. Faraday's law says a changing magnetic field induces an electric field. Head tissue is not magnetic, so the magnetic field passes through scalp and skull almost unchanged. Inside the head, the induced electric field pushes charge, the same kind of push as the field from electrodes. The magnetic field is the messenger. The electric field does the work.

The sizes differ a lot. As in episode 2.2, a magnetic pulse at motor threshold gives roughly fifty to a hundred volts per metre in the cortex, according to Numssen and colleagues in 2024. Transcranial current at two milliamps gives at most about point eight volts per metre, from recordings in patients by Huang and colleagues in 2017.

Door three is acoustic. A transducer sends a pressure wave through the skull. Typical frequencies run from a quarter to about two thirds of a megahertz. Sound travels in soft tissue at roughly fifteen hundred metres per second, so at half a megahertz the wavelength is about three millimetres. A curved transducer, or an array of small elements, makes all the waves arrive together at one spot. The push here is mechanical, not electrical. How that push changes neurons is still debated, and chapter five takes it on.

Now the question. For a source outside the head, how does the push change with depth? In 1992, Heller and van Hulsteyn proved something sharp. At the frequencies used for brain stimulation, no arrangement of outside current sources can make the electric field peak at a point inside the head. The strongest field always sits on a boundary, where the conductivity jumps. In a uniform head, that boundary is the surface. You can shape the field, but you cannot put its summit in the middle.

Think of a sower at the edge of a field. Throw the seed a short way, and it lands in a tight patch near your feet. Throw harder to reach the far rows, and the seed scatters wider, while the rows nearest you still get the thickest layer. Distance is depth. That is the trade-off.

In 2013, Deng, Lisanby and Peterchev measured it for magnetic stimulation. They simulated fifty coil designs in a model head, a uniform sphere. For each coil they asked two questions. How deep does the field stay above half of its strongest value at the cortex? That is the half-value depth. And how much tissue gets at least that half value, divided by that depth? That is the tangential spread, in square centimetres. Smaller means more focal.

All fifty coils fell on the same trade-off. A coil that reaches deeper cannot be as focal as a shallower one. The most focal figure-eight coil had a half-value depth of nine millimetres and a spread of five square centimetres. The most focal round coil, a mini coil, reached one centimetre, with a spread of thirty-four. The ideal reference, the most any coil could reach in that model, went three and a half centimetres deep, with a spread of three hundred and eight. The H coils, built for depth, reached between one point seven and two point four centimetres, and their fields were broad. No coil beat the figure-eight curve.

Electrodes obey the same boundary rule. In a head model with folded cortex, Datta and colleagues found in 2009 that two large rectangular pads give a diffuse field. Its peaks scatter over the cortex, and the strongest one fell between the pads, not under them. A centre electrode ringed by four return electrodes confined the field between them, with almost nothing far away. That is more focal. But the peak is still at the cortex, never in the middle.

Two approaches escape the trade-off, and each pays its own price. Focused ultrasound makes a real peak inside the head, because waves can be timed to meet at one place. The focus is small across and long along the beam. In a human study, Legon and colleagues reported a beam 4.9 millimetres across and 18 long. A worked example in the ITRUSST reporting guidance gives about five millimetres across and thirty along. The price is the skull. It weakens and bends the beam. The intensity in the brain can sometimes be ten times lower than outside, and the focus can shift or change shape.

Implants escape by placing the source at the target. The field from a contact falls away quickly with distance, so it is focal because it is close. The price is surgery. A 2023 meta-analysis of one hundred fifty-one studies, by Tiefenbach and colleagues, found intracranial bleeding in two point five percent of deep brain stimulation patients, and one point four percent per implanted lead.

Put it together. Non-invasive electric and magnetic stimulation sit on a diagonal. Deeper means wider, and the surface always receives more than the depth. Ultrasound and implants sit off that diagonal. This picture is a schematic. The axes cannot be shared, because a field in volts per metre and a pressure in pascals are different things.

Here is the classic trap. A position on this chart is not a verdict. The coil numbers come from a uniform sphere. Real heads fold, and the layers of bone and fluid bend the field. Chapter seven models that. And focal is not selective. A focal field still crosses every neuron in its path. Whether one fires depends on its orientation, from the last episode, and on timing, which is the next one.

To sum up. There are three ways to push: electric, magnetic and acoustic. Magnetic pulses work by inducing an electric field. For electric and magnetic stimulation from outside, the field cannot peak inside the head, and deeper means wider. Only focused ultrasound and implants escape, one at the price of the skull, the other at the price of surgery.

Next: time matters. Why a neuron follows a slow current and ignores a fast one.

PLATE 2.56:54

Time matters: pulses, rhythms and the membrane filter

A membrane needs time to respond, as a pan of milk needs time to heat: short pulses are about charge, long ones about the rheobase, with the chronaxie between. The membrane is a low-pass filter that follows slow currents and smooths fast ones. Two kilohertz currents beating together form a slow envelope, the idea behind temporal interference, which a passive membrane alone cannot read.

TRANSCRIPTEN

So far, we have asked where a push goes, and how strong it is. This episode asks when. A neuron is not a light switch. Its membrane takes time to respond, and that delay decides which currents can move it at all.

Here is the picture for this episode. A pan of milk on a stove. The heat from the plate is the current. The temperature of the milk is the voltage across the membrane. Boiling is a spike. This stove can also chill the pan, the way a negative current pulls the membrane the other way. And the pan leaks. It always loses heat to the kitchen.

Try a very short blast of heat. The pan hardly warms, because the heat has no time to build. To boil the milk in that time, you must turn the plate up a lot. A gentler flame needs longer. And below a certain flame, the milk never boils, however long you wait, because the leak carries the heat away as fast as it arrives. That lowest flame is the rheobase. The time that a flame of twice the rheobase needs to boil the milk is the chronaxie.

This is the strength-duration curve. In 1901, Georges Weiss found that the charge needed to stimulate a nerve grows steadily with the length of the pulse. In 1907, Louis Lapicque gave the curve a circuit: a membrane is a capacitor with a leak. And in 1909, he named the rheobase and the chronaxie. In that circuit, the curve has a simple form. The current needed is the rheobase, divided by one minus e to the minus t over the membrane time constant. At twice the rheobase, the time is about seven tenths of the time constant. The curve you see is computed, for two example time constants.

Look at the two ends. For short pulses, what counts is the charge, the current multiplied by the time. For long pulses, the length no longer matters. Only the rheobase does.

What are real values? The membrane time constant of cortical pyramidal neurons is usually reported between about ten and thirty milliseconds, depending on the study and the cell. But the chronaxie depends on which part of the cell the current acts on. In the central nervous system, myelinated axons have chronaxies of fifty to a hundred microseconds, wrote Ranck in 1975. When grey matter is stimulated, the chronaxie is often two hundred to seven hundred microseconds. In rat cortical slices, Nowak and Bullier found that cell bodies had a chronaxie about forty times longer than axons. In cultured hippocampal neurons, Stern and colleagues measured about one millisecond for dendrites, and about a tenth of a millisecond for axons. So axons answer short pulses. Cell bodies and dendrites answer slower ones.

Now the techniques. In episode 3.1, the biphasic pulse of a magnetic stimulator lasted from one hundred seventy-six to three hundred fifty-three microseconds. That is the same order as the chronaxie of an axon. Direct current lasts seconds to minutes, far longer than any chronaxie. So it is a rheobase story. Only the level matters. And at two milliamps, the field is at most about point eight volts per metre, and each volt per metre moves the membrane by a tenth to half a millivolt. Steady, that does not fire a neuron.

Now put four currents through the same membrane. This is a simulation of a passive membrane with a ten-millisecond time constant. It is not a real neuron. First, a short strong pulse. The voltage jumps a little, and drains away. Second, a steady current. The voltage climbs, and settles on a plateau. Third, a sinusoid at ten hertz. The membrane follows it, a little smaller and a little late. At a hundred hertz, the output is only about a sixth as large. Fourth, a current at two kilohertz. The output is almost a flat line, under one percent of the steady response. The membrane is a low-pass filter. It follows slow changes, and smooths fast ones.

That is the passive part. A real neuron adds more. Its channels open and close with their own delays, and a current that changes sign every quarter of a millisecond is too fast for them to follow cycle by cycle. So a neuron barely responds to a current of a few kilohertz. At high amplitude, such currents can even block conduction in a nerve, as Kilgore and Bhadra reviewed in 2014.

Now the strange part. Take two currents, at two thousand and two thousand and five hertz. Alone, each is almost ignored. Together, they add up to a wave that swells and fades five times a second, the difference between the two frequencies. That slow swelling is the envelope. In 2017, Grossman and colleagues delivered fields like this to mice. They reported that neurons in the living mouse brain could follow the envelope, and they stimulated the hippocampus without recruiting the cortex above it. This is the idea of temporal interference. Episode four point four takes it on.

But be careful. A passive filter alone cannot do it. If you pass the sum of the two currents through the passive membrane, you get a flat line, as the simulation shows. Something else must turn the swelling into a slow signal. Mirzakhalili and colleagues argued in 2020 that this needs ion channels that act as a rectifier. And they warned that the same mechanism can block conduction in tissue that was never the target. In 2023, Violante and colleagues reported a first human test, with these same carriers, and a five hertz envelope. They used modelling and measurements in a cadaver to aim at the hippocampus, then imaging and a memory task in healthy volunteers. What this shows, and how focal it is in a human head, is still debated.

Here is the classic trap. A current that a neuron seems to ignore is not an inert current. It can still block conduction, and a map of the envelope alone hides the carriers, which fill the tissue all the way to the target. Whenever you read a time-varying protocol, ask two questions. How long does each pulse last, compared with the chronaxie? And how fast does the current change, compared with what the membrane can follow?

To sum up. A membrane takes time to respond. Short pulses are about charge. Long ones are about the rheobase. Slow currents are followed, and fast ones are smoothed, like a low-pass filter. A neuron barely responds to a few kilohertz, yet two of them beating together make a slow envelope, which is the idea behind temporal interference.
