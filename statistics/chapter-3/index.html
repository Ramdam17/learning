Events and the three rules

Probability is a share of a board of tiles obeying three rules: never negative, the whole board is one, and separate regions add. Adding overlapping regions counts shared tiles twice, so for at least one, count the opposite and subtract from one. Four dice rolls give 0.5177 for at least one six, and twenty checks give 0.6415 for a false alarm.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Chance feels slippery. Yet it runs on just three rules. Today we learn them, and we use them on a question that fools almost everyone.

Roll a die four times. What is the chance of at least one six? Hold on to your guess. We will come back to it.

Start with a board. Two rolls of a die give thirty-six outcomes: six for the first roll, six for the second. Each outcome is a tile, and every tile is equally likely.

An event is a region of the board. Take this event: the two rolls add up to seven. Six tiles lie along the diagonal. So the chance is six out of thirty-six. That is one in six, or 0.1667.

Is that real? Here are twelve thousand simulated pairs of rolls. The share of sevens wobbles at first, then settles. After twelve thousand pairs, it reads 16.5 percent.

In 1933, Andrey Kolmogorov showed that all of probability can be built on a few axioms. Three rules are enough for today.

Rule one: a probability is never negative. An empty region has probability zero. A sum of thirteen is impossible, because no tile makes it.

Rule two: the whole board has probability one. Something always happens. A sum between two and twelve covers every tile.

Rule three: if two regions do not overlap, their probabilities add. A sum of seven fills six tiles. A sum of eleven fills two tiles, and shares none of them. Together they fill eight tiles. That is 0.2222, and the simulation finds 22 percent.

Rules one and two also give a bonus. No probability can be larger than one, because no region can hold more than the whole board.

Now use the rules as a check. A colleague shows you three tables of severity scores for a questionnaire. Each table claims to give the chance of mild, moderate, severe and critical. Which tables are legal? Pause and look.

The first table adds up to one, and nothing is negative. It is legal. The second adds up to 1.05, so it breaks rule two. The third adds up to one, but it holds a minus 0.1, so it breaks rule one.

Tiles do not have to be equally likely. Draw the legal table as four strips, each as wide as its chance. The rules work the same way. Severe and critical are different strips, so they add: 0.15 plus 0.05 gives 0.20. And not mild is one minus 0.50. That is 0.50, the same as 0.30 plus 0.15 plus 0.05.

Now a common mistake. Rule three only works for regions that do not overlap. What is the chance of at least one six in two rolls? A tempting answer is one sixth plus one sixth, which is 0.3333.

But the tile where both rolls are sixes lies in both regions, and adding counts it twice. The union holds eleven tiles, not twelve. The chance is 0.3056. Twenty thousand simulated pairs give 30.5 percent.

Let us repair the rule in a lotto hall. A drum holds ten balls, numbered one to ten. Event A: an even number, five balls. Event B: a multiple of three, three balls. What is the chance of A or B? Guess first. Adding gives 0.80.

But ball six is in both events. Count the balls directly: two, three, four, six, eight, nine, ten. That is seven balls, so 0.70. Ten thousand simulated draws give 69.5 percent. The repair is to add, then subtract the shared part: 0.5 plus 0.3 minus 0.1.

Back to four rolls. Four rolls give one thousand two hundred ninety-six outcomes. That is too many tiles to list. So look at the opposite event instead: no six at all.

An event and its opposite never overlap, and together they fill the board. By rule three their chances add. By rule two they add to one. So the chance of the opposite is one minus the chance of the event. This is called the complement.

The chance of no six in one roll is five out of six. In four rolls, it is five times five times five times five: 625 tiles out of 1,296. That is 0.4823.

So the chance of at least one six is one minus 0.4823, which is 0.5177. Slightly better than even. Twenty thousand simulated games give 51.9 percent. Compare that with your guess. Adding four sixths would have said 0.6667. It counts overlaps again and again.

This trick is everywhere. A clinic books ten appointments. Each patient has a two percent chance of not showing up. These numbers are illustrative.

The chance that everyone shows up is 0.98 to the power ten, which is 0.8171. So at least one no-show has a chance of 0.1829. Adding would have said 0.20.

One more setting: a study. A questionnaire has six items. Each participant skips any given item with a ten percent chance, and the skips are independent. Everything here is illustrative.

The chance of answering all six is 0.9 to the power six: 0.5314. So 0.4686 of participants have at least one missing item. Almost half. Ten thousand simulated participants give 46.9 percent.

Now a lab runs twenty checks on pure noise. Each check has a five percent chance of a false alarm, and the checks do not influence one another. The chance of no false alarm at all is 0.95 to the power twenty: 0.3585.

So the chance of at least one false alarm is 0.6415. Twenty thousand simulated labs give 64 percent. Adding would have claimed a hundred percent, which is nonsense.

Turn the knob. Guess first: how many checks until a false alarm is more likely than not? With a five percent rate, fourteen checks are already enough. With a one percent rate, twenty checks give 0.1821. Chapter fifteen will come back to this exact calculation.

Remember: a probability is a share of the board. It is never negative, the whole board is one, and separate regions add. For at least one, count the opposite, and subtract from one.

PLATE 3.27:06

Conditional probability

Learning something shrinks the board: knowing the first die is a six lifts the chance of a total of at least ten from 0.1667 to 0.5. A conditional probability is a count inside the known group, so the denominator changes. The alarm that is right 0.9 when it fires fires in only 0.01 of noisy sessions: swapping the two is the classic error.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Probabilities are not carved in stone. They shift the moment you learn something new. Today we see how, and why it matters so much for data.

Roll two dice. The chance that the total is at least ten is six tiles out of thirty-six. That is 0.1667. Now a friend peeks and says: the first die is a six. What is the chance now? Hold on to your guess.

Look at the board. Learning that the first die is a six rules out every tile outside one row. Only six tiles remain, and three of them reach ten. So the chance is three out of six, which is 0.5. The board shrank, and the chance tripled. Had the friend said the first die is a one, no tile would reach ten, and the chance would drop to zero. New information can raise a chance or lower it.

Is that real? Here are twelve thousand simulated pairs. Overall, 16.6 percent reach ten. Now keep only the pairs whose first die is a six. That leaves 2,043 pairs, and we throw the rest away. Among them, 48.4 percent reach ten. Conditioning is just this: keep the cases where the condition holds, and count again.

Now a crowd. A thousand students answer a survey. These numbers are illustrative. Some slept under seven hours: call them group B. Some drank coffee before class: call them group A. Walls split the hall into two rooms, with group B on the left. In each room, the coffee drinkers are pink.

Four counts fill the hall. Three hundred and twenty students slept little and drank coffee. Eighty slept little and did not. Two hundred and forty slept well and drank coffee. Three hundred and sixty slept well and did not.

What is the chance that a student drinks coffee? 560 out of a thousand, or 0.56. Now suppose we know the student slept under seven hours. We walk into room B and close the door. Four hundred students remain, and 320 of them drank coffee. The chance is 320 over 400, which is 0.80. And if the student slept well? We walk into the other room: 240 over 600, which is 0.40. Knowing about sleep moves the chance of coffee from 0.56 to 0.80 or 0.40.

Same count, new denominator. The 320 never changed. Divide it by the whole hall, and you get 0.32: students who slept little and drank coffee. Divide it by room B, and you get 0.80. Divide it by all 560 coffee drinkers, and you get 0.5714. Three questions, three denominators.

Let a die pick twenty thousand students at random. About eight thousand land in room B. Among those, 79.8 percent drank coffee. The simulation agrees with the count.

Mathematicians write this as the probability of A given B. The bar means given. It is the count of A and B, divided by the count of B. Divide top and bottom by a thousand, and the rule reads: the probability of A and B, over the probability of B. That is the definition.

Rearranged, it counts two things at once. Multiply both sides by the probability of B: the probability of A and B is the probability of B times the probability of A given B. Try it on a pile of twenty paper forms, five of them incomplete. Draw two, and keep the first out. The first is incomplete with chance 5 over 20, which is 0.25. Given that, four incomplete forms remain among nineteen, so the second is incomplete with chance 4 over 19, which is 0.2105. Multiply: the chance that both are incomplete is 0.0526. Twenty thousand simulated piles give 5.4 percent.

Same idea, no table. Here are ten thousand simulated adults, with heights in centimeters. These are illustrative, with means near national survey values. Women are pink, men are blue. Event A: at least 170 centimeters tall. Guess before you see: among women, what share reach it? Overall, 42.6 percent reach it.

Now condition on men. The pink bars fade. Among 5,000 men, 3,787 reach 170 centimeters. That is 0.7574. Condition on women instead: 470 out of 5,000, which is 0.0940. The event never changed. Only the room did. A conditional probability is a slice of a histogram, scaled up so that the slice counts as one.

Now the mistake that fools almost everyone. A very noisy lab records a thousand sessions. In 900 of them, some channel is noisy. An alarm fires in just ten sessions. In nine of those ten, a channel really was noisy. Before you see the answer, guess. When the alarm fires, how often is a channel noisy? And when a channel is noisy, how often did the alarm fire?

Read the alarm column first. Ten sessions, nine noisy. That is 0.9. Now read the noisy row. Nine hundred sessions, nine with an alarm. That is 0.01. The same nine sessions sit in both fractions. Ninety percent against one percent. The alarm is rare, and when it speaks it is almost always right. But most noisy sessions never trigger it.

The two answer different questions. One asks: inside room B, how many are in A? The other asks: inside room A, how many are in B. Nothing forces them to be equal. Swapping them is the most common error in probability.

You will meet it again. In chapter six, a p-value is the chance of data like ours if nothing is going on. It is not the chance that nothing is going on, given our data. That is the same swap.

One last consequence, for real studies. Suppose the true share of coffee drinkers is 0.80 inside a subgroup. A study of a thousand students estimates it from the subgroup alone. Here are two thousand simulated studies. When the subgroup is forty percent of students, about 400 people, the estimates stay close to 0.80, with a spread of 0.02. When the subgroup is one percent, about ten people, the estimates scatter, with a spread of 0.13. The study had a thousand students both times. What counted was the size of the room.

Every dataset is already conditional. It holds only the people who agreed, and stayed. Keep asking: conditional on what?

Remember: a conditional probability is a count inside the group you know about. Change the group, and the denominator changes. Next time: Bayes' rule.

PLATE 3.36:45

Bayes' rule

In a town of a thousand where one in a hundred is sick, a test flags ninety percent of the sick and nine percent of the healthy. Counting people in the flagged basket shows only nine of ninety-eight are sick, about 0.092. Bayes' rule formalises this. Repeating the test or a more common disease lifts it; a lab's discoveries can be 36 percent false.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A test comes back positive. Surely that means you are probably sick? Today we count people, and the answer may surprise you.

Here is a screening, with illustrative numbers. A town has one thousand people, and one in a hundred has the disease. The test flags ninety percent of the sick people. It also flags nine percent of the healthy people, by mistake. You test positive. Hold on to your guess: how likely is it that you are sick?

Let us send the whole town through the gate, one person at a time. Ten people are sick. Nine hundred ninety are healthy. Of the ten sick people, nine are flagged, and one slips through. Of the nine hundred ninety healthy people, nine percent are flagged. That is eighty-nine people.

Now look only at the people who were flagged. Nine are sick, and eighty-nine are healthy. That makes ninety-eight people in the basket, and nine of them are sick. Nine out of ninety-eight is 0.092. About one in eleven. A positive result, and still probably healthy.

Is that real? Here are two hundred thousand simulated people. 19,707 of them are flagged, and 1,896 of those are sick. That is 9.6 percent, close to the count. And a clear result? Among the 180,293 people who passed the gate, only 199 are sick. That is 0.1 percent.

Now the misreading to avoid. The test flags ninety percent of the sick. That does not mean that ninety percent of the flagged are sick. One is the share of sick people who are flagged: 0.90. The other is the share of flagged people who are sick: 0.092. It is the swap from last time, and it hurts more when the disease is rare.

Counting people is easier than juggling percentages. In 1995, Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage gave sixty students fifteen problems like this one. In the standard versions, with probabilities, 16 percent of the answers followed Bayes' reasoning. With counts of people, 46 percent did. Same problems, same information. Only the format changed.

Now the formula, after the counting. Take the nine sick people who were flagged, and count them in two ways. First: one in a hundred is sick, and 0.9 of them are flagged. That is 0.009 of the town. Second: 0.098 of the town is flagged, and some share of them is sick. Both count the same nine people. So the chance of being sick, given a positive result, is 0.01 times 0.9, divided by 0.098. That is 0.092.

That is Bayes' rule: the chance of A given B equals the chance of B given A, times the chance of A, divided by the chance of B. Thomas Bayes never published it. Two years after he died, in 1763, his friend Richard Price had his essay read to the Royal Society.

Turn a knob. What if the disease were more common? Guess first: how common must it be before a positive result is fifty-fifty? At one in a thousand, a positive result means about one percent. At one in a hundred, 9.2 percent. At one in ten, 52.6 percent. At one in two, 91 percent. Two hundred thousand simulated people at each level agree.

The fifty-fifty point sits at 9.1 percent. A positive result is ten times more likely in a sick person than in a healthy one. So the sick must reach one in eleven before the two groups match in the basket.

Could a better test fix this? Let it flag ninety-nine percent of the sick, and only one percent of the healthy. With one in a hundred sick, a positive result is exactly fifty-fifty. About ten sick people are flagged, and about ten healthy ones too. The rarity still wins.

There is a remedy for false alarms: test again. Give the ninety-eight people in the basket a second test, which makes its mistakes independently of the first. Of the nine sick, about eight are flagged again. Of the eighty-nine healthy, also about eight. Now the basket is half sick, half healthy.

In the simulation, 3,280 people are flagged twice, and 1,641 are sick. That is 50.0 percent. A third positive result lifts it to 91 percent. The chance after the first test became the starting point for the second. Chapter sixteen will call these the prior and the posterior. Repeating the same flawed test may not work, because its mistakes may repeat.

Evidence can also arrive flip by flip. A jar holds one thousand coins, and forty are two-headed. This is illustrative too. Pull one coin out and flip it five times. It lands heads every time. Guess first: how likely is it that the coin is two-headed?

Count the coins. A two-headed coin always shows five heads, so all forty stay. A fair coin does it only one time in thirty-two, so thirty fair coins stay. Forty out of seventy is 0.571. Every heads halves the fair coins, and leaves the two-headed ones alone. After three heads, it is one in four. The simulation gives 56.7 percent.

The same counting works in a lab. A lab tests a thousand hypotheses, and these numbers are illustrative. One in ten is real. A study detects eighty percent of real effects, and raises a false alarm on five percent of empty ones. Guess first: of the significant results, how many are real?

A hundred hypotheses are real, and eighty are detected. Nine hundred are empty, and forty-five raise a false alarm. That makes one hundred twenty-five significant results, and eighty are real. That is 64 percent. So 36 percent of the discoveries are false, although each passed the usual five percent threshold. David Colquhoun published this counting in 2014.

Now suppose only one hypothesis in fifty is real. Sixteen real effects are detected, and forty-nine false alarms appear. Of sixty-five significant results, only 25 percent are real. That is why a p-value is not the chance that an effect is real. Chapter six returns to it.

Remember: Bayes' rule counts the people in the basket. Of everyone flagged, how many are truly sick? When the disease is rare, the basket fills with false alarms. Next time, in 3.4, independence.

PLATE 3.46:56

Independence

Two events are independent when the chance of both equals the product of their chances, like two ink plates overprinting; never happening together is the opposite of independent. Rows of data can break independence: the same person measured twice, a common cause, or neighbours in time. A hundred such rows carried the information of only fifty-five, thirty-five or twelve.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two things happen. Does knowing one of them tell you anything about the other? If it does not, they are independent. Today we check that by counting, and then we see what dependence does to a table of data.

Roll two dice, one after the other. Event A: the first die is even. Event B: the second die shows five or six. Think of two ink plates printed over the same sheet. The pink plate is A. The blue plate is B. Where they overprint, both happen.

On the board, A is three columns, and B is two rows. They overprint on six tiles out of thirty-six. A covers half of the board. Inside the blue rows, A still covers half. Knowing that B happened changed nothing. That is independence: the chance of both equals the chance of A times the chance of B. A half times a third is one sixth.

Is that real? Here are a hundred thousand simulated pairs. A happens in 50.1 percent of them, and B in 33.3 percent. Both happen in 16.6 percent. Multiply 50.1 percent by 33.3 percent, and you get 16.7 percent. The count and the product agree, up to chance. And among the pairs where B happened, A happened 49.9 percent of the time. Almost the same as 50.1.

Another pair. Note the month someone was born, and their blood type. Here we simulate people whose month and blood type are drawn separately. The shares are illustrative. Born in July: 8.2 percent. Blood type O: 44.7 percent. Both: 3.7 percent. And 8.2 times 44.7 percent is 3.7 percent. Same story.

Now a pair that is not independent. Back to the two dice of last time. Event A: the total is at least ten. Event B: the first die is a six. We learned that knowing the first die is a six triples the chance. So the plates should not line up. Simulated: A happens in 16.9 percent of pairs, B in 16.8 percent, and both in 8.4 percent. The product of the two shares is only 2.8 percent. The product rule fails, and that failure is how dependence shows itself. Inside the blue plate, the pink one covers half, instead of a sixth.

Cards give a clean test. Draw two cards from a deck. What is the chance that both are hearts? First, put the first card back and shuffle. Then the draws are independent. A heart first happens in 25.1 percent of the deals, a heart second in 25.0, and both in 6.3 percent. The product is 6.3 as well. Now keep the first card out. If the first card was a heart, only twelve hearts remain among fifty-one cards. Both hearts now happen in 5.9 percent of deals, but the product of the two shares is still 6.3. The deck remembers what left it.

Now a misreading. Many people think that independent means never together. Guess first: two events that can never happen together, are they independent? Let A be a first die of one, two or three. Let B be a first die of five or six. No tile belongs to both, so the plates never overprint. In a hundred thousand simulated rolls, both happened zero times. Independence would have asked for 16.7 percent. Worse, if B happened, A has zero chance. Knowing B tells you that A did not happen. That is information. Two events with a positive chance that never meet are about as dependent as events can be. Independent events must overlap, by exactly the product.

Why does this matter for data? Because a table has rows, and we like to count rows. Here is a simulated machine. A study measures reaction times: 400 milliseconds on average, each row with the same spread. Fifty independent people: across twenty thousand simulated studies, the mean wobbles by 6.3 milliseconds. A hundred independent people: 4.5. More independent rows, less wobble.

Now guess first. Fifty people, each measured twice, make a hundred rows. Will the mean wobble like a hundred independent rows, or like fifty? The answer: 6.0 milliseconds. Close to fifty people, far from 4.5. A fast person is fast both times, so the second row repeats the first. Those hundred rows carry about as much as fifty-five independent ones. This is the first way to break independence: the same person measured twice. Your reaction time now, and the next one, are not independent.

The second way: a common cause. Ice cream sales and sunburns both rise on hot days. In twenty thousand simulated days, which are illustrative, ice cream sells well on 49.9 percent of days. Many sunburns come on 39.5 percent. Both: 28.5 percent. Independence would give 19.7. Now look only at hot days. Ice cream: 80.1 percent. Sunburn: 68.7 percent. Both: 55.0 percent, which matches their product. Fix the cause, and the link vanishes. Siblings' heights share a family. Two neighbouring EEG electrodes pick up the same brain source: in a simulation, their correlation is 0.80.

A common cause can also shift whole groups of rows together. Take a hundred patients in ten clinics, with each clinic shifting its ten patients up or down. The mean wobbles by 7.5 milliseconds. That is more than for fifty independent people, and a hundred rows carry the information of only about thirty-five.

The third way: two measurements close in time. Today's temperature and tomorrow's. In a simulated drifting series, today is above the middle in 50.0 percent of days, and so is tomorrow. Both: 39.7 percent. Independence would give 25.0. Now take a hundred consecutive values of the series. The mean wobbles by 13.1 milliseconds. A hundred rows, worth about twelve. Measuring faster adds rows, not information, because neighbours repeat each other.

So n is not the number of rows in the table. It is the number of independent pieces of information. A hundred rows were worth fifty-five, thirty-five, even twelve. Chapter eleven treats rows that come in groups, such as several measures per person. Chapter thirteen treats values that are close in time.

Remember: two events are independent when knowing one tells nothing about the other, and then the chance of both is the product. Never together is not independent. And rows that are not independent carry less information than their number. Next time, in 3.5, random variables.

PLATE 3.56:50

Random variables, expectation and variance

A random variable is a number on each tile; its expectation is the long-run average, here exactly three in the die game although half of plays pay nothing. Expectations always add, but variances add only for independent variables, and standard deviations do not. That gives sigma over root n, and explains why correlated insurance claims or neighbouring EEG channels carry less information.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A die. A raffle ticket. A bus you are waiting for. Each gives a number that nobody can predict. Today we give such numbers a name, we summarize them with two values, and we learn what those values do when we add numbers up. Last time, a hundred rows were sometimes worth only fifty-five. Now we see why.

Take a game. Roll one die. Faces one, two and three pay nothing. Faces four and five pay three points. Face six pays twelve. Write each prize on its tile of the board. That is a random variable: a number attached to each tile. Before the roll, nobody knows it. After the roll, it is just a number.

How much is one play worth? Let us play. After ten plays, the average payout is 3.6. After a hundred plays, 2.8. After a thousand, 3.1. After ten thousand, 3.1. Two more simulated games do the same, and all three settle near three. That value is the expectation: the long-run average, the law of large numbers of episode 1.5 at work.

We can find it without playing. Weight each value by its chance. Zero, half of the time. Three, a third of the time. Twelve, a sixth of the time. Zero plus one plus two makes three. The expectation is exactly three. Yet half of all plays pay nothing. The expectation describes the long run, not one play.

Now the spread. Take each payout's distance from three, square it, and average the squares with the same weights. Nine times a half is four and a half. The threes add nothing. Eighty-one times a sixth is thirteen and a half. The variance is eighteen. The ten thousand simulated plays give 18.2. One more fact we will need: double every payout, and every distance doubles, so every squared distance is four times bigger. The variance goes from 18.2 to 72.8.

More examples, all illustrative. A raffle sells a thousand tickets at one dollar, with one prize of four hundred dollars. Your gain is minus one, almost always, and plus three hundred ninety-nine once in a thousand. Weighted, that gives minus sixty cents. Over two hundred thousand simulated tickets, the average gain was minus 0.60. No ticket ever loses sixty cents. A bus comes every ten minutes, and you arrive at a random moment. Your wait is anything from zero to ten minutes, and the simulated average is 5.0: half the gap.

Now let us add numbers. Play the die game twice, with two different dice, and add the payouts. The expectation of the total is 6.0: three plus three. Expectations always add, whatever links the two games.

Do variances add as well? Guess first. Each game has a variance of about eighteen. What is the variance of the total? Here are two independent dice: 36.2. That is eighteen plus eighteen. Now tie the games together. Read the same die twice, and add. The total is double the payout, and the variance is 72.1, twice as much. Tie them halfway, so the second game copies the first die half of the time: 54.1, in between. Variances add only for independent variables. Otherwise an extra term appears, positive when the two move together.

Another case. A task has three steps, one after the other. They take about twenty, fifteen and thirty minutes, and they wander by three, four and twelve minutes. The expectations add: sixty-five minutes. But the spreads do not. Guess first: does the total wander by nineteen minutes, three plus four plus twelve? The simulation says 13.0. The variances add: nine, sixteen and a hundred forty-four make a hundred sixty-nine, and its square root is thirteen. Variances add. Standard deviations do not.

Now the payoff, in three lines. The mean of n values is their sum, divided by n. Line one: dividing by n divides the variance by n squared. Line two, which needs independence: the variance of the sum is n times sigma squared. Line three: so the variance of the mean is n sigma squared over n squared, which is sigma squared over n. Take the root: sigma over root n.

Check it on the reaction times of chapter one, where sigma is 153. Means of twenty-five people: the rule says 30.6, the simulation 30.9. Means of a hundred: 15.3 and 15.3. Means of four hundred: 7.7 and 7.7. This is the root-n law of episode 1.4, in a fresh simulation. It was no accident. It comes from adding variances.

So when independence breaks, the law breaks. Last time, fifty people measured twice wobbled like about fifty-five independent rows, and a drifting series like about twelve. Here is another place. An insurer has a thousand customers. Each has a 2.8 percent chance a year of claiming two thousand dollars. The expected payout is 56 per customer, and the premium is 80. With independent claims, the average payout wobbles by 10.4, and the insurer loses money in 1.2 percent of years. Now let the weather set everyone's chance together, a little higher or lower each year. The chance is still 2.8 percent on average, and the expectation is still 56. But the average wobbles by 25.2, and the insurer loses in 18.6 percent of years. A thousand customers, worth about a hundred seventy.

The brain does the same. Two neighbouring EEG channels pick up one shared source, plus their own noise. Simulated, each has a variance of 1.24. The total variance is 4.48, not 2.49. The shared source adds twice its covariance: 1.99. Averaging the two channels gives 1.12, not half of 1.24. Averaging cannot remove what the channels share.

Here is the ledger of the day: a die game, a ticket, a bus, two games, a task, a mean, a book of insurance and two channels. For each, a random variable, its expectation, and whether the variances add.

Remember: a random variable is a number on each tile. Its expectation is the long-run average, and expectations always add. Variances add only for independent variables, and that is why the standard error shrinks like one over root n. Next time, in 4.1: why the bell curve is called normal.
