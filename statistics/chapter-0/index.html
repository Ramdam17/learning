From a sample to a decision

Twenty dots split into two groups show a gap, but rerunning the experiment flips it, so luck alone moves gaps. A machine that draws a thousand studies shows luck opens a gap past a hundred milliseconds in about fourteen studies out of a hundred. The series gives the p-value, confidence interval and Bayes factor their exact meaning, built by simulation first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to statistics. Not the kind that starts with formulas. The kind you can watch happen.

Twenty dots fall on a sheet. Ten of them are group A, and ten are group B. The two groups have different averages. Is that a real difference?

Now run the same experiment again, with new people. The gap is bigger this time, and it leans the other way. Once more. Now it flips again. Same kind of people, same rules. Only luck has changed.

So how far can a gap wander by luck alone? Here is a machine that answers by trying. It picks twenty people, splits them into two groups, and measures the gap. Then it does it again, and again, all on its own. Each tile is one gap, from one study that nobody ran. A thousand studies later, a pile appears. In about fourteen studies out of a hundred, luck alone opens the gap past a hundred milliseconds, in one direction or the other.

This series is about one question. From a sample to a decision: what can I say, and how sure am I?

Papers answer it with three phrases. The p-value. The confidence interval. The Bayes factor. You meet them in almost every paper. This series gives each one its exact meaning, and says what it does not mean.

We build every idea the way you just saw: first by simulation, then with the formula. There is a core path for everyone, which needs nothing beyond high school. And there are deeper episodes, for those who bring some Python and a little linear algebra. By the end, you will be able to read the methods section of a paper, choose an analysis, and justify it.

Remember: from one sample you can say something, and you can say how sure you are. Next time, in episode 1.1: Population and sample.
