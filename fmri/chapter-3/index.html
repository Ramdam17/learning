Choosing your acquisition

Choosing an acquisition means setting four linked dials: repetition time, echo time, voxel size and multiband factor. A short TR gives more images but less signal each, an echo time near gray-matter T2 star adds contrast and dropout, small voxels sharpen maps but weaken signal, and multiband adds speed at a cost in noise. No sequence is best, only the one fitting the question.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 3. Before you scan, picture a railway station and a ticket machine with four dials. TR, TE, voxel size, and multiband. They are linked by gears. Turn one dial and the others move. Today, we learn to choose our acquisition.

First dial the TR the repetition time. It is the time the scanner needs to cover the whole brain once. A classic single-band protocol takes 2 to 3 seconds with voxels of 3 to 4 millimeters. A short TR gives you more images per minute, and it samples the heartbeat and breathing better, so less of them folds into your signal. But spins need time to recover between excitations. When the TR gets very short, each image carries less signal.

Second dial the TE the echo time. It is when we listen after the excitation. Remember T2 star. The BOLD contrast is strongest when the echo time is close to the T2 star of gray matter. At 3 Tesla, studies report values between about 40 and 65 milliseconds. Yet most studies at 3 Tesla use about 30. Why so short? Because a long TE also lets the signal vanish near the sinuses and the ear canals. So 30 milliseconds is a compromise. A little less contrast everywhere, much less drop out where it hurts.

Third dial the voxel size. Signal grows with volume. A voxel of 3 mm holds more than 3 times the volume of a voxel of 2 mm, so it gives a much stronger signal. Smaller voxels mix fewer tissues, follow the cortex better, and lose less signal near the sinuses. But they need more slices to cover the brain, which pushes the TR up. And each image needs a longer readout, which makes distortions worse.

Fourth dial, multiband. Instead of exciting one slice at a time, we excite several at once. The many small coils of a modern head coil each see the slices a little differently, and that is how they are separated afterwards. The Human Connectome project used a multiband factor of 8 to get the whole brain at 2mm every 720ms. That is the gear that unlocked short TRs and small voxels together. The price? Some noise is amplified when the slices are separated. A little signal can leak from one slice into another. And with such a short TR, each image is weaker.

So the four dials pull on each other. Smaller voxels push the TR up. Multiband pulls it back down at a cost in noise. A longer TE adds contrast and dropout. There is also in-plane acceleration, which shortens the readout and reduces distortions, again at a cost in signal. Nothing is free.

So which ticket should you buy? It depends on where you are going. Look at the departures board. To decode fine patterns, take small voxels. To study connectivity and fast dynamics, take a short TR. For an exploratory task, take full coverage from the top of the cortex down to the cerebellum and keep things robust. There is no best sequence. There is only a compromise that fits your question.

Three practical tips. Start from a protocol that already works at your site or from a large study. Ask your physicist before changing several dials at once. And always run a pilot. Check the coverage, look for dropout in your regions of interest, and measure the signal stability over time.

To sum up, four link dials TR, TE, voxel size, and multiband. Short TR, more images, less signal each. TE close to T2 star, more contrast, more dropout. Small voxels, sharper maps, weaker signal. Multiband: speed at cost in noise. Choose for your question and test it before the real study. Next time we design for slow signal.

PLATE 3.23:57

Designing for a slow signal

Because the response is a slow cloud of steam, the timetable of trials matters. Blocks of about sixteen seconds give the strongest detection, while jittered event-related designs let trial types be mixed and the response shape estimated; detection and estimation trade off. Efficiency can be computed before scanning, and correlated regressors, such as a target fixed two seconds after a cue, cannot be separated.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back at our station, trains pull into the platform and each one leaves a cloud of steam. The cloud rises slowly, peaks after about 5 seconds and takes around 20 seconds to clear. The trains are your trials. The steam is the BOLD response. With a signal this slow, the timetable matters enormously. Today we design for a slow signal.

The first kind of timetable is the block design. Trains arrive back to back for a while, and then nothing comes for a while. The clouds pile up into one big plateau, then fade. A block design gives the largest difference between conditions, so it is the strongest design for detecting an effect. How long should a block be? For a simple on-and-off design, Rik Henson's analyses point to about 16 seconds. Much longer blocks start to look like drift. With a typical high-pass filter at one hundredth of a hertz, two conditions alternating in blocks longer than about 50 seconds lose most of their signal.

The second kind of timetable is the event-related design. Trials come one at a time. Different trial types can be mixed in an unpredictable order, so participants cannot anticipate. And trials can be sorted afterwards by what actually happened. Remembered or forgotten. Correct or wrong. For a long time, people believed that events had to be spaced by 15 seconds or more so that each cloud could clear. Slow, long, and boring experiments.

Then came jitter. In 1999, Anders Dale showed that a fixed short interval is very inefficient because the clouds merge into a flat haze. But if the interval between trials varies randomly from trial to trial, efficiency keeps improving as the mean interval gets shorter. The haze goes up and down. And up and down is exactly what the model needs. A signal that barely varies is hard to detect.

The good news is that efficiency can be computed before you scan. It depends only on your timing, the model, and the question you ask. So tools like OptSeq or genetic algorithms can try thousands of trial orders and keep the most efficient ones.

But there is a trade-off. Thomas Liu and colleagues showed in 2001 that detection power and estimation efficiency pull in opposite directions. Blocks are best to detect an effect but tell you little about the shape of the response. Randomized events are best to estimate the shape but detect less. Semi-random and mixed designs sit in between. Choose according to your question.

Now a trap. Here are two designs with the same length and the same trials. In each trial a cue is followed by a target. In the first design, the target always comes exactly two seconds after the cue. Their two clouds overlap a lot. In the design matrix, the two columns look very much alike, and the model struggles to tell which one caused the response. In the second design, the delay varies between two and eight seconds, and a few cues come alone with no target. Now the columns differ, and both responses can be estimated. And you can see all of this in the design matrix, before a single minute of scanning.

A few practical rules. Scan as long as your participants can stay focused. Keep trials short and frequent. Plan enough trials per condition. Avoid predictable sequences. And always check the efficiency and the correlations of your actual timing files, not of the design you had in mind.

To sum up, the BOLD response is a slow cloud. Blocks of about 16 seconds give the best detection. Jittered events let you mix trials and estimate the shape. Detection and estimation trade off. Correlated regressors cannot be separated, so check your design matrix before you scan. Next time a person in a tube.

PLATE 3.33:47

A person in a tube

Preparing a participant starts with safety: the magnet is always on, so screening for metal, implants and other risks is essential. The loud scanner calls for hearing protection, and a mirror, response box, cushions and squeeze ball make the session workable. Head motion is easier to prevent than to correct, through clear instructions, mock-scanner practice, movies or real-time feedback.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At our station, a night train is waiting. Its sleeping compartment is narrow, long, and very loud. Your participant is about to board. Before the door is closed, there is a security gate, a comfort kit, and a conductor who stays in touch the whole time. Today, a person in a tube.

First, the security gate. Remember, the magnet is always on. Anything made of iron or steel near the scanner can be pulled in with enormous force. Keys, hairpins, a pen, a wheelchair, an oxygen bottle. This is the projectile effect, and it is the reason the screening questionnaire is not a formality. Every participant and everyone who enters the room goes through it. Pacemakers and many other implants, metal fragments in the body, some tattoos and patches, pregnancy, all must be checked against the rules of your site. And when there is a doubt, the answer is no scan today.

The scanner also does two quieter things to the body. The radio waves warm tissue very slightly, and the scanner keeps this within safety limits. And fast gradient switching can sometimes cause a tingling or a small twitch, often in the back or the arms. Warn your participants so that a tingle does not turn into a panic.

Now the noise. Echo-planar imaging is loud. In one study at 3 Tesla, the sound inside the scanner reached between 122 and 131 decibels. Foam earplugs reduce noise by about 25 decibels, and they are usually combined with headphones or pads. Hearing protection is not optional for anyone inside the room.

The comfort kit. A mirror on the head coil lets the participant see a screen behind the scanner. Response boxes without metal collect button presses. Foam cushions wedge the head gently inside the coil. A squeeze ball is always within reach to call for help. Glasses that are safe for the magnet if vision needs correcting. A blanket because the bore can feel cool. And a visit to the bathroom before boarding because the trip is long.

Then the conductor. Between runs you talk through the intercom. Tell the participant what comes next, how long it lasts and what sounds to expect. Some people feel trapped in the tube. Anticipate it. Show the room before, explain everything, and remind them that they can stop at any time. That is part of their consent.

And now the most important lesson of this episode. Head motion is far easier to prevent than to correct. Even a fraction of a millimeter can leave traces that no software fully removes. So prevention starts before the scan. Give clear instructions. Practice in a mock scanner, a replica with the same tunnel and recorded sounds, where children and anxious participants can rehearse lying still.

During the scan there are more tools. In a study with 24 children aged 5 to 15, Deanna Greene and colleagues found that head motion dropped when they watched a movie, and when they received real-time feedback about their movement. The effects were strongest in the youngest children. Software like FIRMM tracks motion in real time, so the team can keep scanning until enough clean data has been collected. One caution, a movie changes brain activity and connectivity, so it is not a neutral rest condition. Choose it on purpose.

To sum up, screen everyone every time because the magnet never sleeps. Protect hearing. Give a comfort kit and stay in touch. Respect the right to stop. And prevent motion with instructions, mock scans, cushions, movies, and feedback, because what moves cannot be fully repaired. Next time, the scans you'll thank yourself for.

PLATE 3.43:38

Scans you'll thank yourself for

A few extra scans taken at every session pay off later. A sharp T1 anatomical image, a field map or a pair of opposite-phase-encoding EPI scans to correct distortions, a single-band reference, pulse and breathing recordings, and stimulus and trigger timings on one clock all matter. A session log records anything unusual, since a few minutes now give a more accurate analysis later.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Our train is ready to leave. The locomotive and its main carriages are your functional runs, the BOLD images you came for. But a few small wagons can still be coupled at the back. They take only minutes. Forget them and you will regret it for months. Today, the scans you'll thank yourself for.

First wagon, the anatomical image. A T1 weighted scan with voxels of about 1 mm in roughly 5 minutes. Functional images are blurry and distorted. The anatomy is sharp. It is used to separate gray matter, white matter, and fluid to reconstruct the cortical surface and to carry your functional data into a standard space. Without it, almost every later step becomes harder.

Second wagon the field map. Remember the price of EPI speed. Near the sinuses and the ear canals, the image stretches along one direction called the phase encoding direction. To correct it, we need to measure the field. There are two common ways. The first is a pair of short EPI scans with opposite phase encoding directions. One is stretched forward, the other backward. A tool like TopUp finds the image in the middle and the field that explains both. The second way measures the phase of two gradient echoes at slightly different echo times. The phase difference gives the field directly.

Either way, it takes about a minute, but it must match your functional runs. Same position, same slices, same readout. And the scanner settings, such as the phase encoding direction and the readout time, must be saved with the data.

What if you forget it? Some tools can estimate the distortion without a field map by bending the functional image towards the anatomy. In fMRIPrep, this option is labeled experimental. It is better than nothing, but it is a guess. A minute today gives you a measurement instead.

Third Wagon a reference image. With multiband sequences, the scanner can also record a single band reference, a quick image with sharper contrast. It is a good anchor for alignment. It is often just one tick box.

Fourth Wagon Physiology. A pulse sensor on a finger and a belt around the chest for breathing. Remember the heartbeat and the breath folded into your signal. With these recordings, models like RETROICOR from Gary Glover and colleagues in 2000 can remove the cardiac and respiratory components. Breathing depth also lets you model the slow carbon dioxide effects we met in Chapter 2. Without the recordings, you can only guess.

Fifth wagon, timing. The scanner sends a pulse at each volume. Your stimulus software should record every one of them, together with every stimulus and every button press on the same clock. Then you know exactly when each event happened in scanner time. Note also how many initial volumes are discarded while the signal settles into a steady state.

And one last item, not a scan at all. A session log. Who was scanned when, which runs, and anything unusual. A cough, a run stopped early, a participant who fell asleep. Six months later, this page will answer questions that no file can.

To sum up, couple these wagons to every session. A sharp anatomy. A field map that matches your runs. A single band reference with multi-band. Pulse and breathing. Every trigger and every response on one clock. And a session log. A few extra minutes today, a more accurate analysis tomorrow. Next time we close this chapter with BIDS from day one.

PLATE 3.53:55

BIDS from day one

BIDS, the Brain Imaging Data Structure, gives raw scanner output a standard address label. Converters such as dcm2niix and HeuDiConv turn DICOM files into NIfTI images with JSON sidecars, files follow key-value names in set folders, and a validator turns green when the dataset is correct. Tools like fMRIPrep and MRIQC, and the OpenNeuro archive, then understand the data without configuration.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every station has a sorting room. Parcels arrive from everywhere in every shape with handwritten addresses. Without rules, nothing reaches its destination. Scanner data arrive the same way. Today, we close chapter 3 with BIDS from day one.

When a session ends, the scanner hands you DICOM files. Often hundreds or thousands of them, sometimes one per slice, with cryptic names and series numbers. Somewhere in there are your anatomy, your field maps, and your functional runs. Six months later, nobody remembers which series was the second run or the one that was stopped early.

BIDS, the Brain Imaging Data Structure, was published in 2016 by Chris Gorgolewski, Russell Poldrack, and many colleagues. It is a simple convention. How to name your files, how to arrange your folders, and how to describe what is inside. Like a standard address label on every parcel.

The first step is conversion. A tool called dcm2niix turns DICOM files into NIfTI images, one file per scan. Next to each image it writes a small JSON file, a sidecar with the acquisition parameters. Repetition time, echo times, slice timing, phase encoding direction. Everything we needed in the last episode saved automatically. HeuDiConv goes one step further. With a short set of rules it decides which series goes where and with which name.

Now the labels. Each subject gets a folder sub-01. Inside the anatomy goes in anat, the functional runs in func and the field maps in fmap. File names are made of key and value pairs. Subject, session, task, run in a suffix that says what the file is. For example sub01 task tapping run1 bold. Anyone anywhere knows what that file contains.

Next to each functional run and events file a simple table. Onset, duration, and trial type in seconds in scanner time. At the top of the data set three files describe the whole collection. A data set description, a participants table, and a readme.

Then comes the inspector. The BIDS validator reads your whole data set and checks names, folders, and required fields. Red means something is missing or misnamed. Green means you are ready.

And green opens doors. Tools that understand BIDS called BIDS apps run on your data with a single command. One input folder, one output folder, one participant label. That is how fMRIPrep and MRIQC work with no configuration of file paths. Their outputs go into a derivatives folder next to the raw data and never mixed with it. OpenNeuro, the open archive, only accepts data sets that pass validation and already shares data from tens of thousands of people. BIDS also has extensions for EEG, MEG, PET, and fNIRS, so the same habits travel across modalities.

A few habits. Convert right after each session, not at the end of the study. Keep the original DICOM files archived and never edit them by hand. Write the events files from your log files with a script, not by copying numbers. And before sharing, remove identifying information. Anatomical images are often defaced, so that a face cannot be reconstructed.

To sum up, raw DICOM files are a pile of parcels. dcm2niix and HeuDiConv convert them into NIfTI images with JSON sidecars. BIDS names and arranges everything with standard labels. The validator turns green. Then fMRIPrep, MRIQC and OpenNeuro understand your data at once. That closes chapter 3. Next time we open chapter 4, preprocessing with the first rule of all. Look at your data first.
