PLATE 1.14:38

From neuron to scalp · The dipole

An EEG trace begins as ion flow at pyramidal-neuron synapses, forming current dipoles. Like a gust bending a whole hillside of trees, the scalp only sees many parallel dipoles active together: slow postsynaptic currents add up, brief spikes cancel. Both geometry and synchrony are needed, and several square centimetres of coordinated cortex are required to make a scalp wave.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every EEG trace starts with something tiny: ions crossing the membrane of a neuron. By the time it reaches the scalp, it is a wave of a few tens of microvolts. In this episode we follow that journey, and we find out what an electrode actually hears.

Picture a forest on a windy hillside. One tree swaying is invisible from the valley. But when a gust bends thousands of trees in the same direction at the same moment, the whole hillside ripples, and you can see it from far away. The EEG is that view from the valley.

The trees of our forest are pyramidal neurons in the cortex. Each one has a long apical dendrite that climbs towards the surface of the brain, like a trunk. And crucially, their trunks are parallel, all perpendicular to the cortical surface.

Now a synapse becomes active on that dendrite. Positive ions flow into the cell at that spot. Seen from outside, current disappears there: a sink. The current travels along the inside of the dendrite and leaks back out further away: a source. A sink at one end, a source at the other. That is a current dipole, a tiny arrow of current with a positive and a negative pole.

Two kinds of electrical events could, in principle, make that arrow. Action potentials, the spikes, and postsynaptic potentials, the slower responses to synaptic input.

Spikes are loud but brief, about a millisecond. Their field looks less like a simple dipole and falls off quickly with distance. And across thousands of neurons, they rarely line up in time, so they mostly cancel out.

Postsynaptic potentials are weaker, but they last tens of milliseconds. Slow events overlap easily in time, so across a population they add up. That is why the scalp EEG is thought to reflect mainly summed synaptic currents in pyramidal cells, not spikes.

So two conditions must meet: the arrows must point the same way, and they must be active at the same time. Geometry and synchrony.

Geometry first. Neurons whose dendrites spread in all directions, like a star, produce fields that cancel themselves out. They form what is called a closed field. Pyramidal cells, with their long parallel trunks, form an open field, and their arrows add up.

Then synchrony. Imagine a crowd clapping. If everyone claps at random, you hear a soft rustle. If they clap in unison, the hall shakes. Synchronous postsynaptic activity in a patch of cortex adds up the same way.

How big must the patch be? In 1965, Ray Cooper and colleagues compared recordings on the brain and on the scalp, and estimated that at least six square centimetres of cortex must be active in near synchrony to show up at standard gains. They also found that, from cortex to scalp, strictly local activity could be attenuated by as much as five thousand to one, while coherent activity over a wide area lost only a factor of about two.

Later work in epilepsy patients went further. In 2005, James Tao and colleagues found that ninety percent of cortical spikes covering more than ten square centimetres appeared on the scalp, against only ten percent of smaller ones.

A modelling study by Murakami and Okada, in 2006, suggested that bursts of tens of thousands of synchronously active pyramidal cells could be detectable. And a 2016 simulation by von Ellenrieder and colleagues added a nuance: many small, scattered, out of phase generators can still produce a measurable scalp signal. Synchrony helps a lot, but the picture is not all or nothing.

Put it together. The EEG is not the voice of single neurons. It is the summed synaptic current of large populations, aligned in space and coordinated in time, seen from a distance. That is its strength, a direct, millisecond view of population activity, and its limit: most of what single neurons do never reaches the electrode.

Let's recap. A synaptic input on a pyramidal dendrite creates a current dipole. Spikes are too brief and too poorly aligned to add up; slow postsynaptic currents do. Parallel geometry and synchrony are both needed. And it takes several square centimetres of coordinated cortex to make a wave on the scalp.

PLATE 1.24:05

Volume conduction · The blurry window

Between cortex and electrode, current crosses fluid, poorly conducting skull and scalp, which smears a coin-sized source into a wide bump, like ink spreading on blotting paper. Every source reaches every electrode and every channel mixes many sources, instantly and linearly. Neighbouring electrodes therefore look correlated and in phase without any communication between the regions beneath them.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we built a current dipole out of thousands of pyramidal neurons. Now that current has to travel. Between the cortex and your electrode, there is fluid, bone and skin. This journey is called volume conduction, and it shapes everything we will ever see in EEG.

Think of a drop of ink falling on blotting paper. The drop is small and sharp. But the paper spreads it out into a soft, wide stain, and by the time you look, you can no longer tell exactly where it landed. The skull is our blotting paper.

Let's cross the layers one by one. First the brain itself, then the cerebrospinal fluid, which conducts electricity very well, about one point eight siemens per metre. Then the skull, a poor conductor. Then the scalp, around a third of a siemens per metre.

The skull is the key layer. How much worse does it conduct than the brain? The classic answer, from measurements by Rush and Driscoll in 1968, was eighty times worse. Later studies on fresh and living skull found much smaller ratios: about fifteen in Oostendorp and colleagues in 2000, and around twenty five in epilepsy patients a few years later. The honest summary is that the ratio is uncertain, and it varies from person to person.

Whatever the exact number, the effect is the same. Current struggles to cross the skull, so it spreads sideways first, inside the fluid and along the bone. A source the size of a coin becomes, on the scalp, a smooth bump many centimetres wide.

Now watch what that means for a cap of electrodes. One single source lights up all of them at once: strongly nearby, weakly far away, and with opposite signs on either side of the dipole. Not one electrode. All of them.

And it works in the other direction too. Each electrode receives a little from every active source in the brain, weighted by distance and orientation. Every channel is a mixture.

Two more properties matter enormously. First, at the frequencies of EEG, volume conduction is effectively instantaneous. There is no travel delay between the source and the electrodes: they all see the same source at exactly the same moment, with zero lag. Second, it is linear. The scalp map of two sources is simply the sum of their two maps.

Put those together, and you can predict a trap. Take two neighbouring electrodes over one active source. Their signals will be strongly correlated, perfectly in phase, and coherent, without any communication between the brain regions beneath them. They are simply listening to the same voice.

So when we see similar signals at two electrodes, the first suspect is always volume conduction. Chapter ten will show how connectivity measures such as the imaginary part of coherency, or the weighted phase lag index, are designed to ignore zero lag interactions for exactly this reason.

And the mixture runs both ways. If every channel is a weighted sum of sources, then recovering the sources means undoing the mixture, with a head model and its uncertain conductivities. That is the inverse problem of chapter thirteen.

Meanwhile, some tools reduce the blur directly. The surface Laplacian, from episode three point thirteen, sharpens local activity and suppresses broad spread. Component methods, like ICA, try to unmix the channels statistically.

Let's recap. Current from cortex crosses fluid, skull and scalp. The skull, a poor conductor with an uncertain conductivity, spreads the signal sideways, so one source reaches every electrode, and every electrode hears many sources. Volume conduction is instantaneous and linear. Neighbouring electrodes look correlated and in phase for free, so similarity is not communication.

Next time, we look at the folds of the cortex, and why some sources are almost invisible to EEG, yet easy for MEG.

PLATE 1.34:09

Orientation matters · Gyri, sulci, EEG and MEG

Pyramidal neurons stand perpendicular to the folded cortex, so dipoles are radial on gyral crowns and tangential in sulcal walls, like sunflowers on a hilly field. EEG sees both, but a tangential source peaks beside its map maximum, not under it. MEG favours tangential sources, and opposite sulcal walls cancel each other, so absent EEG effects do not mean absent activity.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The cortex is not a flat sheet. It is crumpled into ridges, called gyri, and valleys, called sulci. And since pyramidal neurons always stand perpendicular to the local surface, their little arrows point in very different directions depending on where they sit. That turns out to decide what EEG can see, and what MEG can see.

Imagine a garden of sunflowers planted on a hilly field. Every flower faces straight out of the ground beneath it. On top of a hill, they all face the sky. On the slopes, they face sideways, towards each other across the valley. Seen from a drone above, the hilltops shine, and the slopes look very different.

At the crown of a gyrus, the dipole points straight out towards the scalp. We call it radial. In the wall of a sulcus, the dipole lies sideways, parallel to the scalp. We call it tangential.

For EEG, a radial source produces one tidy bump right above it, positive or negative depending on which way the current flows. A tangential source produces a pair: a positive pole on one side and a negative pole on the other, with the source sitting underneath the boundary between them, not under the peak.

That is a classic trap. The maximum of an EEG map is not always above its source. For a tangential dipole, the source is closer to the line where the sign flips.

Now MEG, which measures the tiny magnetic fields of the same currents. Currents create magnetic fields that circle around them. In an idealised spherical head, a perfectly radial source produces no magnetic field outside the head at all. MEG is blind to it. A tangential source, on the other hand, gives MEG a clean signal.

So, roughly: MEG favours the walls of sulci, while EEG sees all orientations. And because the skull barely distorts magnetic fields, MEG maps are also less blurred than EEG maps.

Real heads are not perfect spheres, and the story is more nuanced. In 2002, Arjan Hillebrand and Gareth Barnes computed MEG sensitivity over realistic cortical surfaces. They found that the truly invisible regions are thin strips, about two millimetres wide, along the crests of gyri, a small fraction of the cortex. For MEG, depth matters more than orientation.

Folding has a second consequence, which hurts both techniques: cancellation. Picture activity spreading over both walls of a sulcus. The two walls face each other, so their dipoles point in opposite directions. Their fields cancel, and much of the signal vanishes before it reaches any sensor.

Seppo Ahlfors and colleagues quantified this in 2010, on a realistic cortex. Cancellation was prominent for both EEG and MEG, even with only a few simultaneously active sources. And for large patches of activity, EEG and MEG cancelled differently, because radial and tangential parts are affected differently.

What does that mean in practice? First, a big brain response can produce a small scalp signal if its geometry cancels itself. Absence of an EEG effect is not absence of activity. Second, EEG and MEG are complementary. Each sees some sources better than the other, and recording both, then analysing them together, gives a more complete picture.

Let's recap. Pyramidal dipoles stand perpendicular to the folded cortex: radial on gyral crowns, tangential in sulcal walls. EEG sees all orientations, but a tangential source peaks beside it, not above it. MEG is nearly blind to radial sources, but sees tangential ones sharply, with less skull blur. Opposite walls of a sulcus cancel each other, for both techniques. So EEG and MEG are partners, not rivals.

Next time, a short history of listening to brains, from Richard Caton's galvanometer to wireless headsets.

PLATE 1.44:32

A short history of listening to brains

A timeline from Caton's 1875 galvanometer recordings in animal brains to Berger's first human EEG in 1924, published in 1929 with alpha and beta rhythms. Adrian and Matthews confirmed it in 1934, and the ten twenty system of 1958 made recordings comparable across labs. Paper traces gave way to digital, dense and wireless EEG, but the basic recipe, scalp differences amplified over time, is unchanged.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Today, a short trip through time. A century and a half ago, nobody knew that the brain produced electricity you could measure. Today, you can record it with a wireless headset in a park. Let's walk along that timeline, one postcard at a time.

Our first stop is Liverpool, in 1875. The physician and physiologist Richard Caton places electrodes on the exposed brains of rabbits and monkeys. With a reflecting galvanometer, whose tiny mirror throws a spot of light onto the wall, he sees feeble currents that fluctuate on their own, and that change when the animal sees a light. He reports it in the British Medical Journal.

Fifteen years later, in 1890, the Polish physiologist Adolf Beck confirms and extends this work in rabbits and dogs, and notices rhythmic oscillations that a flash of light can interrupt.

In 1912, the Ukrainian physiologist Vladimir Pravdich Neminsky publishes the first photographed recording of a mammal's brain activity, from a dog, using a string galvanometer. Two years later, Napoleon Cybulski and his colleague photograph recordings of experimentally induced seizures.

Now Jena, in Germany. The psychiatrist Hans Berger has been chasing a strange dream for years: to find a physical trace of mental life, even of telepathy. In 1924, he finally records electrical activity from a human head. It takes him another five years, and better galvanometers, before he dares to publish, in 1929.

In that first report, Berger describes a regular wave of around ten cycles per second, which he calls alpha, faster and smaller waves he calls beta, and a crucial observation: the alpha rhythm fades when the eyes open.

At first, almost nobody takes him seriously. Then, in 1934, two Cambridge physiologists, Edgar Adrian and Bryan Matthews, repeat his experiments, partly expecting to refute them. Instead, they confirm the rhythm, and the field takes off. The same year, the first epileptic spikes are described, and within a few years, clinical EEG laboratories open.

As labs multiply, a new problem appears: everyone places electrodes differently. In 1958, Herbert Jasper publishes the ten twenty system on behalf of the International Federation. Electrodes are placed at ten and twenty percent intervals of measured distances between landmarks on the skull. Suddenly, a recording in Montreal can be compared with one in Tokyo.

For decades, EEG is ink on paper: pens scratching across folding sheets, miles of them. In the second half of the twentieth century, computers arrive. Digital recording, then averaging of evoked potentials, then spectral analysis become routine.

The caps get denser. In the nineteen nineties, nets of a hundred and twenty eight, then two hundred and fifty six electrodes appear, opening the door to topographic mapping and source imaging.

And the devices shrink. Amplifiers move into the cap itself, dry electrodes skip the gel, and wireless headsets let people walk, cycle, and even talk to each other while being recorded.

Watch the hardware on our timeline: from a room sized galvanometer projecting a spot of light on a wall, to a headset that fits in a pocket.

Yet the basic recipe has not changed since Berger. Electrodes on the scalp, a difference between two points, amplified and written down over time. What changed is how many points, how precisely, and what we are able to compute afterwards. Most of this series is about that last part.

Let's recap. Caton recorded brain currents in animals in 1875. Pravdich Neminsky photographed a dog's EEG in 1912. Berger recorded the first human EEG in 1924, published in 1929, and named alpha and beta. Adrian and Matthews confirmed it in 1934. Jasper's ten twenty system, in 1958, made recordings comparable. Then came digital, dense and mobile EEG.

Next time, we zoom in on the very first link of the chain: where metal meets skin, the electrode.

PLATE 1.54:29

Electrodes · Where metal meets skin

The electrode is a border post translating ionic current in the body into electrons in the wire. Silver silver chloride is non polarizable, and the skin interface behaves like a resistor, capacitor and half cell battery. High impedance does not shrink the brain signal but invites drift and mains hum, which active electrodes tolerate better than dry ones.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Everything we have seen so far happens inside the head. Now the current reaches the outside world, and the very first link of the chain is a small disk of metal pressed against skin. It looks trivial. It is not. Many noisy recordings are lost right here.

Think of a bridge between two countries that speak different languages. Inside the body, electricity is carried by ions swimming in salty water. Inside the wire, it is carried by electrons. The electrode is the border post where one must be translated into the other, and every border post has its queues and its tolls.

The classic research electrode is silver coated with silver chloride. At its surface, a chemical reaction swaps ions for electrons smoothly, in both directions. That is why it is called non polarizable: it lets slow signals through without building up charge.

Between the metal and the skin sits an electrolyte gel. It fills the gaps, wets the outer layer of the skin, and creates a continuous conductive path.

Engineers like to draw this interface as a tiny circuit. A resistor and a capacitor side by side, for the layers of skin and gel, plus a small battery. That battery is the half cell potential: a steady voltage that appears whenever a metal meets an electrolyte. If it drifts, because the gel dries, sweat appears or the electrode moves, the drift shows up in your signal as slow waves that have nothing to do with the brain.

The combined opposition to current flow through this interface is called impedance. It is measured in kilo ohms, and it depends mostly on the outermost layer of skin, dead cells and oils. Preparing the skin, gently abrading it and working the gel in, lowers it.

Why does impedance matter, if the brain signal itself is not reduced? Because high impedance invites noise. Skin potentials, slow drifts and movement artifacts all grow when the contact is poor. And if two electrodes have very different impedances, the amplifier becomes worse at rejecting interference common to both, like mains hum.

How much does it matter in practice? In 2010, Emily Kappenman and Steven Luck recorded the same participants with low and high impedance electrodes side by side. High impedance increased low frequency noise, especially in a warm, humid room, and more trials were needed to reach significance for the P3. A cool, dry room, high pass filtering and artifact rejection reduced the damage.

Now a small zoo of electrodes. Passive wet electrodes, the classic: a metal disk, gel, and a long cable to the amplifier. They need low impedance, traditionally a few kilo ohms, because the cable picks up interference along the way.

Active electrodes carry a tiny amplifier right on the electrode. The signal is boosted before it travels down the cable, so cable movement and nearby interference matter much less. That is why active systems tolerate higher impedance.

Saline electrodes replace gel with sponges soaked in salt water. Quick to apply, but they dry out within an hour or so.

Dry electrodes skip the liquid entirely, often with little pins that reach through the hair. They are fast and comfortable for mobile and consumer uses, but their impedance is very high. In a 2017 comparison by Kyle Mathewson and colleagues, dry active electrodes could measure spectra and ERP components, but with more noise, so more trials were needed.

So the choice of electrode is a trade off between setup time, comfort, mobility and noise. A quick cap for a demo, a careful gel cap for a subtle ERP effect.

Let's recap. The electrode translates ionic currents into electronic ones. Silver silver chloride is non polarizable and stable. The skin interface behaves like a resistor, a capacitor and a half cell battery, and impedance measures how hard it is for current to cross. High impedance does not shrink the brain signal, but it invites low frequency noise and hum. Active electrodes tolerate it better; dry electrodes trade data quality for convenience.

Next time, we follow the signal into the amplifier, and discover that in EEG, there is no zero.

PLATE 1.64:27

The amplifier · There is no zero

An EEG amplifier is differential: like measuring waves against a boat's deck, it amplifies only the difference between each electrode and a reference. Mains hum swings the whole body and is common to all inputs, so a high common mode rejection ratio removes it, provided impedances are balanced. Every channel is therefore a difference between two places, and EEG has no absolute zero.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The electrode has turned ionic current into a voltage on a wire. Now that voltage needs to grow, from a few microvolts to something a computer can read. That is the job of the amplifier. And the way it works hides one of the most important facts in EEG: there is no zero.

Imagine measuring the height of waves from a boat. The boat itself rises and falls with the swell. If you measure the water against the boat's deck, the swell disappears from your reading, and only the small ripples between two points remain. An EEG amplifier does something similar: it only cares about differences.

Let's open the box. Each channel has two inputs. One is connected to the electrode you are interested in. The other is connected to a reference electrode. The amplifier multiplies the difference between the two. Whatever the reference does is subtracted from every channel.

There is usually a third electrode too, often called ground, or in some systems a driven electrode. It does not appear in the signal. Its job is to hold the body close to the amplifier's own electrical level, so that both inputs stay within the range the amplifier can handle.

Why not just measure each electrode on its own, against some absolute zero? Because there is none. A voltage is always the difference between two points. And on a living body, surrounded by mains wiring, there is no quiet point anywhere.

Your body acts like an antenna. Mains electricity in the walls and lights couples into it capacitively, and your whole body swings up and down at fifty or sixty hertz, with an amplitude that dwarfs the brain's few microvolts. The good news: this swing is almost identical at every electrode. It is common to both inputs. We call it common mode.

A differential amplifier rejects what is common to both inputs and amplifies what differs. How well it does this is measured by the common mode rejection ratio, the C M R R, expressed in decibels. Clinical guidelines from the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society ask for at least ninety decibels. Ninety decibels means the common signal is attenuated more than thirty thousand times relative to the difference.

This is where the electrode episode comes back. Rejection only works if both inputs see the common signal in exactly the same way. If one electrode has a much higher impedance than the other, part of the common mode is converted into a difference, and mains hum leaks into the channel. Balanced impedances matter as much as low ones.

Modern amplifiers also have a very high input impedance, many megaohms, so that they draw almost no current from the electrodes. That makes them much more tolerant of imperfect skin contact than the amplifiers of the past.

Now, the consequence that will follow us through the whole series. Every EEG channel is a difference between two places on the head. A channel labelled Pz is really Pz minus the reference. If the reference electrode sits over active brain tissue, its activity is subtracted from every channel, and appears, inverted, everywhere.

Some systems record against one physical electrode, such as Cz or a mastoid. Some record against a combination. Some, like BioSemi systems, record every electrode against a common mode sense electrode, and expect you to choose the reference afterwards. In all cases, what you save is a set of differences, and you can re-reference later by subtracting a new common signal. Episode three point twelve showed what that choice does to your maps.

Let's recap. An EEG amplifier is differential: it amplifies the difference between each electrode and a reference. A ground or driven electrode keeps the body within the amplifier's range. Mains interference is common to all inputs, and the common mode rejection ratio measures how well it is rejected, at least ninety decibels in clinical guidelines. Impedances must be balanced for that rejection to work. And because every channel is a difference, there is no absolute zero in EEG.

Next time, we turn that amplified voltage into numbers: resolution, dynamic range, sampling, and the filter that must come first.

PLATE 1.74:33

Digitizing · From voltage to numbers

Digitization cuts the smooth amplified voltage into a staircase in time and amplitude. The sampling rate sets step width, with an anti aliasing filter acting before sampling, and bits over an input range set step height. Wide ranges with twenty four bits let offsets be recorded and removed later, but saturation clips the signal flat and loses it for good.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The amplifier gives us a smooth, continuous voltage. But a computer cannot store a smooth curve. It stores numbers, a finite list of them. So the last step of the hardware chain is to cut the signal into steps, in two directions at once: in time, and in amplitude. This is digitization, and it sets hard limits that no analysis can lift later.

Picture a staircase built over a hillside. The hill is smooth. The staircase only has steps of a fixed width and a fixed height. The width of each step is the time between samples. The height of each step is the smallest voltage change you can record. And the staircase has a top floor and a bottom floor: anything outside them simply does not fit.

Start with time. The converter takes a snapshot of the voltage at regular intervals: the sampling rate. Two hundred and fifty, five hundred, a thousand or two thousand samples per second are common in EEG research. As we saw in episode two point five, you can only represent frequencies up to half the sampling rate, the Nyquist frequency.

That is why the amplifier contains an anti aliasing filter, a low pass filter that acts on the analog signal, before sampling. Anything faster than Nyquist that slips through would fold back and pretend to be a slower rhythm. Once it has been sampled, it can never be separated again. The order matters: filter first, then sample.

Now amplitude. The analog to digital converter maps each voltage to one of a finite number of levels. With n bits, there are two to the power n levels. Sixteen bits give about sixty five thousand. Twenty four bits give almost seventeen million.

Those levels are spread over the input range. The step size, the least significant bit, is the range divided by the number of levels. A concrete example: the BioSemi ActiveTwo uses twenty four bits over an input range of plus or minus two hundred and sixty two millivolts. Its step is thirty one point two five nanovolts, far below the brain's microvolts.

Why such a wide range, if EEG is only tens of microvolts? Because the electrodes themselves carry slow offset voltages, the half cell potentials of episode one point five, which can reach tens of millivolts. A wide range lets the amplifier record everything, offsets included, without a high pass filter, and you remove the offsets afterwards. Older designs used a narrower range and a hardware high pass filter instead, to keep those offsets out.

Notice the trade off. For a fixed number of bits, a wider range means bigger steps. Modern twenty four bit converters make the trade almost painless. And in practice, the amplifier's own noise, around half a microvolt, is usually much larger than the step size.

Now the dangerous part: the top and bottom floors of the staircase. If the voltage leaves the input range, the converter saturates. The recorded signal sits flat at the maximum or minimum value, a clipped plateau. This happens when an electrode pops off, when a cable is pulled, or when offsets drift too far.

And here is the hard rule. A saturated stretch contains no information about the brain. No filter, no ICA, no clever algorithm can rebuild it. The only cure is prevention: good contact, stable electrodes, and watching the offsets during recording.

A last practical point: each sample is a number, so the data volume grows quickly. Sixty four channels at a thousand samples per second, in twenty four bits, is about two hundred kilobytes every second, around twelve megabytes per minute. Choose a sampling rate that fits your question, not the maximum the amplifier allows.

Let's recap. Digitization cuts the signal in time, with the sampling rate, and in amplitude, with the number of bits over an input range. The anti aliasing filter must act before sampling. The step size is the range divided by two to the power of the number of bits. Wide ranges with many bits let modern amplifiers record offsets without filtering. And saturation is final: a clipped signal cannot be recovered.

Next time, we put the whole chain together in an exploded view, and tour the zoo of EEG systems, from high density nets to consumer headbands.

PLATE 1.84:39

The hardware, exploded · A zoo of systems

A research EEG setup is a chain: cap, electrodes, amplifier with converter, a trigger interface writing event markers into the data, and acquisition and stimulation computers. Like different bicycles, the systems trade channels, contact quality, setup time, mobility and price, from high density nets and clinical arrays to mobile and consumer headbands. The right one fits the question and its details are reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have followed the signal from a neuron to a number. Time to step back and look at the whole machine at once, taken apart piece by piece, like an exploded diagram. Then we will visit a small zoo of EEG systems, and see that each one is a different compromise.

Think of bicycles. A road racer, a mountain bike, a city bike and a folding bike all have two wheels and pedals. But each sacrifices something to excel at something else: speed, ruggedness, comfort or portability. EEG systems are the same.

Let's explode a typical research setup. At the top, the cap: a stretchy fabric that holds the electrodes at standard positions. Then the electrodes themselves, with their gel, or their sponges, or their pins.

Cables, or a small headbox, lead to the amplifier, where the differences are amplified, filtered against aliasing, and digitized.

Next to it sits a box that is easy to forget and crucial to get right: the trigger interface. Every time the stimulus computer shows an image or plays a sound, it sends a code, a marker, that is written into the EEG at that exact sample. Without markers, an event related analysis is impossible.

Finally, the acquisition computer stores the data, and a second computer usually runs the experiment. Episode six point three will show why their clocks, and the delay between a trigger and what actually appears on screen, deserve a careful check.

Now the zoo. First, high density research systems: a hundred and twenty eight to two hundred and fifty six electrodes, often in a geodesic net that covers the whole head, down to the cheeks and neck. They sample the scalp field finely, which helps source imaging and average referencing. The price: long setup, many channels to check, and a lot of data.

Second, the standard research cap, typically thirty two to sixty four electrodes on the extended ten twenty system. The workhorse for event related potentials and time frequency studies.

Third, clinical systems. For routine diagnosis, the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology recommends an array of twenty five electrodes, the ten twenty positions plus a lower temporal row, described by Margitta Seeck and colleagues in 2017. Clinical systems are built for long, robust recordings, often with synchronized video, rather than for experimental flexibility.

Fourth, mobile EEG. Small wireless amplifiers, sometimes clipped to the cap, with eight to sixty four channels. They let people walk, cycle, play music or interact with each other outside the lab. The cost: more movement artifacts, and less control over the environment.

Fifth, consumer headbands. A handful of dry electrodes, often on the forehead and behind the ears, connected to a phone. Cheap, quick and comfortable, perfect for demos, meditation feedback or very large samples. But with so few channels in fixed places, and with dry contacts, they cannot do what a research cap does.

Let's put them in one table. Rows: number of channels, contact quality, setup time, mobility, price. High density wins on channels, loses on setup time. Clinical wins on robustness. Mobile wins on mobility. Consumer wins on price and setup, and loses on almost everything else.

There is no best system. There is a system that fits your question. Need sources or fine topographies? Go dense. A classic ERP effect? A good thirty two or sixty four channel cap. Brains in the wild? Mobile. Thousands of participants at home, for a simple measure? Perhaps a headband, with its limits clearly stated.

And whatever you choose, report it: manufacturer, number and placement of electrodes, electrode type, reference, sampling rate and hardware filters. Without that, nobody can judge or reproduce what you measured.

Let's recap. A research EEG system is a chain: cap, electrodes, amplifier with its converter, a trigger interface, and computers for stimulation and acquisition. Systems range from high density nets to consumer headbands, each trading channels, contact quality, setup time, mobility and price. Pick the one your question needs, and report its details.
