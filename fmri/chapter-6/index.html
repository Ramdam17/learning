PLATE 6.14:14

The brain at rest

The brain uses about 20% of the body's energy and barely more during a task. Biswal found that slow BOLD fluctuations in the left motor cortex correlate with the right motor cortex at rest, and Raichle described a default mode network more active at rest. Resting networks match those used in tasks, though uncontrolled thoughts, sleep and motion make rest easy to misread.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to Chapter 6. Our new world is a city at night. No parade, no concert, no event. And yet the city is not dark. Windows light up and dim all the time. Watch long enough and you notice something strange. Some windows far apart seem to flicker together.

For the first chapters, we treated the brain like a stage. Show a stimulus, measure the response, and treat everything else as noise. But the brain never switches off. It uses about 20% of the body's energy while weighing about 2% of its mass. And this consumption is high at rest. A task adds remarkably little on top, often less than 5%.

So what is the brain doing when we ask it to do nothing? In 1995, Bharat Biswal and colleagues looked at exactly that. They scanned people lying still, doing no task, and focused on the slow fluctuations of the BOLD signal, below 0.1 Hz. These had always been treated as noise.

Then they took a time series from the left motor cortex and correlated it with every other voxel. The right motor cortex lit up, along with other motor regions. Two areas that worked together during finger tapping were also fluctuating together at rest, with nobody tapping anything. Like two windows on opposite sides of town, dimming and brightening in the same rhythm.

This is resting state functional connectivity, correlation in spontaneous slow fluctuations between regions that belong to the same system.

The second surprise came from the other direction. When people do almost any demanding task, some regions consistently decrease their activity. In 2001, Marcus Raichle and colleagues described these regions as a network more active at rest than during tasks. They called it the default mode of brain function. Today we speak of the default mode network. It includes the medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate and precuneus, and the angular gyrus. It is linked to thinking about oneself, remembering the past, and imagining the future.

Back to our city, the motor district has its own rhythm. The visual district at the back has another one. The default mode is like the residential neighborhood, busiest when the offices are closed.

Over the following years, resting state studies found a whole family of such networks. Visual, somatomotor, auditory, attention networks, a salience network, and frontoparietal control networks. They are reproducible across people and across sites.

And here is the striking part. In 2009, Stephen Smith and colleagues compared two very different things. On one side, resting state networks from 36 people lying still. On the other side, activation maps from thousands of task experiments gathered in the BrainMap database. They decomposed both. And the main networks matched. The brain at rest replays in slow motion the networks it uses in action.

So rest is not nothing. It is organized activity, and it carries the shape of the brain's functional architecture.

Rest is also practical. It needs no task so it works with children with patients who cannot follow instructions even during sleep or anesthesia. 10 minutes of rest are easy to add to any study, and huge open datasets exist.

But rest comes with its own cautions. What participants do in their heads is not controlled. Eyes open or closed changes the signal. People fall asleep more often than you think. And as we will see in Episode 3, head motion and breathing can create correlations that look like networks. Rest is a powerful view, but it is easy to fool.

To sum up, the brain never switches off, and most of its energy goes to ongoing activity. Biswal and colleagues showed that slow fluctuations at rest are correlated within the motor system. Raichle and colleagues described the default mode network, more active at rest. Resting networks match the networks used in tasks. Next time, measuring connectivity, seeds, parcels, and matrices.

PLATE 6.24:08

Measuring connectivity

A seed map correlates one region's time series with every voxel, while a parcellation reduces the brain to stations whose pairwise correlations fill a connectivity matrix. Full correlation picks up shared inputs, like windows flickering to the same broadcast, whereas partial correlation removes them. A link is a co-fluctuation, not a cable, and the parcellation changes the matrix.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, two windows flickered together across the city. Today, we turn that observation into a measurement. How do you put a number on connectivity?

The simplest method is the one Biswal used, a seed. Choose a small region, for example the posterior cingulate. Average its time series. Then correlate that series with the time series of every other voxel. The result is a seed map. Bright where the brain fluctuates with the seed, dark where it does not. A seed in the posterior cingulate gives you the default mode network in one picture.

Seed maps are easy to compute and easy to read. But they answer only one question at a time, who goes along with this region? To see the whole city, we need another view.

So we divide the brain into regions called parcels. Think of the metro map of our city. Each station is a parcel. For each parcel, we average the voxels inside, which gives one time series per station. Then we correlate every station with every other station. The result is a connectivity matrix. With 100 parcels that is a 100 by 100 table and almost 5000 unique pairs.

Correlations are usually converted with the Fisher Z transform before averaging or comparing them, so they behave better statistically.

Now the first choice that matters, which parcellation. Anatomical atlases follow folds and landmarks. Functional atlases follow the boundaries found in resting data. For example, the Schaefer parcellation comes in versions from 100 to 1,000 parcels. The multimodal parcellation by Matthew Glasser and colleagues from the Human Connectome Project has 360 cortical areas. Change the parcellation and you change the nodes, the signals, and the matrix. Two studies with different atlases are not drawing the same metro map.

The second choice, full or partial correlation. Imagine that tonight there is a big football match. In thousands of apartments, the television flickers with the same goals. Two windows and two buildings flash together. Are these neighbors talking to each other? No. They are both watching the same broadcast. A full correlation sees a link. A partial correlation asks a sharper question. Do these two windows still flicker together once we remove what every other window explains? The shared broadcast is explained away, and the false link disappears.

Partial correlation estimates more direct relations, but it needs more data because it uses all regions at once, and it is usually computed with regularization, a mathematical brake that keeps the estimate stable. In comparisons of network modeling methods, regularized partial correlation often performed well.

Whatever the method, keep in mind what a link means. A strong correlation between two regions is a co-fluctuation. It is not a cable. Two regions can fluctuate together without a direct anatomical connection through a third region, a shared input, or even a shared artifact. And the absence of correlation does not prove the absence of a fiber.

Practical habits. Report the parcellation, its version, and the number of parcels. State whether you used full or partial correlation and which regularization. Say which denoising came before because, as we will see next time, it changes everything. And when you compare groups, use the same atlas, the same preprocessing, and enough data per person. 10 minutes of rest gives a noisy matrix, and more data makes it much more stable.

To sum up, a seed map shows which voxels fluctuate with one region. A parcellation turns the brain into stations, and their pairwise correlations fill a connectivity matrix. The choice of parcellation changes the matrix. Full correlation sees shared broadcasts, partial correlation removes them. And a link is a co-fluctuation, not a cable. PLATE 6.34:29

The traps of connectivity

Head motion raises short-distance and lowers long-distance correlations even after correction, so groups that move differently can show purely artifactual connectivity differences. Strong denoising and motion as a covariate help. Global signal regression removes shared fluctuations but forces correlations around zero and can create anticorrelations, so conclusions should be tested with and without it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Connectivity is beautiful. It is also one of the easiest measures in fMRI to fool. Today, three traps, head motion, the global signal, and the groups we compare.

Imagine photographing our night city from a hill with a camera on a wobbly tripod. When the tripod shakes, nearby windows smear into each other. They look more alike than they are. And the subtle shared rhythm of two windows far apart gets drowned in the blur.

That is what head motion does to connectivity. In 2012, two papers changed the field. Jonathan Power and colleagues showed that subject motion creates spurious but systematic correlations. Short-distance correlations go up, long-distance correlations go down. And this happens even after motion correction and regression of the motion parameters.

At the same time, Koene Van Dijk, Mert Sabuncu and Randy Buckner showed that people who move more have weaker long-range connectivity, for example within the default mode network. The effect appeared even when the difference in average motion between groups was tiny, a few thousandths of a millimeter.

Why is this so dangerous? Because the pattern looks biological. Weaker long-range connections and stronger local ones is exactly the kind of story people tell about development, aging, or disorders.

Which brings us to the third trap, the one that hurts most. Groups often differ in how much they move. Children move more than adults. Many clinical groups move more than controls. So imagine two groups with a connectivity difference. You match them for motion or you include motion properly in the model and the difference vanishes. It was never a difference in the brain. It was a difference in the tripod.

What helps? First prevent motion, comfort, padding, practice short runs and for children movies or mock scanners. Then measure it frame by frame with frame-wise displacement. Use a strong denoising strategy as in chapter 4, many motion regressors, CompCor or ICA-AROMA, and scrubbing or censoring of the worst volumes. Check the result with quality control plots. For example, correlate each connection with participants' mean motion across the group. After good denoising, that correlation should be close to zero and should not depend on distance. Finally, report the motion of each group and include it as a covariate.

Now the second trap, the global signal. Back to the city. Sometimes the whole grid dims for a moment and every window flickers together. In the brain, the average signal over all voxels, the global signal, carries such shared fluctuations. Some come from motion and breathing, some from changes in arousal and some may be neural.

Global signal regression removes that average from every voxel. It is very effective against motion artifacts and respiration, but it has a cost. In 2009, Kevin Murphy and colleagues showed mathematically that it forces the correlations to be centered around zero. Some regions then appear negatively correlated, anticorrelated, partly because of the regression itself. The famous anticorrelation between the default mode network and attention networks became the center of a long debate.

So is global signal regression right or wrong? The honest answer is, it depends on the question and it changes the meaning of the numbers. Many labs now run their analyses both with and without it and report whether the conclusions hold. What you must not do is choose the version that gives the result you like.

Breathing deserves a final word. Changes in breathing depth and rhythm alter carbon dioxide in the blood, which changes the BOLD signal across large parts of the brain. Deep breaths and yawns leave widespread traces. Recording respiration, as we advised in Chapter 3, lets you see and model them.

To sum up, head motion increases short distance and decreases long distance correlations, even after correction. Groups that move differently can show connectivity differences that are pure artifact. Prevent measure denoise check and report. Global signal regression is powerful but shifts correlations and can create anticorrelations. Test your conclusions with and without it. PLATE 6.44:30

ICA · Unmixing the brain

Each voxel is a mixture of sources, like a microphone at a cocktail party. Spatial independent component analysis unmixes the data into independent maps with time courses, and the number of components sets the zoom level. Some components are networks, others motion, vessels or fluid, so the same method finds networks in resting data and removes noise in preprocessing.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Tonight our city is noisy. A street band plays on one square. The metro rumbles underground. A dog barks somewhere. On every balcony, someone has left a microphone running. Each microphone records a mixture of all these sounds, in different proportions depending on where it sits. Could you recover each sound pure from the mixtures alone? That is the cocktail party problem. And it is exactly the problem of a voxel.

Each voxel's time series is a mixture. A bit of one network, a bit of another, plus head motion, a pulsing vessel, the cerebrospinal fluid scanner artifacts. Seeds and parcels from episode 2 make us choose regions in advance. Independent component analysis, or ICA, takes the opposite road. It assumes nothing about where things are. It looks for a small number of sources that, mixed together explain the data.

In fMRI, we usually run spatial ICA. The data are a big table, voxels by time. Spatial ICA splits it into components. Each component has a spatial map where it lives and a time course how it fluctuates. The rule that guides the split is independence. The algorithm looks for maps that are as statistically independent from each other as possible. Mixtures look Gaussian ordinary, while pure sources tend to look less Gaussian. So the algorithm pushes each map away from Gaussian, and that pulls the sources apart.

ICA was first applied to fMRI by Martin McKeown and colleagues in 1998. FSL's tool, Melodic, implements a probabilistic version described by Christian Beckmann and Stephen Smith in 2004. For groups, maps can be estimated across many participants at once, then brought back to each person, for example with dual regression.

You must choose how many components to extract. With about 20, you get the large resting networks, visual, somatomotor, default mode, attention, control. With a hundred or more, those networks split into smaller pieces. Neither is the true number. It is a zoom level and you should say which one you used.

Now the moment that defines ICA in practice. Out come dozens of components and some are networks while others are noise. A motion component often shows a ring at the edge of the brain. A vessel component follows the big veins and sinuses. A cerebrospinal fluid component sits in the ventricles. Their time courses also differ. Real networks fluctuate slowly. Many artifacts carry fast jitter or sudden jumps.

Sorting components is a skill that can be learned, and it should be done with explicit criteria. In 2017, Ludovica Griffanti and colleagues published a practical guide with examples of signal and noise components from many datasets, including the UK Biobank and the Human Connectome Project. Look at the map, the time course, and its frequency spectrum together.

Classifying by hand is slow, so automatic classifiers exist. FIX, from Gholamreza Salimi-Khorshidi and colleagues in 2014, learns from hand-labeled examples and then labels new components. ICA-AROMA, which we met in Chapter 4, targets motion components with a few simple features. Even with automatic tools, check a sample of components by eye, especially on a new kind of data.

So the same method has two jobs. In a resting state study, ICA finds the networks. In preprocessing, ICA finds the noise, and you remove its time courses from the data. Same unmixing, different destination.

Some cautions. ICA components are sorted by an algorithm, and their order and sign can change between runs. Results depend on the number of components and on preprocessing, and a component that looks like a network is still a statistical source, not a proof of a single biological process.

To sum up, a voxel is a mixture of sources, like a microphone at a cocktail party. Spatial ICA unmixes the data into independent maps and their time courses. The number of components sets the zoom. Some components are networks, others are motion, vessels, or fluid, and sorting them is a learnable skill. The same tool finds networks and cleans data. Next time, graphs of the brain.

PLATE 6.54:01

Graphs of the brain

Graph theory turns a connectivity matrix into nodes and edges, like bus stops and routes: degree finds hubs, modularity finds neighborhoods, and high clustering with short paths defines a small world. These measures depend on threshold, density and node count, so one brain can look very or barely modular. Groups should be compared at matched density against null models.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We now have a connectivity matrix, a table of numbers. Graph theory turns that table into a map and gives us words to describe its shape.

Think of the city's night buses. The bus stops are nodes. A route between two stops is an edge. In a brain graph, the nodes are our parcels and the edges come from the connectivity matrix. A strong correlation becomes a link.

The first decision is how to turn numbers into links. The simplest graph is binary. Keep an edge if the correlation is above a threshold, drop it otherwise. Or keep a fixed proportion of the strongest edges, a fixed density, for example the top 10%. Or keep all edges with their weights, a weighted graph. Each choice gives a different graph from the same matrix.

Then come the measures. The degree of a node is its number of links. A node with many links connecting different parts of the city is a hub, like the central station. In brain graphs, regions of the default mode and frontoparietal networks such as the posterior cingulate and precuneus often come out as hubs.

Modules are groups of nodes more connected to each other than to the rest, like neighborhoods with many local buses. Modularity, often written Q, measures how well a graph splits into such groups. Resting state graphs are usually quite modular, and the modules look a lot like the networks we met in episode 1.

The clustering coefficient asks whether your neighbors are also neighbors of each other. The path length counts how many hops it takes, on average, to go from any stop to any other. A small world network, a term made famous by Duncan Watts and Stephen Strogatz in 1998, has high clustering like a lattice and short paths like a random graph. A few express buses link distant neighborhoods. Brain networks, both anatomical and functional, have been described as small world, a story told in a widely read review by Ed Bullmore and Olaf Sporns in 2009.

Now the trap and it is a big one. Graph measures depend strongly on how many nodes and edges you have. Watch this simulated brain graph with the same underlying matrix. Keep only the strongest 5% of edges and the modules stand out and modularity is high. Keep 40% and many weak links bridge the neighborhoods and modularity drops. Same brain, same data, two different stories.

This matters most when comparing groups. If one group has weaker overall connectivity, a fixed threshold gives it fewer edges and almost every graph measure changes just because the density changed. Bernadette van Wijk and colleagues showed in 2010 how measures like clustering and path length depend on the number of nodes and on the average degree, and that comparing networks of different size and density is genuinely difficult. The same goes for the parcellation. 100 nodes and 400 nodes give different values.

So, good habits. Compare graphs that match density, not at a single raw threshold. Show results across a range of densities rather than at one convenient point. Compare against null models, random graphs with the same degrees to know what is special. Report the parcellation, the number of nodes, and the thresholding rule. And remember that a hub in a correlation graph is a region that co-fluctuates with many others. That is interesting, but it is not proof of a cable or of control.

To sum up, graph theory turns a connectivity matrix into nodes and edges. Degree finds hubs, modularity finds neighborhoods, and clustering with short paths defines a small world. But graph measures depend on the threshold, the density, and the number of nodes. The same brain can look very or barely modular. Compare at matched density across ranges against null models. PLATE 6.64:40

Who drives whom? · Effective connectivity

Functional connectivity is symmetric; effective connectivity asks about direction and context. Psychophysiological interaction tests whether coupling changes with the task, and dynamic causal modeling compares a few explicit circuits of hidden neural activity. Granger causality on raw fMRI is fooled by slow, variable hemodynamic delays, like a slow bulb hiding which switch came first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Everything we have measured so far in this chapter is symmetric. If A correlates with B, B correlates with A. Functional connectivity tells us who moves together, not who drives whom. To ask about direction, influence, and context, we need effective connectivity. It is our last network episode and the hardest.

The gentlest step is called psychophysiological interaction, or PPI, introduced by Carl Friston and colleagues in 1997. It asks, does the coupling between two regions change with the task? Back to the night city, two windows across the street flicker together, but only during the football match. On a normal evening, they ignore each other. The coupling depends on context.

In practice, you take the time course of a seed region, the time course of the task, and their product, the interaction. You put all three into a GLM for every voxel. A voxel that loads on the interaction term is more coupled to the seed during one condition than another. A generalized version, GPPI, handles designs with several conditions. PPI is easy to run, but the interaction term is noisy, and it says nothing about direction. It tells you that coupling changes, not who changed it.

The heavier tool is dynamic causal modeling, or DCM, proposed by Friston, Lee Harrison, and Will Penny in 2003. DCM is a model of hidden neural activity in a few regions. You draw a small circuit, which regions receive the input which connections exist and which connections the task can strengthen or weaken. A model of neurovascular coupling then predicts the BOLD signal each region should produce. The model is fitted to the data.

Then the key move. You build several circuits, each with a different wiring, and you compare them with Bayesian model comparison. Which diagram best explains the light show, while staying as simple as possible? DCM does not discover the network from scratch. It compares the hypotheses you give it. A good DCM study starts with a small set of well-motivated models, not a hundred random ones.

Now a tempting shortcut, Granger causality. If A's past helps predict B's future beyond B's own past, say that A Granger-causes B. In electrophysiology, with millisecond timing this idea is useful. In fMRI it is treacherous.

Here is why. Imagine two houses. In the first, someone flips the switch, and a slow old bulb takes a moment to glow. In the second, a neighbor flips the switch a little later, but has a fast bulb that lights instantly. From across the street, the second window seems to light first. You conclude wrongly that house 2 drives house 1.

The hemodynamic response is the bulb. Its delay varies across regions and people, by a second or more, which is often longer than the neural delays we want to detect. In 2008, Olivier David and colleagues tested this in a rat model of epilepsy where the true driver was known from electrodes inside the brain. Granger analysis applied directly to the fMRI signal failed because the hemodynamics differed between regions. The driver was found only when the hemodynamic effects were explicitly modeled and removed, for example with DCM.

One last idea, connectivity is not frozen. Dynamic functional connectivity looks at how correlations change over time, for example by sliding a window of a minute or so along the data and computing a matrix in each window. The brain seems to visit recurring states. But be careful, with short windows correlations are noisy, and even a perfectly stationary signal produces fluctuating windowed correlations. Motion and arousal also change over a scan. Always compare against null data that has no true dynamics.

To sum up, functional connectivity is symmetric. Effective connectivity asks about direction and context. PPI tests whether coupling changes with the task. DCM compares a few explicit circuit models of hidden neural activity. Granger causality on raw fMRI is fooled by slow and variable hemodynamic responses, as a validation in rats showed. And dynamic connectivity needs null models to separate real states from noise. That closes Chapter 6. Next, Chapter 7, Decoding Patterns, Not Blobs.
