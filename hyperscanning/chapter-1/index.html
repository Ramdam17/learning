PLATE 1.13:57

From watching to interacting · The second-person turn

Watching a conversation through a window is the third-person view; sitting at the table and answering is the second-person view, where social cognition really happens. A live interaction has reciprocity, turn-taking and mutual adjustment, properties of the pair that neither brain has alone. That is why both brains must be recorded together, while reading what the signals share remains the hard part.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the café. Today we start the series with a question about where you stand. Do you watch other people, or do you take part?

Here is the terrace. Inside, two people are talking. Outside, a passer-by looks through the window. She sees everything. The gestures, the smiles, the pauses. But she is not in the conversation.

This is the third-person view. For a long time, it was the default view of social neuroscience. A person lies in a scanner and looks at faces, or at actions, or at stories about other people. The brain is measured. The other person is only a picture.

Now the passer-by opens the door and sits down at the table. Something changes. She is spoken to. She has to answer. What she does next depends on what the others just did.

This is the second-person view. In 2013, in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, Leonhard Schilbach and colleagues argued for exactly this shift. Much of social neuroscience had studied people as observers. Taking part in an interaction, they said, is not the same thing as watching one. Real social cognition happens between people who are engaged with each other.

Let's place the main positions on the table, one by one.

The first one is the interactive brain hypothesis. In 2012, the philosophers Ezequiel Di Paolo and Hanne De Jaegher proposed that interaction is not just a place where social brain mechanisms get used. Interactive experience and skills help to build those mechanisms, during development, and help to run them today.

The second one comes from the Finnish neuroscientist Riitta Hari and her colleagues. In 2015, in the journal Neuron, they argued that social interaction is central to human brain function. They also described the promise, and the difficulty, of imaging several interacting people at once.

The third one is brain-to-brain coupling. In 2012, Uri Hasson and colleagues proposed a multi-brain frame of reference. Their idea is simple. One brain produces a signal, a voice, a gesture, a look. The signal travels through the shared environment. And it reaches another brain, which becomes coupled to the first. They compared this to a wireless connection between two brains.

An early example came from Hasson's lab. In 2010, Greg Stephens, Lauren Silbert and Uri Hasson recorded a speaker telling a story, then recorded listeners hearing it. The brain activity of speaker and listeners was linked in time. And the link disappeared when communication failed, when the listener did not understand the story.

Notice one thing. The listeners heard a recording. Nobody could answer. So this is coupling, but it is not yet an interaction.

So what does a live interaction have that a recording does not?

Three things. First, reciprocity. What I do changes what you do, and what you do changes what I do again. Second, turn-taking. The two of us share one stream of time, and we hand it back and forth. Third, mutual adjustment. We speed up, slow down, and pick up each other's rhythm, within a fraction of a second.

None of these three belongs to one person. A single brain has no turn to take. A pair does. These are properties of the dyad.

And that is the point of the whole series. If an interaction has properties that neither brain has alone, then to study it, we have to record both brains at the same time. That is what hyperscanning means.

One warning, to keep for later. Recording two brains is the easy part. Reading what the two signals have in common is much harder, and that is what the rest of the series is about.

In the next episode, we open the café's guest book, and read a short history of hyperscanning.

PLATE 1.24:43

A short history of hyperscanning

Hyperscanning begins in 2002 with two MRI scanners linked through the internet, then spreads to EEG, fNIRS and MEG, to classrooms and to animals. A PubMed count of papers mentioning the word climbs from one in 2002 to 136 in 2025. fNIRS and EEG carry most of the growth, plausibly because they let people sit up, talk and move.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Recording two brains at once feels natural today. But the idea has a history, and it is shorter than you might think. In this episode we open the guest book of our cafe and read it page by page. Then we count how many papers it adds up to.

The first page is dated 2002. A team led by Read Montague linked magnetic resonance scanners through the internet. Their paper in NeuroImage carried a new word in its title. Hyperscanning. Pairs of people played a simple game of deception, and functional MRI was recorded in step with their exchanges.

Page two belongs to EEG. In 2007, a team in Rome recorded groups of four people playing a card game. In 2009, Lindenberger and colleagues recorded eight pairs of guitarists playing a short melody together. Phase synchronization between the two brains rose while the players set the tempo and began to play. In 2010, a team in Paris recorded nine pairs of people imitating each other's hand movements. They found a network between the two brains, in the alpha-mu band, over right centroparietal regions.

Page three is near-infrared light. In 2012, a Stanford team used a single fNIRS instrument to record two people playing a cooperation game side by side. Coherence in the right superior frontal cortex went up during cooperation, but not during competition. The same year, a team in Beijing asked partners to talk. Synchronization in the left inferior frontal cortex rose during a face-to-face dialog. It did not rise during a back-to-back dialog, or during a monologue.

Page four is MEG. In 2012, a team at Aalto University in Finland connected two magnetoencephalography devices in two different laboratories, five kilometres apart. As a proof of concept, they recorded the responses of both brains to sounds.

Then the guests left the laboratory. In 2017, a team at New York University used portable EEG to record twelve high school students over a semester, eleven classes in all. The more the students' brain activity was synchronized, the better it predicted their engagement in class.

And not only humans. In 2019, one issue of the journal Cell carried two papers, one on bats and one on mice. In both, neural activity was correlated between the brains of animals interacting. We come back to them in episode 1.5.

Now, the count. We searched PubMed for papers with the word hyperscanning in the title or abstract, year by year. One in 2002. Ten in 2012. One hundred and thirty-six in 2025. And this year is not over yet, with one hundred and fifty-five so far.

Now we split the same search by modality. In 2025, seventy papers mention fNIRS, forty-seven mention EEG, nine mention fMRI, and two mention MEG. A paper can mention several modalities, or none, so these numbers do not add up to the total. Since 2015, fNIRS and EEG have carried most of the growth. fMRI has stayed at ten papers a year or fewer.

Why these two? Reviews give the same reasons. fMRI needs two scanners, and its mobility is low. In a 2020 review, a team in Osnabruck notes that only a few studies have used it. EEG systems are comparatively cheap, and mobile ones exist. fNIRS is valued for its mobility and its resistance to motion artifacts. MEG is less mobile than EEG. Conversation, cooperation and classrooms all need people who can sit up, talk and move. That is a reasonable reading of the curve. This search alone cannot prove it.

One warning. This curve counts papers that use the word. It does not count quality, and it misses studies that never use the word. It is a snapshot taken on the fifteenth of November 2026. Run the search again, and the numbers will be higher.

To keep. Hyperscanning began in 2002, with two scanners wired together. EEG, fNIRS and MEG followed, then classrooms, then animals. Today fNIRS and EEG carry the growth, because they let people move and talk.

PLATE 1.34:16

Synchrony, coupling, alignment · Words that matter

Papers use synchrony, coupling, alignment, entrainment, contagion and inter-subject correlation as if they were one word, but each names something different. Synchrony is a statistical measure between two signals, coupling is a mechanism by which one system changes another, and alignment is an outcome. A metronome can entrain two listeners who never touch, so synchrony alone never proves coupling.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the terrace. Today the waiter hands us a menu, and every dish on it is a word. Synchrony. Coupling. Alignment. Entrainment. Contagion. Inter-subject correlation.

Papers use these words as if they were the same dish. They are not. Let's taste them one at a time, and give each one an operational definition. That means a definition you could check against data.

First, synchrony. Synchrony is a statistical statement about two signals. Their activity goes up and down together in time, more than chance would predict. That is all. It says that a measurement exists. It does not say why.

Second, coupling. Coupling is a statement about a mechanism. One system changes the state of the other, through some channel, and the channel can be named. In a well-known review of brain-to-brain coupling, Hasson and colleagues, in 2012, describe the channel as a signal that travels through the shared environment: light, sound, touch. Coupling is a claim about causes. Synchrony is a claim about numbers.

Third, alignment. Alignment means that two people end up in similar states, such as similar representations. The classic case is dialogue. In 2004, Pickering and Garrod proposed that during a conversation, speakers come to share words, grammar and meaning, level by level. Alignment describes where the two people arrive, not how the signals move.

Fourth, entrainment. Entrainment means that an oscillation is pulled toward the rhythm of something else. A neural rhythm that locks to a beat or to a voice is entrained. Lakatos, Gross and Thut reviewed this in 2019. Notice one thing. Entrainment can be one way. A metronome can entrain two listeners, and the listeners never touch each other.

Fifth, contagion. Contagion means that a state spreads from one person to another, as a yawn or a mood does. Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson described emotional contagion in 1993. It names a process in behaviour and feeling. It does not name a brain measure.

Sixth, inter-subject correlation. Take several people who watch the same film. Compare one person's brain responses with the others'. Nastase and colleagues explain this method in a 2019 tutorial. No one needs to interact. The correlation comes from the shared stimulus.

So how do these words fit together? Look at them in two groups. Some words name a measure: synchrony, and inter-subject correlation. Some words name a process or a mechanism: coupling, entrainment, contagion. And alignment names an outcome.

Here is the moment to remember. In this series, synchrony will always mean a measure between two signals. Coupling will always mean a mechanism, where one influences the other. We will keep the two words apart.

Why do papers mix them up? Because the measure is easy, and the mechanism is hard. It takes one line of code to compute a correlation between two recordings. It takes a careful design to show that one brain, or one body, actually drives the other. So a title says coupling when the data only show synchrony.

Antonia Hamilton made this point clearly in 2021, in a Neuron article called Hyperscanning: Beyond the Hype. Hyperscanning results are hard to interpret on their own. She argues for pairing them with behaviour and with models of how people predict each other.

A simple habit helps. When you read the word synchrony, ask what was computed. When you read the word coupling, ask what the evidence is for the mechanism. And when you write a paper, use each word only for what you showed.

Let's recap. Synchrony is a measure. Coupling is a mechanism. Alignment is an outcome. Entrainment is a rhythm pulled toward another rhythm. Contagion is a state that spreads. Inter-subject correlation is shared response to a shared stimulus.

PLATE 1.44:23

Dyads, triads, groups and crowds

Pairs grow as N times N minus one, over two: three for a trio, 66 for twelve people, almost half a million in a thousand-seat hall. Three oscillators, the third built as the sum of the other two, show pairwise locking near zero but a three-way score of 0.89, so a triad can hold structure no pair shows.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back to the café terrace. So far, every table had two chairs. Today, the terrace fills up.

Start with the two we know. Ava and Otto share a table. Between two people there is exactly one pair, and so one link to measure. Everything in the series so far lives on that single thread.

Now Eve sits down. Three people, and the number of pairs jumps from one to three. Ava and Otto, Ava and Eve, Otto and Eve. The count follows a simple rule. For a group of N people, the number of pairs is N times N minus one, divided by two. Four people give six pairs. Twelve people give sixty-six.

But going from two to three does something deeper than adding threads. It changes the question. With two people, you ask whether they are in sync. With three, you can ask that of every pair, and you can also ask whether the three of them, together, share something that no pair shows alone.

Here is a toy example. It is simulated, and built on purpose. Three oscillators. The phase of the first one wanders on its own. The phase of the second wanders on its own. The phase of the third is made from the other two: it is their sum, plus a little noise.

Look at the pairs first. Each pair has no stable phase difference. The phase locking value, the P-L-V from earlier in the series, is zero point zero one for every pair. Seen two at a time, these three people look unrelated.

Now combine all three. Add the first two phases, subtract the third, and the result stays almost constant. A three-way version of the same average gives zero point eight nine. The three are tightly bound, and no pair shows it.

This is a construction, not a claim about real brains. It only shows that pair after pair is not the whole story. A group can carry structure that lives in the triad itself. Measures built for that exist, and we give them their own episodes later in the series: six point eleven and six point twelve. Today, we only need to know that the question exists.

Let the terrace grow. Four people: six pairs, and four triads. Twelve people: sixty-six pairs, and two hundred and twenty triads. Pairs grow with the square of the group. Triads grow faster still. And a concert hall with a thousand seats holds four hundred ninety-nine thousand, five hundred pairs. With so many links, a study has to choose which ones to look at, and how to summarise the rest.

Has anyone actually recorded groups? Yes, and here are three published examples.

In 2017, Dikker and colleagues recorded EEG from a class of twelve high school students, across a semester of eleven classes. The more their brain activity was synchronised across students, the higher their engagement and the better the social dynamics of the class. The paper was in Current Biology.

In 2016, Nozawa and colleagues used fNIRS with twelve groups of four people, in a cooperative verbal task. Communication enhanced synchrony over the frontal pole of the forehead. It was a study in NeuroImage.

In 2021, Reinero, Dikker and Van Bavel recorded EEG from one hundred seventy-four people, in groups of four, solving problems alone or as a team. Inter-brain synchrony predicted how well the teams performed. Self-reported group identification did not. It appeared in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience.

And what about the hall? Czepiel and colleagues, in 2021, measured audiences at live string quintet concerts, in Scientific Reports. Careful: they did not record brains. They recorded heart, breathing, skin conductance and facial muscles, and found shared responses at important moments in the music. It is a group of bodies, not of brains, but it shows what a crowd can share.

Let's recap. Two people give one pair. Three give three, and a new question about the triad. Pairs grow as N times N minus one, over two. Groups have been recorded in classrooms, teams and concert halls. And beyond pairs, some structure needs its own measures.

PLATE 1.54:18

Other species, other answers

In bats and mice, recordings from inside the brain show correlated activity between interacting animals that disappears when the animals are in separate environments. Removing the bats' behaviour left a high correlation, while in mice some neurons encode the animal's own actions and others its partner's. Single cells can separate causes that scalp recordings cannot, yet part of brain-to-brain synchrony may follow shared behaviour.

TRANSCRIPTEN

So far, most of the brains in this series have worn a cap. But the café has other guests. At the neighbouring table, two bats are hanging side by side. This episode asks what other species can tell us about two brains in interaction.

Why would we go to the animals? Because in animals, researchers can put electrodes or tiny microscopes inside the brain and record single neurons. A cap on the scalp cannot do that.

Let's start with the bats. In 2019, in the journal Cell, Wujie Zhang and Michael Yartsev recorded from pairs of Egyptian fruit bats. The bats moved freely and interacted naturally. The recordings were wireless, and they came from the frontal cortex of both animals at the same time.

The two brains turned out to be correlated, over timescales from seconds to hours. The correlation was strongest in the fast local field potentials, above 30 hertz, and it was followed by the spiking of nearby neurons.

Now the important part. The correlation depended on a shared social environment. In a control with the bats in separate environments, there was little to no correlation across their brains. The correlation also followed how much the bats interacted. And it rose before an interaction began.

Could the bats' movements alone explain this? The authors tested it. They removed the behaviour of both bats from the neural signals. A high correlation remained. So in the bats, the correlation was not simply two similar movements seen from the inside.

Two years later, in Science, Michael Rose and colleagues studied whole groups of Egyptian fruit bats, again with wireless recordings from the frontal cortex. Here single neurons could tell a call made by the bat itself from a call made by another bat, and even tell which bat was calling. Across brains, the correlation patterns were specific to spontaneous communication between bats.

Now the mice. In the same issue of Cell, Lyle Kingsbury, Weizhe Hong and colleagues recorded from pairs of socially interacting mice. Each mouse carried a miniature microscope, which imaged calcium activity in the prefrontal cortex.

Again the two brains were correlated, and again this depended on ongoing social interaction. But the single cells told us more. The synchrony came from two groups of neurons. One group encoded what the animal itself was doing. The other group encoded what its partner was doing.

And the correlation was useful. It predicted future social interactions, and it predicted which mouse would dominate in a competitive context.

Let's look at the logic with a toy example. These traces are simulated, and the example is illustrative. Two animals follow one shared level of social engagement, and each adds its own noise. When the two interact, the correlation between their traces is 0.73. Now put them in separate chambers, so that each follows its own level. The correlation falls to 0.00. Same animals, same measure. Only the shared situation changed.

This is what the animal work adds. Because we can record single neurons, we can see why two brains correlate. In the mice, part of the answer is that cells follow what each animal and its partner are doing. In the bats, the correlation was not fully explained by behaviour. Recordings from the scalp of two people cannot see single cells, so they cannot split these apart in the same way.

In 2020, Lyle Kingsbury and Weizhe Hong reviewed this field in Trends in Neurosciences. They describe social interaction as a dynamic feedback loop that couples action, reaction and internal processes across individuals.

Here is the honest warning of this episode. Part of what looks like brain-to-brain synchrony may follow what two individuals do together. That is what the second source in our series, rhythm and movement, is about. Where does the synchrony come from? Animals help us ask that question with cells instead of caps.
