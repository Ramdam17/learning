The random walker

A shuffled deck is a random walker on a graph with about eight times ten to the sixty-seven nodes. On a seven-node graph, a thousand walkers settle into shares proportional to degree. Bayer and Diaconis show a riffle-shuffled deck stays far from random until five shuffles and falls to a third by seven. A bottleneck between two clusters slows the mixing.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every bridge club shuffles. How many shuffles does a deck of fifty-two cards need before it is truly mixed?

Here is a way to see the question. Write down every order the deck can be in, and make each order a node. That is fifty-two factorial nodes: about eight point oh seven times ten to the sixty-seven.

A shuffle takes the deck from one order to another, and it does so by chance. So a shuffle is a step along an edge, picked at random.

It does not look like a graph. But it is one, and there it comes.

Shuffling cards is a random walk on a graph with eight times ten to the sixty-seven nodes. That graph is far too big to draw, so start with one you can see.

Seven nodes, ten edges. This graph is illustrative, and the positions mean nothing.

A walker stands on node G. At each step it looks at the neighbours of its node, picks one, all equally likely, and walks there. G has a single neighbour, so the first step is forced. Then the walker chooses: G, F, G, F, E, D.

Count where it stands, step after step, and the tally is ragged. One walker tells us little. So release a thousand.

All thousand start on G. After the first step, every one of them stands on F. After the second, they split over D, E and G: three hundred forty-one, three hundred twenty-four, three hundred thirty-five.

Keep going. Walkers leave nodes and arrive on others, and the jars fill and drain. But soon the levels stop changing. Walkers keep moving, and the shares do not.

That settled picture is the stationary distribution. When the graph has no arrows, its rule is simple: the share of a node is its degree, divided by twice the number of edges.

Here that is twenty. Node B has degree four, so its share is four twentieths, zero point two. Sixty steps in, we count two hundred eight walkers out of a thousand: zero point two zero eight. Node G has degree one, so one twentieth, zero point zero five. We count fifty-four.

Why degree? Look at a single edge. When shares follow degree, one walker in twenty crosses that edge each way, at every step. What comes in equals what goes out, on every edge, so nothing changes.

One condition: the graph is connected, and it has triangles, so no two-step rhythm can lock the walkers.

In episode 6.4, this crowd had a twist. PageRank is the stationary distribution of surfers who sometimes jump to a random page. The same walk, with one extra rule.

How long does the crowd take to forget where it started? Measure the distance between the crowd and the final shares: add up the gaps, node by node, and halve the total. One means nowhere near. Zero means arrived.

Here the distance starts at zero point nine five. It falls below a quarter at step five, and it is zero point zero four at step ten. The dots are the thousand walkers. They hover a little above the line, because a crowd of a thousand is never perfectly smooth.

The number of steps this takes is the mixing time.

Now go back to the cards. The usual way to shuffle is the riffle: cut the deck in two, then let the halves fall together, card by card, in a random interleaving.

In nineteen ninety-two, Bayer and Diaconis computed exactly how far a deck is from random after each riffle shuffle. Their distance is the same one: how far the chance of each order is from equal.

After one, two, three or four shuffles, the distance is still one, to two decimals. Then it drops. Five shuffles: zero point nine two. Six: zero point six one. Seven: zero point three three. Eight: zero point one seven. Nine: zero point zero nine. Ten: zero point zero four.

Why does it wait? A riffle can at most double the number of rising sequences in the deck, the runs of cards still in their original order. After four shuffles, at most sixteen. A truly random deck has twenty-six and a half on average. A deck with at most sixteen can almost always be told from a random one.

From five shuffles on, it can no longer be told so easily. Bayer and Diaconis made this exact: the chance of an order depends only on its number of rising sequences.

By seven shuffles the distance has fallen to a third. That is the famous seven. It is a statement about this distance, and the deck keeps improving after seven.

The mixing time depends on the shape of the graph. Take two clusters of five nodes, each node linked to every other in its cluster. Join the clusters with a single link, and start a thousand walkers in the left cluster.

The distance falls below a quarter after ten steps. After twenty steps, only three hundred seventy-one walkers have crossed to the right cluster.

Now join the same two clusters with five links instead of one. Two steps are enough to fall below a quarter, and after twenty steps, four hundred ninety-eight walkers are on the right.

The walkers must squeeze through the one link, like the parcels through the narrowest belt in episode 3.3. A bottleneck slows the walk.

To sum up. A random walker picks a neighbour at each step. A thousand walkers settle into shares proportional to degree. The steps they need are the mixing time, and it depends on the shape of the graph: about seven shuffles for a deck, and a bottleneck makes it longer.

PLATE 12.26:55

Heat on a graph

Heat on a graph flows along edges from hot to cold, following the Laplacian in motion, and it is the density of a crowd of walkers. Each piece of a graph levels out at its own average. Because walkers linger in dense groups, the length of the walk acts as resolution: on the karate club, time 0.3 gives thirteen groups and time eight gives two.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we let a thousand walkers loose on a graph. Now picture far more of them, so many that you stop seeing walkers. You only see how thick the crowd is at each node. What else spreads like that?

Heat does. A hot spoon in a cup of tea warms along its handle, and nothing walks along it. On a graph, heat spreads from node to node, along the edges. This episode makes that exact, and then asks what it reveals about communities.

Take the seven-node graph from episode 12.1. It is illustrative, and the positions mean nothing. Give each node a temperature. Put seventy units of heat on node G, and nothing anywhere else.

The rule has one line. Across every edge, heat flows from the hotter end to the colder end, at a pace equal to the gap between the two temperatures. A big gap, a fast flow. No gap, no flow.

Watch. At time zero, node G has one edge, and the gap across it is seventy. So G loses seventy units per unit of time, and F, its neighbour, gains seventy. After a tenth of a time unit, G holds sixty-three point six, and F holds five point eight. After one unit, G holds thirty-four point five, F holds sixteen point one, and every node has felt it.

There is a name for this rule. Remember the Laplacian of episode 11.1. For each node, it adds up the gaps between that node and its neighbours. Here, the temperature of a node changes at the pace of that sum. In symbols, the change of x over time equals minus L times x. It is the same matrix, set in motion.

Remember also the roughness of episode 11.1, the sum of the squared gaps over all edges. It starts at four thousand nine hundred. By time one, it is five hundred seventy. By time four, eleven point six. By time eight, zero point zero seven. Heat always smooths the graph.

Nothing is lost on the way. The seventy units stay seventy, at every moment. After eight time units, every node holds between nine point nine and ten point three. In the end, each node holds ten: the total, shared equally.

Now cut the graph in two. Next to our seven nodes, add an invented triangle that has no link to them, and put sixty units on one of its corners. The two pieces settle on their own averages. The seven nodes level at ten. The triangle levels at twenty. Heat cannot cross where there is no edge. These are the two zeros of episode 11.2: one for each piece.

Now the link with walkers. Release a thousand walkers on node G, as in episode 12.1, with one change. A walker leaves along each edge at rate one. So a walker on a node with three edges leaves three times as often as one on a node with a single edge.

Count the walkers on each node, and scale so that a thousand walkers stand for seventy units. After one time unit, the crowd on G is thirty-two point four, against thirty-four point five for the heat. On F, sixteen point nine against sixteen point one. Over all the nodes and all the times we looked at, the crowd never differs from the heat by more than two point two units, out of seventy. The walkers are simulated, with seed one hundred twenty-two.

Why do they agree? Look at one node. Walkers arrive from each neighbour at a pace set by that neighbour's crowd, and leave along each edge at a pace set by this one. Arrivals minus departures is the sum of the gaps. So the expected crowd follows the same equation as the heat. Heat is the density of a crowd of walkers.

One difference from episode 12.1. There, every walker took one step per tick, and the shares ended up proportional to degree. Here a busy node is left faster, so the crowd ends level: about one hundred forty-three walkers on each node.

Now to communities. If heat and walkers spread over a graph, they linger in dense groups, because the many links inside keep them there. To see it, use Zachary's karate club, the real network of episodes 8.1 to 8.3: thirty-four members, seventy-eight friendships.

Delvenne, Yaliraki and Barahona turned this into a method in twenty ten. Their walkers are the ones of episode 12.1: each leaves its node at rate one, whatever its degree. Pick a time, the Markov time. For a partition, count the walkers still inside their own group when the time is up, and subtract what chance alone would give. That score is the stability of the partition. Then look for the partition with the best stability.

At a short time, walkers have hardly moved, so even small groups keep them. At time zero point three, the best partition has thirteen groups. Time one gives six. Time three gives three groups, of eighteen, eleven and five members. Time eight gives two groups, of eighteen and sixteen members.

Remember the resolution parameter of episode 8.3. It was a dial, with no meaning except its effect: turn it up, and the groups shrink. Here it gets a physical meaning. It is time. Delvenne and colleagues show that at short times, stability becomes the modularity of episode 8.2, with a resolution set by the time. A long walk sees only the big picture. A short walk sees the neighbourhood.

Two years earlier, Rosvall and Bergstrom had asked the same question in another language: compression. Describe the path of one walker with as few bits as possible. You can give every node a name, and the path becomes a long list of names. Or you can give every group a name, reuse short names inside groups, and pay only when the walker changes group. A good partition makes the description short. The average cost is the map equation, in bits per step, and the method is called Infomap.

On the karate club, one big group costs four point seven bits per step. The two camps of the club cost four point four six. The three groups that Infomap finds, of seventeen, twelve and five members, cost four point three one. Fewer bits, because the walker stays inside a group for a long time.

To sum up. Heat on a graph flows along the edges, from hot to cold, at a pace set by the gaps: the change of x equals minus L times x, the Laplacian in motion. Each piece of the graph levels out at its own average. The heat is the density of a crowd of walkers. And since walkers linger in dense groups, the length of the walk decides which groups you see: time is the resolution.

Next time, a question. Heat flows evenly, a little at every contact. A disease does not: it passes on, or it does not. How does an infection travel through a population?

PLATE 12.36:38

Epidemics on networks

In the susceptible-infected-recovered model, each infected person passes the infection to each susceptible neighbour with probability T. Hubs lower the epidemic threshold, from 0.17 on a random network to 0.07 on one with hubs. Vaccinating a random contact of a random person works far better than vaccinating random people: 1.8 percent infected against 13 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

How does a disease travel through a population? Here is the simplest model of it, and it lives on a graph.

Every person is in one of three states. Susceptible: healthy, and able to catch it. Infected: carrying it. Recovered: finished, and out of the game. Those three words give the model its name, S I R.

The rule has two parts. Each infected person meets each susceptible neighbour once, and passes the infection on with probability T, the transmissibility. Then that person recovers.

Here is a small invented network, twenty-six people and forty-eight links. It is illustrative, and the positions mean nothing. T is zero point four, and one person falls ill: someone with only two contacts.

Step one: two of their contacts catch it. Step two: two more, and one of them has fourteen contacts. Step three: that hub passes the infection to six people at once. The outbreak dies out after seven steps, with sixteen of the twenty-six people infected. This is a simulation, seed twelve thirty-two.

In a population where everyone meets everyone at random, a simple rule says when an epidemic takes off. Each case must infect more than one other person, on average. Below one, outbreaks fizzle. Above one, they grow.

On a network, the shape matters. Take two networks with the same two thousand people and the same five thousand nine hundred ninety-one links, so about six contacts each. One is random, as in episode 9.1. The other grows by preferential attachment, as in episode 9.3, and has hubs. Its biggest hub has one hundred fifty-seven contacts, against sixteen in the random one.

Now sweep T from zero to one half. For each value, run three hundred outbreaks, each from one random person, and average the share of the network that ever got infected. Both networks are simulated, with the same seed.

The random network waits. Almost nothing happens until T is near zero point one seven, and then the curve climbs. The network with hubs starts to climb earlier. At T equal to zero point one four, four point six percent of its people get infected, against zero point three percent in the random one. From zero point two six on, the random network is slightly ahead.

Why does the network with hubs start earlier? Follow the infection along one link. Someone who catches it from a friend was reached by a link, and a link is more likely to lead to a hub. That is the friendship paradox of episode 6.1.

Count the other contacts such a person has, on average. Five point nine four in the random network. Thirteen point eight in the network with hubs. Each of them catches it with probability T. The epidemic takes off when T times that number passes one, so the threshold is one divided by that number: zero point one seven, and zero point zero seven.

Newman wrote this result in two thousand and two, for this very model. The threshold is the average degree, divided by the average of the squared degrees minus the average degree. The more uneven the degrees, the bigger the squares, and the lower the threshold.

And who is infected first? Take the outbreaks that take off at T equal to zero point two. In the network with hubs, the people infected at step one have seventeen point nine contacts on average, against six for everyone. In the random network, the same people have seven point two.

In two thousand and one, Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani studied a close cousin of this model, called S I S. There, the cured become susceptible again. On networks with very uneven degrees, of the scale-free kind, they found no epidemic threshold: in a large enough network, the infection persists however weakly it spreads. In the simplest version of their analysis the threshold follows a ratio of the same two averages. Later work has refined that claim, so read it as a statement about large networks, and about that model. Our two thousand people are far from infinite, so their threshold is low, but not zero.

Now a question every health service asks. With a limited number of doses, whom do you vaccinate? A vaccinated person cannot catch the disease, so cannot pass it on. In the model, that person is removed from the network.

Take two hundred doses, a tenth of the two thousand people, on the network with hubs, with T equal to zero point two. With no dose, the average outbreak reaches nineteen percent of the people.

Strategy one: vaccinate two hundred people picked at random. The average outbreak shrinks to thirteen percent.

Strategy two: vaccinate the two hundred people with the most contacts. Now it is down to about a tenth of one percent. But this needs the whole network. Who knows everyone's contacts?

A third way came in two thousand and three, from Cohen, Havlin and ben Avraham. Pick a person at random. Do not vaccinate that person. Vaccinate one of their contacts, picked at random. Repeat until the doses run out.

Why would that work? Because your friends have more friends than you do. A random contact is more likely to be a hub. Nobody has to know who the hubs are. It is enough to ask: who are your contacts?

In the simulation, the two hundred people vaccinated this way have twelve point six contacts on average. Random picks have six, and the true hubs twenty point six. Here the outbreak drops to one point eight percent.

Now sweep the doses, from none to thirty percent of the people. Random vaccination still leaves seven point four percent with four hundred doses. Acquaintance vaccination leaves point one five percent. The paradox of episode 6.1 has become a strategy.

Remember what this is: a model, on a simulated network, with a perfect vaccine and one first case. It speaks about shape, not about any real disease.

To sum up. An epidemic on a network takes off when each case reaches, on average, more than one new person through its contacts. Hubs raise that number, so they lower the threshold, and they catch the infection early. And since friends have more friends than you, vaccinating a random friend does far better than vaccinating a random person.

PLATE 12.45:39

Behaviours need more than one contact

A habit often needs more than one contact, a threshold. With two stamps required, a clustered ring of forty people adopts fully in ten steps, while the rewired network stops at two people. Shortcuts carry a rumour fast but starve the habit, because a weak tie gives one stamp. Centola's online experiment found 54 percent adoption in clustered networks against 38 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Catching a virus takes one contact. Does a new habit work the same way?

Think of a new gym, a new app, a new diet. One friend who does it is a story. Two or three friends, and you start to wonder. Many behaviours need more than one contact.

The sociologist Mark Granovetter wrote this down in nineteen seventy-eight. Give every person a threshold: the number of others who must act before she does. In his example, a hundred people mill about in a square, ready to riot. One has threshold zero. One has threshold one, one has threshold two, and so on, up to ninety-nine.

The first person starts. That tips the second, then the third. A hundred people end up rioting.

Now change a single person. Take the one with threshold one, and put a second person with threshold two in their place. Nobody has threshold one anymore. The first person acts alone, and the riot ends with one rioter. The two crowds look almost the same. Their outcomes are a hundred, and one.

Let us put thresholds on a network. Each person holds a stamp card. Every neighbour who has adopted gives one stamp. When the card is full, the person adopts, and stamps her own neighbours in turn.

A rumour is a card with one slot. One stamp is enough. That is a simple contagion, like the virus of the last episode. A card with two slots is a complex contagion. Centola and Macy studied it under that name in two thousand and seven: adoption needs contact with several sources.

Here are two networks of forty people, both illustrative. On the left, a ring. Each person is linked to the three nearest on each side, so six contacts. Friends of a person are friends of each other: the clustering is zero point six. On the right, the same people, rewired. Pick two links, swap their ends, and repeat. Everybody keeps exactly six contacts. But the clustering falls to zero point one.

Start with a rumour. One person knows it. On the ring, it reaches everybody in seven steps. On the rewired network, in three. The random links are shortcuts, as in episode nine point two. Shortcuts speed up a rumour.

Now the habit. Every card has two slots. One person alone can start nothing, so two neighbours start together.

On the ring, the two seeds stamp the people next to them. Those people each hold two stamps, so they adopt. The wave moves around the ring, and in ten steps all forty have adopted.

On the rewired network, the two seeds stamp their neighbours once each. Nobody holds two stamps. It stops at once, with two people out of forty.

Why? Look at the two ends of the seed link. On the ring, they share four neighbours. In the rewired network, they share none. A person needs two stamps from two different people, and those two people have to be close to each other. Clustering is what puts two stamps on the same card.

Now a bigger test. A thousand people, six contacts each, simulated. The rumour crosses the ring in a hundred and sixty-seven steps, and the rewired network in six. The habit starts from three hundred random links. On the ring, it reaches everybody every time. On the rewired network, it grows beyond its two seeds in two point three percent of the starts. The average share stays at point two percent: the two seeds themselves.

So how many shortcuts does it take to starve a habit? Swap links little by little. With sixty swaps among three thousand links, the rumour already crosses in seventeen steps instead of a hundred and sixty-seven. The habit still reaches ninety-two percent. With three hundred swaps, it reaches twenty-four percent. With six hundred swaps, one percent.

The same shortcuts that speed up the rumour starve the habit.

A shortcut is a narrow bridge: one tie between two neighbourhoods. It carries one stamp. A habit needs a wide bridge, with several ties between the same two groups. For a complex contagion, wrote Centola and Macy, the width of the bridges matters more than their length. These are the weak ties of episode seven point one. They are precious for news, and Granovetter showed it with jobs. But a weak tie gives one stamp, and one stamp is not enough.

In twenty-ten, Centola tested this with real people. He built an online health community of fifteen hundred and twenty-eight participants, recruited from health websites. Each had an anonymous profile and six or eight health buddies, and learned by email what the buddies did. The behaviour was to register for a health forum that nobody else could reach.

Some people were placed in a clustered network, others in a rewired one with the same number of buddies each. Six matched pairs, twelve networks of ninety-eight to a hundred and forty-four people. In the clustered networks, fifty-four percent adopted on average. In the random ones, thirty-eight percent. And the behaviour spread more than four times faster.

It is a small experiment, with one behaviour. But it points the same way as the simulation.

To sum up. A habit often needs more than one contact: that is a threshold. On a network, a threshold of two needs two stamps from people who are close to each other, which is clustering. Shortcuts carry a rumour fast, one stamp at a time, so they help news and starve habits.

Next time, a question. Rumours, heat, diseases and habits all pass from one person to the next. What about things that beat on their own, like clocks? Can a whole network of them fall into step?

PLATE 12.56:02

Falling into step

Huygens saw two pendulum clocks fall into step, and on a network the shape decides how well oscillators synchronise. Forty clocks on a ring reach an r of 0.38, but 0.90 once the links are rewired. For identical oscillators, the ratio of the largest to the smallest non-zero Laplacian eigenvalue predicts it: fifty-one for the ring, 9.6 for the rewired network.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Hang two clocks side by side. Will they ever tick together?

On the twenty-second of February sixteen sixty-five, Christiaan Huygens wrote a letter to the mathematician René-François de Sluse. He had hung two pendulum clocks from one wooden bar, held up by two chairs. After about half an hour, the pendulums swung in opposite directions, beat for beat. Huygens called it the sympathy of two clocks.

The two clocks were not identical. Each had its own rhythm. But the bar carried a tiny push from one to the other, and that was enough.

Here is a simulated pair. One runs at a steady speed of one point zero zero, the other at one point zero four. Each pushes the other away, gently. Watch the gap between them. It settles at zero point nine four of a half turn: nearly opposite, and it stays there.

Now put many oscillators on a network. In nineteen seventy-five, Kuramoto wrote down the simplest model of this. Every oscillator has a phase, the position of the hand on its dial. Alone, it turns at its own speed. With neighbours, it gets a nudge toward each of them. In his model, everyone feels everyone. Above a critical coupling, a part of the crowd locks together.

On a graph, only neighbours nudge each other. Here are forty clocks on a ring, each linked to its two nearest on each side. Every clock has four neighbours. Their natural speeds are random, simulated. Every link has the same strength.

Watch the hands. Small patches fall into step. The whole ring does not.

To measure it, use one number, called r. It is zero when the hands point everywhere, and one when they all agree. Over twenty random draws of the speeds, r averages zero point three eight on the ring.

Now keep the same forty clocks, the same speeds, the same strength. Only rewire the links, by swapping ends, as in episode twelve point four. Everybody still has four neighbours. The hands line up. The average r is zero point nine zero.

Nothing changed but the shape of the network.

Raise the strength and the story stays the same. On the rewired network, r climbs steadily. The ring stays near zero point four, even at twice that strength.

So can the shape be summed up in one number? For one class of systems, yes.

In nineteen ninety-eight, Pecora and Carroll showed how to test it. Take identical oscillators, linked by a graph. Whether the all-in-step state is stable is decided by one curve, the master stability function, read at the coupling times each non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian. These are the tuning forks of episode eleven point two.

For many oscillators, that curve is negative on a single window, between two values. Then every fork, scaled by the coupling, must fit in the window. That is possible only if the ratio of the largest to the smallest non-zero eigenvalue is smaller than the ratio of the two ends of the window. Barahona and Pecora wrote this down in two thousand and two. For the oscillators they tried, the window ratio runs from five to a hundred. For the chaotic one they simulated, it is about thirty-eight.

Let us read our two networks. Take the ring first. Its lowest fork is at zero point one two, and the highest at six point two four. The ratio is fifty-one. For the rewired network, the lowest is at zero point seven seven, and the highest at seven point three four. Its ratio is nine point six.

The ring is far above thirty-eight. The rewired network is far below. Rewiring lifted the lowest fork, and the ratio fell.

The same ratio, on forty nodes, for other shapes. A complete graph: one. A star with a hub: forty. A plain cycle, each node with just two neighbours: a hundred and sixty-two.

Now add shortcuts one at a time. Start from the ring and swap four pairs of links. The ratio falls to thirty-eight, and is under the limit in seven draws out of ten. Swap eight pairs, and it is twenty-four. Swap forty, and it is nine point two.

Meanwhile, the clocks of the first experiment follow. With no swaps, r is zero point four three. With eight swaps, zero point seven seven. With forty swaps, zero point nine one. A handful of shortcuts does most of the work, as in episode nine point two. Small-world networks showed the same thing to Barahona and Pecora.

Be careful about what this says. The eigenratio is a result of the master stability framework. It needs identical oscillators, a stable window that is a single interval, and it speaks of the stability of the in-step state. It is not a law for every system. Our Kuramoto clocks are not identical: their speeds differ. Here, the ordering still agrees. But that is a measurement on these networks, not a proof.

Now a bridge to the Hyperscanning series. There, two people are recorded at once, and one measures how far their brain rhythms fall into step between the two heads.

A warning goes with it. Two signals in step do not prove a link between them. Take A and B, never linked. Both follow C. Their phase gap is locked: the locking value is one point zero zero. Left alone, the same two give zero point zero three. Chapter fourteen returns to this.

To sum up. Oscillators that push each other can fall into step, as Huygens saw. Across a graph, the shape decides how well. For identical oscillators in the master stability framework, the verdict is read in the ratio of the largest to the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian.

Next time, a question. So far, a graph has carried things from node to node. What if every node simply carried a number: a temperature, an opinion, a brain signal? What does it mean for those numbers to vary smoothly across a graph?
