Estimators: bias and variance

An estimate is an arrow and the truth the bullseye; an estimator is judged by bias, spread and the mean squared error that adds them. Dividing by n has lower error than n minus one for the variance, while the rescaled maximum removes a real bias. A timid archer shrinking estimates beats the honest one when effects are small.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Which estimator is the best one?

Think of an archery range. An estimate is an arrow, and the true value is the bullseye.

Today we learn to score the archer, not the arrow. Every study is one shot. In real life, we never see the archer shoot twice. But a simulation can.

Here is a hidden truth. The average score of a whole clinic is exactly fifty, and individual scores spread by ten. These are simulated scores, not real patients.

Our first archer is the sample mean. Each study measures ten people. Shot one lands at fifty point one. Shot two, shot three, and so on, forty shots in all. They scatter around the bullseye, some to the left and some to the right.

Now five thousand shots. Guess first: will the centre of the pile sit on the bullseye? The arrows pile up into a hill. The centre of the hill is fifty point zero three. The gap between that centre and the bullseye is the bias. Here it is zero point zero three, which is just simulation noise. The width of the hill is the spread: three point one eight.

So an archer has two faults, and they can be told apart. Bias is aiming off target, on average. Spread is shaking from one study to the next.

One number adds the two faults together: the mean squared error. Square each miss, then take the average. It equals the bias squared, plus the variance, which is the spread squared. For the sample mean it is ten point zero nine, and almost all of it is spread.

Now a different archer. It estimates the variance of the scores, and you met it in episode two point three. The true variance is one hundred, and each study has only five values.

Divide the squared distances by n minus one, and the arrows centre on one hundred point six. Divide by n instead, and the centre slides down to eighty point five. That is a bias of nineteen point five, about a fifth too low.

Why? A sample's values sit closer to their own mean than to the true mean. So their squared distances come out too small, by a factor of n minus one over n.

Guess before you look. Which divisor gives the smaller mean squared error?

The unbiased one, with n minus one, scores about five thousand one hundred. The biased one, with n, scores about three thousand six hundred. The biased archer wins by more than a quarter.

Look at the two parts. Its bias squared costs about three hundred and eighty. But its shaking is much smaller, so the total falls.

Unbiased does not mean good.

Divide by n plus one, and the score improves again, to about three thousand four hundred. The bias grows, and the shaking shrinks faster. That result is known for bell-shaped data. Software usually divides by n minus one anyway, because unbiasedness is a handy property. But it is a choice, not a law.

Does a biased archer always win? Let us meet a third one.

A machine gives a random time between zero and an unknown limit. The true limit is one hundred. We time it ten times, and we use the longest time as our estimate of the limit.

Every arrow falls short. The longest of ten can never be above the limit. Over twenty thousand shots, the centre of the hill is ninety point nine, a bias of minus nine point one, and every single arrow is below the bullseye.

Now rescale the maximum by n plus one over n, which here is eleven tenths. The bias vanishes. The centre is one hundred point zero. The error drops from about one hundred and fifty-one to about eighty-two. The shaking grows a little, from eight point three to nine point one, but the bias squared disappears.

So here, removing the bias wins. No rule says that biased is better, or that unbiased is better. You have to measure the total, for your own estimator and your own kind of data.

Next, a trade you can dial.

A treatment changes patients' scores by two points on average. Individual changes spread by ten, and the study has ten patients. The usual archer is the average change. It is unbiased, its spread is three point one eight, and its error is ten point zero nine.

Now meet a timid archer. It multiplies the average by a factor c, between zero and one, which pulls every estimate toward zero. At c equal to zero, it always says zero. It never shakes, but it is wrong by two points every time, and its error is four.

Guess again. Which c gives the smallest error?

The curve dips. At c equal to zero point three, the error is two point eight eight, less than a third of the usual archer's. Even halving every estimate gives three point five four. A timid archer beats the honest one by a wide margin.

It works because the true effect is small next to the noise. The cost of the bias is small, and the gain in steadiness is large. For a pilot study of ten patients, that is the difference between a steady estimate and a lucky one, or an unlucky one.

Now suppose the true change is eight points. Same dial, same ten patients. The best setting moves to nine tenths, and the error is only eight point eight five, barely better than ten point one. Halve every estimate now, and the error is eighteen point six, almost double.

So shrinking is a bet that effects are small, and the right setting depends on the very truth you are trying to estimate. This trade comes back three times in this series: in mixed models, in hierarchical models, and in regularization.

One more look. What happens as the data grow?

Take the variance with divisor n. At ten values, its bias is minus ten percent of the truth. At a hundred values, minus one percent. At five hundred, minus zero point two. The spread, over the same range, goes from forty-two percent to fourteen, then to six.

The bias fades like one over n. The spread fades only like one over the square root of n. With plenty of data, a small bias hardly matters. With little data, it can pay to accept one.

Finally, a common misreading. People say: this estimator is unbiased, so my estimate is right.

Unbiased is a statement about the archer's aim, over many imaginary studies. You hold only one arrow. With ten people, almost twelve percent of the sample mean's arrows miss by more than five points, even though the archer is perfectly aimed.

So we score an archer by three numbers: the bias, the spread, and the mean squared error that adds them up.

Remember: an estimator is judged by its whole flock of arrows, aim and shaking together, and unbiased does not mean good.

Likelihood

Seven tails in ten throws are sent through a canal of ten gates, one per throw, and the water coming out is the chance of that sequence for each candidate coin. The curve over candidates is the likelihood: it peaks at seven tenths, is not a probability distribution, and gets narrower with more data. It is the chance of data given the coin.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Seven tails in ten throws. What does that say about the coin?

Until now, we always knew the target, and asked where the arrows would land. Last time, with the binomial of episode four point two, we knew the coin, and asked how many tails to expect. Today the arrow is already stuck in the board. We ask which target it was aimed at.

Here are ten throws, simulated, and fixed forever: heads, tails, tails, tails, tails, heads, heads, tails, tails, tails. Seven tails and three heads, in this order. We do not know the coin's chance of tails. So we will try every value, and see how each one copes with these ten throws.

Picture a canal with ten gates, one for each throw. One hundred percent of the water enters. A gate for tails lets through the chance of tails. A gate for heads lets through the chance of heads, which is one minus that. The water that comes out of the tenth gate is the chance of exactly this sequence. It is the rule from chapter three: multiply the chances of what happened. Watch the water thin to a thread, because every gate can only take some away.

Turn the dial to one half. Every gate lets half through, and after ten gates, point oh nine eight percent comes out. Turn it to seven tenths. The tails gates open wider, the heads gates narrow, and point two two two percent comes out, more than twice as much. At nine tenths, only point oh four eight percent.

Guess before you look. Across the whole dial, where will the most water come out?

One detail first. Seven tails can come in one hundred and twenty different orders, as in episode four point two, and each order gets the same water. So the chance of seven tails, in any order, is one hundred and twenty times the water. At seven tenths, that is twenty-six point seven percent.

Now sweep the dial, and at each setting, draw that height. The curve appears. It peaks at seven tenths, exactly the share of tails we saw, and it falls to nothing at zero and at one.

This curve is the likelihood. The data are fixed, and the dial is the unknown. Ronald Fisher set it out in nineteen twenty-two, in a paper on the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics. Each height says how well one candidate copes with what we saw.

Is that computed, or real? Check it by simulation. Take a coin with a chance of one half, and throw it ten times, a million times over. Exactly seven tails came out one hundred seventeen thousand, two hundred eighty-five times. That is eleven point seven percent, and the curve says eleven point seven.

At seven tenths, the simulation gives twenty-six point six percent, against twenty-six point seven. At nine tenths, five point eight, against five point seven. The simulated sets land on the curve, three times out of three. Our exact sequence, at seven tenths, came out point two two four percent of the time. The water said point two two two.

Now a table, to read in two directions. Each row is a coin, with its chance of tails. Each column is a result, from zero tails to ten. Each cell says how likely that result is for that coin.

Read along a row, and you hold one coin with all its possible results. They add up to one hundred percent, because something has to happen. That is a probability distribution, the binomial of four point two. It looks forward from a coin to its results.

Read down a column instead, and you hold one result, with all the candidate coins. That is the likelihood. It looks back from a result to its coins, and nothing forces it to add up to anything.

Pick another column, three tails in ten, and the whole curve slides to three tenths. Pick ten tails, and it climbs to a wall at one. Every result carries its own curve.

Measure the area under our curve: nine point one percent, not one hundred. With twenty throws and fourteen tails, four point eight. With a hundred throws and seventy tails, one point oh. The area is not the probability of anything, and it changes with the data. What matters is the shape, and the ratio of two heights.

How much better does seven tenths cope than one half? Divide the heights: two point three. A ratio of one would mean the data cannot tell the two apart. Two point three is a slight preference, from only ten throws. But now take seventy tails in a hundred throws. The curve narrows, and the ratio climbs to three thousand, seven hundred and forty-five.

Guess before you look. With seven hundred tails in a thousand throws, how big does the ratio get? A number with thirty-six digits. More data does not make the curve taller. It makes it narrower, and the ratios enormous. How a narrow curve gives a precise answer is for episode five point three.

The same question hides everywhere. Each time, the data are fixed, and we ask which values of the unknown make them least surprising. All six cases are illustrative. In each, the data cannot move. Only our candidates do.

A free-throw shooter makes fourteen out of twenty. A chance of seven tenths copes about five times better than one half. A vaccine: thirty-six of forty volunteers respond. Ninety percent beats seventy percent by about one hundred and five.

A factory finds two defective items in eighty. A defect rate of two and a half percent beats ten percent by about thirty-two. A survey: five hundred sixty yes in a thousand people. Fifty-six percent beats fifty percent one thousand three hundred sixty-three times over.

A neuron fires on nine trials out of twelve. A firing chance of seventy-five percent beats fifty percent by four point eight. And once without coins: eight reaction times, with a mean of four hundred thirty-four milliseconds, and a known spread of sixty. The curve over candidate means is a bell, and a mean of four hundred fifty copes two point six times better than four hundred. Six settings, one move: hold the data still, and turn the unknown.

Now the sentence everyone wants to say: the probability that the coin's chance of tails is seven tenths is twenty-six point seven percent. Cross it out. Twenty-six point seven percent is how often a coin of seven tenths gives seven tails. That is the chance of the data, given the coin. It is not the chance of the coin, given the data.

To say how probable a value of the coin is, you also need how plausible it was before the throws. That is a prior, and episode sixteen point one adds it. The likelihood alone cannot say it.

Remember: the likelihood is the chance of the data you saw, for each candidate value. It is not the chance of the candidate.

PLATE 5.37:02

Maximum likelihood

A walker in fog climbs the likelihood curve to its summit, the maximum likelihood estimate; for a coin it is simply the observed share of tails. The same climb handles two unknowns, a regression line (least squares for bell-shaped noise) and skewed gamma data. The sharpness of the peak predicts how much estimates would spread across studies.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have a curve. Where is its top?

Last time, the data stayed still, and the dial was the unknown. The curve said how well each candidate copes with what we saw. Today we climb it. The summit is the candidate that copes best, and it has a name: the maximum likelihood estimate.

Picture a ridge in thick fog. A walker cannot see the summit. She can only read the height under her feet, and the height one step to each side. Our ridge is the curve of seven tails in ten throws, and a height is the chance of that result, in percent.

She starts badly, at a chance of tails of point two, where the height is point one percent. The step to the right is higher, so she takes it. Point three: point nine percent. Point four: four point two. Point five: eleven point seven. Point six: twenty-one point five. Point seven: twenty-six point seven.

At point seven she looks both ways. To the left, twenty-one point five. To the right, twenty point one. Both are lower, so she stops. That is the summit, and it sits exactly on the share of tails we saw.

Start her at the other end, at point nine, where the height is five point seven percent. She steps down. Point eight: twenty point one. Point seven: twenty-six point seven. The same summit. This hill has one top, so every start ends in the same place.

Is that a coincidence? Change the data. With three tails in ten, the summit moves to point three. With seven in ten, point seven. With seventy in a hundred, point seven again, on a much narrower hill. Every time, the top lands on the observed share. For a coin, the estimator is simply tails divided by throws. Ronald Fisher gave the method its name in nineteen twenty-two, and had used the recipe ten years before. A warning: the summit is the best candidate for this data, not the truth. Another set of throws has another summit.

Now two unknowns at once. Here are eight blood pressures, illustrative: one thirty-six, one thirty-nine, one oh nine, one fourteen, one nineteen, one twenty-five, one thirty, one thirty-four. A bell has two dials: its centre, and its spread. Every pair of dials has a height, so the ridge becomes a map, with contour lines like a hiking map.

The walker starts at a centre of one hundred eight point five, and a spread of twenty-four, where the height is point three five percent. She turns whichever dial climbs best. When no step helps, she halves her steps. She keeps going, and she stops at a centre of one hundred twenty-five point eight, and a spread of ten point two.

Check it against the data. The mean of the eight values is one hundred twenty-five point seven five. Square each distance from the mean, average the squares by dividing by eight, and take the square root: ten point two. The climb and the arithmetic agree.

Notice the divisor. It is n, not n minus one. So maximum likelihood gives the divide-by-n variance of episodes two point three and five point one, slightly too small on average. In twenty thousand simulated studies of eight people, it averaged eighty-eight percent of the truth.

Now a line. Twelve doses, twelve responses, simulated with bell-shaped noise. The two dials are the line's start and its slope. The walker climbs the map again, and the contours are tilted ellipses. She stops at a start of one point five seven and a slope of one point four five.

Now the least-squares line, from episode nine point one, the one with the smallest total of squared misses. Its start is one point five seven, and its slope one point four five. The same line. When the noise is bell-shaped, the likelihood falls as the squared misses grow, so the highest likelihood is the smallest sum of squares. Least squares is a maximum likelihood.

So the summit says where. How sure can we be? Look at the hills. Seven in ten gives a wide peak. Seventy in a hundred gives a narrow one. Measure it: walk away from the summit until the height has fallen to sixty-one percent of the top. For ten throws, that takes you from point five five to point eight three, a width of point two eight. For a hundred throws, the width is point zero nine one. For a thousand, point zero two nine. Ten times the data, about three times narrower.

Guess before you look. We repeat the whole study five thousand times, each time with new throws and its own summit. How far will the summits spread?

Ten throws, simulated, true chance point seven. The summits spread by point one four four. Half the peak's width, measured on our one data set, was point one four two. With a hundred throws, the summits spread by point zero four five seven, and the half-width is point zero four five seven. With a thousand, point zero one four five, and point zero one four five.

A sharp peak means summits that barely move: a small standard error. A flat peak means summits that wander: a large one. The sharpness of one data set predicts the spread of all of them. And it shrinks like the square root of n, as in episode one point four. Four times the data, half the spread.

One more place, where no formula exists. Reaction times are usually skewed, with a long tail of slow trials. So take a skewed family, the gamma, with a shape dial and a scale dial. Here are forty simulated times, with a mean of three hundred sixty-three milliseconds. For any shape, the best scale is the mean divided by the shape. So only the shape is left to climb.

There is no school formula for that summit. The walker starts at a shape of six, where the height is point two percent. Ten: twenty-six percent. Fourteen: ninety-eight. She halves her steps, and stops at fourteen point seven. The climb found what no formula gives.

The simulated truth was nine. At nine, the height is twelve percent of the top. The peak is flat, so forty times leave the shape loose. That is what flatness means.

The same climb works everywhere, and every case here is illustrative. A survey: five hundred sixty yes answers in a thousand. The summit is fifty-six percent. A vaccine: thirty-six of forty respond. Ninety percent. A factory finds two defective items in eighty. Two point five percent.

A neuron, counted in ten one-second windows: four, three, seven, two, three, six, nine, seven, seven, three spikes. For a Poisson rate, the summit is the average count: five point one spikes per second. And a growth rate: a culture that starts at ten and is counted every hour, with noise. There is no formula for it either, and the climb gives point three per hour.

Remember: climb the likelihood to its summit, and you have an estimator for any model. The sharpness of the peak says how precise that estimate is.

The confidence interval

A confidence interval is a hoop thrown at a fixed peg: the estimate plus or minus about two standard errors. Of one hundred hoops about ninety-five catch the peg; the ninety-five percent describes the throwing, not the hoop in your hand. Quadrupling the people halves the margin, so a poll's margin of one point needs ten thousand voters, and five people need a t multiplier.

TRANSCRIPTEN

How far can an estimate be wrong?

Last time, an estimate was an arrow, and the truth was the bullseye. But one arrow cannot tell you how far it might be from the bullseye.

So today we throw a hoop instead. A hoop is an estimate with a margin around it. And the truth is a peg, planted in the sand. The peg never moves.

Here is the clinic again. The true average score is fifty, and individual scores spread by ten. These are simulated scores, not real patients. One study measures thirty people.

Study one. The average is fifty point seven seven. The thirty scores spread by eleven point four six, so the standard error, from episode one point four, is two point oh nine.

The hoop reaches two standard errors to each side. Two times two point oh nine is four point one eight. So the hoop runs from forty-six point five nine to fifty-four point nine five. That is the whole recipe: the estimate, plus or minus about two standard errors.

The peg at fifty is inside the hoop. This one caught it.

A second team studies the same clinic with thirty other people. Their average is forty-nine point four seven, their standard error is two point one six, and their hoop runs from forty-five point one five to fifty-three point seven nine. Another catch.

Two teams, two different answers, from one recipe. They differ because their thirty people differ, not because anyone made a mistake.

In real life, the peg is hidden. You can never see whether your hoop caught it. But a simulation can show the peg.

Guess first. We throw one hundred hoops, one per study. How many will catch the peg?

Here they come. Ninety-five caught the peg. Five missed, and they are drawn in pink. Each miss looked just as confident as the others, and had no idea.

Look at one miss. Its centre is fifty-three point three nine, and it reaches three point oh eight each way. It starts at fifty point three one. The peg is at fifty, a hair outside. A miss is not a wild hoop. It is a hoop from a slightly unlucky study.

Throw another hundred. Ninety-seven catch. Again: ninety-three. Then ninety-seven, then ninety-four. The count moves around, but it stays near ninety-five.

Now ten thousand studies. The hoops catch the peg in ninety-four point five percent of them. About half the misses fall below the peg, and half above. Cut into batches of a hundred, the count of catches runs from eighty-nine to a hundred.

Why does a reach of two work? Turn the picture around. Here are the ten thousand estimates, piled up like the sampling distribution of episode one point three. Ninety-five point three percent of them land within two standard errors of the peg. So the peg lies within two standard errors of ninety-five point three percent of the estimates. A hoop is that same fact, seen from the estimate's side. It is a little lower in practice, ninety-four point five, because each study must estimate its own standard error.

That is what ninety-five percent means. The peg stands still. The hoops are what vary. The ninety-five describes the throwing: how often this recipe catches the truth, over many studies.

It does not describe the one hoop in your hand. Once thrown, it has either caught the peg or not, and with the peg hidden, you do not know which.

Jerzy Neyman formalised this in the nineteen-thirties. And Geoff Cumming, in a twenty fourteen paper, drew this very picture, a dance of intervals, to teach it.

Now the same recipe, in a different bag.

A poll asks one thousand voters whether they support a proposal. The truth is that fifty-two percent do. Poll one finds fifty-one point two. For a share, the standard error is computed from the share itself: one point five eight points.

Two of them make three point one six. The hoop runs from forty-eight point oh four to fifty-four point three six, and it catches fifty-two. Pollsters report that as a margin of error of about three points.

Is the proposal winning? The poll says fifty-one point two, but the hoop reaches down to forty-eight. It includes fifty, so this poll cannot say.

Ninety-five of the first hundred polls catch the peg. Over ten thousand polls, ninety-five point eight percent do. A touch above ninety-five, because two is slightly more than needed with a thousand voters.

Now a knob: the number of people. Back to the clinic.

With thirty people, the hoops are plus or minus three point six three wide, on average. With one hundred and twenty people, four times as many, they are one point eight two. With four hundred and eighty, they are zero point nine one. Four times the people halves the margin. The width shrinks like the square root of n.

A pilot study of ten people is the other extreme. Its hoop reaches six point one five each way, a span of twelve points, when the scores themselves spread by ten. It is honest, and it says very little.

A narrow hoop is not a safer hoop, though. With four hundred and eighty people, the coverage is still near ninety-five percent, so about four hoops in a hundred miss. More data buys precision, not certainty.

Guess again. A poll of one thousand has a margin of three points. How many voters for a margin of one point?

Not three times as many. Four thousand voters give one point five eight. A margin of one point needs ten thousand voters, ten times the poll. Precision is expensive.

A second knob: how far the hoop reaches. Guess first: at plus or minus one standard error, how often does it catch the peg?

Plus or minus one standard error catches the peg sixty-seven percent of the time. One and a half: eighty-five and a half. Two: ninety-four point five. Two and a half: ninety-eight point two. Three: ninety-nine point four.

That is the sixty-eight, ninety-five, ninety-nine point seven rule from episode four, now applied to the standard error. Ninety-five percent is a convention, not a law. A wider hoop catches more, and says less.

One last warning about the words about two. Take studies of only five people. Two standard errors catch the peg just eighty-seven point nine percent of the time. The standard error is itself estimated from five scores, so it is sometimes far too small.

The fix is a bigger multiplier: two point seven seven six, from a table of Student's t. The hoop is one point three nine times wider, and it catches the peg ninety-five point two percent of the time. With thirty people, the multiplier is two point oh four five. That is why about two works there. Software does this for you.

So a confidence interval is a hoop: the estimate, plus or minus about two standard errors.

Remember: the ninety-five percent belongs to the throwing, not to the hoop in your hand.

What a confidence interval does not say

A 95 percent interval describes the procedure, like hoops thrown at a peg, not the truth, the data, or the next study. Simulations break three popular sentences: the chance the truth is inside, the share of patients inside, and the share of repeat studies inside, which averages only 83.7 percent. Researchers endorsed these errors as often as first-year students.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Three sentences that sound right, and are wrong.

Last time, the hoops caught the peg ninety-five times in a hundred. The ninety-five belonged to the throwing, not to the hoop in your hand. Today we put that to the test. There are three sentences people love to say about one interval, and each one sticks the ninety-five on the wrong thing.

Here is one trial, illustrative. A blood pressure treatment lowers pressure by six point zero millimetres of mercury, with a standard error of two. Two standard errors, more exactly one point nine six, make three point nine two. The interval runs from two point oh eight to nine point nine two.

Now read it aloud. Sentence one: there is a ninety-five percent chance that the true effect is inside. Sentence two: ninety-five percent of the patients are inside. Sentence three: ninety-five percent of repeat studies will land inside.

In twenty fourteen, Rink Hoekstra and colleagues showed students and researchers a ninety-five percent interval, and six statements about it. All six were false. Only three percent of the researchers judged them all false. On average the researchers endorsed three point four five of them, as many as first-year students, at three point five one, who had never studied inference.

So we will test each sentence, with a simulation, and peel the sticker off.

Sentence one. A lab runs one hundred thousand trials like ours. Every treatment has a true effect, drawn from a pool where most treatments do little, with a spread of two. Each trial measures its treatment with a standard error of two, and builds its interval.

Over all of them, ninety-five point oh percent of the intervals catch the true effect. So far, the sentence holds.

But our trial estimated six. Guess first. Among the trials that estimated about six, how many intervals caught the truth?

Three thousand, one hundred and fifty-nine trials estimated between five and seven. Their intervals caught the truth in seventy-six point two percent. Not ninety-five.

Why? Treatments with big true effects are rare, so a big estimate is often a modest treatment with a lucky measurement. Near zero it goes the other way: ninety-nine point four percent catch the truth. The ninety-five is an average over all estimates. It is not a fact about the interval in front of you.

To say how likely the truth is to be inside, you need the pool. That is the prior, HUNCH's map. With it, the interval you can read as a probability is a credible interval. For an estimate of six, it runs from zero point two three to five point seven seven, shifted toward zero. Among those trials, it catches the truth ninety-five point one percent of the time. Episode sixteen builds it.

Now turn the knob on the pool. With a spread of one, the confidence interval catches the truth in twenty-six point eight percent. With four, ninety-three point three. With twenty, a pool so wide it says almost nothing, ninety-five point two, and the credible interval, two point oh four to nine point eight four, nearly matches ours. That is why the numbers often agree. The meaning differs, and Morey and colleagues, in twenty sixteen, collected cases where the two split apart.

Why does it matter? A headline says: we are ninety-five percent sure this treatment lowers pressure by at least two. In a field where most treatments do little, an estimate of six deserves less belief than that. The interval alone cannot tell you how much less.

Sentence two. Back to the clinic: scores spread by ten, and thirty people. This study's interval runs from forty-six point seven two to fifty-four point four four. Nine of the thirty scores are inside. That is thirty percent, not ninety-five. Over two thousand studies of this size, twenty-eight point oh percent.

Now the knob is the number of people. Guess first: with four times as many people, does the share of scores inside go up, or down?

Down. With one hundred and twenty, fourteen point one percent of the scores are inside. With four hundred and eighty, seven point two. With seven thousand six hundred and eighty, one point eight. More data narrows the interval, and it does not narrow the data. An interval for the mean says where the mean is. It does not say where people are.

The interval that does hold about ninety-five percent of the scores is the mean, plus or minus about two standard deviations, from episode two point three. Here it runs from twenty-nine point three nine to seventy-one point seven seven, and twenty-eight of thirty scores are inside.

Another bag. The resting heart rate of eighty students, simulated. The average is seventy point one nine, and the interval runs from sixty-eight point four seven to seventy-one point nine one. A reader may think students rest between sixty-eight and seventy-two. Only sixteen of the eighty do. They range from forty-eight point six to ninety point zero.

So if a patient asks what pulse to expect, do not hand over the interval for the mean. Give the range for people. One sentence, two very different answers.

Sentence three. An exam: true average sixty, spread fifteen, twenty-five students. The first study averages sixty-three point five seven, and its interval runs from fifty-seven point six nine to sixty-nine point four five. It caught the truth.

Now repeat the exam a thousand times, with new students each time. Guess: how many of the new averages land inside that interval?

Seven hundred and sixty. Not nine hundred and fifty. And all two hundred and forty misses are below it. This first study was lucky high, so its interval sits to the right of the truth, and the new averages pile up around sixty.

How lucky was it? Take one hundred different first studies, and for each, compute exactly how many repeats land inside. The share runs from seven point five percent to ninety-five point oh. The average is eighty-three point seven. Over two hundred thousand first studies it is eighty-three point four, a figure Cumming and Maillardet published in two thousand six.

So about one repeat in six lands outside the original interval, not one in twenty. A repeat that falls outside is not a failed replication. It is expected.

Would a bigger exam fix this? No. With one hundred students, the average share is eighty-three point four. With four hundred, eighty-three point five. The interval shrinks, and so does the spread of the new averages, so the share does not move.

Yet ninety-five of those hundred first studies did catch the truth. The procedure kept its promise. That is where the sticker belongs. Not on the truth, not on the data, not on the next study, but on the throwing.

Remember: the ninety-five percent describes the procedure. It is not the chance that the truth is inside, not the share of the data, and not the share of repeats.
