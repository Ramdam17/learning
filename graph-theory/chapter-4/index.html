PLATE 4.15:14

No two neighbours alike

A sudoku, an exam timetable and radio frequencies are all graph colouring: no two linked dots share a colour. The fewest colours is the chromatic number. Greedy colouring gives each dot the first free colour and always works. On eleven exams, one order used five slots and another used three, so order matters and the best result is not guaranteed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the tiler's workshop. This is chapter four: colouring and matching. On the wall hangs today's panel. It is a sudoku grid. Nobody would call that a graph.

But look closer. Make every cell a dot. That makes eighty-one dots. Now join two cells when they share a row, a column, or a three by three box. Take any cell. Eight others share its row. Eight share its column. Four more share its box, and are not counted yet. So every cell has twenty neighbours. Eighty-one cells, twenty neighbours each, and every link counted twice: eight hundred and ten links.

And there is the hidden graph. To fill the grid, you give every dot one of nine numbers, so that no two neighbours get the same one. A number is a colour. Filling a sudoku is colouring a graph with nine colours. The sudoku will come back. Today we start smaller, with an exam timetable.

A school has eleven exams to place in time. Some students sit two of them. Those two exams cannot happen at once. So draw a dot for each exam, and a link between two exams when one student sits both. This timetable is invented. It has twenty-two links.

Now give every exam a slot. Think of each slot as a colour. The rule is the one from the sudoku: two linked exams never share a colour. Fewer colours means fewer slots, and a shorter exam week. The smallest number of colours that works has a name. It is the chromatic number.

How do we find a colouring? Here is the simplest method. It is called greedy. Take the exams one at a time, in some order. Give each exam the first colour that none of its neighbours already holds. That is all.

Let us run it. The exams arrive in the order they were registered. Maths takes slot one. History does not clash with maths, so it also takes slot one. Physics clashes with history, so it takes slot two. Law clashes with maths, and takes slot two as well. Chemistry meets slots one and two, so it takes slot three. And so on down the list. Statistics comes last. It clashes with slots one and two, so it takes slot three.

The greedy method has used five slots. A whole working week, for eleven exams.

Now deal the same exams in another order. First the five on the left, then the five on the right, then statistics. The five on the left never clash with each other, so they all take slot one. Each exam on the right clashes with some of them, and none of its own side, so they all take slot two. Statistics takes slot three. Three slots, not five.

Same graph. Same rule. Five slots in one order, three in the other. The order matters.

And notice what the screen never told us. Nothing said the first timetable could be improved. Greedy always gives a valid colouring: no two linked exams ever share a slot. But it does not promise the best one.

Is three the best here? Yes. Maths, law and statistics clash with each other, so they need three different slots. No timetable can use fewer. The chromatic number of this graph is three.

How bad can an order be? We tried a hundred thousand random orders. Nearly ninety-five in a hundred found three slots. About one in eighty needed five. Bad orders are rare. But on a big timetable, nobody can check.

In nineteen sixty-seven, Welsh and Powell published a two-page paper in The Computer Journal. Its title: an upper bound for the chromatic number of a graph, and its application to timetabling problems. Their advice is to sort the exams by degree, the most clashes first, and colour in that order. And they showed that, sorted this way, greedy never needs more colours than a number you read straight from the degrees. For our graph, that number is five.

Largest first is a good habit. But here the clashes are nearly all equal, four or five, so the habit has nothing to grip. That order also uses five slots.

Colouring hides in many places. Two radio stations close enough to disturb each other must use different frequencies. A station is a dot, a possible disturbance is a link, and a frequency is a colour. Engineers have written frequency assignment as graph colouring, and a review by William Hale, in nineteen eighty, is a classic.

One more disguise has a famous history. In eighteen fifty-two, Francis Guthrie asked whether four colours always suffice to colour a map, so that countries sharing a border differ. In nineteen seventy-six, Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved it, with a computer checking the cases. A map is a special kind of graph. We will stop at the mention, and keep every graph in view.

To sum up. Colouring gives each dot a colour, so that no two neighbours share one. The fewest colours is the chromatic number. Greedy takes the dots in some order, and gives each the first free colour. It always works, and the order decides how well.

Next time, we ask a harder question. Can we always find the best colouring, quickly? Our sudoku wall will help us find out. Why colouring is hard.

PLATE 4.25:56

Why colouring is hard

Two colours force every move, so the answer comes quickly: a colouring or an odd loop. With three colours, choices branch into a tree, and search cost climbs steeply, about 256,000 tries at thirty dots. Colouring is on Karp's list of hard problems. Compilers meet it when assigning registers, spilling a variable to memory when colours run out.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the tiler's workshop. Last time we coloured with the greedy method, and the order mattered. We ended on a question. Can we always find the best colouring, quickly? Today we find out. We start with two colours.

Here is a small graph. It has ten dots and eleven links, and it is invented. Pick one dot and make it pink. Now look at its neighbours. Each could be pink or blue. But pink is taken by their neighbour. So only blue is left. There is no choice at all. The neighbours are forced. And their neighbours are forced in turn, back to pink, like dominoes.

In the whole graph we made one free choice, at the start. The other nine dots were forced, and we never stepped back. Looking at each link from both ends makes twenty-two checks. The answer is in: yes, two colours are enough.

Can two colours fail? Yes, in one way. Take two dots of the same colour, at the ends of a path of four links. Join them. The new link closes a loop of five. Look at the last dot of the path. Its neighbour on the path is blue, and its new neighbour is pink. It has no colour left. This is the odd loop from episode one point four, and with two colours it is the only obstacle. So the answer is always quick: a colouring, or an odd loop.

Now give the dots a third colour. Take a new graph, with nine dots and seventeen links. Put the dots in a fixed order, A to I, and colour them one by one. A takes pink. Look at a neighbour. Pink is taken, but two colours remain: blue and yellow. Now there is a choice, and nothing says which one is right.

So we guess, and carry on. Each dot takes the first colour that none of its neighbours holds. Sometimes a dot finds all three colours taken. That is a dead end. Then we step back and change the last guess. Here dot I is stuck. Dot H has no other colour to try. Neither has G, and neither has F. Only at E is there a colour left to change. That is backtracking.

Thirty tries and four dead ends, for just nine dots.

Let us see how this grows. For each size from ten to thirty dots, we draw forty random graphs with four links per dot. These graphs are simulated. We run the same method, and count the tries until it says yes or no. At ten dots, the median is three hundred sixty-eight tries. At twenty dots, about nineteen thousand. At thirty, about two hundred fifty-six thousand. Three times as many dots, and about seven hundred times the work.

Asked with two colours, the same graphs cost at most twice the number of links: a hundred and twenty checks at thirty dots. Trying every assignment would mean three to the power thirty, about two hundred six trillion. Our method prunes, but the curve keeps climbing. This is one simple method, on one family of graphs, and cleverer methods go further. What nobody has is a method that stays fast on every graph.

That is no accident. In nineteen seventy-two, Richard Karp listed twenty-one problems that are hard in the same way. It is the list from episode three point four. Colouring is on it: given a graph and a number of colours, can the graph be coloured with that many? In nineteen seventy-six, Garey, Johnson and Stockmeyer showed that three colours are already enough to make it hard. A fast method for three colours would give a fast method for all twenty-one. Nobody has found one, and nobody has proved that none exists.

So why does the difficulty flip between two and three? Take two colours: a coloured dot forces its neighbours. Forced moves make a chain, and a chain has one way to go. With three colours, a coloured dot leaves its neighbours two options. Choices make a tree, and a tree branches at every step. One extra colour turns a chain into a tree. That is the tree we walked, and the curve we measured.

Hard problems still get solved every day. One sits inside your computer. A compiler turns a program into instructions, and the processor keeps values in a few fast boxes called registers. Two variables alive at the same time cannot share one. So draw a dot for each variable, and a link between two variables that are alive together. A register is a colour.

A tiny invented program has seven variables. Each bar shows the lines where a variable is alive. The bars give eleven links, and three registers are enough. Now add one more variable, alive together with three others. Four variables at once, and only three registers. No colouring exists. One variable must wait in memory, which is slower. It is called a spill.

Chaitin and five colleagues published this idea in nineteen eighty-one, and Chaitin added the spilling in nineteen eighty-two. Because exact colouring is hard, compilers settle for quick rules that give good colourings, not always the best.

And the sudoku on our wall? Its graph has eighty-one dots, twenty neighbours each, and eight hundred ten links. On our panel, thirty cells are already filled. The plain method fills the rest in three hundred twenty-three tries. A nine by nine grid is small, and the filled cells remove most of the choices. The hardness is about growth. For grids of any size, Yato and Seta showed in two thousand three that completing a partly filled sudoku is hard in the same way as colouring.

The Quantum Computing series comes back to problems like this. In its episode sixteen point three, they are written as an energy to minimise.

To sum up. Two colours force every move, and the answer comes at once. Three colours leave a choice, and the search becomes a tree. Nobody knows a method that is fast on every graph, and colouring sits on Karp's list.

Next time, a different question. Some participants, and some time slots. Who can come to which slot, and can everyone be served? Making pairs.

PLATE 4.35:31

Making pairs

Six participants and six slots form a bipartite graph, and a matching gives each person a slot. The greedy choice places four, but an augmenting path improves it to five. Hall's condition says when everyone can be served, and a matching is a flow in disguise. Its largest size equals the smallest vertex cover, Kőnig's theorem.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tiler's workshop. Chapter four began with conflicts: dots that must not share a colour. Now the question turns around. Instead of keeping things apart, we want to put them together, two by two. Today, we make pairs.

Here is a small problem. An experiment has six participants and six time slots. Everyone has told us which slots they can attend. The names and the slots are invented. Put the participants on a top row, and the slots on a bottom row. Draw a link when a participant is free at that slot. If this reminds you of the coat rack, it should. Two kinds of dots, and links only between the kinds. It is a bipartite graph.

We want to give each participant a slot. But a slot holds one participant, and a participant needs one slot. A choice of links that share no dot is called a matching. Each link in it is a pair: one participant, one slot. We look for the biggest matching.

Let us try the simple way. Go through the participants in order, and give each the first free slot they can attend. Ana takes slot one. Ben can only come to slot one, and it is gone. So Ben waits. Cleo takes slot two. Dan takes slot five. Eve takes slot six. Fay can only come to slots five and six, and both are gone. That makes four pairs, with Ben and Fay left out.

Is four the best? Look at Ben again. He could have slot one, if Ana moved. Ana can also attend slot two. But Cleo holds slot two, and she can also attend slot three, which is free.

Follow that chain. Ben takes slot one. Ana gives it up, and takes slot two. Cleo gives that up, and takes slot three. Everyone in the chain still has a slot, and Ben has gained one. Five pairs. A path that alternates between new links and old links, and starts and ends on a free dot, always gives one more pair. It is called an augmenting path.

Now try the same for Fay. She could go to slot five, held by Dan. Dan could move to slot six, held by Eve. Eve could move to slot five, held by Dan. We are going round in circles, and nothing is free. The search reaches Dan, Eve and Fay, and slots five and six. Then it stops.

Look at who is stuck. Three participants, who together can attend only two slots. Three people cannot fit into two slots, whatever method we use. That is the whole obstruction. In 1935, Philip Hall proved that it is the only one. His paper is called On representatives of subsets. A matching that serves every participant exists exactly when every group of participants has, all together, at least as many available slots as it has members.

We can check every group. There are sixty-three groups of participants. Sixty pass. Three fail: Dan, Eve and Fay, then those three with Ben, then those four with Ana as well. The worst is short by one slot. And, as it happens, the best matching leaves exactly one participant out.

Now the strong moment. A matching is a flow in disguise. Add a source above the top row, joined to every participant. Add a sink below the bottom row, joined from every slot. Give every belt a capacity of one. Then send parcels through, as in the sorting hall.

A parcel goes from the source to a participant, along a link to a slot, and on to the sink. That path is a pair. Capacity one means that no participant and no slot is used twice. With whole-number capacities, the method only ever pushes whole parcels, so a flow is always a set of pairs.

And what was our chain? It was the backward push from the sorting hall. When Ana gave up slot one, a parcel went back along a belt. The largest flow here is five. And the biggest matching is five. NetworkX finds both, and they agree.

Now bring back the fence from the bottleneck theorem. When the search stopped, it had reached some dots. Here they are: Fay, Dan and Eve, and slots five and six. Draw a fence around them. It crosses three belts from the source, to Ana, Ben and Cleo. And it crosses two belts into the sink, from slots five and six. Five gates, each of capacity one.

Each gate belongs to a dot: Ana, Ben, Cleo, slot five and slot six. These five dots touch every link. Every link has at least one end among them. A set of dots that touches every link is called a vertex cover.

No matching can beat a cover. Each pair has an end in the cover, and a dot can serve only one pair. So the pairs can never outnumber the dots of the cover. Here, five pairs meet a cover of five.

In 1931, Dénes Kőnig proved that, in a bipartite graph, this always happens. The largest matching equals the smallest vertex cover. Jenő Egerváry found a version with weights the same year. This theorem is a cousin of the bottleneck theorem. Largest flow, smallest cut. Largest set of pairs, smallest set of dots. And with capacities of one, the second comes straight out of the first.

To sum up. Pairs are links that share no dot: that is a matching. An augmenting path makes it bigger. Hall says when everyone can be served. The matching is a flow, and its largest size equals the smallest vertex cover.

PLATE 4.44:59

Stable pairs

Five interns and five hospitals rank each other. A blocking pair would both rather leave, and a stable assignment has none. Of 120 possible assignments, only three are stable. Gale and Shapley's proposal method finds one, and the proposing side does best: interns average choice 1.6 when they propose, and 2.4 when hospitals propose.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tiler's workshop. Last time we made pairs from lists of who is free. Now everyone has preferences. Today, we make stable pairs.

Here is a small market. Five interns, A to E, and five hospitals, one to five. Each hospital has a single place. Every name and every list is invented. Each intern ranks the hospitals, best first. Each hospital ranks the interns, best first.

Suppose someone hands out the places. Intern A goes to hospital three, B to one, C to four, D to two, and E to five. Everybody has a place. Is that fair?

Look at intern E. Hospital five is only E's second choice. Hospital four is E's first. And hospital four holds intern C, but ranks E above C. So E and hospital four would both be better off leaving the plan together. Such a pair is called a blocking pair. An assignment with no blocking pair is called stable.

We can check this by brute force. An assignment has twenty-five pairs to test. And there are one hundred and twenty ways to place five interns in five hospitals. Test them all. One hundred and seventeen have a blocking pair. Only three are stable.

That works for five. We want a method that finds a stable assignment directly. In 1962, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley gave one, and it runs on proposals.

Every intern without a place proposes to the best hospital on their list that has not refused them yet. Each hospital keeps the best proposal it has seen, and refuses the rest. A refused intern tries the next hospital. Nothing is final until the end.

Round one. A and D both propose to hospital two. B proposes to one, C to three, and E to four. Hospital two prefers D, so it refuses A. The other hospitals hold their single proposal.

In round two, A tries hospital three, their next choice. Hospital three prefers A to C. It keeps A, and lets C go.

In round three, C tries hospital four. Hospital four holds E, and prefers E. So C is refused again.

In round four, C tries hospital five. It is free, so it holds C. Everyone has a place, and the method stops.

Four rounds, eight proposals, three refusals. B has hospital one, D has two, A has three, E has four, and C has five.

Is this stable? Suppose intern X prefers some hospital to the one they got. Then X proposed there earlier, and was refused. A hospital only ever trades up. So that hospital likes its own intern better than X. No blocking pair is possible. And no intern asks the same hospital twice, so there are at most twenty-five proposals, and the method always stops.

Swap the roles. Same market, same lists. This time the hospitals propose, and each intern keeps the best offer.

It takes four rounds and eight proposals again. And it ends differently. A has hospital three, B has five, C has two, D has one, E has four. Three interns change hospital.

Both results are stable. But look at who gains. When interns propose, they receive on average their choice number one point six. When hospitals propose, two point four. For the hospitals it is the other way round: two point six, against one point six.

Our market has three stable assignments. The third lies in between, at two point zero for the interns and two point two for the hospitals. Gale and Shapley proved something stronger. The side that proposes gets the best stable assignment it could hope for, and the other side gets the worst. Here, every intern does as well in the first run as in any of the three. We checked.

So the method looks neutral, and it is not. Who proposes is a choice with consequences.

This is no toy. Hospitals were already using such a method, without a name for it. From 1952, the American programme that places medical interns used a procedure of its own. Gale and Shapley's paper came ten years later. In 1984, Alvin Roth showed that the 1952 procedure was essentially the same method, with the hospitals proposing. For the 1998 match, the programme switched to students proposing.

In 2012, Roth and Shapley received the Nobel prize in economics. The citation reads: for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design. David Gale had died in 2008.

To sum up. A blocking pair is two people who would both rather leave. A stable assignment has none. Proposals find one, and the side that proposes does best.

PLATE 4.56:30

Pairs within one group

Pairing within one group, such as tandem riders, creates odd loops that confuse the augmenting-path search. Edmonds wrapped such a loop into a blossom and called his method good because its work grows as a power of the size. The Hungarian method handles costs on two sides, and kidney swaps use cycles, serving six of seven pairs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the tiler's workshop. Last time, every pair had two sides, interns and hospitals. Today there is one group, and nobody has a side.

Eight people plan a ride. Each tandem bicycle seats exactly two. A link means that two people can share one. Every link here is invented. We want as many tandems as possible, and nobody rides two.

Start with a quick choice. B rides with C, D with E, and G with H. That is three tandems. Only A and F are left, and they are not linked. So nobody else can be added.

Is three the best? In 4.3 we improved a matching with an augmenting path. Start from a person without a tandem. Take a free link, then a tandem link, and so on, until you reach another person without a tandem. Then flip every link on the way.

Try it from A. A links to B, and B's tandem goes to C. A also links to E, and E's tandem goes to D. Now C and D are linked. That closes a loop of five people, A, B, C, D and E. Five is odd.

Between two sides, a loop is always even. Here it is odd, and the search gets lost. It reaches B by a free link, so it can only leave B by its tandem link. Yet going the other way round the loop, B is reached by its tandem link, and leaves by a free link. That link leads to F. The search never sees it, and reports that there is no path.

Jack Edmonds found the way out in 1965. Wrap the whole loop in one big ring, and treat it as a single person. He called such a loop a blossom. The group shrinks to four: the ring, F, G and H.

Now the search is easy. The ring has no tandem, because its base, A, has none. The ring links to F, who has none either. That is an augmenting path.

Open the ring again. We need a way through it: from A to E, E to D, D to C, C to B, and then out to F. Flip every link on the path. Now A rides with E, C with D, and B with F. With G and H, that makes four tandems, and everyone has a ride.

Edmonds's paper is called Paths, trees, and flowers. In it, he says what a good method should be. Trying every way to pair people is always possible. For our eight, there are one hundred and five ways. For twenty people, about six hundred and fifty-five million. For forty, about three point two times ten to the twenty-third. Edmonds wrote that the obvious method grows exponentially with the size of the problem. He asked for a method whose difficulty grows only algebraically, and claimed that one exists for matching. He called it good.

His first bound was about n to the fourth, where n is the number of people. At twenty people, that is one hundred and sixty thousand. At forty, two million five hundred and sixty thousand. This idea became the usual test for an efficient method: the work grows as a power of the size.

Now put costs on the choices. Four helpers, four jobs, and a cost in minutes for each helper on each job. Every number is invented. If each helper in turn takes the cheapest job still free, the total is eighteen minutes. The best total is sixteen.

Here the two sides are back, so Harold Kuhn's method of 1955 applies. It is called the Hungarian method, after two Hungarian mathematicians whose ideas it uses, Kőnig and Egerváry.

First, subtract the smallest number of each row: six, two, three and two. Then do the same for each column: zero, zero, one and one. Each zero is a free choice. Cover all the zeros with as few lines as possible. Here, three lines are enough. But four helpers need four zeros that share no row and no column, and Kőnig's theorem from 4.3 says that needs four lines.

So take the smallest number that no line covers, which is one. Subtract it from every uncovered cell, and add it where two lines cross. Now four zeros stand apart. A takes job four, B takes two, C takes one, and D takes three. The cost is sixteen. The amounts we subtracted add up to the same total: thirteen, two and one. Edmonds extended his blossom method to weights too, for any group, not only two sides.

Now pairs that save lives. A kidney patient may have a friend or a relative who is willing to give a kidney, but who is not compatible. Two such pairs can swap: each donor gives to the other's patient.

In 2004, Alvin Roth, Tayfun Sönmez and Utku Ünver studied how to organise such swaps on a larger scale. In their model, each patient points to a kidney, and each kidney points to its own patient. Trades happen along the cycles of that directed graph.

Here is a small example. Every pair is one dot, a patient with a donor. An arrow from pair i to pair j means that patient i can receive the kidney of donor j. There are seven pairs and fourteen arrows. All invented.

A swap is a cycle of arrows. There are three cycles of two pairs, and two cycles of three pairs. All the operations of one cycle are done at the same time, so cycles stay short. Here we allow up to three.

Swaps of two pairs alone are a matching, in the graph where two pairs are joined when each can give to the other. The best matching serves four pairs. Now allow cycles of three, with no pair used twice. The best choice serves six pairs out of seven. Take the first swap in sight instead, and you serve five. Choosing well here means that more patients get a kidney.

The same problem appears in the Hyperscanning series. To record pairs of volunteers together, someone must decide who is paired with whom. Link two volunteers when they could do the task together. There are no two sides, so it is a matching in a graph with odd loops.

To sum up. Pairs inside one group form a matching in a graph that may have odd loops. A blossom wraps an odd loop, so the search can run, and then it opens. Edmonds called this method good, because its work grows as a power of the size. With costs, the Hungarian method solves the two-sided case. And a kidney swap is a cycle, not only a pair.
