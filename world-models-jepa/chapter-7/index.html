PLATE 7.16:35

Two views of the same thing

Two random views of one photo, made by cropping, color distortion and blur, go through a Siamese network, one encoder with shared weights, like binoculars with a single focus wheel. Pulling the two necklaces together asks the encoder to ignore what the augmentations change. A loss that only pulls pairs is won by giving every image the same necklace, a failure called representation collapse.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the masked ball. Every dancer here wears a mask, and the game of the evening is simple. Take two different masks on the same dancer. Are these two people the same? In this chapter, learning means answering yes, and answering it without anyone telling us the names. Today we build the first piece: two views of the same thing, and a network that looks at both. By the end, you will also know why that is not enough.

Start with one photo. A view is that photo after a small, random change. Crop a piece of it, and stretch the piece back to full size. Shift the colours, brighter here, paler there, or turn it grey. Blur it a little. The scene is the same, but the pixels are very different.

The SimCLR paper, from 2020, uses exactly these three changes, one after another. The crop keeps a random share of the picture, anywhere from eight percent of its area up to the whole of it. The colours are jittered, most of the time. And a Gaussian blur is applied half of the time. Each photo gets two such draws, and the two results are a pair of views.

Why colours and not only crops? The authors report that one combination stands out: random cropping together with colour distortion. With crops alone, most pieces of a photo share a similar colour distribution, and a network can match two views just by comparing colour histograms. That is a shortcut, and a shortcut teaches nothing about the scene. Distorting the colours closes it.

Now the network. Picture a pair of binoculars. Two barrels, side by side, one for each view. Between them sits a single focus wheel, and turning it moves both barrels together. That is the trick. We build two copies of ENKO, our weaving machine, and we force them to share the same weights. Whatever one copy learns, the other copy learns at the same instant. Because they are always identical, the pair is really one encoder, used twice.

This idea is older than deep learning's current fame. In 1993, Bromley and colleagues at AT and T Bell Laboratories published a paper at the NIPS conference called Signature Verification using a Siamese Time Delay Neural Network. Their network had two identical sub-networks, joined at their outputs, and the two were constrained to have identical weights. They fed in two signatures and compared the two feature vectors. For a genuine pair, they asked for a cosine of one. For a genuine signature against a forgery, they asked for a large angle. The name stuck, and we still call this a Siamese network.

Why must the weights be shared? Imagine two separate encoders. They could agree on a private code that has nothing to do with the photo, and the two necklaces would match without any meaning. With one shared encoder, there is only one rule to learn. Whatever it picks up must work on any image, on any view, because the same machine reads them all.

Now look at the two necklaces. Left barrel, one necklace. Right barrel, another. We measure how far apart they are, bead by bead, square the gaps, and add them up. This is the squared distance. The training signal is simple: turn the wheel so that this distance shrinks.

What does shrinking it ask for? Think back to episode four point four. A necklace that stays put when the photo changes is invariant to that change. Pulling the two views together asks ENKO to ignore exactly what the augmentations change: the position of the crop, the tint, the blur. And it must keep whatever the two views share, which is the scene itself.

Let's measure this on a toy example, labelled illustrative. Four small images, each a list of six numbers. For each one, two views: a different brightness and tint, plus a touch of noise. A first encoder, with random weights, gives two necklaces for each image that are not alike. The mean squared distance between paired necklaces is zero point five three. Now use an encoder that first removes the average and the scale of its input, which is just what the tint changes. The mean squared distance falls to zero point zero one. The pairs now match, and the four images still have clearly different necklaces: the spread of the beads, across the batch, is about zero point six five.

So the pull works. Pull harder, and the loss goes to zero. And here is the strong moment of this episode: that is exactly where it goes wrong.

Ask the network for the easiest way to make the distance zero. It does not need to read the photo at all. Let it write the same necklace for everything. Every view of every image, the same beads. Then every pair is identical, and the distance is exactly zero.

Try it in NumPy. The constant encoder returns zeros of length four, whatever you give it. Compute the mean squared distance between paired necklaces: zero, exactly. Compute the spread of the necklaces across the batch: zero as well. The loss is perfect, and the necklaces know nothing. In the earlier example, the good encoder had a distance of zero point zero one and a spread of zero point six five. The cheat has zero and zero. By the loss alone, the cheat wins.

This is called representation collapse. Giving everyone the same necklace is the simplest solution to a loss that only pulls pairs together. Everything else in this chapter is about preventing it.

There are four ways out, and each gets its own episode. The first is to add negatives: push apart the necklaces of different images. That is Contrastive learning: SimCLR, and then InfoNCE and mutual information, which explains the loss it uses. The second is asymmetry: no negatives, but the two branches are not the same, as in No negatives, one asymmetry: BYOL. The third is explicit rules: keep the spread of the beads above a floor, and stop beads from copying each other, as in No negatives, explicit rules: VICReg. And Representation collapse shows how to diagnose the problem, with measures that count the directions a crowd really uses.

To sum up. A view is a photo after a random crop, colour change and blur. A Siamese network sends both views through one shared encoder, and pulling the two necklaces together asks it to ignore what the augmentations change. But a loss that only pulls pairs can be won by giving everyone the same necklace. Next time: Contrastive learning: SimCLR.

PLATE 7.25:30

Contrastive learning: SimCLR

SimCLR makes two masked versions of one photo, a random crop plus a color distortion, and passes both through the same encoder. A softmax over the rest of the batch rewards the loss when the twin takes most of the share, so the other dancers act as negatives. The projection head is discarded after training, and the price is large batches and false negatives.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the masked ball. The rule of the evening is simple: two different masks on the same dancer must be recognised as the same person. Today we turn that rule into a way of learning, with a method called SimCLR. Like every method in this chapter, it comes in four parts: the intuition, the architecture in one picture, what it learns, and the classic pitfall.

First, the intuition. Take one photo and make two versions of it. Crop it differently, shift its colors, and you have two masks on the same dancer. Two identical copies of ENKO, sharing the very same weights, turn each version into a necklace. Now the game has two moves. Pull the two necklaces of the same dancer together. Push the necklaces of every other dancer away. The pairs to pull are called positives. The ones to push are called negatives.

Which masks matter? The authors, Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton, tried many, and one pair stood out: a random crop, together with a random color distortion. There is a good reason. With crops alone, most patches of a photo share the same colors, so a network can match two views just by comparing their color histograms. That is a shortcut, not understanding. Distorting the colors closes it.

Second, the architecture in one picture. Two views go in. The same encoder, ENKO, turns each one into a necklace. Then comes a small addition, the projection head: a tiny network that reshapes the necklace once more. In the paper it has two layers, with a ReLU between them, and it ends in one hundred twenty-eight numbers. The balance sits after the head, in the representation panel, and it compares the two necklaces.

And the other dancers? Take a batch of N photos. Each gives two views, so there are twice N necklaces on the floor. For one anchor, one of them is its twin. The other twice N, minus two, are negatives. Nobody has to choose them. The rest of the batch is the crowd.

Now the loss. Compare the anchor with every other necklace using the cosine, from episode 2.1. Divide by a temperature, called tau. Then do what we did in episode 2.5: a softmax across all the other necklaces, and the cross-entropy of the right answer, the twin. The loss is small when the twin takes most of the share. The paper calls it N T X ent, the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy.

Here is a toy batch, illustrative: four dancers, eight necklaces. For our anchor, the twin scores a cosine of zero point nine two. The six others score between minus zero point eight four and minus zero point oh five. At a temperature of zero point five, the twin takes sixty-eight percent of the softmax, and the loss is zero point three nine. The temperature is a focus knob: lower it, and small differences are stretched, so the hardest strangers count more.

Third, what it learns. Necklaces that ignore the masks, since the masks are exactly what the loss must see through, and yet keep different dancers apart. One detail is worth remembering. After training, the projection head is thrown away. We keep the necklace before the head. The paper found it better by more than ten percent, under a linear probe, than the one after the head. Their guess is that the head lets the loss throw away information that later tasks might need.

That linear probe is the single taut thread from episode 4.3. With a ResNet four times wider than usual, a linear classifier on the frozen necklaces reached seventy-six point five percent top-one accuracy on ImageNet, in that 2020 paper.

Why do the negatives matter? Remember collapse, from episode 7.4. Give every dancer the same necklace and the twin rule is satisfied. Not here. If all necklaces are equal, every cosine is one, and the twin gets only one share in seven. The loss would be the logarithm of seven, one point nine five, far above our zero point three nine. The crowd of negatives makes the cheat expensive.

Fourth, the classic pitfall, in two parts. First, the crowd must be big. The negatives come from the batch, so the batch must be large. The paper uses four thousand ninety-six by default, and goes up to eight thousand one hundred ninety-two, which gives sixteen thousand three hundred eighty-two negatives for each positive pair. It also reports that the gap between batch sizes shrinks with longer training. A big batch is a price, not a law.

Second, false negatives. The batch is drawn without labels. So a dancer who is not the twin can still be the same kind of thing: two different photos of cats. The loss pushes them apart anyway. In our toy, give a second dancer a cosine of zero point nine with the anchor. The twin's share falls from sixty-eight to forty-two percent, and the loss climbs from zero point three nine to zero point eight seven. Chuang and colleagues, in 2020, studied exactly this bias, which comes from drawing negatives without labels.

To sum up. Two masks, one dancer: pull the twins together and push the rest of the batch away, with one softmax. Keep the necklace before the head. Big batches and false negatives are the price. Next time: InfoNCE and mutual information.

PLATE 7.35:34

InfoNCE and mutual information

The SimCLR game of finding the twin on a rack of necklaces is a softmax followed by a cross-entropy, called InfoNCE, which lower-bounds the mutual information between two views. The bound can never exceed the logarithm of N: eight hooks certify three bits at most. A tighter bound does not always give a better representation.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the masked ball. Last time, SimCLR taught the dancers to find their twin in a crowd. Today we look at the score that this game really keeps, and at what it says about information. By the end of this video, you will know why the loss of SimCLR is also a bound on mutual information, and why that bound has a ceiling.

Let's play the game of episode 2.5. An anchor necklace stands at the door of the cloakroom. On the rack hang eight necklaces, and exactly one of them is its twin, the other mask of the same dancer. The player must say which hook holds the twin.

How does the player decide? Compare the anchor with each necklace using the cosine from episode 2.1, then divide by a temperature, as in the last episode. Those are the scores. In our toy, which is illustrative, the twin scores zero point nine two. The seven strangers score between minus zero point eight four and zero point three five. Now the softmax from episode 2.5 turns the eight scores into eight shares that add up to one. At a temperature of zero point five, the twin gets forty-nine percent.

The answer is revealed: the twin was on the first hook. The player is graded on one thing only, the share it gave to the twin. The surprise is minus the log of that share, and BIT keeps the count in bits. Forty-nine percent costs one point oh three bits. In the natural logarithm that libraries use, it is zero point seven one. A softmax, followed by a cross-entropy: that is the whole loss.

This loss has a name, InfoNCE. The name comes from a 2018 paper by van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, about contrastive predictive coding. The N C E stands for noise-contrastive estimation. And the loss of SimCLR is exactly this game, played with the two views of a photo: the other views of the batch fill the rack.

Now, why call it information? Mutual information measures how many bits one thing tells you about another. Here, how much does one view of a dancer tell you about the other view? If you followed the EEG series, episode 11.1 drew this quantity in one picture.

Here is the key moment. The same paper shows that InfoNCE is a lower bound on this mutual information. The information between the two views is at least the logarithm of N, minus the loss. For our rack, N is eight. In base two, the logarithm of eight is exactly three bits. So the bound reads three bits, minus one point oh three, which is one point nine seven bits. In this toy, we can certify about two bits.

Minimizing the loss raises the bound. Suppose the player gets sharper. The twin takes ninety-nine point six percent of the share, and the loss falls to zero point oh oh six bits. The bound climbs to two point nine nine bits.

Now look at what happens next. A loss can never be negative. At best, the player is certain and right, and the loss is zero. Then the bound is three bits, and not one bit more. This is the ceiling: the bound can never exceed the logarithm of N. A rack of eight hooks can only certify three bits, however much the two views really share. Imagine they share five bits. With eight hooks, we would still read three.

Why three? Each yes-or-no question cuts the rack in half. Eight hooks, three halvings, three bits. To certify more, you need a longer rack. A thousand and twenty-four necklaces would raise the ceiling to ten bits. A paper by Poole and colleagues, in 2019, points this out: the InfoNCE bound is loose when the true information is larger than the logarithm of N. That is one more reason why SimCLR wants a large batch.

And collapse, from episode 7.4? Give every dancer the same necklace. All eight scores are equal, the twin gets one share in eight, and the loss is three bits. The bound is three, minus three: zero. A collapsed crowd certifies no information at all.

So far this is a tidy story. Maximize the bound, and you maximize the information. But there is a limit, and honest science says it out loud. Tschannen and colleagues, in 2020, studied representation learning by mutual information. Their finding is that these methods cannot be explained by mutual information alone. In their experiments, maximizing a tighter bound could give worse representations. One reason: mutual information does not change when you scramble a necklace by any invertible recipe, so it cannot tell a useful necklace from a tangled one. What matters also depends on the encoder and on how the score is built. Their paper links InfoNCE to metric learning, the old idea of pulling twins together and pushing strangers apart.

The Information series gives you the tools to read this: mutual information, and variational bounds. Here they are put to work, and also put in their place. The bound is a good guide, and it is not the goal.

To sum up. The game of the masked ball is a softmax followed by a cross-entropy. That is InfoNCE, a lower bound on the mutual information between two views. It can never exceed the logarithm of N, three bits for eight hooks. And a tighter bound does not always mean a better representation. Next time: representation collapse.

PLATE 7.44:18

Representation collapse

Representation collapse is the cheat of giving every dancer the same necklace, which satisfies the matching-masks rule while saying nothing. Variance per bead catches complete collapse, but dimensional collapse hides when beads move together along few patterns. The effective rank, the exponentiated entropy of singular value shares, counts the directions really used; a low rank warns, a high rank proves nothing.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the masked ball. Every dancer here wears a necklace, and the rule of the evening is simple. Two different masks on the same dancer must give the same necklace. Today we meet the cheat that satisfies this rule perfectly, and we learn how to catch it.

Here is the cheat. Give every dancer the exact same necklace. Whatever the mask, whoever is dancing, the necklace never changes. Two views of one dancer? Identical necklaces, so the loss is zero. But the necklaces now say nothing at all. This is called representation collapse.

Why would a network ever do this? Because it is the easiest road to a perfect score on the rule alone. Every method in this chapter has to add something that pushes against the cheat. Contrastive learning pushes the necklaces of different dancers apart. The next two episodes show two other ways.

Watch what it looks like. Sixteen dancers, eight beads each. At the start, the necklaces are all different. Then, step by step, they drift together. The colors fade, the sizes level out, and in the end every bead is the same grey. A collapsed representation is a necklace that has forgotten how to tell people apart.

That one is easy to catch. Take a single bead, say the third one, and read it on all sixteen dancers. If the dancers differ, the readings spread out. If the representation has collapsed, every dancer shows the same value. The spread of a bead is its variance, and here it falls to zero. Check this for each bead, and a complete collapse cannot hide.

But collapse can be more sneaky. Look at this crowd. Every bead still varies from dancer to dancer. The least lively bead has a spread of about zero point two, and the liveliest, about zero point five. The variance check says: all fine.

Look closer. The beads are not moving independently. They move together, following just two patterns, like two dancers shared among eight costumes. Eight beads, but only two directions of freedom. This is called dimensional collapse. The paper by Jing and colleagues, from 2022, shows that even contrastive learning, which uses negatives to avoid complete collapse, can lose dimensions this way.

So we need a measure that counts directions, not beads. Stack the necklaces into a table: one row per dancer, one column per bead. Compute its singular values. Each one says how strongly the crowd spreads along one independent direction. Scale them to add up to one, and you get a set of shares, like the bars of an equalizer.

If all the bars are about equal, the crowd uses all its directions. If one bar towers over the rest, the crowd uses one. To turn the bars into a single number, take their entropy, then exponentiate it. The result is the effective rank, an idea from Roy and Vetterli in 2007. Garrido and colleagues, in 2023, called it RankMe.

Think of it as counting how many bars are really lit. On our toy crowds, the healthy one scores seven point five, out of a maximum of eight. The sneaky one scores two point zero. The fully collapsed one scores exactly one.

Why does this matter? Because it needs no labels. In their experiments, Garrido and colleagues found that this rank tracks how well a representation does on later tasks, and that it helps to choose training settings without any labels. A word of caution, though. Their own figure shows a few degenerate solutions that have full rank and still do badly. A high rank does not prove a good representation. A low rank is a warning.

If you followed the other series, you have met this before. The EEG series, episode 14.3, studies the geometry of covariance matrices. The Quantum series, episode 14.6, uses the von Neumann entropy, the entropy of a normalized spectrum. Same family of ideas.

To sum up. Collapse means the necklaces stop telling dancers apart. Variance per bead catches the complete kind. The effective rank also catches the sneaky kind, where beads move together. Next time: BYOL, a method that avoids collapse without a single negative.

PLATE 7.55:35

No negatives, one asymmetry: BYOL

BYOL avoids collapse with no negatives by making the two sides different: an online encoder with a predictor guesses the necklace written by a slow target twin. A stop-gradient keeps learning on the online side, while the target follows through an exponential moving average, like a heavy weight tied to a hand by an elastic band. Removing the predictor or the stop-gradient brings collapse back.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the masked ball. Last time we met the cheat: give every dancer the same necklace, and the twin rule is satisfied for free. SimCLR stops the cheat with a crowd of strangers, and that crowd has to be big. Today a method called BYOL stops it with no strangers at all. Like the other methods of this chapter, it comes in four parts: the intuition, the architecture in one picture, what it learns, and the classic pitfall.

First, the intuition. With one network and two masks, the easy answer is to say the same thing about everybody. Grill and colleagues, in 2020, changed the game. Instead of asking two identical dancers to agree, they made the two sides different. One side, the online side, has to guess what the other side says. The other side, the target, is a slow copy that only watches. The name stands for bootstrap your own latent: the targets come from the network's own past, and the network learns to predict them.

Second, the architecture in one picture. A photo gets two masks. The first goes through ENKO, the online encoder, and then through PREDI, the predictor, who guesses a necklace. The second mask goes through a twin of ENKO, drawn with a dashed outline. This is the target, and it writes a necklace too. The balance sits in the representation panel. It compares the guess of PREDI with the necklace of the twin. In the paper, a small projection layer follows each encoder, as in SimCLR. We leave it out of the picture.

Both necklaces are rescaled to length one before the comparison. So the loss is two, minus two times the cosine, the cosine of episode 2.1. That is all. No strangers, no batch of negatives.

Now the two special marks on the drawing. The red stroke is the stop-gradient. When the loss says how to change the network, the target receives nothing. Learning flows into the online side only. So how does the target ever change? Through the dotted ribbon. After every step, the target moves a small way toward the online network. New target equals tau times the old target, plus one minus tau times the online network. This is an exponential moving average, or EMA.

Picture a heavy weight tied to your hand by an elastic band. Move your hand, and the weight follows, late and smooth. Here is a toy, illustrative. The online weight jumps from zero to one. With tau at zero point nine nine, ten steps later the target has covered about ten percent of the way. After a hundred steps, sixty-three percent. In the paper, tau starts at zero point nine nine six and rises to one during training, so the weight slows to a halt.

Third, what it learns. Necklaces that ignore the masks, as in SimCLR, but obtained without negatives. At the end, everything is thrown away except the online encoder. A linear probe on its frozen necklaces, the probe from episode 4.3, reaches seventy-four point three percent top-one accuracy on ImageNet with a standard ResNet fifty. In the same table of the paper, SimCLR at that size scores sixty-nine point three.

Two more results from the paper. BYOL stays stable for batch sizes from two hundred fifty-six to four thousand ninety-six, while SimCLR drops quickly as its crowd of negatives shrinks. And when the masks are only random crops, with no color changes, SimCLR loses more than a third of its score. BYOL loses much less.

Fourth, the classic pitfall, and it is a strange one. Nothing in the loss forbids the cheat. A constant necklace still gives a loss of zero. Yet BYOL does not collapse, as long as it keeps its pieces. The paper reports that the representation collapses when the predictor is removed, and when the target network is removed. A few months later, Chen and He studied a simpler version, called SimSiam. They found that the stop-gradient is essential: without it, the outputs fall to a constant.

Why the pieces together prevent collapse was long poorly understood. The BYOL authors offered a hypothesis, not a proof. Tian, Chen and Ganguli, in 2021, built a theory for simple linear networks, in which the predictor, the stop-gradient, the moving average and the weight decay all play a part.

We can watch it in a toy, illustrative. A linear encoder, eight beads, three thousand steps. The number is the average spread of a bead across dancers, the variance check of episode 7.4. With the full recipe, the spread stays near one half. Without the stop-gradient, it falls to zero in under a thousand steps. With the stop-gradient but no predictor, it falls to zero too. Same data, same learning rate. One piece taken away, and the cheat wins.

Keep these four pieces in mind: an online encoder, a predictor, a slow twin, and a stop-gradient. In chapter 11, JEPA meets them again.

To sum up. BYOL makes the two sides different. The online side, with a predictor, guesses the necklace of a slow target twin. The stop-gradient keeps learning on the online side, and the moving average moves the target. It matches contrastive methods without a single negative, and it collapses if the predictor or the stop-gradient is removed. Why it works was long unclear. Next time: no negatives, explicit rules, with VICReg.

PLATE 7.65:56

No negatives, explicit rules: VICReg

VICReg writes the rules against collapse down as three terms on a batch of necklaces: the two masks of a dancer stay close, every bead varies, and no bead repeats another. Each form of collapse trips its own alarm, so no negatives, slow twin or predictor are needed. The price is tuning the three weights, since a weak variance term lets collapse return.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the masked ball. In the last two episodes we met two ways to stop the cheat of the constant necklace. SimCLR fills the floor with a big crowd of strangers. BYOL makes the two sides different, with a predictor, a slow twin and a stop-gradient. Today a method called VICReg does neither. It simply writes the rules down. Like the other methods of this chapter, it comes in four parts: the intuition, the architecture in one picture, what it learns, and the classic pitfall.

First, the intuition. Remember episode 7.4. Collapse comes in two kinds. In the complete kind, every dancer wears the same necklace. In the sneaky kind, the beads move together, so a necklace of eight beads has only two real directions. Bardes, Ponce and LeCun, in 2021, asked: why not forbid both, in so many words? Look at a whole batch of necklaces and write three rules. Rule one: the two masks of one dancer give nearly the same necklace. Rule two: every bead must vary from dancer to dancer. Rule three: no bead may simply repeat another. Each rule gets a number, and the three numbers are added up. The name is the list: variance, invariance, covariance.

Second, the architecture in one picture. A photo gets two masks. Each mask goes through an encoder, ENKO, and then through a small network called the expander. In the paper, the expander has three layers of eight thousand one hundred ninety-two numbers, wider than the necklace itself. The loss is computed on its output, and after training it is thrown away, like the head of SimCLR. The two ENKOs usually share their weights, but the paper says they do not have to.

Now the balances. There are three kinds, and all sit in the representation panel. The first, in the middle, compares the two necklaces of the same dancer. The other two look at one branch alone. One asks whether each bead varies. The other asks whether beads repeat each other. The paper applies these two to each branch separately, so each branch gets its own pair. Notice what is missing. No predictor, no slow twin, no red stroke, and no crowd of negatives.

Now the three rules on a toy batch, illustrative: eight dancers, four beads. Think of the calipers on a pegboard. Rule one, invariance. Measure the distance between the two necklaces of each dancer, square it, and average. No normalization. In our healthy toy, that is zero point one five.

Rule two, variance. Take one bead and read it on all eight dancers. Measure its spread, the standard deviation. The paper sets a target of one. If the spread is at least one, no penalty. If it is smaller, the penalty grows as the spread shrinks. This is a hinge, and a tiny constant keeps the square root stable. The paper insists on the standard deviation, not the variance, because the variance has almost no gradient near collapse. In our healthy toy, every bead spreads between one point oh three and one point two seven, so the penalty is zero.

Rule three, covariance. Take two beads, subtract their averages, multiply them dancer by dancer, and average the products. That is the dot product of episode 2.1, between two columns of the batch. If the two beads dance independently, the result is near zero. If one copies the other, it equals their variance. The rule sums the squares of all these off-diagonal entries, divided by the number of beads. Healthy toy: zero point one four.

Now watch the alarms, counting both branches. Collapse every dancer into one necklace: invariance says zero, perfect. Variance says one point nine eight, loud. Covariance says zero. Now collapse sneakily, with the second bead copying the first. Invariance is still fine, zero point one eight. Variance is silent, zero. Covariance rings: one point eight two. Each collapse sets off its own alarm, and only one.

Third, what it learns. A representation that stays spread over all its beads, with no moving average and no negatives. In the paper, a linear probe on a ResNet fifty reaches seventy-three point two percent top-one accuracy on ImageNet, as an average of three runs. In the same table, SimCLR scores sixty-nine point three, and BYOL seventy-four point three. And the ablation of the paper is blunt: remove the variance term and the representation collapses.

Fourth, the classic pitfall: the weights. The loss is lambda times invariance, plus mu times variance, plus nu times covariance. The paper uses twenty-five, twenty-five and one, found by a grid search. With all three at one, its ablation reports collapse. Here is our own toy, illustrative: a linear encoder, eight beads, invariance weight twenty-five. Variance weight twenty-five: spread zero point six nine, and the effective rank from episode 7.4 is seven point nine out of eight. Weight ten: seven point five. Weight five: five point eight. Weight one: three point eight, with the spread near zero. Weight zero: complete collapse. Drop the covariance term instead, and the spread looks fine but the rank falls to three point six. Both weights matter. One caution: in our toy, weights of one still hold, unlike the paper's run. A toy is not the paper.

To sum up. VICReg writes three rules on the necklaces of a batch: twins stay close, every bead varies, no bead repeats another. Each collapse trips its own alarm. It needs no negatives, no slow twin and no predictor. The price is tuning the weights, and a weak variance term lets the dimensional collapse come back.
