What does “complex” mean?

A sine wave is perfectly regular, white noise perfectly random, and a resting EEG sits between them with structure at many scales, like ripples and dunes on a beach. Many complexity measures actually quantify irregularity or compressibility and peak for noise, and they disagree with each other. All are shaped by spectrum, amplitude, filtering and artifacts, so the spectrum should be reported and surrogates used.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter twelve: complexity and criticality. First question: what does "complex" actually mean?

Look at three signals. The first is a pure sine wave. Perfectly regular: once you have seen one cycle, you can predict the rest forever. The second is white noise. Perfectly random: each value tells you nothing about the next one. The third looks like a resting EEG. It has rhythms that come and go, bursts, slow drifts, and fast details. It sits somewhere in between.

Which one is the most complex? Most people would say the EEG. But many measures would disagree. A measure of unpredictability gives its highest score to white noise. White noise is maximally disordered, but there is nothing interesting going on in it.

So complexity is not the same as disorder. Intuitively, a complex signal has structure at many scales: neither frozen nor random, it is organized, but never repeats exactly.

Picture a beach. A perfectly flat, raked sand surface is regular. A cloud of blowing sand is random. Ripples, dunes and footprints, each with smaller patterns inside, are complex.

The measures in this chapter capture different pieces of this intuition.

Some measure irregularity: how hard it is to predict the next value from the previous ones. Sample entropy and permutation entropy belong here. They are highest for noise.

Some measure compressibility: how short a description of the signal can be. Lempel-Ziv complexity belongs here. It is also highest for noise.

Some try to look across scales: multiscale entropy, or fractal dimension, which asks how the signal's roughness changes with the scale you look at.

And some ask a different question altogether: is the brain operating close to a critical point, a tipping point between order and disorder? That is the second half of the chapter: avalanches, long-range temporal correlations, and the critical brain hypothesis.

A key warning, before we start. These measures do not agree with one another about what they call complex. A change in one does not imply a change in the others. And many of them are strongly influenced by simple properties of the signal: its power spectrum, its amplitude, its filtering, the length of the recording, and artifacts.

For example, if one condition has more alpha power, the signal becomes more regular, and entropy measures drop. That is a real change, but it is a change in the spectrum, not necessarily a new kind of dynamics.

So, in this chapter, each episode follows the same pattern: what the measure does, what it is sensitive to, and how to check that it is telling you something beyond the spectrum. A good habit is to always report the power spectrum alongside any complexity measure, and to test against surrogates that keep the spectrum but scramble everything else.

In Python, the antropy package, from Raphael Vallat, implements many of these measures, including sample entropy, permutation entropy, Lempel-Ziv complexity, Higuchi's fractal dimension, and detrended fluctuation analysis.

To recap. Regular signals are predictable, random signals are not, and complex signals lie in between, with structure at many scales. Many measures in this chapter actually measure irregularity or compressibility, which peak for noise. They do not agree with one another, and they are sensitive to the spectrum, amplitude, filtering, length and artifacts. Always report the spectrum, and use surrogates.

Sample entropy and approximate entropy

Sample entropy asks whether two stretches that match for m samples still match at m plus one: if most matches survive, the signal is regular and entropy is low. It improves on approximate entropy by excluding self-matches. Results depend on m, the tolerance, recording length, sampling rate and spectrum, so conditions may differ only in power spectrum, which spectrum-preserving surrogates test.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Sample entropy and approximate entropy. How often does a pattern repeat?

Here is the question these measures ask. If two short stretches of the signal look alike, will they still look alike one step later? If yes, the signal is regular, and the entropy is low. If no, the signal is irregular, and the entropy is high.

Let's make it concrete. Choose a pattern length, called m, for example two samples. Choose a tolerance, called r, for example zero point two times the standard deviation of the signal. Two patterns "match" if every pair of corresponding values differs by less than r.

Now take every pattern of length m in the signal, and count how many other patterns match it. Then extend all patterns by one sample, to length m plus one, and count the matches again. Some matches survive the extension. Others break.

Sample entropy is the negative logarithm of the ratio between the two counts: matches of length m plus one, divided by matches of length m. If almost all matches survive, the ratio is close to one, and the entropy is close to zero. If most matches break, the ratio is small, and the entropy is high.

Picture footprints on a beach. If two short trails of footprints look alike, does the next step also land in the same place? For someone walking in a straight line at a steady pace, yes. For someone wandering, no.

Approximate entropy, proposed by Steven Pincus in 1991, came first. It has a known weakness: it counts each pattern as matching itself. That self-match pushes the result toward regularity, and makes it depend strongly on the length of the recording. Joshua Richman and Randall Moorman proposed sample entropy in 2000 to fix this, by excluding self-matches. In most cases, sample entropy is preferred.

Now, the parameters. The values m equals two and r equals zero point two standard deviations are common defaults, but they are conventions, not laws. Changing m or r can change the result, and even reverse a difference between conditions. So choose them before analysing, and report them.

The length of the signal matters too. With short recordings, there are few matches, and the estimate becomes unstable or undefined. A few thousand samples is a safer range than a few hundred.

And then the spectrum. Sample entropy depends strongly on the frequency content of the signal. A signal dominated by slow, smooth waves is more predictable sample by sample, so its sample entropy is lower. Filtering changes it. A higher sampling rate, with the same content, makes consecutive samples more similar, and lowers the entropy too. Even the tolerance r depends on the signal's standard deviation, so changes in amplitude or in the strength of one rhythm can shift the result.

That means two conditions can differ in sample entropy simply because their power spectra differ. To check, compare with surrogates that keep the spectrum, such as phase-randomized surrogates. If the surrogates show the same difference, the effect is spectral.

Artifacts matter as well. A single large artifact inflates the standard deviation, which widens the tolerance, and makes everything else look more regular.

In Python, antropy offers sample entropy and approximate entropy.

To recap. Sample entropy asks how often patterns that match for m samples still match for m plus one. Low values mean regular, high values mean irregular. It improves on approximate entropy by excluding self-matches. The parameters m and r, the signal length, the sampling rate, the spectrum and artifacts all influence it. Fix the parameters in advance, report them, and test against spectrum-preserving surrogates.

Permutation entropy

Permutation entropy keeps only the order of values. Each window of three samples becomes one of six ordinal patterns, and the Shannon entropy of the pattern counts is computed; white noise reaches the maximum of about 2.6 bits. It is fast and unchanged by gain, offset or monotonic distortion, but it ignores amplitude and depends on order, delay and spectrum.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Permutation entropy. Forget the values, keep the order.

In 2002, Christoph Bandt and Bernd Pompe proposed a simple idea. Instead of comparing exact values, look only at their order.

Take a small window of three consecutive samples. They can be ordered in six different ways. Rising, rising: an upward ramp. Falling, falling: a downward ramp. Up then down: a peak. Down then up: a valley. And two more asymmetric shapes. Each window is turned into one of these six ordinal patterns.

Slide the window along the signal, and count how often each pattern appears. Then compute the Shannon entropy of this distribution of patterns.

If one pattern dominates, the entropy is low: the signal is regular. If all six patterns are equally common, the entropy is at its maximum, the logarithm of six, about two point six bits. That is what you get from white noise. Usually the result is normalized between zero and one.

Picture a walk along the beach, noting only whether each step goes up or down the dunes. You ignore the exact height. Only the shape of the path counts.

The size of the window is called the order. Order three gives six patterns, order four gives twenty-four, order five gives one hundred and twenty. Larger orders capture longer shapes, but need many more samples to estimate all patterns reliably. A common rule is to have many more samples than possible patterns. There is also a delay parameter: you can take every sample, or every second, or every tenth, which changes the time scale you look at.

Permutation entropy has real strengths. It is very fast. Because it only uses order, it is unaffected by any transformation that keeps the order: a change of gain, an offset, or a monotonic distortion leave it unchanged. And it is fairly robust to small amounts of noise and to slow drifts.

But it also has weaknesses. It is blind to amplitude: a tiny wiggle and a huge wave with the same shape give the same pattern. It ignores how far apart values are. Ties, when two values are equal, have to be handled somehow, which matters for low-resolution or clipped signals. And like sample entropy, it depends on the spectrum: slow, smooth signals produce mostly ramps, and a lower entropy. Changing the sampling rate or the delay changes which frequencies the patterns reflect.

So it is an excellent first tool: fast, simple and robust. But it is not a pure measure of nonlinear dynamics. Spectrum-preserving surrogates are again the way to check whether a difference goes beyond the spectrum.

Permutation entropy has been used, for example, to track the depth of anaesthesia, and to compare states of consciousness. It is one of the most popular complexity measures in EEG.

In Python, antropy offers it, with the order and the delay as parameters.

To recap. Permutation entropy turns each short window into an ordinal pattern, and computes the entropy of the patterns. With order three, there are six patterns, and the maximum is about two point six bits. It is fast, insensitive to gain and offset, and robust to noise. But it ignores amplitude, depends on the order, the delay and the spectrum, and needs enough samples for the number of patterns.

Next time: multiscale entropy.

PLATE 12.44:05

Multiscale entropy

Multiscale entropy computes sample entropy on progressively coarse-grained signals, averaging consecutive samples, giving an entropy profile across scales: white noise falls quickly, while one-over-f signals stay high. Averaging is a crude filter and a fixed tolerance mixes in variance changes, so scales and frequencies can be mismatched. Report the spectrum, adapt the tolerance, and use surrogates.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Multiscale entropy. Is the signal irregular at every scale?

A single entropy value describes the signal at one time scale: the scale of its samples. But brain signals contain fast and slow dynamics. Madalena Costa, Ary Goldberger and Chung-Kang Peng proposed in 2002 to look at several scales at once.

The method is called coarse-graining. At scale one, take the original signal. At scale two, average every two consecutive samples, without overlap, to make a new, shorter signal. At scale three, average every three samples. And so on. Then compute sample entropy at each scale.

The result is a curve: entropy as a function of scale. White noise is irregular at fine scales, but averaging smooths it out quickly, so its entropy falls with scale. Signals with structure at many scales, like one-over-f noise, keep a high entropy across scales. This was the original motivation: a complex signal should stay irregular across scales, while noise should not.

Picture sand on the beach. Up close, each grain is different. Step back, and white noise would blur into a flat grey surface, while a beach with ripples, dunes and bays keeps revealing new shapes at every distance.

The profile across scales carries more information than any single number. In the EEG literature, for example, studies have reported that older adults show higher entropy at fine scales and lower entropy at coarse scales than younger adults.

But what does "scale" really mean here? This is where we need to be careful.

Averaging consecutive samples is a crude low-pass filter. Coarse scales therefore contain only slower frequencies. But the filter is not sharp, so some fast activity leaks through.

There is a second subtlety. In the standard version, the tolerance r is set once, from the standard deviation of the original signal, and kept fixed at all scales. As averaging removes fast activity, the variance of the coarse signals drops, and the fixed tolerance becomes relatively larger. Part of the change in entropy across scales is simply this change in variance.

Julian Kosciessa, Niels Kloosterman and Douglas Garrett examined these issues in 2020. They showed that standard multiscale entropy reflects neural dynamics at mismatched time scales. Entropy at coarse scales, thought to reflect slow dynamics, can be biased by fast, high-frequency activity. And entropy at fine scales, thought to reflect fast dynamics, is highly sensitive to broadband spectral power, which is dominated by slow frequencies.

So an age difference in fine-scale entropy might really be a difference in the power spectrum, at a very different frequency than one would think.

What can you do? Report the power spectrum, and check whether spectral differences explain the entropy effect. Consider recomputing the tolerance at each scale. Consider replacing simple averaging with proper filters, so that each scale corresponds to a known frequency band. And use spectrum-preserving surrogates.

Recordings also need to be long, because coarse-graining shortens the signal: at scale twenty, a signal of twenty thousand samples becomes one thousand samples long.

In Python, multiscale entropy can be built from antropy's sample entropy, and the neurokit2 package offers ready-made versions.

To recap. Multiscale entropy computes sample entropy on progressively coarse-grained signals, and gives a profile across scales. The profile can be informative. But coarse-graining is a crude filter, a fixed tolerance mixes in variance changes, and Kosciessa and colleagues showed that scales and frequencies can be mismatched. Report the spectrum, adapt the tolerance, use filters, and check with surrogates.

Next time: Lempel-Ziv complexity.

PLATE 12.54:21

Lempel–Ziv complexity

Lempel-Ziv complexity binarizes the signal around its median, cuts the sequence into the shortest words not seen before, and counts them: repetitive signals need few words, random ones many. It is lower in deep sleep and anaesthesia, and inspired the perturbational complexity index, which compresses the EEG response to magnetic stimulation. Threshold, spectrum, length and muscle artifacts all affect it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Lempel-Ziv complexity. How short can the description be?

Some signals can be described in a few words. "Zero one, repeated five hundred times." Others need almost as many words as there are samples. Lempel-Ziv complexity measures where a signal falls between these extremes.

The algorithm was introduced by Abraham Lempel and Jacob Ziv in 1976, and its descendants still power everyday file compression.

Here is how it works on EEG. First, turn the signal into a sequence of zeros and ones. The usual rule: one if the value is above the median of the signal, zero if it is below. Then read the sequence from left to right, and cut it into words. Each new word is the shortest piece that has not appeared before. Count how many words you need. A repetitive sequence reuses old pieces, and needs few new words. A random sequence keeps producing new words.

The count is usually normalized, so that a random sequence of the same length gives a value close to one.

Picture writing in the sand. A pattern of repeated waves can be summarized with a short rule. A random scatter of footprints has to be described step by step.

Lempel-Ziv complexity has become important in research on consciousness. It tends to be lower in deep sleep and under general anaesthesia than in wakefulness. For example, Michael Schartner and colleagues reported in 2015 that it decreases under propofol anaesthesia.

These findings connect with a related idea, the perturbational complexity index, or P-C-I, introduced by Adenauer Casali and colleagues in 2013. Instead of recording spontaneous activity, they stimulate the cortex with transcranial magnetic stimulation, and record the EEG response. They estimate the sources of the response, binarize them, and compress the resulting pattern in space and time with a Lempel-Ziv algorithm.

The logic: in a conscious brain, the response spreads widely but differently across regions, which is hard to compress. In deep sleep or anaesthesia, the response either stays local or becomes a stereotyped wave, which compresses well. A follow-up study by Silvia Casarotto and colleagues in 2016 proposed an empirical cutoff of zero point three one, above which subjects were conscious in their benchmark population.

Now, the cautions.

First, the binarization threshold is part of the measure. Median, mean, or a threshold on the envelope give different results. Report it.

Second, like the entropies, Lempel-Ziv complexity is strongly influenced by the spectrum. Slow waves produce long runs of zeros and ones, which compress well. So anything that increases slow-wave power, like deep sleep, lowers the complexity, partly for spectral reasons. Spectrum-preserving surrogates help separate the two.

Third, length and sampling rate matter. Normalization reduces, but does not remove, the dependence on the number of samples. And oversampling the same content creates longer runs.

Fourth, noise and artifacts. Broadband muscle activity looks very complex. A difference in muscle tension between conditions can look like a difference in brain complexity.

And finally, the spontaneous Lempel-Ziv complexity and the perturbational complexity index are different measures. The second requires stimulation, source estimation and a specific protocol. Do not transfer conclusions from one to the other without care.

In Python, antropy provides Lempel-Ziv complexity for binary sequences, with normalization.

To recap. Lempel-Ziv complexity binarizes the signal and counts the new words needed to describe it. It is low for repetitive signals and close to one for random ones. It drops in deep sleep and anaesthesia, and inspired the perturbational complexity index, which compresses responses to magnetic stimulation. The threshold, the spectrum, the length, the sampling rate and muscle artifacts all influence it.

Next time: fractal dimension.

Fractal dimension · Higuchi

Higuchi's fractal dimension measures a curve's length with rulers of increasing step size and takes the slope of the log-log relation, from one for a smooth signal to two for noise, like measuring a coastline. It is fast and works on short segments. It is closely tied to the spectral slope, depends on the maximum step, and is raised by noise and artifacts.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Fractal dimension, with Higuchi's method. How rough is the curve?

A straight line has dimension one. A filled surface has dimension two. A very wiggly curve that almost fills the page sits somewhere in between. Its fractal dimension tells you how its apparent length grows when you measure it with finer and finer rulers.

Picture measuring the outline of a beach. With a ruler one kilometre long, you skip all the small bays. With a ruler one metre long, you follow every inlet, and the coastline looks much longer. With a ruler one centimetre long, longer still. The way the length grows as the ruler shrinks is the fractal dimension.

Tomoyuki Higuchi proposed in 1988 a way to apply this idea directly to a time series.

Here is the method. Choose a step size, k. Starting from the first sample, jump forward k samples at a time, and add up the absolute differences between successive points. This is the length of the curve seen with a ruler of size k. Do it from several starting points, normalize, and average. Repeat for k equal one, two, three, up to a maximum value, called k max.

Then plot the logarithm of the length against the logarithm of one over k. If the points lie on a straight line, its slope is the fractal dimension. For a time series, the value lies between one and two. A smooth signal gives a value close to one. White noise gives a value close to two.

Higuchi's method is fast, needs no embedding, and works on fairly short segments. It has been used in EEG to compare sleep stages, levels of anaesthesia, ages, and clinical groups.

Now, what is it sensitive to?

Mostly, to the spectrum. For signals that scale like one-over-f noise, the fractal dimension is directly linked to the slope of the power spectrum: a steeper spectrum means a smoother signal and a lower dimension. So a change in fractal dimension can reflect a change in the aperiodic exponent from chapter four, or in the strength of a rhythm.

The choice of k max matters. A small k max focuses on fine details. A large one includes slower trends. And the log-log plot may not be a straight line at all; then a single slope is a poor summary. Always look at the plot.

Noise and artifacts push the value up. Line noise, muscle activity, or a noisy electrode can make a signal look rougher. Filtering pushes it down. The sampling rate changes what one step represents.

In other words, the same value can come from very different causes: noise, a one-over-f slope, a strong rhythm, or artifacts. As for every measure in this chapter, you need to control what makes it vary: report the spectrum, check the log-log plot, and compare with surrogates.

There are other fractal dimension methods, like Katz or Petrosian. They give different values on the same signal, so results cannot be compared across methods.

In Python, antropy provides Higuchi's fractal dimension, with k max as a parameter, along with the Katz and Petrosian versions.

To recap. Higuchi's method measures the length of the curve with rulers of different sizes, and takes the slope of the log-log relation. Values go from one, smooth, to two, noise-like. It is fast and works on short segments. But it is closely tied to the spectral slope, depends on k max, and is raised by noise and artifacts. Look at the plot, report the spectrum, and use surrogates.

PLATE 12.74:17

Criticality I · The sandpile

In the sandpile model, cells topple onto neighbours and the pile organizes itself into a state with avalanches of all sizes whose frequency follows a power law: self-organized criticality. The critical brain hypothesis proposes that networks sit near a similar tipping point between dying out and exploding. Power laws can arise from non-critical processes, and the awake brain may sit slightly below criticality.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Criticality, part one: the sandpile.

Take a flat table, and drop grains of sand on it, one at a time, at random places. At first, nothing much happens: the grains pile up. But as the pile grows steeper, some grains start to slide. Sometimes, one grain makes a few neighbours slide. Sometimes, it triggers an avalanche that runs all the way down one side of the pile.

In 1987, Per Bak, Chao Tang and Kurt Wiesenfeld studied a simple model of this. In their model, each cell of a grid holds some grains. When a cell holds four grains or more, it topples: it gives one grain to each of its four neighbours. Those neighbours may then topple in turn, and so on.

Here is what they found. The pile organizes itself, without any tuning, into a special state. In that state, avalanches of all sizes happen. Small ones are very common. Large ones are rare. And the relation between size and frequency follows a power law: on a log-log plot, it is a straight line. There is no typical avalanche size.

They called this self-organized criticality.

Why "critical"? In physics, a critical point is a tipping point between two phases, like water at the exact temperature and pressure where liquid and gas become indistinguishable. At that point, fluctuations happen at all scales, and power laws appear.

Picture the beach. A dry dune, where a single footstep can trigger a slide of any size.

Now, the brain. The critical brain hypothesis proposes that neural networks operate close to such a tipping point, between two regimes. On one side, activity dies out quickly: the network is subcritical, too quiet. On the other side, activity explodes and spreads everywhere, like a seizure: the network is supercritical. At the boundary, activity can propagate over all scales without dying out or exploding.

Why would that be useful? Models suggest that networks near criticality have advantages: a wide dynamic range, meaning they can respond to both weak and strong inputs; good information transmission; and a large repertoire of possible patterns. Osame Kinouchi and Mauro Copelli showed in 2006, for example, that the dynamic range of a model network is maximal at the critical point.

The idea has been very influential. In 2003, John Beggs and Dietmar Plenz recorded cortical slices and found cascades of activity, which they called neuronal avalanches, whose sizes followed a power law. That is the next episode.

But there is a real debate, and it is worth presenting fairly.

First, power laws can arise from many mechanisms that have nothing to do with criticality. Alain Destexhe and Jonathan Touboul, among others, showed that some noise processes and thresholding procedures can produce apparent power laws.

Second, recent work by Viola Priesemann and colleagues suggests that the awake brain may operate slightly below criticality, in a "reverberating" regime: close to the tipping point, but on the safe side.

Third, "the brain is critical" is not a single testable statement. Different measures, recorded at different scales, can point in different directions.

So, criticality is an attractive framework for thinking about brain dynamics, with real evidence and real open questions. The next two episodes show how it is measured in practice: avalanches, branching, and long-range temporal correlations.

To recap. In the sandpile model, grains topple onto neighbours, and the pile organizes itself into a state with avalanches of all sizes, following a power law. The critical brain hypothesis proposes that neural networks operate near a similar tipping point, between dying out and exploding, which could maximize dynamic range and information transmission. It is an influential idea, with active debate about the evidence and about how close to criticality the brain really is.

Criticality II · Neuronal avalanches and branching

Neuronal avalanches are cascades of threshold crossings bounded by empty time bins, with sizes following a power law of exponent near minus three halves. The branching ratio, events triggered per event, equals one at the critical point. A straight log-log line is not proof, thresholds and bins shift results, subsampling biases the ratio, and volume conduction creates false avalanches in EEG.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Criticality, part two: neuronal avalanches and branching.

In 2003, John Beggs and Dietmar Plenz recorded the activity of cortical slices with a grid of sixty electrodes. They noticed that activity did not appear all at once, nor completely at random. It came in cascades: a few electrodes became active, then a few more, then the cascade stopped. They called these cascades neuronal avalanches.

Here is how avalanches are detected. First, choose a threshold, and mark every moment when a signal crosses it as an event. Then divide time into small bins. An avalanche is a run of consecutive bins that contain at least one event, bounded by empty bins before and after. Its size is the total number of events. Its duration is the number of bins.

Beggs and Plenz found that avalanche sizes followed a power law, with an exponent close to minus three halves, the value predicted for a critical branching process. Durations followed a power law too.

The same idea was later applied to M-E-G and E-E-G. For example, Oren Shriki and colleagues reported avalanche-like cascades in resting-state M-E-G in 2013.

A second measure is the branching ratio. On average, how many events in the next time bin does one event lead to? If the ratio is below one, activity tends to die out: subcritical. Above one, it tends to grow: supercritical. Exactly one is the critical point.

Picture a cascade of sand grains on a dune. If each falling grain knocks loose, on average, less than one other grain, slides stop quickly. If it knocks loose more than one, the slide grows. Right at one, slides of every size occur.

Now, the traps. They are important.

First: a straight line on a log-log plot does not prove a power law. Aaron Clauset, Cosma Shalizi and Mark Newman showed in 2009 that many claimed power laws in science do not survive proper testing. The right approach is to fit the power law by maximum likelihood, estimate where the power law starts, and compare it statistically with alternatives, like a log-normal or an exponential with a cutoff.

Second: the threshold and the bin size change the results. A lower threshold creates more events and larger avalanches. A wider time bin merges separate cascades into bigger ones. The estimated exponent can shift with both. So test a range of thresholds and bin sizes, and report how stable the results are.

Third: subsampling. Electrodes see only a tiny fraction of the neurons in a network. Viola Priesemann and colleagues showed that subsampling distorts avalanche distributions, and biases the naive branching ratio downwards. Jens Wilting and Viola Priesemann proposed in 2018 a multistep regression estimator that corrects for this bias.

Fourth, specific to EEG: volume conduction spreads one source across many electrodes at once. A single event can then look like a large avalanche. And slow waves or artifacts that cross the threshold everywhere create huge "avalanches" with no cascading at all.

Fifth: power laws can come from non-critical processes too, as we saw last episode. A power law is consistent with criticality, but does not prove it.

So, a careful avalanche analysis includes: a robust event detection, a range of thresholds and bin sizes, a proper statistical fit of the distributions, a subsampling-corrected branching estimate, and surrogate data.

In Python, the powerlaw package, from Jeff Alstott and colleagues, implements the Clauset method. The mrestimator package implements the multistep regression estimator of the branching ratio.

To recap. Neuronal avalanches are cascades of threshold crossings, separated by silent bins. Beggs and Plenz found power-law sizes, with an exponent close to minus three halves. The branching ratio asks how many events one event triggers: one is critical. But log-log lines are not proof, thresholds and bins matter, subsampling biases estimates, and volume conduction distorts EEG avalanches. Fit properly, vary parameters, correct for subsampling, and use surrogates.

PLATE 12.94:55

Criticality III · Long-range temporal correlations, DFA and fE/I

Long-range temporal correlations mean the amplitude envelope of an alpha rhythm remembers its past over seconds to minutes, like slow persistent tides under fast waves. Detrended fluctuation analysis measures them: an exponent of 0.5 means no memory, between 0.5 and 1 means long-range correlation. They peak near excitation-inhibition balance, which inspired the functional E-I ratio, and need long clean recordings.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Criticality, part three: long-range temporal correlations, D-F-A, and the functional excitation-inhibition ratio.

Avalanches look at fast cascades. Another signature of criticality is slower: memory over long time scales.

Take the amplitude envelope of the alpha rhythm, as in episode ten point eight, over several minutes of rest. It rises and falls. Is the envelope now related to the envelope a few seconds ago? A minute ago? If correlations decay slowly, following a power law, the signal has long-range temporal correlations.

In 2001, Klaus Linkenkaer-Hansen and colleagues showed that the envelopes of alpha and beta oscillations in human E-E-G and M-E-G have such long-range temporal correlations, extending over hundreds of seconds.

The tool they used is detrended fluctuation analysis, or D-F-A. Here is how it works. First, subtract the mean of the envelope and compute its cumulative sum, called the signal profile. Then cut the profile into windows of a given size. In each window, remove the linear trend, and measure the remaining fluctuation. Average over windows. Repeat for many window sizes, from a few seconds to a few minutes.

Then plot the fluctuation against the window size, on log-log axes. The slope is the D-F-A exponent.

An exponent of zero point five means no memory: like white noise, each moment is independent. An exponent between zero point five and one means long-range temporal correlations: fluctuations persist over long times. An exponent close to one or above means very strong, slow drifts.

Picture the tide on a beach. Waves come and go every few seconds, but the water level also follows slow, persistent trends over hours. D-F-A asks how much of that slow persistence there is.

Why link this to criticality? In models, long-range temporal correlations are strongest when excitation and inhibition are balanced, close to the critical point. Too much inhibition, or too much excitation, shortens the memory.

This led to the functional excitation-inhibition ratio, or f-E-I, introduced by Hilgo Bruining, Richard Hardstone and colleagues in 2020. Their model showed that oscillation amplitude and long-range temporal correlations covary differently depending on the balance. In an inhibition-dominated network, windows with higher amplitude also have stronger correlations. In an excitation-dominated network, the relation reverses. The f-E-I measure estimates this covariation across windows of the recording. Values below one suggest inhibition dominance; above one, excitation dominance.

They validated it in healthy adults, where a drug that enhances GABA-A inhibition lowered f-E-I, and applied it to children with autism, where they found large individual differences.

Now, the cautions.

D-F-A needs long, clean recordings: several minutes at least, without gaps. Artifacts and discontinuities distort the fluctuation curve.

Filtering matters. The band-pass filter used to extract the envelope introduces short-range correlations of its own. The fitting range must start beyond the filter's time scale, or the exponent will be biased. Richard Hardstone and colleagues wrote a practical guide in 2012 on these choices.

The log-log plot should be a straight line over the fitting range. If it bends, a single exponent is a poor summary.

And for f-E-I: it only applies when long-range temporal correlations are present, typically with a D-F-A exponent above about zero point six, and it depends on analysis parameters. A 2023 study in Alzheimer's disease found lower values in healthy controls than earlier reports, and urged caution in interpreting absolute values.

In Python, antropy provides a basic D-F-A. For envelopes, combine it with M-N-E filtering and the Hilbert transform, and choose the fitting range carefully.

To recap. Long-range temporal correlations mean that the envelope of an oscillation remembers its past over long times. D-F-A measures them: an exponent of zero point five is no memory, between zero point five and one is long-range correlation. In models, they peak near excitation-inhibition balance, which inspired the functional E-I ratio. Long recordings, careful filtering and fitting ranges, and caution with absolute values are essential.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Microstates. The scalp map stays still, then jumps.

Look at the topography of the resting E-E-G, moment by moment. You might expect it to change smoothly and continuously. It does not. The map stays quasi-stable for a short time, then switches quickly to another configuration, which stays stable in turn.

Dietrich Lehmann and colleagues described these periods of stability in 1987. They are called E-E-G microstates. Each lasts typically sixty to one hundred and twenty milliseconds.

Picture the beach from above, with the tide pools reflecting the sky. The pattern of light stays the same for a moment, then a cloud passes, and the whole pattern changes at once.

Here is how microstates are computed. First, compute the global field power: the spatial standard deviation of the potentials across all electrodes at each moment. Its peaks are the moments when the topography is strongest and cleanest. Collect the maps at those peaks.

Then cluster them. The most common method is a modified version of k-means, which groups maps by their shape, and ignores their polarity: a map and its inverse count as the same state, because they arise from the same sources with opposite sign. You choose the number of clusters, k.

Many resting-state studies found four classes, which explain most of the topographic variance, often reported around seventy to eighty percent. They are usually labelled A to D. Class A runs from right frontal to left posterior, class B from left frontal to right posterior, class C from front to back along the midline, and class D also along the midline, with a more frontal maximum.

Then each moment of the recording is assigned to the class it best matches. The signal becomes a sequence of states, like A, A, A, C, C, D, B, B.

From this sequence, several parameters are computed. The mean duration of each state. Its occurrence: how many times per second it appears. Its coverage: the percentage of time it occupies. And the transitions: how often each state is followed by each other state. These parameters have been compared across ages, tasks, and clinical groups, including schizophrenia, where changes in classes C and D have been widely reported.

Now, the cautions.

First and most important: the number of classes is an analysis choice, not a discovery. Criteria exist to help choose k, such as cross-validation, or the explained variance. But four is a convention, and recent studies often report more classes. The labels A to D only make sense if the maps resemble the canonical templates.

Second: the assignment step involves choices too. Temporal smoothing, or rejecting very short segments, changes the durations.

Third: preprocessing matters. Microstates are usually computed on data band-pass filtered between about two and twenty hertz, and on an average reference. A different filter or reference changes the maps and the durations.

Fourth: interpretation. Each microstate class has been linked to large-scale networks, for example through simultaneous E-E-G and f-M-R-I studies. But these links are correlational, and a microstate is not the same thing as a network.

Christoph Michel and Thomas Koenig wrote a widely cited review of the method in 2018.

In Python, the pycrostates package, from Victor Férat and colleagues, implements microstate segmentation and its parameters, and integrates with M-N-E.

To recap. Microstates are short periods, typically sixty to one hundred and twenty milliseconds, when the scalp map stays quasi-stable. Maps at global field power peaks are clustered, often into four classes, A to D, ignoring polarity. Durations, occurrence, coverage and transitions describe the dynamics. The number of classes, the smoothing, the filter and the reference are choices that must be reported, and links to brain networks remain correlational.
