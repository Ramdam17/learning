A map of the EEG spectrum

The EEG spectrum is a falling background with a few peaks, and delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma are conventional borders on that landscape, like countries on maps drawn by different cartographers. Edges vary between papers and the alpha peak shifts across people and ages, so edges and individual alpha frequency matter. A band is not a rhythm, and band power mixes peaks with background.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter four: the rhythms of the brain. Before we meet them one by one, we need a map. Where does each rhythm live on the frequency axis, how did those territories get their names, and how firm are their borders?

Think of a map of countries drawn by different cartographers. Everyone agrees there is a mountain range and a river. But each map draws the borders a little differently, and the borders move over the centuries. EEG frequency bands are exactly like that: real landscapes, conventional borders.

Here is a resting EEG spectrum, from half a hertz to around a hundred, on a logarithmic frequency axis. Two things jump out. A background that falls steadily as frequency rises, and, rising above it, a few bumps. The biggest one, for an adult resting with closed eyes, sits around ten hertz.

Now the classic names, mostly given in the twentieth century in order of discovery rather than frequency. Delta, the slowest, below about four hertz. Theta, roughly four to eight. Alpha, roughly eight to twelve or thirteen. Beta, from there to about thirty. And gamma, above thirty, sometimes split again into high gamma above seventy or eighty.

Notice how often I said roughly. Let's lay the band edges used by several papers on top of each other. Alpha starts at seven and a half in one, eight in another, eight point five in a third. Beta ends at twenty five, thirty or thirty five. Theta may begin at three or at four. The borders never coincide exactly.

So the first lesson of this chapter: bands are conventions. They are a useful way to summarize a spectrum, not natural kinds. Whenever you report band power, report the exact edges you used.

The second lesson: the landscape itself moves. Take the alpha peak. Its frequency differs from person to person, and changes across life. In a 2022 review of studies from birth to eighteen years, Freschl and colleagues found the occipital peak rising from about six hertz at six months, to about eight point four at five years, and close to ten in adolescence. In adulthood it slowly declines again with age.

That peak is called the individual alpha frequency, or I A F. If one person's alpha peaks at nine hertz and another's at eleven, a fixed band from eight to twelve cuts their rhythms in very different places. A shift in peak frequency can then masquerade as a change in power.

One remedy is to define bands relative to each person's peak, for example a few hertz below and above it, instead of using fixed edges. Another is to model the peaks directly, which we will do with specparam in episode four point eleven.

The third lesson: a band is not a rhythm. The same eight to thirteen hertz window contains the posterior alpha rhythm, the motor mu rhythm, and sometimes other things. Topography, reactivity and waveform shape tell them apart, as episode four point three will show.

And the fourth: under every bump, there is that falling background. Band power mixes the two. A change in the background can look like a change in a rhythm. Episodes four point ten and four point eleven are entirely about separating them.

In practice, MNE computes the spectrum for you, with Welch's method or multitapers from chapter three. Plot it on a log scale first, find the peaks with your eyes, and only then decide on bands.

Let's recap. The EEG spectrum shows a falling background with a few peaks on top. Delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma are conventional bands whose edges vary between studies, so always report them. Peak frequencies differ between people and change with age, which is why the individual alpha frequency matters. A band is not a rhythm, and band power mixes peaks with background.

PLATE 4.24:06

Alpha · Berger's rhythm

Alpha, about eight to twelve hertz and strongest over posterior regions, grows when the eyes close and shrinks when they open, a reliable quality check. Like shop signs lit over closed shutters, it is thought to mark functional inhibition: in spatial attention it rises over the hemisphere processing the ignored side. This remains a hypothesis, with several sources and an arousal confound.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Of all the rhythms on our map, one is so strong you can sometimes see it with the naked eye in raw EEG. It was the first one ever described in humans, by Hans Berger, and for a while it was simply called the Berger rhythm. Today we call it alpha.

Imagine a shop street at night. When a shop closes, its shutter comes down, and a little sign lights up: closed. Walk down the street and you can tell which shops are serving customers and which are resting. Alpha behaves a lot like those signs.

Let's watch it happen. A participant sits quietly. Eyes open: the EEG at the back of the head shows small, irregular activity. Then she closes her eyes. Within a fraction of a second, a regular wave of about ten cycles per second appears, large enough to dominate the trace. She opens her eyes again, and it shrinks back. Berger described exactly this reaction in his first report in 1929.

In the spectrum, closing the eyes makes a sharp peak grow out of the background, at the individual alpha frequency we met in the previous episode. On the scalp map, the increase is strongest over occipital and parietal electrodes, above the visual cortex.

This reaction to eye opening, sometimes called alpha blocking, is one of the most reliable effects in all of EEG. It is also a perfect quality check: if you cannot see alpha grow when a healthy adult closes their eyes, look at your electrodes before anything else.

For a long time, alpha was described as an idling rhythm: the sound of the visual cortex with nothing to do. Since the late nineteen nineties, a more active idea has taken over. Wolfgang Klimesch and colleagues, and then Ole Jensen and Ali Mazaheri in 2010, proposed that alpha reflects functional inhibition. Strong alpha marks regions that are being actively held back, so that resources flow elsewhere. Jensen and Mazaheri called this gating by inhibition.

A classic test uses spatial attention. A participant fixates the centre of a screen and is told to attend to the left side, without moving their eyes. Alpha power then decreases over the right hemisphere, which processes the left visual field, and increases over the left hemisphere, which processes the side to be ignored. The closed shutters sit over the regions that are being ignored.

Similar patterns appear in memory tasks. When people hold items in working memory and must shield them from distraction, alpha often increases over regions that would process the distracters.

A few cautions keep this story honest. First, alpha is not a single thing. The posterior alpha rhythm differs from the motor mu rhythm, which we meet next time, and from alpha seen in other regions. Second, where alpha is generated is still debated: thalamic circuits and cortical layers both play a role. Third, inhibition is a hypothesis with strong support, not a settled law. Finally, alpha power also depends on arousal: it drops out as people become drowsy, which can make tiredness look like attention.

And remember the lessons of the map. Alpha peak frequency differs between people, and band power mixes the peak with the aperiodic background. Measure alpha at each person's own peak, or separate the two components, before you compare groups.

Let's recap. Alpha is a rhythm of roughly eight to twelve hertz, strongest over posterior regions, that grows when the eyes close and shrinks when they open. That reaction makes it an excellent quality check. Alpha is thought to reflect functional inhibition: it increases over regions being held back, for example the hemisphere processing an ignored side of space. It is a hypothesis, alpha has several sources, and arousal matters.

Next time, a rhythm in the same frequency band but with a different shape, place and purpose: mu, the motor alpha.

PLATE 4.33:46

Mu · The motor alpha

Mu shares alpha's band but sits over sensorimotor cortex, often with arched, wicket-like waves and a companion peak near twenty hertz. It desynchronizes during movement, motor imagery and, weakly, action observation, then rebounds, which powers motor imagery interfaces, though mirror neuron claims need caution. Topography, reactivity and shape separate mu from posterior alpha, since a band alone cannot name a rhythm.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we met alpha, at the back of the head. Today, its neighbour in frequency but not in place: the mu rhythm. Same band, roughly eight to thirteen hertz, but a different location, a different shape, and a different job.

Picture a row of fence posts with a rope looped over them. The rope dips into rounded arches between the posts, with sharp points on top. Hold that image: mu often looks exactly like that, a string of arches, which is why it was once called the wicket rhythm, after the hoops in a game of croquet.

Where do we find it? Over the sensorimotor cortex, the strip that controls and feels the body, under electrodes C3 and C4 on either side of the vertex. In the spectrum, it shows a peak in the alpha range, often with a second, smaller peak around twenty hertz.

That second peak is partly a consequence of the arch shape. A wave that is not a pure sinusoid needs harmonics to be described by Fourier, and a sharp, arched wave puts energy at twice its base frequency. We will come back to this trap in episode four point twelve: a peak in the spectrum does not always mean a separate oscillation.

Now the key behaviour. Mu is strong when the body is at rest. Move your right hand, and mu over the left hemisphere, at C3, drops. This drop is called event related desynchronization, a term made popular by Gert Pfurtscheller and colleagues. After the movement, mu and especially beta rebound above their resting level.

Remarkably, you do not even need to move. Simply imagining the movement also desynchronizes mu over the corresponding hemisphere. This is the basis of many brain computer interfaces: a person imagines moving the left or the right hand, and a classifier reads the lateralized drop in mu. We will build one with common spatial patterns in chapter fourteen.

Mu also decreases, more modestly, when people watch someone else move. That observation made mu famous, as a possible window on the human mirror neuron system. But be careful. In 2016, Hannah Hobson and Dorothy Bishop reviewed this literature and showed that observation effects on mu are often small, and easily confounded by attention and by posterior alpha leaking into central electrodes. Mu suppression alone is not a mirror neuron meter.

So why does mu matter for analysis? Because it shares a band with posterior alpha. If you average power from eight to twelve hertz over the whole head, you mix two rhythms with different sources and opposite behaviour. Closing the eyes boosts posterior alpha; moving the hands suppresses mu. The band is the same; the meaning is not.

To tell them apart, use three clues. Topography: mu sits over central sites, alpha over posterior sites. Reactivity: mu responds to movement and touch, alpha to opening and closing the eyes. And shape: mu is often arched. A surface Laplacian, or a source or component decomposition, helps separate them spatially.

Let's recap. Mu is an eight to thirteen hertz rhythm over the sensorimotor cortex, often arch shaped, with a companion peak near twenty hertz. It desynchronizes during movement, motor imagery, and to a lesser degree action observation, then rebounds. It powers motor imagery interfaces. Claims about mirror neurons need caution. And the deepest lesson: the same frequency band can host different rhythms, so a band alone cannot name a rhythm.

Next time, we slow down to theta, and the rhythm that rises at the front of the head when you have to think hard.

PLATE 4.43:58

Theta · Frontal midline theta

Frontal midline theta, four to eight hertz at Fz and FCz, rises like a conductor's baton when the score gets hard: with working memory load, errors and conflict, possibly signalling the need for cognitive control. It differs from drowsiness theta, children's theta and rodent hippocampal theta. Evoked and induced activity must be separated, and the aperiodic slope can mimic theta changes.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Let's slow down to theta, roughly four to eight hertz. Theta appears in many places and many states, but one version has become a favourite of cognitive neuroscience: frontal midline theta, the rhythm that rises when you have to think hard.

Picture a conductor in front of an orchestra. When the music is easy, she barely moves. When the score gets tricky, a difficult entry, a sudden change of tempo, her baton starts beating clearly and steadily to keep everyone together. Frontal midline theta behaves a little like that baton.

Where is it? Over the midline of the frontal scalp, around electrodes Fz and FCz. Source studies point to the medial frontal cortex, including the anterior cingulate.

When does it rise? Classic tasks include mental arithmetic and working memory. Ask someone to hold more and more items in mind, and frontal midline theta tends to grow with the load.

It also responds to moments that call for control. After an error, after conflicting information, or after unexpected feedback, a burst of frontal theta appears within a few hundred milliseconds. In 2014, James Cavanagh and Michael Frank proposed that frontal theta acts as a common mechanism for signalling the need for cognitive control, a kind of alarm that recruits other regions.

That is the elegant story. Now the cautions.

First, not every theta is frontal midline theta. Theta also increases diffusely as people become drowsy, and it dominates the posterior EEG of young children. A theta increase over the whole scalp in a long, boring task may say more about sleepiness than about cognitive control. Check the topography and the time course.

Second, beware of analogies with animal studies. In rodents, the hippocampus produces a strong, regular theta rhythm, typically between about six and twelve hertz, during exploration and movement. It is tempting to treat scalp theta in humans as the same thing. It is not. Scalp EEG barely sees the deep hippocampus, and recordings inside the human hippocampus suggest that its theta like activity is often slower than in rodents. Frontal midline theta at the scalp is a cortical signal with its own story.

Third, theta in event related designs mixes two things: evoked activity, phase locked to the event, which also appears in the average ERP, and induced activity, which does not. The error related negativity, for instance, carries a lot of theta band energy. Episode nine point three shows how to separate the two.

Fourth, as always, the band is a convention. Some studies use four to seven, others four to eight, others three to eight. And the aperiodic background lifts low frequencies, so a change in its slope can masquerade as a change in theta power. Parameterizing the spectrum, as we will do in episode four point eleven, helps.

So how do you study frontal midline theta well? Choose a task with a clear contrast, such as high versus low memory load, or errors versus correct trials. Look at Fz and FCz, but check the topography. Use time frequency analysis around events, and control for vigilance.

Let's recap. Frontal midline theta, around four to eight hertz at Fz and FCz, rises with working memory load, mental effort, errors and conflict, and may signal the need for cognitive control. It is not the same as drowsiness theta, the theta of children, or rodent hippocampal theta. Separate evoked from induced activity, and watch the background.

Next time, faster rhythms over the motor cortex: beta, the sensorimotor rhythm, and the bursts that hide behind their averages.

PLATE 4.54:14

Beta and SMR · Motor rhythms

Motor beta, about thirteen to thirty hertz, drops during movement and rebounds above baseline after it stops, a sign of holding the status quo. The sensorimotor rhythm launched neurofeedback after Sterman's cat studies. Like fireflies whose steady glow is only an average, beta on single trials comes in brief bursts, so higher average power may mean more, longer or stronger bursts.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Above alpha lies beta, roughly thirteen to thirty hertz. Beta is everywhere in the EEG, but over the motor cortex it tells a particularly clear story about moving, stopping, and holding still. And it hides a surprise behind its averages.

Think of fireflies in a summer meadow. Watch the whole meadow from far away for an hour, and you see a steady, soft glow. Look closely at one patch, and there is no steady glow at all: just brief flashes, here and there, now and then. The steady glow is an average. Beta is a lot like that meadow.

Let's start with what the average shows. Over the hand area of the motor cortex, at C3 or C4, beta power is present at rest. When a person prepares and makes a movement, beta drops: an event related desynchronization, like mu. Then, about half a second to a second after the movement stops, beta comes back stronger than before, overshooting its resting level. This is the post movement beta rebound, described by Gert Pfurtscheller and colleagues in the nineteen nineties.

The rebound is often interpreted as an active stop, or a resetting of the motor system once the movement is over. Holding a posture steady is also associated with relatively high beta. One popular summary is that beta signals the status quo: keep things as they are.

Now a close cousin, with a famous history: the sensorimotor rhythm, or S M R, around twelve to fifteen hertz over the sensorimotor cortex. In the late nineteen sixties, Barry Sterman and colleagues described it in cats that waited motionless for a food reward, and trained the cats to produce more of it.

Then came an accident of history. Sterman's lab was testing a rocket fuel component, monomethylhydrazine, which causes seizures. Cats that had been trained to produce S M R had seizures later, or not at all. This led to S M R neurofeedback for epilepsy in humans, and to a large neurofeedback field, with results that range from solid to debated depending on the application.

Now the fireflies. When we average beta power over many trials, it looks like a smooth, sustained rhythm that rises and falls. But look at single trials, and beta is not sustained at all. It comes in brief bursts, often lasting less than about a hundred and fifty milliseconds.

In 2017, Hyeyoung Shin, Stephanie Jones and colleagues showed, in human MEG and in mice, that differences in average beta power before a stimulus mainly reflected differences in the number of beta events per trial: the event rate. And bursts that occurred shortly before a faint touch made it less likely to be detected.

That changes how we should think and measure. A higher average beta can mean more bursts, longer bursts, stronger bursts, or a genuinely sustained rhythm. The average cannot tell them apart. Looking at single trials, and counting bursts, can.

There is also a link with waveform shape. Beta and mu events are often not sinusoidal. Their asymmetric shapes can create harmonics and apparent couplings between frequencies, a trap we unpack in episode four point twelve.

A practical caution to finish. At high beta and beyond, muscle activity from the scalp, jaw and neck starts to contaminate the EEG, especially at temporal and frontal sites. A beta effect that is strongest at the edges of the cap deserves a careful look.

Let's recap. Motor beta, around thirteen to thirty hertz over the sensorimotor cortex, drops during movement and rebounds above baseline after it stops, and is linked to holding the status quo. The sensorimotor rhythm, around twelve to fifteen hertz, launched S M R neurofeedback after Sterman's cat studies. And beta on single trials comes in short bursts: an average can hide whether bursts became more frequent, longer, or stronger.

Next time, the fastest rhythms of all: gamma and high gamma, and why they must be handled with care.

PLATE 4.64:28

Gamma and high gamma · Handle with care

Gamma is real inside the brain but fragile on the scalp, like a violin solo recorded next to someone chewing crisps. Paralysis studies showed much scalp power above twenty hertz is muscle, and the classic induced gamma burst was tied to miniature saccades. Trust scalp gamma only with controls: topography, narrow spectral peaks, eye tracking, spatial filtering and a careful reference.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have reached the fast end of our map: gamma, above about thirty hertz, and high gamma, above about seventy. Gamma is linked to some of the most exciting ideas in neuroscience: binding, attention, local computation. It is also the band where scalp EEG is easiest to fool. Today, we learn to handle it with care.

Imagine trying to record a quiet violin solo in a room where someone is chewing crisps right next to the microphone. The violin is real, but the crunching is louder, at the same high pitch, and it sits closer to your microphone than the musician does.

Let's look at the violin first. Inside the brain, gamma activity is real and well established. Recordings from electrodes placed directly in or on the cortex show gamma and high gamma tightly linked to local processing: when a region is active, its high frequency power rises. High gamma in particular is thought to track local population firing.

Now the microphone. On the scalp, gamma is small, because the background spectrum keeps falling with frequency, and because fast activity tends to be local, and the skull attenuates local activity far more than widespread activity.

And now the crisps. Muscles produce electrical activity too, the electromyogram, with energy spread broadly from about twenty hertz to several hundred. The scalp is surrounded by muscles: the temporal muscles you use to chew, the forehead, the neck, the muscles around the eyes.

How much do they contaminate the EEG? In 2007, Emma Whitham and colleagues answered this in the most direct way possible. They recorded scalp EEG from two volunteers before and during complete neuromuscular blockade, a temporary pharmacological paralysis. Power above twenty hertz was ten to two hundred times lower during paralysis. In other words, much of what we normally call scalp gamma, even at rest, was muscle.

The eyes add a second trap. For years, a broadband burst of gamma power around two to three hundred milliseconds after a visual stimulus was a celebrated finding, the induced gamma band response. In 2008, Shlomit Yuval Greenberg, Leon Deouell and colleagues recorded EEG and eye movements together. Trial by trial, the gamma burst was locked to tiny involuntary eye movements, miniature saccades, and reflected the saccadic spike potential produced by the eye muscles. When they kept only trials without such saccades, the response vanished.

So does that mean scalp gamma is useless? No. It means that gamma claims need strong controls. Here is a checklist.

Look at the topography. Muscle artifacts are strongest at the edges of the cap, near temporal, frontal and neck muscles. A gamma effect maximal at central sites is more credible.

Look at the spectrum. Muscle activity is broadband and rises toward high frequencies. A narrow peak, for instance a steady gamma response to a flickering stimulus at a known frequency, is more convincing than a broad smear.

Record and remove eye movements. Use an eye tracker or electrodes around the eyes to detect saccades, including tiny ones, and check that your effect survives without them.

Use spatial tools. Independent component analysis can isolate some muscle and saccade components. And the surface Laplacian helps, because it suppresses distant sources such as the muscles of the scalp edge, especially at central sites.

Finally, consider the reference. A reference near a muscle, or near the eyes, spreads that artifact into every channel. Episode three point twelve explains why.

Let's recap. Gamma, above thirty hertz, and high gamma, above seventy, are real and important inside the brain. On the scalp, they are small, and muscle activity shares their frequencies. Paralysis studies showed that much scalp power above twenty hertz is muscle, and the classic induced gamma response turned out to reflect miniature saccades. Trust scalp gamma only with controls: topography, spectral shape, eye tracking, spatial filtering, and a careful reference.

Next time, the slowest rhythms: delta and the slow oscillations of deep sleep.

PLATE 4.74:37

Delta and slow oscillations

Delta, below about four hertz, and the sub-hertz slow oscillation dominate deep sleep, when cortical neurons switch together between up and down states, like a stadium wave. They help organize other sleep rhythms and track sleep pressure. In an awake adult, delta suggests drowsiness, a clinical finding or, most often, artifacts, so context decides meaning.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We now slow right down, to the bottom of the frequency map: delta, below about four hertz, and the slow oscillation, below one hertz. These are the great rolling waves of deep sleep. And in an awake adult, they are a signal to look twice.

Picture a stadium crowd doing a wave. A whole section stands up together, cheers, then sits down and goes quiet, then stands again a moment later. In deep sleep, large populations of cortical neurons do something similar, together, roughly once every second or two.

Let's look at a night of sleep. Sleep is scored in thirty second pages into stages: wake, rapid eye movement sleep, and three stages of non rapid eye movement sleep, N1, N2 and N3. N3 is deep sleep, also called slow wave sleep. By the standard scoring rules of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, a page is scored N3 when at least a fifth of it contains slow waves of half a hertz to two hertz, larger than seventy five microvolts peak to peak, over frontal regions.

These waves are huge by EEG standards, and they come from a remarkable behaviour of cortical neurons. Recordings inside the cortex, starting with the work of Mircea Steriade and colleagues in the early nineteen nineties, showed that during deep sleep, neurons alternate between two states. In the up state, they are depolarized and fire. In the down state, they are hyperpolarized and almost silent. The whole population switches together, roughly once per second: this is the slow oscillation.

On the scalp, each down state appears as a large wave, typically negative over frontal sites, followed by a positive swing as neurons switch back up. That is our stadium wave: everyone sits down, goes quiet, and stands up again.

These waves are not just a sign of being deeply asleep. They are thought to help organise other sleep rhythms in time, such as the spindles we meet in episode four point eight, and they have been linked to memory consolidation. Their amplitude and number are also highest early in the night and after sleep deprivation, which makes slow wave activity a marker of sleep pressure.

Development matters too. Slow waves are large in children and adolescents, and they decline in amplitude with age. Delta is also normal in the waking EEG of infants and young children.

Now the twist. In an awake, healthy adult with eyes closed, there should be little rhythmic delta. If you see it, think of three possibilities.

First, drowsiness. As people slide towards sleep, slow activity increases. A long, dull resting recording can drift into stage N1 or beyond without anyone noticing.

Second, a clinical finding. In neurology, focal delta over one region can point to a structural problem underneath, and diffuse delta can accompany encephalopathies. That interpretation belongs to clinicians, not to a research pipeline, but it is a reason to look at raw data before averaging it away.

Third, and most common in research: artifacts. Eye movements, blinks, sweating, slow electrode drifts and head movements all produce large, slow deflections. They live exactly in the delta range. A delta effect strongest at frontal or lateral sites, or growing over the session as the room warms up, deserves suspicion.

So, context changes everything. The same two hertz wave means deep sleep at night, normal development in a baby, drowsiness in a bored participant, a possible lesion in a patient, or sweat in a warm lab.

A practical note for analysis. Low frequencies need long windows: to resolve half a hertz, you need several seconds of signal. And high pass filters, often set at half a hertz or one hertz, remove part of this range. If slow waves matter for your question, choose your filters accordingly, a point episode seven point seven will develop.

Let's recap. Delta, below about four hertz, and the slow oscillation, below one hertz, dominate deep sleep. They reflect cortical neurons switching together between up and down states, help coordinate other sleep rhythms, and track sleep pressure. In awake adults, delta suggests drowsiness, a clinical finding, or, very often, artifacts. Context is everything.

Next time, we stay asleep a little longer: sigma, sleep spindles and K complexes.

PLATE 4.84:23

Sigma · Sleep spindles and K-complexes

Stage N2 is marked by sleep spindles, brief eleven to sixteen hertz sigma bursts generated by thalamo cortical loops, and by K complexes, single large biphasic waves. Spindles are the fireworks over a quiet harbour, K complexes the big wave. A crowdsourced benchmark showed automatic detectors do worse than experts, who themselves disagree, so detector and parameters must be reported and checked by eye.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We stay asleep for one more episode. Between light drowsiness and the deep slow waves of N3 lies stage N2, the stage where we spend the largest share of a typical night. It has two signatures you can learn to spot by eye: sleep spindles, in the sigma band, and K complexes.

Picture a quiet night in a harbour. Most of the time the water is calm. Every now and then, a short string of fireworks crackles over the bay, then stops. And once in a while, a single big wave rolls in and crashes on the quay. Spindles are the fireworks. K complexes are the big wave.

A sleep spindle is a brief burst of rhythmic activity, typically between eleven and sixteen hertz, most often around twelve to fourteen, lasting at least half a second. Its amplitude waxes and wanes, which gives it its spindle shape, like thread wound on a spindle. In the scoring rules of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, spindles are usually maximal over central electrodes.

That frequency range is called sigma, a band you will rarely see in waking studies but often in sleep research. Spindles are generated by a loop between the thalamus and the cortex, with a key role for the reticular nucleus of the thalamus, a thin shell of inhibitory neurons that paces the rhythm.

Spindles are not all alike. Slower spindles, around eleven to thirteen hertz, tend to dominate frontal sites. Faster spindles, around thirteen to sixteen hertz, dominate central and parietal sites. Their density and features change with age, and they have been linked to memory consolidation and to several neurological and psychiatric conditions.

A K complex is a single large wave: a sharp negative deflection followed by a slower positive one, lasting at least half a second, usually largest over frontal regions. It can occur spontaneously, or in response to a sound or a touch during sleep, as if the brain were checking whether to wake up. Spindles often ride on the tail of a K complex.

Both are hallmarks of N2. And they link to the slow oscillation from last episode: during deep sleep, spindles tend to appear on the rising part of slow waves. This nesting of rhythms is one of the reasons sleep researchers care so much about timing.

Now a practical question. A night of sleep contains hundreds or thousands of spindles. Nobody wants to mark them all by hand, so we use automatic detectors. Most of them band pass filter the signal in sigma, compute an envelope or a power measure, and keep segments that stay above a threshold for long enough. Python tools such as YASA implement this.

How good are they? In 2014, Simon Warby and colleagues crowdsourced spindle scoring in recordings from middle aged and older adults, and built a consensus from several experts as a gold standard. Individual experts agreed with the consensus best, followed closely by a consensus of non experts. The six automated detectors they tested did worse. Even between experts, agreement was far from perfect.

So what do we learn? A spindle is partly a decision. Its count depends on the detector, its threshold, the frequency band, and the population, and even human experts disagree on borderline events. Report the detector and its parameters, check a sample of detections by eye, and validate on your own data, especially in older adults or patients, where spindles are smaller and detectors struggle.

Let's recap. Stage N2 is marked by sleep spindles and K complexes. Spindles are bursts of roughly eleven to sixteen hertz, the sigma band, generated by thalamo cortical loops, with slow frontal and fast centro parietal types. K complexes are large biphasic waves, often frontal, sometimes evoked by sounds. Automatic detectors are essential but imperfect, and even experts disagree: report your method and check it by eye.

Next time, we drop below the slowest rhythms we have seen so far: infraslow activity and DC shifts, the part of the spectrum your amplifier may never show you.

PLATE 4.94:25

Infraslow and DC shifts

Infraslow activity, below about a tenth of a hertz, vanishes under AC coupled amplifiers and common high pass filters, like a camera that never lets the glacier's movement show. Full band DC recordings with stable electrodes reveal slow oscillations linked to faster rhythms and perception, and slow shifts in the clinic. They are also exposed to skin potentials and drift, so filters decide what exists.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Below delta and below the slow oscillation, the EEG map has one more floor: the infraslow range, below about a tenth of a hertz. One cycle can last ten seconds, a minute, or longer. Most EEG studies never see it. Today, we find out why, and what lives down there.

Imagine filming a glacier with a camera that keeps only the last few seconds of every scene, and recentres the picture every time. You would see snowflakes, wind and birds. You would never see the glacier move.

Many EEG systems behave like that camera. Some amplifiers are A C coupled: a capacitor at the input blocks constant voltages and very slow changes. In practice, this acts as a hardware high pass filter, often around a tenth of a hertz, sometimes lower. Then many pipelines add their own high pass filter, at half a hertz or one hertz. Everything below the cut-off is gone before the analysis even starts.

Other amplifiers are D C coupled. They pass the full band, down to zero hertz. That is what you need to record infraslow activity, and it comes with demands.

The electrodes must be stable. Silver silver chloride electrodes are preferred because they are non polarisable: they do not build up their own slow drifts at the skin contact. The skin itself is a battery. Sweat glands and changes in skin hydration produce slow potentials of their own. And a D C recording needs an amplifier with enough input range to absorb electrode offsets without saturating.

So what have full band recordings found? In 2004, Sampsa Vanhatalo and colleagues recorded D C coupled EEG during sleep. They found large infraslow oscillations, between about two hundredths and two tenths of a hertz, across the cortex. These were not isolated: faster EEG activity, K complexes and interictal epileptic events tended to occur at particular phases of the infraslow cycle.

In 2008, Simo Monto and colleagues went further, during a task. Participants tried to detect faint touches at threshold. Their performance fluctuated slowly over time, and so did the full band EEG, between one hundredth and one tenth of a hertz. Detection depended on the phase of these infraslow fluctuations, not on their amplitude. And the amplitude of faster rhythms, from one to forty hertz, was locked to the same slow phase.

Slow potentials also have a long history in event related research. The contingent negative variation, a slow negative shift that builds while a person waits for an expected stimulus, and the readiness potential, which grows before a voluntary movement, both unfold over a second or more. A strong high pass filter can shrink or distort them.

And in clinical neurophysiology, D C recordings matter for spreading depolarisations, slow waves of near complete neuronal depolarisation that travel across injured cortex. They appear as large, slow D C shifts, invisible with conventional filters.

A word of caution. Infraslow signals are especially exposed to non neural sources: skin potentials, sweating, electrode drift and movement. Full band EEG is not just a setting to switch on. It needs stable electrodes, a calm and temperature controlled room, and control recordings.

The lesson of this episode is simple. What you do not record does not exist for your analysis. Your amplifier and your filters decide which part of the spectrum you study, before any hypothesis is tested. So report them, and choose them for your question.

Let's recap. Infraslow activity lies below about a tenth of a hertz. A C coupled amplifiers and common high pass filters remove it. D C coupled, full band EEG with stable electrodes can record it. Infraslow fluctuations are linked to sleep events, to faster rhythms, and to slow changes in perception, and slow D C shifts matter in the clinic. But this range is also the home of drifts and skin potentials.

The aperiodic component · The 1/f slope

Under the peaks of every spectrum lies an aperiodic background, close to a straight line on a log log plot, summarized by an offset and an exponent. Like a ski slope under the moguls, it changes with age, sleep and anaesthesia, and a steeper slope has been linked to more inhibition, a debated hypothesis. A slope change can masquerade as a rhythm change.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Look at any EEG power spectrum on a log scale. You see a few peaks, like alpha. But most of the picture is something else: a background that falls steadily from low to high frequencies. For a long time, this background was treated as noise to subtract. Today, we give it its own episode: the aperiodic component.

Imagine a ski slope covered with moguls. The moguls are bumps you can count and measure. But the slope itself also matters: how high it starts, and how steep it is. You cannot judge the height of a mogul without knowing where the slope is underneath it.

In a spectrum, the peaks are the moguls. They come from rhythmic, periodic activity. The slope is the aperiodic component. It has no characteristic frequency.

On a log log plot, this background is often close to a straight line, over a given frequency range. That is why it is called one over f, or a power law: power falls roughly as one over frequency raised to an exponent, chi. Two numbers summarise it. The offset is the overall height of the line, related to broadband power. The exponent is the steepness. A larger exponent means a steeper spectrum. A smaller one means a flatter spectrum.

Real spectra are not always perfectly straight. At low frequencies, the background can bend and flatten, a feature called the knee, especially in intracranial recordings and over wide frequency ranges. Models can include it.

Why should we care? Because the aperiodic component changes in meaningful ways. In 2015, Bradley Voytek and colleagues showed that the spectrum becomes flatter with age, and linked this flattening to working memory performance in older adults. Across sleep and anaesthesia, the spectrum becomes steeper. In 2020, Janna Lendner and colleagues showed that the exponent tracks arousal level, separating wakefulness from deep sleep, from propofol anaesthesia, and even from R E M sleep, which looks wake like in other ways.

What does the exponent reflect? In 2017, Richard Gao, Erik Peterson and Bradley Voytek used computational models to show that the balance between excitation and inhibition shapes the steepness of the spectrum: more inhibition, steeper; more excitation, flatter. Recordings under the anaesthetic propofol, which enhances inhibition, fit this prediction.

This excitation inhibition interpretation is attractive, and widely used. It is also still debated. Other factors shape the spectrum too: the time constants of synaptic currents, the number and size of active populations, the frequency range chosen for the fit, and the reference. Treat the exponent as a robust descriptor of the signal, and its link to excitation and inhibition as a hypothesis.

Now the practical warning, and the reason this episode matters for every band. Band power mixes peaks and background. Suppose one group has a flatter spectrum. Its power at high frequencies is higher and its power at low frequencies is lower, even with identical rhythms. A band power analysis would report beta up and delta down. Neither rhythm changed. The slope did.

So when you see a band power difference, ask: is there a peak above the background in that band? Did the peak change, or did the background move under it?

To answer these questions, you need to separate the two components. That is exactly what spectral parameterisation does, with a tool called specparam, formerly known as fooof.

Let's recap. Under the peaks of every spectrum lies an aperiodic background, close to a straight line on a log log plot. Its offset and exponent change with age, sleep, anaesthesia and arousal. A steeper slope has been linked to more inhibition, a hypothesis still under discussion. And because band power mixes peaks and slope, a change in the slope can masquerade as a change in rhythms.

Parameterizing spectra · specparam (FOOOF)

Specparam separates the aperiodic background from peaks, like a tailor first cutting the basic shape then adding adjustments. It fits a line, flattens the spectrum, finds Gaussian peaks above a threshold, refits the background without them, and returns offset, exponent, and each peak's centre, power and width. Two groups with equal alpha band power can differ, so fits must be inspected.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the last episode, we saw that every spectrum mixes two things: an aperiodic background and periodic peaks on top. Today, we learn to separate them with spectral parameterisation, a method published in 2020 by Thomas Donoghue, Bradley Voytek and colleagues, and implemented in a Python package called specparam, formerly fooof.

Think of a tailor fitting a suit. First, the tailor cuts the basic shape to the body: the overall line. Then, wherever there is a bump, a shoulder or an elbow, the tailor adds a small adjustment, just where it is needed. The result fits the body, and you can read every measurement from the pattern.

Specparam does the same with a power spectrum, in log power, over a frequency range you choose, for example one to forty hertz.

Step one: fit an initial aperiodic component, the overall line, to the whole spectrum.

Step two: subtract it. What remains is a flattened spectrum, where the peaks stand out above zero.

Step three: find the peaks. The algorithm looks for the highest point in the flattened spectrum. If it is high enough, above a threshold set relative to the noise, by default two standard deviations, it fits a Gaussian there, with a centre frequency, a height and a width. It removes that Gaussian and searches again, until no peak is left above threshold, or until a maximum number of peaks is reached.

Step four: refit the aperiodic component, this time on the spectrum with the peaks removed, so the peaks no longer bias the line.

Step five: combine everything into a single model, and compare it with the data.

What comes out is a short list of numbers with a clear meaning. For the background: the offset and the exponent, and a knee if you ask for one. For each peak: its centre frequency, its power above the background, and its bandwidth. And for the quality of the fit: the error and the R squared.

Here is why this matters. Imagine two groups, with the same alpha band power. A classic analysis says they do not differ. Now parameterise. Group A has a tall alpha peak sitting on a low background. Group B has a small alpha peak sitting on a higher background. The band power was the same, but the rhythms were not. And the reverse happens too: a band power difference that is entirely due to the background.

In Python, it takes a few lines. Compute the spectrum with M N E, using Welch's method. Then create a SpectralModel, choose the settings, and fit it to the frequencies and the power values within your range. To process many channels or participants at once, use SpectralGroupModel.

A few settings deserve thought. The peak width limits prevent the algorithm from fitting very narrow noise or very broad bumps as peaks. The maximum number of peaks, and the minimum peak height, control how much it is allowed to explain. And the aperiodic mode is either fixed, a straight line in log log, or knee, when the spectrum bends.

Now the cautions. Always look at the fit, plotted over the data. Check the error and the R squared, and inspect the worst fits, not just the average. Choose the frequency range carefully: the exponent depends on it, and a range crossing a knee, or a line noise peak at fifty or sixty hertz, can bias it. Use enough data for a smooth spectrum, because a noisy spectrum gives unstable peaks. And fix the settings before looking at group differences, so they are not tuned to the result you hoped for.

Let's recap. Specparam fits the aperiodic component, flattens the spectrum, detects peaks as Gaussians above a threshold, refits the background, and combines them. You get an offset, an exponent, and for each peak a centre frequency, a power and a width. It reveals when band power mixes a real peak change with a background change. And like any model, it must be checked against the data.

Bursts, waveform shape and “a peak is not an oscillation”

A spectral peak is not proof of a sustained oscillation. Like a bouncing ball, rhythms are often non sinusoidal, so sharp shapes produce harmonics, such as a mu shadow near twenty hertz, and can create spurious phase amplitude coupling. Rhythms also come in bursts that spectra average away, which cycle by cycle tools can reveal.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We have spent a whole chapter looking at spectra. Today, we close the chapter by questioning the picture itself. A peak in the spectrum is often read as proof of an ongoing oscillation. Sometimes it is not.

Picture a ball bouncing on the floor. Its motion is periodic: up, down, up, down. But it is not a sine wave. The ball rises and falls in smooth arcs, and turns sharply at every bounce. And if you only drop it once, it bounces a few times, and stops.

Two lessons hide in that ball: shape, and bursts.

Start with shape. The Fourier transform describes any signal as a sum of sine waves. A perfect sine needs only one. A wave with sharp corners, or one that rises faster than it falls, needs several: the fundamental, plus harmonics at two, three or four times its frequency. In the spectrum, a single non sinusoidal rhythm becomes a peak, plus smaller peaks at its multiples.

Brain rhythms are often not sinusoidal. The mu rhythm has an arch shape, sharp on one side and rounded on the other, which is why it produces a harmonic in the beta range. A spectral peak near twenty hertz over motor cortex can partly be the shadow of mu at ten hertz, not an independent beta rhythm. Motor beta itself can be sharp and asymmetric.

Waveform shape is not just a nuisance. It can carry information. In 2017, Scott Cole, Bradley Voytek and colleagues recorded motor cortex in people with Parkinson's disease. Their beta oscillations had sharp, asymmetric peaks and troughs. Deep brain stimulation made them less sharp, and sharper beta was associated with more rigidity.

Shape also creates a trap for analysis. Phase amplitude coupling measures whether the amplitude of a fast rhythm follows the phase of a slow one. But a sharp, non sinusoidal slow wave contains harmonics, and these harmonics have power at high frequencies, locked to its phase. The result looks like coupling between two rhythms, while there is only one rhythm with a sharp shape. Cole and Voytek's 2017 review, titled Brain oscillations and the importance of waveform shape, made this point widely known.

Now bursts. A spectrum is computed over a window, and averaged across windows. A rhythm present for a short burst, a few cycles here and there, still produces a peak. As we saw for beta in episode four point five, the average cannot tell a sustained oscillation from rare bursts. And a peak can even appear in data containing only isolated transients, if they share a typical duration.

So how do we look beyond the peak? One option is to work in the time domain, cycle by cycle. The bycycle package, from Cole and Voytek, splits the signal into individual cycles and measures each one: amplitude, period, rise decay symmetry, and peak trough symmetry. It then marks a cycle as part of a burst only if neighbouring cycles are consistent in amplitude and period, and rise and fall cleanly.

You can then ask better questions. How often do bursts occur? How long do they last? Did the effect come from more bursts, longer bursts, or bigger ones? Did the waveform change shape?

Here is a short checklist. First, check that there is a peak above the aperiodic background, with specparam. Second, look at the raw signal, filtered broadly, and see whether the rhythm is sustained or bursty. Third, look for harmonics, and consider whether a peak could be the harmonic of another rhythm. Fourth, before claiming cross frequency coupling, inspect the waveform shape of the slow rhythm.

Let's recap. Brain rhythms are often non sinusoidal. Their shape creates harmonics in the spectrum, can carry physiological information, and can produce spurious cross frequency coupling. Rhythms often come in bursts, which a spectrum averages away. A peak is evidence of rhythmicity, not proof of a sustained oscillation. Cycle by cycle tools like bycycle help you see what the spectrum hides.
