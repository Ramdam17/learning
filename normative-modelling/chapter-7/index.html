PLATE 7.16:14

Calibration of centiles

A centile chart is an instrument to check on unseen people. A calibrated model puts the promised fraction below each centile line, at every age and site, checked with a quantile-quantile plot and z-score skewness and excess kurtosis. A Gaussian model with true means looks fine on average but misses the tails, especially for older people.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we looked at a very tall sunflower, and asked what a large deviation really means. Atypical does not mean sick. And a deviation only makes sense against the population that built the norm. Today we ask the question that comes before all of that. Can we trust the lines themselves?

Welcome to the bureau of weights and measures. Here, nobody uses an instrument before checking it. A centile chart is an instrument. So we check it.

The check is simple. Take people the model has never seen. Pour them into the chart, one by one, and watch which lane each person lands in.

If the model is well calibrated, the lanes fill up in a precise way. Two point five percent of people fall below the 2.5th centile line. Twenty-five percent fall below the twenty-fifth. Half fall below the median. Seventy-five percent fall below the seventy-fifth. And ninety-seven point five percent fall below the 97.5th. At every age. On every site.

That is what a centile means, written as a promise. Calibration is that promise, kept or broken.

To see it in a single picture, we line up all the promises at once. For each centile, from the very low to the very high, we plot the promised fraction on one axis, and the observed fraction on the other. This is a quantile-quantile plot. A calibrated model draws the diagonal. If the dots bow above the diagonal, the model puts too many people in its lower lanes. If they bow below, too few. The distance between the dots and the diagonal, averaged over all centile levels, is one number. Some toolkits report it as the mean absolute centile error.

Now, a real warning about small numbers. Suppose a chart is perfect, and you test one hundred people. You expect five below the fifth centile line. But by chance alone, you may see as few as one, or as many as ten. A single age bin with a hundred people cannot prove that a model is wrong. Keep the sampling noise in your head, and read patterns, not single bars.

Let us watch this happen on our simulated data. Everything you see now is simulated. A made-up cortical index, in arbitrary units, for four hundred and fifty fictional people, from three sites.

We give a simple Gaussian model the best possible start. At every age and every site, it knows the true mean, and the true standard deviation. Nothing is estimated, so nothing can be overfitted. Only one thing is wrong. This model believes the noise is a symmetric bell. In our simulation, it is not. It leans to the high side, and it leans more as people get older.

Overall, the picture looks almost fine. A little over half the people fall below the middle line. But look at the tails. Of the four hundred and fifty people, only one falls below the 2.5th centile line, while about sixteen fall above the 97.5th. The promise was about eleven of each. Lower lane too empty, upper lane too full.

This is the key moment of the episode. A model can be well calibrated on average, and badly calibrated for the people who matter.

So we slice. First by age. Under thirty years, the lanes are close to their promises. Over sixty-two, they are not. Of one hundred and twenty-two people, seventy-six, about sixty-two percent, fall below the middle line. Nobody falls below the 2.5th centile line, while five fall above the 97.5th. For an older person, the lower lane is almost never reached, and the upper lane is crowded.

Then by site. One site has only people over forty-five. On that site, thirty-seven of sixty people fall below the middle line. Careful. Is that a site effect, or an age effect in disguise? The slices cannot tell us by themselves. You slice by age and by site, and you compare the slices with each other, before you blame a scanner.

Averages hide this, because the errors cancel. A model can be too wide at one age, and too narrow at another, and still look perfect when we pool everyone.

There is a second diagnostic, and it goes straight to the z-scores. Compute the z-score of every test person. If the model is calibrated, those z-scores should look like a standard bell. Mean zero. Standard deviation one. And two numbers describe the shape.

Skewness measures lopsidedness. A symmetric bell has a skewness of zero. Kurtosis measures how heavy the tails are. Here, a convention matters. The raw kurtosis of a Gaussian is three. Excess kurtosis subtracts three, so a Gaussian gets zero. The published pretrained models report excess kurtosis, and so does the PCNtoolkit documentation. In this series, kurtosis always means excess kurtosis. Both numbers should be close to zero.

Be careful with the second one. Kurtosis is very sensitive to a handful of outliers, so one odd measurement can move it a lot.

On our simulated test people, the z-scores have a skewness of zero point seven four, and an excess kurtosis of zero point five four. Under thirty, the skewness is about zero point three. Over sixty-two, it is about one. The diagnostic points at the same place as the lanes. The model's shape is wrong, and wrong most for older people.

This is why we report these numbers by age bin and by site, never only in total. The model label at the end of this episode gets a new line, the test calibration, with its slices.

One last bridge. In the fMRI series, we saw that scanners differ. Calibration by site is how that difference shows up in a normative model.

So, to sum up. A calibrated model keeps the promise of its centiles, on unseen people, at every age and on every site. Check it with a quantile-quantile plot, with slices, and with the skewness and the excess kurtosis of the z-scores.

PLATE 7.26:04

Explained variance and MSLL

Explained variance scores only the predicted mean, so three models with the same mean tie at 0.46 while differing wildly in spread. The mean standardised log loss scores the whole predicted distribution as average surprise against a naive model. A model announcing half the true spread does worse than the naive one, because every z-score doubles.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we poured unseen people into the centile lanes, and counted. Five percent below the fifth centile, half below the median. That told us whether the lanes were honest. Today we put two more instruments on the bench. One weighs the predicted mean. The other weighs the whole predicted distribution.

Welcome back to the office of weights and measures. Everything on this bench is simulated. The measure is our cortical index, in arbitrary units, for the two hundred and forty people of site A.

We cut them at random into two halves. The first half is for fitting. The second half, one hundred and twenty people, stays unseen, and it is the half we weigh on. Why that cut matters is the next episode.

To keep the lesson clean, all three models on the bench are given the true mean curve of the simulation. They differ in one thing only. The spread they predict around that mean.

Instrument one. Explained variance.

For each unseen person, take the error. The observed value, minus the predicted mean. Now take the variance of those errors, and divide it by the variance of the observed values. Subtract that ratio from one.

Explained variance equals one, minus the variance of the errors, over the variance of the data.

If the predicted means were perfect, the errors would vanish, and the score would be one. If the model did no better than guessing the average, the score would be zero. And if it did worse, the score can even go negative.

On our bench, the score is zero point four six. About forty-six percent of the differences between people is carried by age. The rest are differences between people of the same age.

That remainder is not a failure. In a normative model, it is the reference itself, the spread that scores will be measured against.

One small warning. Explained variance forgives a constant offset. Push every prediction up by five units, and the score does not move. It judges the shape of the predicted mean, and nothing else.

Instrument two. The mean standardised log loss, or MSLL.

This time the model does not only predict a mean. It predicts a distribution, here a bell, with a mean and a spread. And we ask one question. How surprised was the model by the person who showed up?

Surprise has a simple definition. Take the height of the predicted bell at the observed value, and take minus its logarithm. A person near the middle of a bell is a small surprise. A person far out in a narrow bell is a very large one.

For a Gaussian, this log loss has two parts. One half of the logarithm of two pi sigma squared. It punishes a bell that is too wide. Then the squared error over two sigma squared. It punishes an error that is large compared with the spread the model announced.

Average this over every unseen person. That is the mean log loss. A number alone means little, so we standardise it.

Build a naive model. One single bell, with the mean and variance of the training data, ignoring age completely. Compute its mean log loss on the same unseen people. Then subtract.

MSLL equals the mean log loss of the model, minus the mean log loss of the naive model.

Zero means no better than the naive model. Negative means better. Lower is better. This is the convention of Rasmussen and Williams, in their book on Gaussian processes, in two thousand and six.

Here is the key moment. Three models, the same predicted mean, so the same explained variance. Zero point four six, three times.

The first model predicts the true spread at each age, which grows from about three to about seven. Its MSLL is minus zero point three nine.

The second model predicts one single spread for everybody, estimated from the training errors, about four and a half. It is too wide for the young, and too narrow for the old. Its MSLL is minus zero point two nine. A little worse.

The third model keeps the right mean, but announces half the true spread. Its MSLL is plus zero point four five. That is worse than the naive model that ignores age.

Explained variance saw nothing. It cannot, because it never looks at the spread.

And for a normative model, the spread is what makes the score. Remember the z-score. The error, divided by sigma. Halve sigma, and every z-score doubles. On our unseen people, the third model would put one person in three beyond plus or minus one point nine six. The true model puts about four in a hundred. An ordinary person would look like an outlier.

So a good mean is necessary, and not enough. The question is whether the model got its uncertainty right.

A few points to keep in mind. These numbers come from a small simulated test set, so read them as an illustration, not as a benchmark. The idea of log loss does not need a Gaussian. Any model that predicts a full distribution can be scored this way, including models with skewed bells. And both metrics are only honest on unseen people.

These are the metrics that pretrained lifespan models report, together with the skewness and the kurtosis of the z-scores. In their paper of two thousand and twenty-two, Rutherford and colleagues reported explained variance and MSLL for each brain region, on data held out from fitting.

A bridge to another series. In the Information series, surprise is minus the logarithm of a probability, and an average surprise is a cross-entropy. The log loss is exactly that, measured against the naive model.

So, to sum up. Explained variance weighs the predicted mean. MSLL weighs the whole predicted distribution, against a naive model. Next time, we ask what unseen really means. Train, test, and cross-validation.

PLATE 7.34:58

Unseen data: train, test, cross-validation

A normative model is like a scale tuned on reference stones: weighing those same stones proves nothing. Controls are split into training and test piles, with patients kept out of the fit, and cross-validation lets every control take a turn at being unseen. Training controls get z-scores that are too small, so patients must be compared with controls the model has not seen.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we put two rulers on the model. Explained variance for the mean, and the mean standardised log loss for the whole predicted distribution. Today we ask a question that comes before both. Measured on which people?

Start with a scale at the weights and measures office. A merchant adjusts it until it reads the right weight for a set of reference stones. Then he proves it is accurate by weighing those same stones. Of course it passes. It was tuned on them.

A normative model is that scale. Fitting it means adjusting it on a set of healthy controls. If we then score those same controls, the model has already seen them. It bends toward them, a little. Their scores look better than they should.

So the first rule is simple. Cut the controls in two piles.

One pile is for fitting. That is the training set. The model learns the centiles from it, and from nothing else.

The other pile is locked away. That is the test set. The model never touches it. Only after the fit is finished do we pour the test people into the centile lanes, and compute our metrics. Those are called out-of-sample metrics. They are the honest ones.

This is exactly what the large pretrained models of Rutherford and colleagues did, in twenty twenty-two. They split their reference sample into two equal halves, stratified by site, so that every scanner was represented in both piles. They report out-of-sample metrics for all models. Each half held twenty-nine thousand four hundred eighteen people.

And the patients? They are a third pile. They are never part of the fit. They are scored by a model that was built from controls only. Three piles. Training controls, test controls, and patients.

Now, a single cut has a weakness. If you only have sixty controls, what do you do? Keep forty for training, and twenty for testing? Then both piles are small. And a different random cut would give a different answer.

This is where cross-validation comes in.

Cut the controls into five equal parts, called folds. Hold out fold one. Fit the model on folds two to five. Score the people of fold one. Now put fold one back, hold out fold two, and repeat. Five fits, five times a different test pile.

At the end, every control has been scored exactly once, by a model that had not seen that person. And we have five estimates of the metric, not one, so we can see how much it moves.

The final model, the one we keep, is then fitted on all the controls. Cross-validation is a way to estimate how good a model will be. It is not the model itself.

One caution. With small samples, even a cross-validated score carries wide error bars. Varoquaux wrote about it in twenty eighteen, for neuroimaging. A score from fifty people deserves a margin, and the margin should be shown.

Now, the key moment. What exactly goes wrong if we score the training controls?

Let us measure it, on the simulated data. This is the simulated cortical index, from site A. We draw forty controls at random, and fit a very flexible curve through them. The remaining controls are our unseen people. Then we compute z-scores for both groups.

Remember that about five in a hundred healthy people should fall beyond plus or minus one point nine six.

Averaged over five hundred random cuts, only about three in a hundred of the training controls do. The curve has bent toward them. For the unseen controls, about ten in a hundred do. Same population, same model, two different stories.

So the z-scores of the training controls are too small. They are too small because the model has already seen them. This is simulated, and the exact figures depend on how flexible the curve is. But the direction never changes.

And here is the trap. Suppose we score the patients with that model, and compare them with the training controls. The controls look tight and well behaved. The patients look far away. Part of that gap is not biology. It is the model's memory.

So, never compare patients to training controls. Compare them to controls the model has not seen. Either the test pile, or the controls scored by cross-validation, each one by a model that left them out.

In the EEG series, a related mistake was called double dipping. Kriegeskorte and colleagues described it in two thousand nine as using the same data to select, and then to test. We will meet its normative cousin in episode eleven point three, when we talk about leakage and double use.

So, to sum up. Fit on one pile, judge on another, and keep the patients out of the fit. Cross-validation lets every control take a turn at being unseen.

PLATE 7.45:32

Transfer to a new site

A pretrained model shifts at a new site, like a reference weight posted to another laboratory, and the shift flows into every z-score. Recalibrating with a handful of local controls corrects it, but too few controls give a noisy offset. In simulation the error falls steeply then plateaus, and published studies suggest roughly twenty-five to fifty same-site controls.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we kept our controls in two piles, training and test, so that a model is judged on people it has never seen. Today the unseen people come from somewhere else. A new site, with its own scanner, and a model that was built before that scanner ever joined the study.

Picture a reference weight at the weights and measures office. It is sent by post to a new laboratory. The journey, and the new balance, shift the reading a little. So the laboratory weighs a few stones whose mass it already knows, notes the offset, and corrects every later measurement. It does not rebuild the standard. It recalibrates it.

A pretrained normative model is that reference weight. Its centiles were built on many sites, far from yours. At your site, the scanner, the protocol and the software add a small shift, and sometimes a small change in spread. If nobody corrects it, that shift travels straight into every z-score. In the fMRI series, scanner differences are something to watch. Here, the same difference becomes a number we estimate.

Let us look at a real transfer set. The braincharts project shares one, derived from OpenNeuro, for its demonstration of pretrained models. It holds measurements from six OpenNeuro datasets, and we treat each one as a site. The shared files contain one hundred ten rows for adaptation, and four hundred thirty-six rows for testing. The ages run from about five to seventy-three years, but only one site goes past forty-four.

Here is the recipe. Take a small group of controls from the new site, and set them aside. This is the adaptation set. Run them through the pretrained model, and look at where they land. If the site shifts everything upward, their z-scores sit above zero. The model then updates its site terms. In the pretrained models of Rutherford and colleagues, in twenty twenty-two, the adjustment is made to the mean and the variance, in the latent Gaussian space, after applying the warp learned on the training data. The authors note that, to remain unbiased, it must be done on a held-out calibration set.

Everyone else at the site is the test set, scored with the adapted model.

The tutorial of the PCNtoolkit software gives a technical minimum. Two data points of the same sex in each site. But it adds that in practice you want many more, because the variance of each site must be estimated reliably.

So how many? Let us measure it. This is the simulated cortical index. Site A plays the big reference sample. We fit the model on site A alone. Site B is the new site. In the simulation, it reads three units higher than site A, and its spread is fifteen percent wider.

First, no adaptation at all. We score every person of site B with the site A model. Their average z-score is plus zero point five four. Their spread is a little too wide. And about eleven in a hundred fall beyond plus or minus one point nine six, where five in a hundred should. The new site looks abnormal. It is only the scale.

Now adapt, with a random handful of site B controls, and test on everyone left. Repeat this two thousand times for each size.

With five controls, the typical error on the average z-score is still about zero point five. As large as having done nothing, and sometimes worse. About fourteen in a hundred fall outside the lines. With ten controls, the error drops to about zero point three. With twenty-five, to about zero point two, and the outside rate is near six in a hundred. With fifty, the curve is flattening. Beyond that, it is nearly flat.

So here is the key moment. Without adaptation, the z-scores of a new site are shifted. With too few controls, they are noisy. The estimate of the offset is itself a measurement, and it has an error bar. Those figures are simulated, and depend on our simple recalibration. But the shape, a steep fall and then a plateau, matches the published studies.

Here is what they say, as of November 2026. Gaiser and colleagues, in twenty twenty-four, used hierarchical Bayesian regression on children scanned by two new scanners. They found that as few as twenty-five samples per site gave good performance, with a ceiling near one hundred. A practical guide posted on arXiv in twenty twenty-five found that about thirty site-matched controls gave stable estimates in their case study, and that ten or fewer were unreliable. And Elleaume and colleagues, in twenty twenty-six, found that adapted models plateaued around fifty people, against about two hundred for a model trained from scratch in the same dataset.

The numbers differ, because the models, the measures and the samples differ. Read them as a range. Roughly twenty-five to fifty controls, from the same site.

One more thing. Those controls should look like the people you will score. The same scanner, the same protocol, and the same ages.

So, to sum up. Without adaptation, a new site shifts every z-score. With a handful of local controls, we recalibrate, but too few controls give a noisy correction. The pretrained model is not to be rebuilt, only recalibrated. Report how many controls you used.

PLATE 7.54:53

Comparing models fairly

Fair model comparison uses the same people, repeated cross-validation, a simple baseline to beat, and error bars on score differences. In simulation, a flexible eighth-degree model remembers its training data but turns worse than naive at rare ages, and symmetric bells miss skewed tails that a warped model follows. A complex model must win on unseen people, rare ages and tails.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a model travelled to a new site, and needed a handful of local people to find its feet. Today the question is simpler, and harder. Two models are on the bench. Which one is better? And how would we know?

Welcome back to the office of weights and measures. Today it holds a contest of instruments. Everything on the bench is simulated: our cortical index, in arbitrary units, for the two hundred and forty people of site A.

The first rule of a fair contest is the same lot of weights for everybody. So every model gets the same people, cut in the same way. We use five-fold cross-validation, repeated twenty times with twenty different random cuts. Every model is fitted on the same four fifths, and scored on the same unseen fifth.

The second rule is a referee. A simple baseline, to beat. Ours is a straight line for the mean, with one single spread for everybody. Anything fancier must earn its place against it.

Now the contestants.

Model one is the baseline. A straight line, and a constant spread.

Model two is a cubic curve for the mean, with a spread that grows with age.

Model three is very flexible. A polynomial of the eighth degree for the mean, and the same growing spread.

Model four is model two, with one more feature. It lets the bell lean to one side, to follow the skewness of the data.

The score is the standardised log loss, the average surprise of the model, compared with a naive model that ignores age. Lower is better.

Here are the results on unseen people. Model one scores minus zero point two eight. Model two, minus zero point two nine. Model three, minus zero point two eight. Model four, minus zero point three four.

Look at the first three. Three different curves, and almost the same score. So we need a margin. For each person, take the difference between two models, and resample the people many times. The gain of model two over model one is about one hundredth, and its interval includes zero. The gain of model four over model two is about five hundredths, and its interval runs from just below zero to about one tenth. This is a small sample. Honest comparisons carry error bars.

And be careful which error bar. The spread of scores from one fold to the next looks reassuring, but it understates the real uncertainty. Nadeau and Bengio explained why in two thousand three. Varoquaux showed in two thousand eighteen that small neuroimaging samples give large error bars.

Now the key moment. A better average is not enough. A complex model has to win where it is hard to win.

First, on people it has not seen. Model three, on the people it was fitted to, has an average surprise of two point nine two. On unseen people, three point oh six. For the straight line, the two numbers are almost the same. Model three was good at remembering.

Second, at rare ages. Site A has only twelve people outside the range of fourteen to seventy-four years. Three are younger, nine are older. On these twelve, models one and two stay below zero. Model three is at plus zero point one. It is worse than the naive model, exactly where the data are thin. Its score also moves by about two tenths from one random cut to the next. The others move by about a hundredth. The average hid all of it.

Third, in the tails. A good model should put two and a half percent of people below its 2.5th centile, and two and a half percent above its 97.5th. The three Gaussian models put about one percent or less below, and more than four percent above. The data lean to one side, and a symmetric bell cannot follow. Model four puts three percent on each side. With twelve rare people, these intervals are wide, and we do not claim a winner there. We only claim that you must look.

The authors of the warped model say it themselves. Explained variance and log loss are not very sensitive to the shape of the predicted distribution. And non-Gaussian models can overfit. The large pretrained models report both metrics, with the skewness and kurtosis of the z-scores, all on out-of-sample data.

In the Information series, log loss is an average surprise. A model with overconfident tails is surprised too often, and a global average hides it.

So, to sum up. Compare models on the same unseen people, against a simple baseline, with intervals. A complex model wins only if it wins in the tails and at rare ages, not only on the average.
