Behaviour: small effects, big noise

Behaviour is the readout closest to the question and one of the noisiest: test-retest reliability of classic tasks runs from zero to 0.82, and difference scores are the least reliable. Practice, expectation and ceilings move scores without stimulation, and pooled single-session tDCS studies in healthy adults found no significant cognitive effect. One primary outcome and a control, fixed in advance, are the remedy.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every telescope in this observatory looks at the same noisy sky. The first one is the simplest: behaviour. Did the person see faster, remember more, move more accurately? It is the readout closest to the question we care about, and it is also one of the noisiest. This episode is about the measurement itself. How big is the noise, what drifts underneath it, and what must be decided before the first participant arrives.

Here is the picture. A runner on a track, timed with a stopwatch. We want to know whether a stimulation session makes the runner a few milliseconds faster. The runner is the participant. Each lap is a trial. The stopwatch is the task.

Start with the plain result: reaction times before and after a session. In this illustration, the average moves by a few milliseconds. Now add the error bars. Each bar shows how far an average could wander if we ran the same session again. The bars are far wider than the shift, and they overlap almost completely. The drawing is illustrative, not data from a study.

How wide is the wobble in real tasks? Hedge, Powell and Sumner asked people to do seven classic tasks twice, three weeks apart. The tasks were flanker, Stroop, stop-signal, go no-go, Posner cueing, Navon and SNARC. They measured test-retest reliability with the intraclass correlation. Across tasks and measures, it ranged from zero to point eight two. For the reaction-time measures of the Posner, SNARC and Navon tasks, the standard error of measurement was between thirteen and thirty-four milliseconds. A shift of a few milliseconds sits well inside that.

The least reliable scores were differences. The SNARC effect on reaction time had a reliability of point two two. The Navon global cost, a difference between two conditions, had a reliability of zero. Subtracting two noisy numbers leaves a noisier one. The authors give the reason: these tasks give robust group effects because people are alike, and that leaves little variation between people to measure. So where the question allows, look at the components and not only at the difference.

Now the runner gets better just by running. This is a practice effect: a score gain from memory of the items, learned strategies, or plain familiarity. Calamia, Markon and Tranel pooled nearly sixteen hundred effect sizes from neuropsychological tests. The size of the gain depended on the use of alternate forms, on age, on diagnosis, and on the time between tests. In a stimulation study, the change after a session mixes the stimulation with practice. Expectation adds to it: someone who believes the session helps may simply try harder. Only a control condition, tested the same way, separates them. Episodes nine point one and nine point two cover those controls.

Then there is the ceiling. If accuracy is already close to a hundred percent, there is no room left to improve, and a real benefit cannot show. The same is true of a floor. The task must be hard enough for the people tested, and that is something to check in a pilot.

So what does the pooled literature show? Horvath, Forte and Carter collected single-session tDCS studies in healthy adults, for every cognitive outcome reported by at least two research groups. In fifty-nine analyses, none showed a significant effect, including working memory and language production. Medina and Cason applied a p-curve analysis to samples of such studies and found no evidential value, with estimated power of about fourteen percent for the cognitive studies. Westwood and Romani pooled fourteen papers on picture naming and word reading and found no significant overall effect. Other meta-analyses were more mixed, so the verdict depends on the analysis. These results do not say that stimulation never works. They say that small effects, noisy tasks and small samples are hard to tell from chance. Power has its own episode, nine point six, and regression to the mean has episode nine point five.

The remedy starts before the data. Choose one primary outcome. Name the task, the measure, the time point and the way it is computed, and write it down before the first participant. Everything else is secondary, and is reported as secondary. Buch and colleagues, in a consensus paper on tDCS and motor learning, recommend stating whether a design is exploratory or confirmatory, and propose prospective pre-registration of hypothesis-driven studies.

Why does that matter? Scheel, Schijen and Lakens compared the standard psychology literature with Registered Reports, where the plan is accepted before the results. In standard reports, ninety-six percent of first hypotheses were supported. In Registered Reports, forty-four percent. The authors note this does not directly show that Registered Reports reduce bias. It does show how much room there is when the outcome is chosen after seeing the data.

To sum up. Behaviour is close to the question, and noisy. The wobble from one session to the next can be larger than the effect. Difference scores are the least reliable. Practice and expectation move the score without any stimulation, and a ceiling hides real gains. So pick the primary outcome in advance, keep a control, and report what you decided.

PLATE 10.27:37

MEPs as a readout

The motor evoked potential is a receipt printed by the muscle at the end of a long chain from cortex through spinal cord and nerve. One pulse gives a noisy receipt, so five to about thirty responses are averaged. Because spinal as well as cortical excitability shapes it, a change in MEP alone cannot be called a change in cortical excitability.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every telescope in this observatory sees the same noisy sky, and each one has its own noise. Today's telescope is the cheapest and the oldest: the motor evoked potential, the MEP. Episode 3.3 showed what it is and how the threshold is found. Today we use it as a measurement. How noisy is it? How many readings do you need? And what exactly does it measure?

Here is the picture for this episode. A company sends one order from head office, the motor cortex, down a chain. First a depot in the spinal cord, then a second depot, the motor neurons, then a delivery route, the nerve, and finally a shop, the muscle. The only thing you ever see is the receipt that the shop prints. That receipt is the MEP. This is a picture, not a model.

Now send the same order again. Same coil, same place, same intensity. The receipt is not the same. Already in 1993, Kiers and colleagues delivered thirty consecutive identical pulses at each intensity and found that the size of the response varied from one pulse to the next, with no regular rhythm. Their conclusion was that the variability is essentially random, and that it comes from constant, rapid fluctuations in the excitability of the pathway.

How big is the scatter? A useful measure is the coefficient of variation, the standard deviation divided by the mean. In a 2009 study of eight volunteers, Julkunen and colleagues recorded twenty consecutive responses from a thumb muscle. The coefficient of variation was about seventy-one percent with a navigated coil, and ninety-one percent without. That is a spread almost as large as the average itself. This figure is from a small study, and later work does not always find the same gap between navigated and hand-held coils. But the message is stable: one receipt tells you very little.

So you print many receipts, and you average. How many? Here the literature gives a range, not a number. In 2016, Goldsworthy and colleagues found that twenty to thirty trials gave a stable mean, and that going beyond thirty added nothing. The same year, Chang and colleagues, with fifty-four volunteers, reported a minimum of twenty-one pulses for the amplitude, and twenty-three for the latency. In 2018, Biabani and colleagues found twenty for corticospinal excitability. Other analyses are more permissive. A 2017 meta-analysis found that five pulses already gave excellent reliability within a single session, and ten between sessions, in healthy volunteers. In 2020, Ammann and colleagues explained why the answers differ. The number you need depends on how much error you accept, and on how variable that person is. So, depending on the study, from five to thirty. And it is wise to plan for about twenty or more when a small change must be detected.

What makes the receipts vary? Some causes are the state of the shop. A slight contraction of the muscle makes responses larger and steadier. In 2006, Darling and colleagues found that holding five or ten percent of maximum force lowered the variability. In 1993, Kiers and colleagues tested mental alertness too, with a simple arithmetic task, and found that variability shrinks as the intensity goes up. Some causes are in the coil. A few millimetres of position, or a few degrees of orientation, change the field in the cortex. That is episode 2.3 again. Neuronavigation helps keep the coil on the same spot, and in Julkunen's study it gave larger and more stable responses.

And some causes are in the head itself. In 2018, Zrenner and colleagues fired pulses at chosen phases of the sensorimotor mu rhythm. Pulses at the negative peak gave larger responses than pulses at the positive peak. Same order, same coil, different brain state. Episode 8.1 uses exactly this idea to time the stimulation.

Now the strong point. We said that the MEP is a receipt from the shop. A change in the receipt does not tell you which depot changed.

Look at what happens inside. A single pulse does not send one message down the spinal cord. It sends a short train of volleys. Di Lazzaro and colleagues recorded them directly in conscious patients, through electrodes placed on the spinal cord for pain relief. The earliest volley, the D wave, comes from the direct activation of axons. The later ones, the I waves, are made through synapses in the cortex, and they follow each other about one and a half milliseconds apart. With a focal coil and a low-intensity pulse that sends current from back to front, the first volley is mostly a single I wave, the I-one. So the cortex does not send one order. It sends a rapid burst of them.

Then the motor neurons in the spinal cord sum up what arrives, and decide how much of it to pass on. That decision depends on their own state. Which is why a slight contraction changes the MEP so much. In a 2013 review, McNeil, Butler, Taylor and Gandevia put it plainly. The MEP is affected by both cortical and spinal excitability, and cannot measure either in isolation. They advise caution when reading changes in MEP size as changes in cortical excitability.

So how do you separate the depots? You test the spinal depot directly. One way is the H-reflex, where a nerve is stimulated electrically and the signal goes through the spinal cord without passing the cortex. Another is the F-wave, which is also spinal, but in one experiment was about ten times less sensitive. The most direct method is the cervicomedullary motor evoked potential. A stimulus is given at the top of the spinal cord, and it bypasses the cortex. It is painful and not easy to obtain, but it is, in the authors' words, the most direct way to test motor neuron excitability in conscious humans. Then you can compare. If the MEP changes and the spinal response changes in the same way, the change may be in the spinal cord. If only the MEP changes, the cortex becomes a stronger suspect, but still not the only one. Kiers found that the variability of the MEP is larger than the variability of the H-reflex, and is not correlated with it. Both depots fluctuate, on their own.

The classic trap is the sentence, we increased cortical excitability, written after a single MEP result. What was measured was the shop's receipt. Whether the stimulation worked on the right brain region, and for which participants, is another question. Episode 9.5 looks at responders, and episode 10.5 at what we can really infer.

To sum up. A single MEP is a noisy receipt, with a coefficient of variation that can approach the mean. Depending on the study, you need from five to about thirty pulses, and twenty is a common minimum. Contraction, alertness, coil position and brain state all move it. And it measures the whole chain, from cortex to muscle, so a change in MEP alone does not place the change in the cortex.

Next: the EEG readouts. A telescope that looks at the cortex directly, and its own artefacts.

PLATE 10.36:59

EEG readouts

EEG reads stimulation through three lenses: the TMS-evoked potential, oscillatory power before and after, and event-related potentials. Before-and-after designs are clean but need a sham group, because alpha drifts alone. During stimulation the artefact is as large as the signal, shares its frequency and is modulated by heartbeat, like a radiator humming the room's own note.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the observatory. Different telescopes, pointed at the same noisy sky. Stimulation changes something in the brain, and each readout sees only a slice of it. This episode is about EEG as a readout. Three lenses, and then one problem that waits for all of them when the stimulator is switched on.

First, a picture to carry through the episode. A piano tuner stands in a small room. She is listening for the room's own note, an A. In the corner stands a radiator, and it hums. Its hum is also an A. The note is the brain rhythm. The radiator is the stimulator. Keep the room in mind.

The first lens is a knock on the wall. That is the TMS-evoked potential, or TEP, from episode three point seven. One pulse, then a few hundred milliseconds of EEG. We will not replay the parade of artefacts. Keep only the rule: the later peaks are the ones to doubt, because the click and the touch evoke responses of their own. A sensory control is the condition for calling a peak cortical. The full processing chain is in episode B point two.

The second lens is the room's own hum, before and after. Record a few minutes of resting EEG. Stimulate. Record again. The measure is oscillatory power, most often alpha.

In 2010, Zaehle and colleagues applied alpha-frequency tACS over the back of the head. The current averaged about one point one milliamps, for ten minutes, during a detection task. Ten volunteers received it, and ten more received sham. Alpha power rose by fourteen percent from before to after, and the sham group showed no significant change. The recordings were three minutes each.

How long does it last? Depending on the study. In 2016, Kasten and colleagues stimulated for twenty minutes, and kept recording for ninety. Alpha stayed above baseline. The gap with the sham group shrank after about seventy minutes, but mostly because alpha in the sham group rose by itself, not because the stimulated group came back down. This is why a sham group is not optional. Alpha drifts, even when nothing is applied.

Is it entrainment, still ringing? In 2015, Vossen and colleagues tested this in twelve volunteers. The after-effect did not depend on whether the phase continued between short trains, and it showed no lasting phase alignment. They concluded that plastic changes are enough to explain it, and that it is not an echo. And people differ. In 2019, Kasten and colleagues found that the size of the after-effect in each person was significantly predicted by measures from a model of the electric field in that person's head. Episode seven point one showed how such models are built.

The third lens is a doorbell. Present the same stimulus many times, average the EEG, and a stable waveform appears: an event-related potential, or ERP. After stimulation, ask whether its amplitude or its timing changed.

In 2022, Mendes and colleagues pooled the studies on tDCS and one component, the P-three. They found thirty-six studies, and twenty-three entered the quantitative analysis. Parietal P-three amplitude rose after anodal stimulation, in oddball and in n-back tasks. The standardised differences ran from about zero point four to zero point seven, depending on the site and the task. The authors judged the number of studies small and the risk of bias considerable. So the P-three is a promising marker, not yet an established one.

The doorbell has its own conditions. The same stimuli, the same task, the same state of the person, before and after. Practice and fatigue move ERPs too, as episode ten point one covers for behaviour.

Now the hard part. Measuring before and after is easy, because the radiator is off. Measuring during is not. Here is the trap.

With tDCS, the problem is large and slow. In 2019, Gebodh and colleagues recorded EEG during tDCS in five volunteers, at up to two milliamps. The steady offset was up to about a thousand times larger than the neural signal. And the heartbeat produced a rhythmic artefact of about forty microvolts near the electrodes, larger than most event-related potentials.

With tACS, the artefact is a sine wave, at the very frequency of the rhythm you are listening for. You might hope it is a clean copy of the current, and that it can be subtracted. It is not. In 2016, Noury, Hipp and Siegel recorded EEG and MEG during tACS and tDCS. They showed that heartbeat and breathing modulate the artefact in a non-linear way. Standard clean-up leaves a residue, and a residue can be mistaken for entrainment. In 2017, Noury and Siegel added that the artefacts have steady phase offsets, and that at common amplitudes the phase deflections can be several times larger than normal brain signals.

In 2014, Helfrich and colleagues built a method to separate artefact from brain activity, and reported more alpha during stimulation. Cleaning is possible in principle, and it is hard in practice. One study of tDCS even cautioned against simple frequency filters, because the artefact overlaps the neural bands. That is why many studies report power changes only after the current has stopped.

Back to the room. Before and after means switching the radiator off and listening again. That is clean. But it says nothing about what the room did while the radiator was on. During, the tuner hears the hum and the room's note together, at the same pitch, and the hum wobbles with her own heartbeat and breath. A claim about entrainment during stimulation needs an analysis shown to separate the two. A claim about an after-effect does not.

The preprocessing itself, filtering, artefact rejection and ICA, belongs to the EEG series. Its steps are the starting point here. Whether they are enough with the current on is the question.

To sum up. EEG gives three lenses: the TEP, the oscillations before and after, and the event-related potentials of a task. Before and after is the simpler design, and it needs a sham group. During stimulation, the artefact is as large as, or larger than, the signal, it has the same frequency as the effect, and it is modulated by the body. Methods exist to separate the two, and each one has to be shown to work on the data at hand.

PLATE 10.47:44

Imaging readouts

MRI sees the whole brain: with a coil in the scanner, effects appear far from the target, but sound, touch and twitches write remote signals too. Results for tDCS inside the scanner are small and rarely replicated, and spectroscopy estimates a pool of GABA before and after, not its release. Every reading is blood or a chemical pool, not firing.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The third telescope looks at the whole brain at once. Behaviour, motor evoked potentials and EEG each see one narrow window. Magnetic resonance imaging sees the entire head, and it can be pointed at the same noisy sky while the stimulator is on, or before and after. This episode covers three ways to use it, and what each one can honestly say.

First, a picture to carry through the episode. A village sits in a valley, and a camera on the hill looks down at it. The camera cannot see inside the houses. It sees chimney smoke, and it can count the logs in each barn. Stimulation is a bell rung in one house. The questions are who else reacts, and what the camera is really seeing.

The first lens is a coil inside the scanner. In nineteen ninety-eight, Bohning and colleagues showed that it can be done. Three volunteers lay in a one point five tesla scanner. A coil made without magnetic metal stimulated the thumb area of the motor cortex, and the images were collected in turns with the pulses. Activity appeared under the coil. It also appeared in the auditory cortex, most likely because of the loud snap of the coil. Keep that second finding in mind.

A year later, the same group stimulated seven adults at one hertz, at one hundred and ten percent and at eighty percent of motor threshold. Stronger pulses produced more activation, both under the coil and elsewhere in the brain. The signal change was about three percent, similar to a motor task. But the coil itself disturbed the images and lowered their quality. The camera does not like the bell.

That is the first constraint. Everything that enters the scanner must be built for it. Even then, small leakage currents in the stimulator can write a pattern on the images. In two thousand and nine, Weiskopf and colleagues measured this with a phantom. At realistic distances between coil and cortex, the artefact exceeded one percent, which is the size of the effects people look for. A relay and a diode in the circuit removed it. The authors recommend testing for it in every experiment.

Now the key moment. The camera sees the whole village, so it can see who reacts to the bell. In two thousand and six, Ruff and colleagues stimulated the right frontal eye field while imaging visual areas one to four. Activity rose for the outer part of the visual field, and fell for the central part. A control site, at the vertex, did not do this. The authors also confirmed a matching change in perceived contrast. The coil was at the front of the head, and the effect appeared at the back. A map drawn only under the coil would have missed it.

But a remote change is not automatically a network effect. In two thousand and three, Bestmann and colleagues stimulated the motor cortex at four hertz, in eight volunteers. When the pulses were strong enough to move the hand, the signal was dominated by the brain processing that movement. At lower intensity, with no movement, some volunteers still showed increases in premotor areas, and a decrease in the opposite motor cortex. Sound, touch and twitches write signals of their own. The village hears the bell, and sees a neighbour's door slam.

The second lens is direct current inside the scanner. Here the stimulator makes no click and no twitch, which is an advantage. In two thousand and eleven, Antal and colleagues stimulated the motor cortex at one milliamp, in blocks of twenty seconds, during fMRI at three tesla. At rest, neither anodal nor cathodal stimulation produced a detectable change in the BOLD signal. During finger tapping, anodal stimulation lowered the BOLD response in the supplementary motor area, compared with tapping alone. Whether a silent result means no effect, or an effect too small for this camera, is open. The field at the target may also be weak, as episodes four point five and seven point two showed.

How solid is this field? In two thousand and twenty, Esmaeilpour and colleagues reviewed a hundred and eighteen tDCS studies that used fMRI, up to October two thousand and eighteen. The studies differed in timing, design, control condition, dose and imaging sequence, and few results were replicated. The review also stresses two practical points: equipment certified as safe inside the scanner, and correction for the image artefacts that the current can cause.

Many designs simply scan before and after. Polania and colleagues did so in fourteen volunteers. They took resting scans just before and after ten minutes of tDCS, with a sham session as control. After anodal stimulation of the motor cortex, coupling between the left thalamus and the motor cortex increased, and so did coupling between the left caudate nucleus and parietal association areas. After cathodal stimulation, coupling between the motor cortex and the opposite putamen decreased. These are changes in how the blood signals of regions rise and fall together, and they sit far from the electrodes.

The third lens does not make a picture. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy estimates the concentration of chemicals in one block of tissue. In two thousand and nine, Stagg and colleagues measured a cube, two centimetres on each side, over the left motor cortex of eleven volunteers, at three tesla. They scanned before and after ten minutes of one milliamp tDCS, with a sham session for comparison. After anodal stimulation, GABA, the main inhibitory transmitter, fell by about nine percent. After cathodal stimulation, the combined glutamate and glutamine signal fell by about nineteen percent, and GABA by about eleven. These scans were taken before and after, not during.

What does that number mean? The authors describe it as the total, MR-visible GABA in the tissue. It is not synaptic transmission. It is the count of logs in the barn, not the logs burning this second. A later study makes the point. In two thousand and eleven, Stagg and colleagues compared MRS GABA with TMS measures of synaptic GABA activity, in two experiments. They found no clear relationship. The correlations were point three three in the first, and minus point two three in the second.

Now the bridge to the fMRI series. Two of these lenses read blood, not neurons. In two thousand and one, Logothetis and colleagues recorded neural signals and fMRI together in the visual cortex of monkeys. The BOLD response followed the local field potentials better than the spikes. It reflects the input and local processing of an area, more than its output. So there are two distances. First, the stimulus is one step from the neuron: a field must become a change in firing. Then the camera is another step away: firing becomes blood. Smoke is not fire. Near-infrared spectroscopy reads blood oxygen too, and it has also been recorded during tDCS.

To sum up. Concurrent imaging can show effects far from the coil, in the network. But sound, touch and movement can write remote signals of their own, so controls matter. Hardware must be built for the scanner, and its artefacts must be tested. For tDCS inside the scanner, results so far are small, varied and rarely replicated. Spectroscopy estimates a pool of chemicals before and after, not their release. And every reading is blood or a pool, not firing.

PLATE 10.56:04

What can we really infer?

A changed measure after stimulation can carry four claims, arranged as a ladder: the stimulation changed the measure, it acted through the targeted region, the region is necessary, and the mechanism is LTP-like plasticity. Each rung needs its own controls, from sham to pharmacology. A null result informs only if the dose arrived, and the claim should match the rung reached.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In this chapter we have pointed different telescopes at the same noisy sky. Behaviour, motor evoked potentials, EEG, imaging. Each one gives a number. Now the number has arrived, and it moved. What can we really say about it?

Here is a result that looks good. After a stimulation session, the measure changed. The title of the paper is ready. It says that stimulating a region improves a function through plasticity. That one sentence hides four different claims. Each one is a rung on a ladder, and each rung needs its own kind of evidence. You may stand on a rung only if you have the controls to hold it.

Rung one: the stimulation changed the measure. This is the lowest rung, and the most solid. To hold it, you compare real stimulation with a sham, as in episode nine point one, and you decide the main measure before you look at the data. You also need enough participants. An average change can hide very different people, which is the subject of episode nine point five. But if the controls are good, rung one says something simple. Something happened, and it was not only the passing of time or the expectation of the participant.

Rung two: it acted through the targeted region. This is a different claim, and a sham cannot support it. A sham tells you that the stimulation did something. It does not tell you where. A pulse of TMS also makes a click, and a tickle on the scalp. Both can change what a participant does, with no help from the cortex underneath. So you add an active control, as in episode nine point two: the same stimulation at another site, or a stimulation of another kind, with the same sounds and the same sensations. And you ask a second question. Did the field really reach the target? That needs the field modelling of chapter seven. A coil is placed over a region, and the field may still be weak there, or strong next door.

Rung three: that region is necessary for the function. This is the logic of the virtual lesion. Pascual-Leone and colleagues described it in nineteen ninety-nine. You disturb a region for a short time. If the task gets worse, the region contributes to the task. If a neighbouring task is spared, the effect is specific. The idea is powerful, and it has limits.

First, a virtual lesion is not a lesion. The stimulation does not delete neurons. It adds activity, and that activity can be noisy. Second, a region is part of a network. Siebner and colleagues pointed out that a change in performance can come from a distant region connected to the stimulated one. Third, the network can compensate. In a study by Hartwigsen and colleagues in twenty thirteen, theta burst stimulation over the left inferior frontal gyrus reduced activity there, while the right homologous area took over. The stronger the influence of the right region on the left, the faster the responses were. So a small effect may not mean a small role. The network may simply have covered for the lesion.

Now a trap. Suppose nothing changed. Does that prove the region is not involved? No. If the field in that region was weak, the stimulation may simply not have reached the neurons. Remember episode four point five: how much actually reaches the cortex is a real question. And episode seven point two: in simulations of fifty heads, the field at the same target differed by more than one hundred percent across people. A null result is only informative when you know the dose that arrived.

Rung four: the mechanism is LTP-like plasticity. This is the highest rung, and the easiest to overstate. Long-term potentiation, or LTP, is a lasting strengthening of a synapse. In a brain slice, you can record it from a single synapse, and block it with a drug. In a person, we cannot see a synapse. We record a motor evoked potential, which is the whole pathway down to the muscle, as in episode ten point two. So when a paper says LTP-like, it means a resemblance. The effect outlasts the stimulation. And in pharmacological studies, it depends on certain receptors, the same receptors that LTP depends on in slices.

Two classic examples. In two thousand and seven, Huang and colleagues gave memantine, a drug that blocks NMDA receptors, to six healthy volunteers. It blocked both the drop in motor evoked potentials after continuous theta burst, and the rise after intermittent theta burst. In two thousand and three, Nitsche and colleagues did the same kind of experiment with tDCS. Dextromethorphan, another NMDA blocker, prevented the lasting after-effects of both anodal and cathodal stimulation. Carbamazepine, which acts on sodium channels, removed only the increase from anodal stimulation.

These are beautiful experiments. But read them carefully. A drug given to the whole body acts everywhere in the brain, not only under the coil. The readout is a muscle response, not a synapse. And the memantine study was small. The pattern fits LTP. It does not show that LTP happened. Calling it LTP-like is the honest name for what was measured.

So here is the ladder. One: it changed the measure. This needs a sham, a fixed outcome and enough participants. Two: it acted through the target. This needs an active control, and a field that reached the region. Three: the region is necessary. This needs a disturbed region, a spared task, and a thought for the network and its compensation. Four: the mechanism is plasticity of LTP type. This needs pharmacology, more than one readout, and, for real LTP, work at the level of synapses. Climb as high as your controls allow. Then write the sentence that matches the rung you reached.

Next: Zap and zip. We hit the cortex with a pulse, record the answer, and compress it like a file.

PLATE 10.68:46

Zap and zip: perturbational complexity

A TMS pulse can probe whether anyone is home without asking the patient to respond. Like one rocket photographed against the sky, a rich, differentiated EEG response compresses poorly and a stereotyped or silent one compresses well. The perturbational complexity index, built on Lempel-Ziv compression, separated conscious from unconscious conditions at 0.31 in one benchmark and finds signs behaviour misses, but remains a research measure.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every telescope in this chapter watches what stimulation changes. This one does something different. It uses stimulation as a probe, to ask a very hard question about a person who cannot answer. Is anyone home?

The usual answer comes from the bedside. A clinician asks the patient to do something, and watches. The Coma Recovery Scale, Revised, or CRS-R, does this carefully: twenty-three items, in six groups, from hearing and vision to movement, speech, communication and arousal. But every item needs a body that can answer. In one study of one hundred and three patients, eighteen of the forty-four labelled vegetative by bedside consensus showed signs of a minimally conscious state on the CRS-R. So the question is whether the brain's own response can say something, without asking it to do anything.

Here is the idea in one picture. Fire a single rocket from one spot, and photograph the sky. If the sky answers with a rich show, bursts of different colours, in different places, at different moments, the show is hard to describe in a few words. If the sky answers with one big flash, or with a flash that never leaves its corner, a few words are enough. Now replace the rocket with a pulse of TMS, and the sky with the cortex, recorded by EEG. Strike, record, then compress the recording like a file. A rich, differentiated response compresses poorly. A stereotyped or silent one compresses well.

The first hint came in 2005. Massimini and colleagues stimulated the premotor cortex of healthy volunteers, with high-density EEG. Awake, the pulse started a short response that travelled, in waves, to connected regions several centimetres away. In deep non-REM sleep, the first response at the stimulated spot was larger, but it faded fast and did not spread. The cortex still reacted. The authors described a breakdown in effective connectivity.

How do you put a number on that? With compression. In 1976, Abraham Lempel and Jacob Ziv defined the complexity of a sequence by counting how many new patterns it takes to write it. A sequence that repeats is quick to describe. A sequence that keeps surprising you takes many patterns. The everyday tools that zip files descend from this idea, and the Information series gives it its own episode. Here only the bridge matters.

Look at two simulated responses, each a grid of lit and dark cells, channels by time. In the first, every channel lights up in the same slow block. In the second, different parts light at different moments. A Lempel-Ziv count finds seventeen new patterns in the first grid, and fifty-six in the second. Zipped, the first takes fifty-eight bytes, and the second takes a hundred and thirty-three. The second grid has fewer lit cells, and it is still harder to compress. That is the whole point: what counts is the variety, not the amount of light.

In 2013, Casali and colleagues turned this into the perturbational complexity index, or PCI. First, stimulate and record. Second, estimate the activity on the cortex from the EEG. Third, keep only the activity that differs from the baseline by statistics, and write it as lit or dark. Fourth, count the Lempel-Ziv patterns, and divide by the entropy of the lit-versus-dark proportion, so that the score reflects variety and not how much is lit. The index runs from zero to one. If almost nothing survives the statistics, it is set to zero.

To validate it, you need people who can say whether they experienced something. In 2016, Casarotto and colleagues built a benchmark: a hundred and fifty people, a hundred and two healthy and forty-eight conscious patients with brain injury, and five hundred and forty sets of recordings. Unconscious meant no report after waking: deep sleep, midazolam, xenon, propofol. Conscious meant a report, at once or after waking: wakefulness, dreaming in REM sleep, ketamine anaesthesia, locked-in syndrome, stroke, and recovery from a minimally conscious state. Each person's highest PCI was kept. A cutoff of 0.31 separated the two groups, with a sensitivity and a specificity of one hundred percent, in that benchmark. Awake volunteers ranged from 0.39 to 0.70. Dreaming and ketamine, reported after waking, ranged from 0.36 to 0.56. Unconscious conditions reached at most 0.31.

One result matters here. In 2015, Sarasso and colleagues recorded eighteen volunteers under propofol, xenon and ketamine. Under propofol and xenon, the response had low complexity, and nobody reported an experience afterwards. Under ketamine, the response was complex, and volunteers described long, vivid dreams. All were unresponsive. The complexity followed the experience, not the behaviour.

Now the patients. Casarotto and colleagues applied the cutoff to eighty-one patients. Of thirty-eight in a minimally conscious state, thirty-six were above it. Of forty-three with unresponsive wakefulness, nine were above it, twenty-one were below it, and thirteen gave no response at all. The authors read the nine as possibly having a capacity for consciousness that behaviour does not show. That is an interpretation, not a proven result. They also state that the index is not a prognostic marker.

Others have looked since. In 2017, Bodart and colleagues compared the index with brain metabolism, measured with FDG-PET, in twenty-four patients. The two agreed in twenty-two. The CRS-R agreed with the index in nineteen. Four patients who gave no behavioural sign had both preserved metabolism and a high index. In 2020, Sinitsyn and colleagues used the same cutoff in twenty-four patients, and found four high-complexity patients among eleven with unresponsive wakefulness. The samples are small. In 2019, Comolatti and colleagues proposed PCIst, which skips the source modelling and computes in under a second, with accuracy similar to the original on data from 108 healthy people and 108 patients.

The limits are real. First, it needs TMS-EEG with expert care. The artefacts of episode 3.7 and the pipeline of episode B.2 decide whether the pattern is cortical, or just a click and a twitch. Second, the site matters. In the 2020 study, lesions, fatigue and agitation limited stimulation to one target in twelve patients, which may underestimate the score. Third, the original authors state that absolute values depend on the protocol. The cutoff was chosen on the same benchmark it was scored on, and subjective reports are an imperfect ground truth. Fourth, the index measures a level of consciousness, not its content. A high value does not say what a person experiences.

What about status and guidance? As of November 2026, no regulatory clearance was found for the index as a diagnostic device. One manufacturer states that its TMS-EEG application for disorders of consciousness is for research use only. Guidance is a separate matter. The 2020 guideline of the European Academy of Neurology suggests that TMS-EEG should be considered, as part of a multimodal assessment, to separate unresponsive wakefulness from a minimally conscious state. Its own rating is low evidence, weak recommendation. The same guideline strongly recommends repeated bedside assessment, preferably with the CRS-R. The two tools are not rivals. One asks the patient to respond. The other does not.

To sum up. Strike the cortex with TMS, record the response, and compress it. A rich, differentiated response scores high. A stereotyped or silent one scores low. In a benchmark of a hundred and fifty people, a cutoff of 0.31 separated conscious from unconscious conditions. In patients, the index finds signs that behaviour misses, and it is still a research measure, with limits.
