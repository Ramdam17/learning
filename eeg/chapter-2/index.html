Sinusoids · Amplitude, frequency, phase

A sinusoid is fully described by three numbers: amplitude, the height of the wave in microvolts; frequency, the cycles per second in hertz; and phase, the position in the cycle as an angle. The playground swing carries the idea. A phase difference is a time lag only once the frequency is known, and phase wraps around the circle.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every EEG analysis in this series from spectra to connectivity rests on one humble shape, the sinusoid. Before complex numbers, before Fourier, let's make sure we can read it fluently. A sinusoid is fully described by just three numbers — amplitude, frequency, and phase.

Picture a playground swing, how high it goes, how many times it swings each second, and the moment you gave it the first push. That is amplitude, frequency, and phase.

Amplitude first. It is the height of the wave, measured from the middle line up to a peak. In EEG, amplitudes are measured in microvolts, millionths of a volt, and scalp rhythms are typically in the range of tens of microvolts. Double the amplitude and the swing goes twice as high. The rhythm keeps exactly the same timing.

Frequency next. It counts how many full cycles happen in one second, in hertz. Its partner is the period, the time one cycle takes. Period equals one over frequency. An alpha rhythm at 10 hertz completes a cycle every 100 milliseconds. A slow delta wave at one hertz takes a whole second for each cycle.

Then phase, where the wave is in its cycle, at a given moment. Phase is an angle. One full cycle is 360 degrees, or 2 pi radians. Picture a clock hand that goes around once per cycle. At 0 degrees the wave is at its peak. At 90 degrees it is crossing the middle line on its way down. At 180 it sits in the trough.

Put the three together and you get the formula of every sinusoid. X of t equals A times the cosine of 2 pi f t plus phi. A is the amplitude, f the frequency, phi the starting phase. The 2π simply converts cycles into radians.

Now two swings side by side. Same height, same rhythm, but one started a little later. They never quite meet. The only difference between them is the phase. And a phase difference is really a time difference in disguise.

Here is the conversion. The time lag equals the phase difference divided by 2πf. So the same angle means a different delay at different frequencies. 90 degrees at 10 hertz is a lag of 25 milliseconds. 90 degrees at 2 hertz is a lag of 125 milliseconds. Keep this in mind when you read connectivity results. A phase lag only becomes a time lag once you know the frequency.

There is also a catch. Phase goes around in a circle. A wave that is 90 degrees ahead is also 270 degrees behind. From phase alone, you cannot always tell who leads and who follows.

Finally, add sinusoids together with different amplitudes, frequencies, and phases, and you can build shapes that look nothing like a swing—sharp peaks, slow drifts, bursts. That is exactly what EEG looks like. Taking such a signal apart into its sinusoids is the job of the Fourier transform in Chapter 3.

Let's recap. A sinusoid is described by three numbers. Amplitude is how high in microvolts. Frequency is how often in hertz, and the period is one over the frequency. Phase is where in the cycle as an angle. A phase difference becomes a time lag once you divide by 2 pi f, and it wraps around the circle.

Next time we give the sinusoid a second dimension and meet complex numbers without fear.

PLATE 2.25:40

Complex numbers without fear · The spinning arrow

An oscillation is the shadow of a spinning arrow, like a cabin on a ferris wheel, since one number alone cannot say whether the wave is rising or falling. The arrow is a complex number: real and imaginary parts, or a length and an angle, meaning amplitude and phase. Euler's formula packs this together, multiplying rotates, and the conjugate yields phase differences.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Fourier coefficients, Hilbert phase, imaginary coherence, the weighted phase lag index. Half of EEG analysis speaks the language of complex numbers. Today we learn that language with one single picture — a spinning arrow.

Let's start with a problem. Here is a sine wave and right now its value is one-half. Is it on its way up or on its way down? From one number alone we cannot tell. Two very different moments of the cycle share the same value. To know exactly where we are in a rhythm, one number is not enough. We need two.

So let's give the oscillation a second dimension. Picture a ferris wheel turning at steady speed. Follow one cabin. The sun is straight above, so the cabin's shadow slides back and forth along the ground. A lamp on the side throws a second shadow onto a wall, sliding up and down.

Now trace each shadow over time. The ground shadow draws a cosine. The wall shadow draws a sine. A smooth oscillation is nothing more than the shadow of something turning in a circle. And with both shadows together, the ambiguity is gone. Up or down, we always know where the cabin is.

Let's turn the wheel into mathematics. Draw two axes. The horizontal axis is called the real axis. The vertical axis is called the imaginary axis. Our cabin is now the tip of an arrow, starting at the center. We can describe that arrow with a single number, written x plus i times y. The x is its real part, the horizontal shadow. The y is its imaginary part, the vertical shadow.

A word about that name. René Descartes coined the word imaginary in 1637, and he meant it as an insult. It stuck anyway. But there is nothing unreal about the imaginary part. It is simply the second axis, the wall shadow.

So what is i? By definition, i squared equals minus one. That sounds strange until you see it geometrically. Multiplying by i rotates an arrow by a quarter turn counterclockwise. Start at one, multiply by i and you point straight up. Multiply by i again and you point at minus one. Two quarter turns make a half turn. That is all i squared equals minus one really says.

There is a second way to describe the same arrow. Instead of its two shadows, give its length and its angle. The length is called the modulus or magnitude. In EEG it becomes the amplitude. The angle is called the argument. In EEG it becomes the phase. Same arrow, two descriptions. Shadows on one side, length and angle on the other.

Now for the most famous formula in this series. Euler's formula published in 1748 says that e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta. Read it as a picture. e to the i theta is simply an arrow of length 1 pointing at angle theta. Its real part is the cosine. Its imaginary part is the sine. The two shadows in one line.

Now let the angle grow with time. Make it 2 pi times f times t plus a starting angle phi. The arrow spins f full turns every second, starting from phi. Scale it by a length r. This is a complex sinusoid. It holds three things at once, an amplitude, a frequency, and a phase. Take its real part and you get the familiar wave r times cosine of 2 pi f t plus phi.

Here is why this picture is so convenient. When you multiply two complex numbers, their lengths multiply and their angles add. So multiplying by an arrow of length 1 is a pure rotation. A phase shift is just a rotation. No trigonometric identities needed.

One more tool, the complex conjugate. It flips the arrow upside down, mirroring it across the real axis. The angle changes sign. Now multiply one arrow by the conjugate of another. The angles subtract. The result points at the phase difference between the two. Remember this move. It is the heart of the cross-spectrum of coherence and of every phase synchrony measure in Chapter 10.

And what about a real signal like EEG with no imaginary part at all? A cosine is actually two arrows spinning in opposite directions, mirror images of each other. Their vertical shadows cancel out. Their horizontal shadows add up. That is why the spectrum of a real signal has a mirror image with negative frequencies. And that is exactly what the Hilbert transform will exploit. It keeps only one of the two arrows.

Let's look ahead. In Fourier analysis, every frequency gets its own arrow, its length is the amplitude, its angle is the phase. With the Hilbert transform, every moment in time gets an arrow. And in connectivity, two electrodes that share a source line up at zero lag, so their phase difference arrow lies flat on the real axis. Imaginary coherence and the weighted phase lag index look at the vertical part only, to step aside from that trap.

Let's recap. An oscillation is the shadow of a spinning arrow. The real part is the horizontal shadow, the imaginary part the vertical one. Length means amplitude, angle means phase. Euler's formula packs all of it into a single expression. Multiplying rotates and the conjugate gives phase differences.

Next time we measure how much two signals resemble each other with one of the simplest ideas in all of signal processing, the dot product.

PLATE 2.33:53

The dot product · Measuring resemblance

The dot product multiplies two signals sample by sample and adds the products, a vote on whether they agree: positive when alike, zero when orthogonal, negative when opposite. Geometrically it equals the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between them. Normalizing it gives the correlation, and the same question, how much a signal resembles a template, sits behind Fourier, wavelets and filters.

TRANSCRIPTEN

How much does one signal look like another? It sounds like a vague question, but signal processing answers it with one precise operation. It is hidden inside Fourier transforms, filters, wavelets, spatial filters, and regressions. Today we meet the dot product.

The recipe has two steps. Take two signals of the same length. Multiply them sample by sample. Then add up all the products. The result is a single number, the dot product.

Think of it as a vote. At each moment the two signals vote. If both are up or both are down, the product is positive, they agree. If one is up while the other is down, the product is negative, they disagree. If one of them sits near zero, its vote barely counts. The dot product is the final tally.

Let's try it with a 10 hertz wave as our template. Compare it with itself, every moment agrees and the tally is large and positive. Flip the template upside down, every moment disagrees and the tally is large and negative. Now compare a cosine with a sine of the same frequency over whole cycles. Half the time they agree, half the time they disagree and the votes cancel exactly. The dot product is zero. We say the two signals are orthogonal.

Orthogonal is a word from geometry and the geometry is worth seeing. Imagine a signal with just two samples. It is a point in a plane or an arrow from the origin. A signal with a thousand samples is an arrow in a space with a thousand axes, one per sample. We cannot draw that space but the rules stay the same. The dot product equals the length of the first arrow times the length of the second times the cosine of the angle between them.

So arrows that point the same way give a large positive value. Arrows at right angles give zero. Arrows pointing in opposite directions give a negative value. Resemblance becomes an angle.

There is a catch, the raw dot product also grows with amplitude. A loud signal gives a big number even if the resemblance is mediocre. To keep only the resemblance, divide by the two lengths. What remains is the cosine of the angle, a number between minus 1 and plus 1. Subtract each signal's mean first, and this normalized dot product is exactly the Pearson correlation.

Another way to read it, projection. If the template has length 1, the dot product tells you how much of that template is present in the signal, like the shadow of one arrow on another. This is the shadow idea again, from episode 2.2.

Now look how often this one operation appears. A Fourier coefficient is the dot product of the signal with a cosine for the real part and with a sine for the imaginary part with a minus sign. In one go, the dot product with a spinning complex arrow. That is the winding of episode 3.2. A wavelet coefficient is a dot product with a short wave packet. Filtering, as we will see next time is a dot product with a small template, repeated at every position. And a spatial filter, like an independent component or a beam former, is a dot product across electrodes instead of across time.

Let's recap. Multiply sample by sample then add, that is the dot product. Agreement votes positive, disagreement votes negative. Zero means orthogonal. Geometrically, it is lengths times the cosine of an angle. Normalize it and after removing the means, you get the correlation. And behind Fourier, wavelets, filters and spatial filters, it is always the same question. How much does this signal resemble that template?

Next time we slide the template along the signal and the dot product becomes convolution.

PLATE 2.43:48

Convolution · Sliding and summing

Convolution slides a small template, the kernel, along a signal and takes a dot product at every position, like a stamp moving over a conveyor belt. A flat kernel smooths, an oscillating kernel detects a rhythm, and the kernel is the impulse response that fully describes a linear filter. Convolution in time equals multiplication in frequency, and edges need care.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the dot product measured how much a signal resembles a template. Today we make that template move. Slide it along the signal, take a dot product at every position, and you have the operation behind every filter in EEG analysis. Convolution.

Picture a conveyor belt. The signal rides along the belt, sample after sample. Above it hangs a small stamp, the kernel a short list of weights. At each step, the stamp presses down on the few samples beneath it, multiplies, adds up, and prints one number onto a second belt. That second belt is the output.

One technical detail, in a true convolution, the kernel is flipped in time before it slides. Without the flip, the operation is called cross-correlation. For symmetric kernels, like most of the ones we use, the two are identical.

Let's try a first kernel, five equal weights of one-fifth. At each position, the output is simply the average of five neighboring samples. Fast wiggles get averaged away. Slow waves pass through almost untouched. A moving average is a smoothing filter, a low pass filter.

Now a second kernel, a short burst of a 10 hertz wave. At each position it asks, is there a 10 hertz rhythm right here? Where alpha is present, the output swings strongly. Where it is absent, the output stays near zero. The kernel has become a detector, a bandpass filter. This is exactly how wavelets work in chapter three.

Here is a neat test. Feed the belt a single spike, one sample at one, everything else at zero. What comes out? The kernel itself laid out in time. That is why the kernel is called the impulse response. A linear filter is completely described by its kernel. Show me the kernel and I know the filter.

Watch the ends of the belt. At the very beginning, the stamp hangs partly over empty space. Something has to fill the gap, zeros, mirrored data, or repeated values. Whatever we choose, the first and last outputs are less trustworthy. These are edge effects and the longer the kernel, the wider they get.

And now the most useful fact of this episode, the convolution theorem. Convolving two signals in time is the same as multiplying their spectra in frequency. The spectrum of the kernel is the frequency response of the filter. Our moving average has a spectrum that is high at low frequencies and low at high frequencies, so it multiplies the slow parts of the signal by nearly 1 and the fast parts by nearly 0. It also explains a practical trick. Long convolutions are often computed through the fast Fourier transform because multiplying spectra is much quicker.

Where do you meet convolution in EEG? Every finite impulse response filter, the FIR filters of chapter 7, is a convolution with a kernel. The wavelet transform is a convolution with wavelets, and even your data can be convolved by accident. If a response arrives with a slightly different latency on every trial, the average is the true response convolved with the spread of latencies, a lower, wider peak. We will meet that blur again with event-related potentials.

Let's recap. Convolution slides a kernel along the signal and takes a dot product at every position. A flat kernel smooths, an oscillating kernel detects a rhythm. The kernel is the impulse response and it fully describes a linear filter. Edges need care. And convolution in time is multiplication in frequency.

Next time we look at what happens when a continuous signal becomes a list of samples. Sampling, Nyquist, and aliasing.

PLATE 2.53:00

Sampling, Nyquist and aliasing

Sampling turns a continuous voltage into a list of numbers, and the highest frequency it can represent is half the sampling rate, the Nyquist frequency. A faster rhythm folds back as a slower fake one, like a wagon wheel that seems to turn backwards on film. Aliasing cannot be undone, so filtering must come before sampling and before downsampling.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The voltage on the scalp changes continuously. Your computer cannot store a continuous curve. It stores a list of numbers, measured at regular instants. Today, what that turning into samples costs and the one rule that keeps it honest.

The number of samples per second is the sampling rate in hertz. Research EEG systems often record somewhere between 250 and 2,000 samples per second. Between two samples, the computer knows nothing. It only has the dots.

You have already seen what can go wrong at the cinema. A film camera takes a fixed number of pictures per second. Point it at a spinning wagon wheel, and the wheel can look frozen or even turn backwards. Nothing is wrong with the wheel. The camera simply takes its pictures too rarely to follow the motion.

The same thing happens to signals. To capture a rhythm, you need more than two samples per cycle. So the highest frequency a recording can represent is half the sampling rate. That limit is the Nyquist frequency. At a sampling rate of 500 Hz, the Nyquist frequency is 250 Hz.

And what happens to a rhythm faster than Nyquist? It does not disappear. It disguises itself as a slower one. This is aliasing. Take a sampling rate of 100 Hz, so a Nyquist frequency of 50. Now record 60 Hz line noise. The samples land on the curve in a way that looks exactly like a 40 Hz wave. 60 folds back around 50 and lands at 40.

Here is the cruel part. Once the samples are taken, the fake 40 Hz wave and a real 40 Hz rhythm are identical, sample for sample. No analysis can tell them apart afterwards. Aliasing cannot be undone. It can only be prevented.

Prevention happens before sampling. The amplifier applies an anti-aliasing filter, a low-pass filter that removes frequencies above Nyquist before the converter takes its samples. Real filters are not perfectly sharp, so in practice you sample comfortably faster than twice your highest frequency of interest.

The same rule applies later in software when you downsample. Keeping one sample out of four divides the sampling rate by four and divides the Nyquist frequency by four too. Anything above the new Nyquist will fold back into your data. So low pass first then downsample. Some functions do both for you. Others like decimation in MNE do not filter. Check the documentation before you rely on it.

Let's recap. Sampling turns a curve into dots. The Nyquist frequency is half the sampling rate. Faster rhythms do not vanish. They fold back as slower fake ones. Aliasing cannot be undone, so filter before you sample and filter before you downsample.

Next time, what an average quietly assumes about a signal. Stationarity.

PLATE 2.63:10

Stationarity · What an average assumes

An average over time assumes stationarity: that the signal's statistics do not change during the window. Like a long exposure photograph, a spectrum keeps what stayed still and blurs what moved, so eyes-open and eyes-closed data averaged together give a medium alpha peak that never existed. EEG is at best locally stationary, so short windows are used and states are split before averaging.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Compute a spectrum over one minute of EEG, and you get one curve. One curve for the whole minute. Hidden inside that single curve is an assumption that is easy to forget, that nothing important changed during that minute. That assumption has a name, stationarity.

Think of a long exposure photograph of a busy street. The shutter stays open for a whole minute. The buildings come out sharp because they never moved. The cars, the people, the bicycles turn into faint streaks or vanish entirely. The photograph is a perfect picture of what stayed still and a poor picture of everything that moved.

A spectrum over a long window works the same way. It is an average over time. Whatever stayed constant shows up clearly. Whatever came and went gets smeared into the average.

So when is a signal stationary? Roughly, when its statistical properties do not change over time. In the weak sense used for spectra, the mean stays the same, and the way the signal correlates with itself at a given lag stays the same, whenever you look. A stationary signal has one true spectrum, and a long window simply measures it more precisely.

EEG is rarely like that for long. Alpha grows when the eyes close and shrinks when they open. Rhythms come in bursts. Vigilance drifts, and a resting participant may slowly become drowsy. Movements and artifacts appear and disappear.

Here is a concrete trap. Record 30 seconds with eyes open, then 30 seconds with eyes closed. Compute one spectrum over the whole minute. You get a medium alpha peak, halfway between the small peak of eyes open and the large peak of eyes closed. That medium peak never existed at any moment. It describes neither state.

So how do we live with a non-stationary signal? We treat EEG as locally stationary, roughly stable over short stretches a second or two even if it drifts over minutes. That is why spectral methods cut the signal into short segments and why time frequency methods like the short time Fourier transform and wavelets slide a short window along the recording. We will build them in Chapter 3.

Averaging across trials is a different kind of average. It assumes that each trial is a fresh repetition of the same process. If participants get tired or learn from the first trial to the last, that assumption weakens too.

A few habits help. Look at a spectrogram before trusting a single spectrum. Compare the first and second half of a recording. And never average across conditions or states that you know are different, split first, then average.

Let's recap. Any average over time assumes stationarity, that the statistics do not change during the window. Like a long exposure photograph, it keeps what stayed still and blurs what moved. EEG is at best locally stationary, so we use short windows, time, frequency methods, and we split states before averaging.

Next time, why short windows come at a price? Time or frequency, pick your blur.

PLATE 2.73:30

The uncertainty principle · Time or frequency, pick your blur

Precision in time and precision in frequency share one fixed amount of dough: a short window pins down when, a long window pins down which frequency. Frequency resolution is roughly one over the window length, and the product of the two spreads has a hard minimum. The limit belongs to signals themselves, so the blur should fit the question.

TRANSCRIPTEN

We would love to know two things at once — exactly when something happened in the EEG and exactly at which frequency. Unfortunately, there is a fundamental limit. You cannot make both perfectly sharp at the same time. Today we see why and what it means for every time frequency analysis you will ever run.

Think of a piece of dough. You can roll it long and thin or short and tall but you cannot make it smaller. The amount of dough stays the same. Precision in time and precision in frequency share the same fixed amount of dough.

Start with a short analysis window, say 100 milliseconds. It tells you precisely when something happened. But 100 milliseconds holds only one cycle of a 10 hertz rhythm. Is it 10 hertz or 11? The window cannot tell. Roughly, the frequency resolution is 1 over the window length. 100 milliseconds gives you about 10 hertz of resolution. One second gives you about one hertz. Four seconds, about a quarter of a hertz.

Now a long window, four seconds. Frequencies become beautifully sharp. But if a burst of beta happened somewhere inside those four seconds, you no longer know when. The timing is smeared across the whole window.

This trade-off was described for signals by Dennis Gabor in 1946, borrowing the idea from quantum physics. If you measure the spread in time and the spread in frequency as standard deviations, their product can never be smaller than 1 over 4 pi. Gaussian-shaped wave packets reach that minimum exactly. That is one reason why Gabor atoms and Morlet wavelets are so popular.

A useful picture, the time-frequency plane cut into tiles. Each tile is one analysis window at one frequency. Its width is its time precision, its height its frequency precision. You can reshape the tiles, but their area cannot shrink below the limit.

Different methods choose different tilings. The short-time Fourier transform uses the same tile shape everywhere. Wavelets change shape with frequency. At high frequencies, tiles are narrow in time and tall in frequency, good for timing fast events. At low frequencies, they are wide in time and short in frequency, good for separating slow rhythms. We will build both in Chapter 3.

This is not a flaw of our equipment, and it is not noise. It is a property of signals themselves. A very short burst genuinely contains a wide range of frequencies. Only a sinusoid that lasts forever has one single, perfectly sharp frequency.

In practice, let the question choose the blur. To time a fast event precisely like the onset of an evoked response, accept blurry frequencies. To separate two neighboring rhythms like an individual alpha peak at 9 versus 11 hertz, accept long windows. And always report the window you used, because it shapes what you can see.

Let's recap. Time precision and frequency precision trade-off. Frequency resolution is roughly 1 over the window length. Gabor showed the product of the two spreads has a hard minimum, reached by Gaussian wave packets. Methods differ in how they shape their tiles, not in escaping the limit. Choose the blur that fits your question and report it.

Next time, Noise, Signal-to-Noise Ratio and Why Decibels Are Everywhere.

PLATE 2.83:23

Noise, SNR and decibels

Signal is whatever the question is about and noise is everything else, like a whisper in a stadium crowd. The signal-to-noise ratio compares powers and is usually expressed in decibels, ten times the log of the power ratio, or twenty for amplitudes. Averaging trials shrinks noise with the square root of their number, provided the signal repeats and the noise does not.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Imagine trying to hear a friend whisper from across a packed stadium. The whisper is there. The crowd is louder. That is EEG analysis in a nutshell. A small signal we care about buried in everything else. Today, three tools to think about it. Noise, the signal-to-noise ratio, and decibels.

First, a definition that depends on you. Signal is whatever your question is about. Noise is everything else. Other brain activity, muscles, eyes, the power line, the amplifier. The same alpha rhythm is signal in a resting state study, and noise in a study of evoked responses.

The signal-to-noise ratio compares the two. Usually it compares powers, the power of the signal divided by the power of the noise. A ratio of 1 means the whisper is as loud as the crowd. A ratio of 100 means the crowd has gone quiet.

Ratios like these span huge ranges, so we often express them in decibels. For powers, decibels are 10 times the base 10 logarithm of the ratio. A few landmarks are worth memorizing. Plus 10 decibels means 10 times more power. Plus 20 means 100 times more. Plus 3 decibels is roughly double, and minus 3 is roughly half. One trap, for amplitudes the factor is 20 instead of 10, because power goes with amplitude squared. 10 times the amplitude is plus 20 decibels.

Why bother with logarithms? Because EEG power falls steeply with frequency, and a log scale keeps slow and fast rhythms on the same plot, because many effects are multiplicative and a log turns them into simple additions and because a doubling and a halving become symmetric plus 3 and minus 3. That is why time frequency maps are so often shown in decibels relative to a baseline as we will see in chapter 9.

Now the oldest trick for improving the ratio, averaging. Ask your friend to whisper the same sentence again and again and average what you hear. The whisper is the same each time, so it adds up. The crowd is different each time, so it partly cancels. The noise amplitude shrinks with the square root of the number of repetitions.

That square root is unforgiving. To halve the noise, you need four times more trials. To divide it by ten, you need a hundred times more. This is why evoked responses, often only a few microvolts on top of a background of tens of microvolts, need many trials.

Averaging only works under its assumptions. The signal must be the same on every trial, locked to the same moment. The noise must be independent from trial to trial, and centered on zero. Break the first and the average blurs. Break the second and the noise does not cancel at all.

Let's recap. Signal and noise are defined by your question. The signal-to-noise ratio compares their powers. Decibels are 10 log 10 of a power ratio or 20 log 10 for amplitudes, plus 3 is about double, plus 10 is 10 times. Averaging n repetitions shrinks the noise by the square root of n, as long as the signal repeats and the noise does not.
