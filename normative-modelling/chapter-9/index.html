PLATE 9.16:08

Functional MRI and connectivity

Functional connectivity measures how much two regions rise and fall together, and the number of pairs explodes with the number of regions. Reliability is low, with an average intraclass correlation of 0.29 for one connection, which widens the band of normal. Head motion shifts correlations systematically, so a deviation may come from the head, not the brain.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we read the label of a pretrained model, line by line. One line said which atlas the model was built on. Today we walk to a gate where that line matters even more. The gate of functional MRI.

Our guest is SPIN, from the fMRI series. Here, the measure is not a thickness or a volume. It is a relationship. Two regions of the brain, at rest, and how much their signals rise and fall together. That is functional connectivity.

Let us count how many such relationships there are. Take N regions. Every region can pair with every other one, but a pair counts only once. So the number of connections is N times N minus one, divided by two.

With seventeen large networks, that gives one hundred thirty-six connections. With one hundred regions, it gives four thousand nine hundred and fifty. With four hundred regions, seventy-nine thousand eight hundred. The list of measures explodes, while the number of people stays the same.

So what does a normative model of connectivity look like? In 2023, Rutherford and colleagues added functional models to the braincharts family. They used the seventeen Yeo networks, which gives exactly one hundred thirty-six pairs of networks, and a second set of ten networks, called Smith ten. The models were trained on a large multi-site sample, of about twenty-two thousand people.

The braincharts repository also ships a small functional example file. In the file we downloaded, there are sixty-four people from one single dataset, and one hundred thirty-six connection values for each of them. Each column is one pair of networks, for example the visual network with a default network. Each row is one person. That is one point on a chart, per pair, per person. The same chart as before, with the same grey dots, and with the centile lines of the pretrained model, adapted to this dataset with sixteen other people from the same study. But a hundred and thirty-six times over.

Now, a first warning. Functional connectivity is less stable than we would like. In 2019, Noble and colleagues pooled twenty-five studies in which the same people were scanned twice. For a single connection, the average intraclass correlation was zero point two nine. The authors call that poor.

Let us say what this number means. It runs from zero to one. Zero says that the second scan tells you nothing about who is who. One says that the second scan gives the same ranking of people. At zero point two nine, much of what separates two people on one connection is not stable. It is noise, or a state of the day.

For a normative model, noise has a cost. It widens the band of normal. A deviation that is real has to stand out from a band that is already blurred. The same study found that some conditions help. More data per person, and connections within a network rather than between networks, both gave better agreement.

Now, the key moment. Head motion.

When someone moves in the scanner, the signal moves too. In 2012, Power and colleagues showed that motion changes resting-state correlations in a systematic way. Many long-distance correlations go down, and many short-distance ones go up. In the same year, Van Dijk and colleagues found the same thing in a thousand young adults. Groups that moved a little differently gave difference maps that could be mistaken for a brain effect.

Look at what this means for a normative model. A deviation of connectivity is a distance from a norm. If a person moved more than the people who built the norm, the distance may come from the head, not from the brain.

We can look at this in the file. For each of the sixty-four people, the file gives the mean framewise displacement, the average head movement from one image to the next. We averaged the one hundred thirty-six connection values for each person, and compared. The correlation with movement is about zero point three seven. That is a description of one small dataset, not a proof of anything. But it is exactly the pattern that makes a careful reader stop.

How do the authors of the models handle it? They required at least five minutes of low-motion data, with framewise displacement below half a millimetre. And they put the mean framewise displacement into the model as a covariate, like age, or site. That is a good idea. It remains a correction, not a proof. A covariate removes what the model can see, and the model cannot see everything that movement does to the signal. And the version we downloaded for PCNtoolkit one point four lists only age, with site and sex, and no movement at all. On our file, the deviations it gives still follow head motion, with a correlation of about zero point three five.

Preprocessing is the first defence, and in the fMRI series it has its own story. In a normative study, the choices made there have to be the same for the reference and for the new person. That is the label again, line after line.

One last caution, from the authors themselves. They write that using the seventeen networks ignores important considerations about brain dynamics. And they note that their samples are most likely overrepresentative of people of European ancestry. Newer work is arriving. In 2025, Sun and colleagues published lifespan charts of the functional connectome in Nature Neuroscience, built from over thirty-three thousand people scanned at more than one hundred sites. Even so, a norm for connectivity is still a young norm, as of November 2026.

So, to sum up. Connectivity brings thousands of measures, a low test-retest reliability, and a strong link with head motion. Before reading a deviation of connectivity, ask how much the person moved.

PLATE 9.25:10

Diffusion and other structural measures

Diffusion MRI follows water, which slides along fibre bundles more easily than across them. Fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity and myelin-sensitive R1 follow different lifespan curves, some symmetric and some lopsided, so each needs its own model. Scanner effects are larger than for anatomy, so a norm does not transfer between measures or protocols.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we walked through the functional MRI gate, and we met a trap. A deviation in a connection can come from the head moving in the scanner, and not from the brain. Today we walk to the next gate of the airport. The diffusion gate.

Diffusion MRI does not photograph tissue. It follows water. In a glass, water molecules wander the same way in every direction. In white matter, the fibres run in bundles, like cables, and water slides along a bundle more easily than across it. That is the signal.

Three measures leave through this gate. Each gets one picture. These sketches are illustrative. They are not data.

First, fractional anisotropy. It says how strongly the water prefers one direction.

Second, mean diffusivity. It says how freely the water moves overall, averaged over all directions.

Third, a measure that is sensitive to myelin, the fatty sheath around the fibres. Take R-one, the inverse of the T-one relaxation time. In white matter, most of its variation is driven by myelin. Be careful here. Fractional anisotropy is not a myelin measure. It depends in large part on how the directions of the fibres are spread inside each voxel.

Now follow the three measures across life.

In 2014, Yeatman and colleagues measured one hundred and two healthy volunteers, aged seven to eighty-five, in twenty-four white matter bundles. R-one rises during development, and comes back to its childhood level by about seventy to eighty years. A symmetric hill.

Diffusivity does not behave like that. It changes quickly in childhood, then declines slowly in old age. At eighty, it has not returned to where it started. A lopsided hill. The authors concluded that these measures reflect different biological processes.

So there is no single diffusion trajectory. Each measure needs its own curve, and its own model. This is why the flexible curves of chapter four matter so much here.

Now the second difference. The scanner.

Fortin and colleagues scanned two hundred and five healthy people on two different scanners. They found that diffusion measures were highly site-specific. Pooling the sites without correction, they wrote, damages the quality of the data. We will meet the correction they favoured, ComBat, in chapter five.

Another study put numbers on the contrast with anatomy. Seventy-three volunteers travelled between two sites, four scanners in all. For volumes and cortical thickness from T-one images, the authors found no meaningful scanner effect. For diffusion, in the same people, the scanner effects were large. In that study, correcting the anatomy was sometimes even harmful, and correcting the diffusion was strongly recommended. It is one study, but it matches the intuition. Diffusion depends on the sequence, the gradients, and the settings, so each site leaves its own signature.

That is the key moment. Each measure has its own life trajectory, and its own site effects, often stronger than for anatomy. A norm for one measure does not transfer to another, and a norm built on one protocol does not transfer to another.

What has been built? As of November 2026, two published models are good places to start.

In May 2026, in Nature Communications, a consortium published a lifespan model of white matter microstructure. It pools nineteen datasets, more than fifty-four thousand people, aged four to ninety-one. It uses hierarchical Bayesian regression, with age and sex as covariates and the site handled inside the model. It gives centile curves for each white matter region.

In another model, published in Biological Psychiatry, a team fitted fractional anisotropy in white matter tracts, in more than twenty-five thousand healthy people. The preprint of that work describes forty-eight tracts and five cohorts. It used a warped Bayesian linear regression, with a B-spline for age and a sinh-arcsinh warp for the skewed distributions. On new people, the preprint reports that the model explained on average thirty-seven percent of the variance.

Two teams, two tools, and both are the models of chapter four at work. These models are very young. In 2024, the authors of the second one wrote that only two preliminary studies had fitted normative models to diffusion data before theirs.

One limit remains. Fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity come from the diffusion tensor, and the tensor cannot describe fibres that cross. Richer measures are left for future work.

So, to sum up. Diffusion gives fractional anisotropy, diffusivity, and myelin-sensitive measures, each with its own trajectory and its own site effects. Read the label of the protocol before you read the z-score.

Next time, we go to the oldest gate in the airport. Normative EEG.

PLATE 9.35:47

Normative EEG

EEG was compared with age-matched references long before modern normative models. An open lifespan example fits age curves for mean and spread on cross-spectral tensors, using Riemannian geometry. Amplifier, montage and reference play the scanner's role, so z-scores need harmonising across devices, and rhythm and background should be modelled separately.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, at the diffusion gate, we saw that every structural measure has its own trajectory, and its own site effects. Today we walk down the concourse to the next gate. The EEG gate.

EEG does not take a picture of the brain. It records electrical rhythms at the scalp, through electrodes, many times per second. Yet the question at this gate is the same as everywhere else in the airport. Is this person's signal typical for their age? And how would we know?

People started asking that long ago. In nineteen seventy-three, Matousek and Petersén published a method to evaluate the background activity of the EEG automatically, using age-dependent quotients. Four years later, in nineteen seventy-seven, John and colleagues published a long article in Science, with a single word for a title. Neurometrics. So the idea of placing a recording against an age-matched reference is more than fifty years old. EEG is a pioneer of normative modelling.

But a norm is only as good as the people, and the machines, behind it. So let us look at one open example, published in twenty twenty-two.

It was built by Li and colleagues, with Valdes-Sosa as last author. The reference population is one thousand five hundred and sixty-four people. They come from nine countries, from fourteen studies, and they were recorded with twelve different devices.

Notice what was shared. Not the raw recordings. Only anonymised metadata, and a summary of each recording called the cross-spectral tensor.

What is that? For each frequency, a small table. On the diagonal, the power at each electrode. Off the diagonal, how much each pair of electrodes moves together. So one recording gives a spectrum, and also a first measure of connectivity, in the same object.

These tables are covariance matrices, and covariance matrices do not live on a flat sheet of paper. They live on a curved space. In the EEG series, we met the geometry for this. It is called Riemannian geometry. The authors use it so that distances between tables respect that curvature.

Then comes the part you already know from chapter three. For each measure, the authors fit a curve of the mean and a curve of the standard deviation, as a function of age. They use additive mixed-effects models, and they call the result developmental equations. Mean by age, spread by age. Then a z-score for any new person.

Now the key moment. The device.

In structural MRI, the scanner was a source of variance that had nothing to do with the brain. In EEG, the same role is played by three things. The amplifier. The montage, which is the set of electrode positions. And the reference, the electrical point against which every voltage is measured. In the EEG series, we saw that the reference is a space of its own. Change it, and the numbers change. A norm built with one reference is valid for that reference, and for no other.

Here is the idea with our own simulated dataset. Remember the three sites. Site B reads three units above site A. Site C reads four units below. Nothing about the people differs. Only the instrument. That is KILO, with the needle of the scale shifted. It is a simulation. But the shift in a real EEG can be just as large, and it can be different at each frequency.

The authors did not hide this. They demonstrate these batch effects in their data. And they give a method to compute harmonised z-scores, so that a person recorded on one device can be compared with a norm built from all of them.

Did it help? The authors report that harmonised Riemannian z-scores gave higher diagnostic accuracy for two conditions. Brain dysfunction after malnutrition in the first year of life. And brain dysfunction caused by COVID. These are results from the paper. We cite them. We do not redraw them as if they were our data.

There is a second reason why EEG norms are hard. The spectrum is not one thing.

A power spectrum has bumps. Rhythms, like alpha. This is the periodic part. And it sits on a background that falls with frequency, roughly one over f. This is the aperiodic part. In the EEG series, we learned to separate the two.

Now think about age. In two thousand fifteen, Voytek and colleagues compared younger adults, aged twenty to thirty, with older adults, aged sixty to seventy. The older adults had a flatter background slope. So the background itself moves with age.

That has a consequence for a normative model. A band power mixes the rhythm and the background. If the background changes with age, a person may look deviant in alpha power, without any change in their alpha rhythm at all. So the two parts should be modelled separately, each with its own age curve. This is our reasoning, not a result of the paper.

And be careful with the background. A twenty twenty-five study of one thousand two hundred and eighty-two resting recordings from people aged eighteen to eighty-eight, made with MEG, argued that much of the age effect on the background can be explained by the heart. So an age curve of the background may also carry cardiac signal. Before trusting a deviation, ask what produced the measure.

So, to sum up. At the EEG gate, the device, the montage and the reference play the part of the scanner. A norm must be built, harmonised and used with them in mind.

Next time, we visit the gate that few people use so far. Normative fNIRS.

PLATE 9.45:11

Normative fNIRS

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy reaches infants and adults, yet no lifespan centile reference turned up in the search of November 2026. A useful norm would first need comparable measurements: the same cap layout, source-detector distances, placement and hair coupling. Scalp blood flow can mimic or hide brain activity, so short channels belong in every recording, and the device should be a level in the model.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, at the EEG gate, we saw that the amplifier, the montage and the reference play the part of the scanner. Today we walk to the next gate. It is quiet. This is the fNIRS gate.

fNIRS stands for functional near-infrared spectroscopy. A small light source sits on the scalp. Near-infrared light goes in, and a detector a few centimetres away catches what comes back. From the change in that light, we estimate changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin in the cortex underneath.

It is used with adults, and with infants. Reaching every age is exactly what a norm needs. So you might expect the gate to be busy. Let us look at the other gates first.

At the structural MRI gate, we have seen the large charts of chapter eight. And, as of November 2026, at the magnetoencephalography gate, there is a lifespan reference too. It was published this year by Zamanzadeh and colleagues, in Communications Biology. It follows one thousand eight hundred and forty-six people, aged six to eighty-eight, recorded on three different systems. The model is a hierarchical Bayesian regression, with age, sex and site as covariates. The same recipe as chapter four.

So we looked for the same thing in fNIRS. A lifespan reference, built with centiles, shared for others to reuse. In our search, in November 2026, we did not find one. That is a statement about our search, and about this month. Check again before you rely on it.

There is one nearby exception, and it makes our point. Newborn teams have published reference ranges for cerebral oxygen saturation, minute by minute over the first fifteen minutes after birth, with tenth and ninetieth centiles. A 2025 study of one hundred and twenty-two neonates compared its ranges with earlier ones from other devices. One device read lower than the others at first. That is a narrow window, not a lifespan, and it is oxygenation, not brain activity. But it already shows the device effect.

Why is the gate quiet? Let us read what a useful fNIRS norm would need. Three things.

First, comparable measurements. Remember the market of scales, in chapter five. The same person, on three scanners, gets three numbers. In fNIRS the scale is not only the device. It is the cap, the layout of the sources and detectors, and how far apart they are. It is also where the cap sits on the head.

In 2021, a group of twenty-two researchers, convened by the Society for Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy, published best practices for reporting. Their checklist asks for the layout of the array, the distances between sources and detectors, and the positions relative to landmarks of the head, like the nasion, the inion, or the ten-twenty points. Without those lines on the model label, a norm cannot be moved to another lab.

And there is a covariate that MRI never had. The same paper says that signal quality depends on hair: its colour, its thickness, its density. Hair sits between the sensor and the scalp. So the coupling between the sensor and the head differs from one person to the next, and a norm has to deal with it.

Second, the scalp and the body. The light crosses the skin twice. Blood flow in the scalp changes. So do breathing and blood pressure. Tachtsidis and Scholkmann, in 2016, described how these changes can look like brain activity, a false positive, or hide it, a false negative.

The remedy is a short channel. It is a source and a detector placed so close together, under fifteen millimetres, that the light only reaches the scalp. The best practices paper gives about eight millimetres for adults. That signal is measured, then regressed out of the long channels. The fNIRS series goes into the method.

Now think about a norm. If one dataset has short channels and another does not, the same person gets two different numbers. That is a site effect. And a deviation may be a heartbeat story, not a brain story. Just like head motion, earlier in this chapter, for connectivity.

Third, a clear question. A norm of what? A response to a task, or the resting signal? Oxygenated hemoglobin, or deoxygenated? Each choice is a different quantity. A reference is only worth something when its measure is defined before the first participant is recorded.

So here is the recipe, in four lines. Record short channels, always. Report the array, the placement and the hair, as the checklist says. Put the device as a level in the hierarchy, like a site. And share enough people, across enough devices, to see the age curve.

So, to sum up. The fNIRS gate is quiet because a norm needs comparable measurements, and fNIRS must first make its own comparable: the device, the placement, and the scalp.

Next time, we leave the head. Heart rate, sleep, activity, test scores. Body and behaviour.

PLATE 9.54:33

Body and behaviour

Body and behaviour follow the same normative rules as the brain: covariates, a device, a named population. Cognitive test norms now use GAMLSS regression instead of age-band tables, while heart rate variability norms depend on how beats are processed. Wearable data show one person's normal differing from another's by up to seventy beats per minute.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, at the fNIRS gate, we asked what a useful norm would need. Today we walk to a gate with no scanner at all. The gate of the body and of behaviour.

Here, the measures are a heart rate, a night of sleep, a day of activity, a score on a memory test. Nobody lies in a tube.

Let us begin with the oldest passengers. Cognitive tests. Their scores have long been compared with norms. The old way is a table: you cut the population into age bands, and each band needs many people. In twenty twenty-one, Timmerman and colleagues wrote a tutorial in Psychological Methods on a modern way to build these norms. They fit a regression of the whole score distribution, on age and on other variables, with the GAMLSS family of models. We met that family in chapter four. They report that it can produce more realistic norms, and can work with smaller samples.

Here is a recent example. In twenty twenty-four, Wragg and colleagues built norms for three tests of the Cambridge computerised battery, given over the web. They had seven hundred twenty-eight adults, aged eighteen to seventy-five. They used Bayesian models, and the norms depend on age, sex and education. The trends came out similar to the older methods, and so did the way individual people were classified. A norm, built like a brain chart.

Now the heart. Heart rate variability is the small variation in the time between two beats. In twenty ten, Nunan and colleagues gathered the studies that had measured it for a few minutes in healthy adults. Forty-four studies, twenty-one thousand four hundred thirty-eight people. They found values lower than the older reference published in nineteen ninety-six. And for the spectral measures, the differences between people were very large. A newer review, in twenty twenty-five, looked at fifty-eight studies and still found no generally accepted normal values.

They pointed at the processing. How the beats are detected, and how the errors are edited, was too often not reported, or not careful. So a heart norm is not only about who is in it. It is also about the processing chain. That is the preprocessing line of our model label, and here it can change the answer.

Now the wrist. In twenty twenty, Quer and colleagues looked at ninety-two thousand four hundred fifty-seven adults in the United States, who wore Fitbit trackers. The median follow-up was three hundred twenty days. The average resting heart rate was sixty-five point five beats per minute. But the average of one person went from thirty-nine point seven, up to a hundred and eight point six. The authors write that a person's normal can differ from another person's normal by as much as seventy beats per minute.

And here is the moment to remember. Age, sex, body mass index and sleep together explained no more than ten percent of that difference between people.

Picture an illustrative chart, not real data. A median curve, with centiles around it. A range this wide is honest, but it says little about one person. The covariates are not wrong. They are just small. For these signals, a norm for the population is a first step. Each person also has a daily value that is normal for them.

So the rules of the series still apply. Covariates, like age and sex. Devices that play the role of the scanner. A reference population that you must name. In the fNIRS series, the heartbeat was a nuisance riding inside the signal. Here it is the measurement itself.

Our key moment is this. Behaviour is sometimes the best first normative model of a study. In twenty twenty-two, a team studied six thousand four hundred eighty-one young people, aged eight to twenty-one, in the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort. They reduced sixteen scores from fourteen computerised tests to one general score. Then a Gaussian process regression, on age and sex, gave every young person a z-score. A score below the norm went with more general psychopathology, and with attention problems and anxiety. A normative model, with no scanner anywhere.

These are examples, not a survey.

So, to sum up. The body and behaviour follow the same rules as the brain: a covariate, a device, a named population. A norm is a good first model, even without an image.

PLATE 9.66:42

Norms for things that aren't one number

When the object is not one number, a norm needs new definitions of centre, spread and distance. The Mahalanobis distance exposes a person whose two correlated measures diverge, which ordinary distance misses, and autoencoders score how badly a brain is rebuilt. Each choice changes who looks deviant, and a single score says far, never where.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we gave the body and behaviour a place on the chart. A heartbeat, a night of sleep, a score on a memory test. Each of them was one number, and the chart knew what to do with it. Today the airport hands us luggage that does not fit on that belt.

Welcome to the oversize baggage hall. Four pieces are waiting here. Several measures at once. A hidden space that a network learns. A whole network of connections. And a whole distribution for each person. None of them is one number. All of them still need a norm.

This is a research frontier. So we will go slowly, and say what is known, and what is not.

First piece. Several measures at once.

So far, we scored one region at a time. One region, one z-score. In the braincharts transfer set, each person has up to a hundred and seventy-six cortical thickness columns. That is a lot of z-scores. But sometimes the question is about the person. Is this person unusual, overall?

The simple answer is a distance. Square each z-score, add them up, take the square root. That is the ordinary distance from the centre of the norm. And it has a flaw. It treats every measure as independent. Brain measures are not. Neighbouring regions rise and fall together.

Here is a small case, with illustrative numbers. Two measures that move together, with a correlation of point nine. One person has a z-score of plus one point five on the first, and minus one point five on the second. Each score alone is ordinary. Neither crosses the usual limit of one point nine six. The ordinary distance is only two point one. But two measures that travel together have just gone in opposite directions. That is rare.

The tool that sees it is the Mahalanobis distance, from nineteen thirty-six. It weighs the person's differences from the norm by the covariance, the way the measures vary together. For our person, it gives six point seven. When the data are Gaussian, the squared distance follows a chi-squared distribution, with one degree of freedom per measure. For two measures, the ninety-five percent limit of the squared distance is about six. Our person is at forty-five.

There is a catch. With a hundred and seventy-six measures, the covariance holds more than fifteen thousand numbers. We have a few hundred people to estimate them. And the norm must still depend on age and sex, as in the earlier chapters. So the covariance has to be the one of what is left after the age model.

Second piece. A hidden space that a network learns.

An autoencoder is a network that squeezes the measures through a narrow bottleneck, then rebuilds them. Train it only on reference people, and it learns to rebuild typical brains well. A brain unlike the reference should be rebuilt badly.

In twenty nineteen, a team did exactly this. They trained an autoencoder on one thousand one hundred and thirteen healthy people from the Human Connectome Project, with one hundred and four regional measures each. The deviation was the average squared rebuilding error. In a schizophrenia dataset and in an autism dataset, patients had a higher average deviation than the controls of the same dataset.

Now read the authors' own caution. The spread of that score was large, and they wrote that this may limit its use for individual patients. The model also cannot say in which direction a region has changed. So this is a distance with no unit and no direction. It says that a brain is far from the norm. Only a map can say where.

Later models are conditional. Age and sex go in as inputs, so the norm moves with them. As of November 2026, a study published in January compared such a model, a conditional variational autoencoder, with three established methods, on one hundred and ninety-five measures from the UK Biobank. The deep model performed comparably across several metrics. Its authors also note that earlier ways of running these models struggle to give reliable probabilistic predictions, and their own method was built to fix that. That is the part to watch.

Third piece. A whole network.

In the EEG series, we met the geometry of covariance matrices. Here it becomes a norm. In twenty twenty-two, an international consortium built lifespan norms for cross-spectral matrices, one per person. Each describes how every pair of electrodes varies together, at each frequency. The norms use one thousand five hundred and sixty-four people, nine countries, twelve devices.

Why not average the matrices entry by entry? Because a covariance matrix must stay a valid one, and the set of valid ones is curved. So the mean, the spread and the distance are computed in Riemannian geometry. The authors showed batch effects between sites, and gave a way to harmonise the z-scores. They reported that harmonised Riemannian z-scores were more accurate at predicting brain dysfunction after malnutrition in the first year of life.

Resting-state connectivity from fMRI got an autoencoder norm too, in twenty twenty-three. Its authors found that group differences did not withstand analysis at the individual level. Averages hide heterogeneity. We have heard that before.

Fourth piece. A whole distribution for each person.

Think of the thousands of heartbeat intervals of one night, an illustrative case. Each person is a histogram. What is the average of a hundred histograms? You can average the heights, bin by bin. Or you can slide each histogram's mass along the axis, and average the shapes. These give different norms, and both are defined. For this piece, we stay with ideas. We have no results to show you.

Now the key moment. For one number, a mean and a spread are obvious. For an object that is not a number, you must choose what the mean is, what the spread is, and what distance means. Each choice changes who looks deviant. The z-score becomes a distance from the centre of the norm, in units of the norm's own spread. And a single score per person says that someone is far, never where. For where, we go back to the maps of episode six point two.

So, to sum up. When the object is not one number, the norm needs a new definition of centre, spread and distance, and each choice belongs on the model label.
