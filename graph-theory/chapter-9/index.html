PLATE 9.15:54

Wiring at random

Milgram's folders reached a Boston stockbroker through chains averaging five point two intermediaries. To ask what makes a world small, the episode wires networks by a coin flip for each pair of nodes. Above mean degree one, an archipelago of small islands becomes a continent with one giant piece. Degrees stay close to the mean.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the nineteen sixties, the psychologist Stanley Milgram ran a strange experiment with the post.

He chose two hundred and ninety-six volunteers. One hundred and ninety-six lived in Nebraska. One hundred lived near Boston.

Each volunteer received a folder, and the name of one stranger: a stockbroker who worked in Boston.

The rule was simple. If you know him personally, send the folder straight to him. If you do not, send it to someone you know personally, who might be closer.

Sixty-four folders reached him. On those chains, the number of intermediaries averaged five point two.

This is a story about people, folders and stamps. It does not look like a graph.

But put a dot on every person, and a line between two people who know each other.

The folders were walking along those lines. And there is the hidden graph.

Milgram's world is small. A stranger is only a few handshakes away. So what shape of network makes the world small?

We will not guess. We will build networks, and see what each way of building produces.

The simplest way uses no judgement at all, only chance.

Take sixty dots in the sky, with no lines.

Between any two dots there is a pair. Sixty dots make one thousand, seven hundred and seventy pairs.

For each pair, flip a coin that lands heads with probability p. Heads, draw a line. Tails, draw nothing.

To grow the network slowly, give every pair one hidden number between zero and one, picked once. A pair is linked when its number is below p.

Turn the dial up, and lines only appear. None ever disappears.

This is the random graph. Edgar Gilbert described exactly this coin for every pair in nineteen fifty-nine.

The same year, Erdős and Rényi studied a close cousin, where the number of links is fixed and placed at random. In nineteen sixty they followed what happens as links are added. Both versions are usually called Erdős-Rényi graphs.

A dot has fifty-nine possible partners. So its expected number of links is fifty-nine times p. We call this the mean degree, c, and turn that dial instead.

At mean degree one half, the sky has fifteen links and forty-five separate pieces. The biggest has three dots.

These are the small islands of episode two point four.

At one, there are thirty links, and the biggest piece has twenty-four dots.

At two, forty-six dots belong to one piece. At four, fifty-four.

The change is blurry, because sixty dots are few. So let us repeat the experiment on bigger networks, many times, and average.

For each mean degree, this chart shows the share of dots in the biggest piece. Three sizes: one hundred, one thousand and ten thousand dots.

Below one, the share stays near zero. With ten thousand dots and mean degree one half, it is under two in a thousand.

At one and a half, it is fifty-eight percent. At two, eighty percent. At three, ninety-four percent.

The bigger the network, the sharper the bend. The dashed line is the limit for infinitely many dots.

That bend is a transition. Below mean degree one, an archipelago: many small islands. Above it, a continent: one giant piece holding a fixed share of everything.

It happens at one precise value, like water freezing at zero degrees Celsius. The threshold goes back to the paper of Erdős and Rényi in nineteen sixty.

Why one? Follow a link to a new dot. On average, it brings c more links to follow.

If c is below one, the exploration dies out. Above one, it keeps growing, and nothing stops it but the edge of the network.

That is a rough argument. The exact one counts branching processes.

What about degrees? Take one thousand dots, and mean degree four.

Here is how many dots have zero links, one, two, and so on.

Most dots sit near the mean. The busiest has twelve links, about three times the mean. Nobody has a hundred.

The dashed dots follow the Poisson distribution, the limit when the network is large.

And fifteen dots have no link at all.

Does the network ever become one single piece? Not at four. In two hundred networks of one thousand dots, none was fully connected.

At six point nine, the natural logarithm of one thousand, seventy-five of the two hundred were. At ten, one hundred and eighty-nine.

Lonely dots disappear slowly.

Remember the word graph of episode two point four: three thousand, nine hundred and three words, twenty-one thousand and two links.

Its mean degree is ten point eight. Twenty random networks of that size and number of links were all a single piece, every word included.

The real word graph has a giant piece of three thousand, eight hundred and seven words, ninety-seven point five percent, and sixty-five words alone.

Words are not linked by coin flips. Spelling decides. So the word graph is not a random graph.

But the same kind of transition is why giant pieces are so common.

Is the world really wired by coins? Chapter ten will test this model on real networks, and show where it fails.

For now, it is the simplest reference: a network with nothing in it but chance.

To sum up. Give every pair a coin, and a network appears.

Cross mean degree one, and an archipelago becomes a continent. And degrees stay close to the mean.

PLATE 9.26:55

Small worlds

A ring where each town links to its nearest neighbours is cosy but wide, while coin-wired networks are short but not cosy. Rewiring a few links to random places, as Watts and Strogatz did, shrinks trips while clustering stays. At one percent of links moved, the trip falls to eighteen percent of its length and clustering keeps ninety-seven percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we asked: what shape of network makes the world small?

Chance gave us a first answer. Wire a network with coins, and the paths come out short.

But look at your own friends. They tend to know each other. In a network wired by coins, they almost never do.

Remember clustering, from episode seven point one. The worm's wiring has a clustering of zero point three two. A random network with the same size and the same number of links has zero point zero five.

So real networks need two things at once. Cosy neighbourhoods, and short paths. Coins give only the second.

Today we build a recipe that gives both.

Start with a ring road. Twenty towns, evenly spaced. Each town is linked to the two nearest towns on each side. That makes four neighbours each.

The clustering is high. Half of the pairs of neighbours are linked: zero point five.

But crossing is slow. The two farthest towns are five steps apart. The average trip takes two point nine steps.

Now make the ring bigger. A thousand towns, ten neighbours each. Clustering stays at two thirds. But a typical trip takes fifty steps. A coin-wired network of the same size needs about three.

Clustering is local. Distance is global. The ring has the first, and lacks the second.

In nineteen ninety-eight, Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz made a very simple move.

Go around the ring, and take each link in turn. With probability p, keep one end where it is, and reconnect the other end to a town chosen at random, anywhere on the ring.

With p at zero, nothing changes. With p at one, every link has moved, and the ring has become a random network.

We care about what happens in between.

Here is a smaller ring. Sixty towns, four neighbours each, one hundred and twenty links. A typical trip takes seven point nine steps.

On this ring, one link turns seven point nine into six point five. Over two hundred rings, one link gives six point nine on average.

Move a second link: five point six. A third: five point two.

Clustering hardly notices. It goes from zero point five to zero point four seven.

After twelve links, one in ten, a typical trip takes four steps on this ring, and four point two on average. Clustering has gone down to zero point three nine.

Why does a single link do so much? A shortcut does not only join two towns. It brings their neighbourhoods closer, and the neighbourhoods of those neighbourhoods.

Clustering is different. Taking a link out of a cosy neighbourhood costs that neighbourhood a few triangles, and no more.

Now the big version. Take a thousand towns, with ten neighbours each. For every value of p, we rewire twenty rings and average. This is the figure of the paper, computed again.

The horizontal axis is logarithmic, because the action happens at tiny values of p. Both curves are divided by their value at p equal to zero, so both start at one.

At one in a thousand, only about five of the five thousand links have moved. The typical trip is already down to fifty-three percent of its length. Clustering is at ninety-nine point seven percent of its value.

At one percent, about fifty links have moved. The trip is down to eighteen percent: from fifty steps to about nine. Clustering keeps ninety-seven percent.

Only when one link in ten has moved does clustering fall noticeably, to seventy-four percent. At one, it is gone, and the trip is about three steps.

In between lies a wide range of p where the network is both cosy and small. Watts and Strogatz called such networks small-world networks.

Do real networks live in that range? They checked three.

Film actors, linked when they appeared in a film together. Two hundred and twenty-five thousand actors. A typical chain is three point six five links long, against two point nine nine for a random network of the same size and density. Clustering: zero point seven nine, against zero point zero zero zero two seven.

The power grid of the western United States. Four thousand, nine hundred and forty-one stations. Eighteen point seven steps, against twelve point four. Clustering: zero point zero eight, against zero point zero zero five.

And the worm, with two hundred and eighty-two neurons. Two point six five steps, against two point two five. Clustering: zero point two eight, against zero point zero five.

Every time, the same signature. Paths a little longer than random. Clustering far higher.

Now back to Milgram.

Your friends know each other. But a few of them know someone far away: a cousin abroad, a former classmate. Those few long links are the shortcuts. These are the weak ties of episode seven point one.

That is the mechanism of the paper: very few long links are enough to make a world small. It is a model. It shows that the effect is possible, and not that it explains every chain of letters.

One warning, for chapter fourteen.

When a paper says that a brain network is small-world, it often relies on one number. Humphries and Gurney proposed it in two thousand and eight.

Divide the clustering of your network by that of a random network of the same size and links. Divide its path length by the path length of that random network. Then divide the first ratio by the second. Above one, small-world.

Here is the catch. A sparse random network has almost no triangles, so the first ratio is huge for any clustered network.

Test it on the ring. Take a thousand towns, and fifty steps to cross. That is no small world. Its score: four point two. Above one.

Bigger rings score higher. Two hundred towns: three point two. One thousand: four point two. Five thousand: five point five. While crossing takes ten steps, fifty, then two hundred and fifty.

Telesford and colleagues made this point in two thousand and eleven. They proposed another index, omega, which compares clustering to a ring instead of a random network. The same ring scores minus zero point nine four, the sign of a lattice.

So the score depends on the reference you choose. Chapter fourteen returns to it, when networks are built from brain data.

To sum up. A ring is cosy, but wide. Move a few links to random places, and the world shrinks while the neighbourhoods stay.

Next time, a different question. In many real networks, some nodes have a hundred times more links than the rest. Neither coins nor rings make such hubs. What rule does?

PLATE 9.37:00

The rich get richer

Growth with preferential attachment builds hubs: in a ten-thousand-node network the busiest node has 397 links, against thirteen in a coin-wired network with the same mean degree of four. Removing the most connected nodes shatters it, while random failures barely hurt. A straight line on a log-log histogram is not evidence of a power law, so look at the cumulative curve.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we gave a ring a few shortcuts, and the world became small.

Now look at the degrees. In a ring, every town has exactly the same number of links. In a coin-wired network, the numbers cluster around the average.

Take ten thousand nodes and about twenty thousand links, wired by coins. The mean degree is four. The busiest node has thirteen links.

Many real networks have hubs far above that. So what rule grows a hub?

Here is a rule. Think of airports opening, one at a time.

Each new airport opens two routes. And it prefers busy airports: the more routes an airport already has, the likelier it is chosen.

Picture a wheel. Every existing airport owns a slice, as wide as its number of routes.

Start with three airports, numbered zero, one and two. Airport zero has two routes, the others one. Airport three arrives, and spins the wheel twice.

Zero owns two slices out of four. It is chosen, together with airport two.

Airport four arrives. Zero now owns three slices out of eight. Chosen again, with airport three.

Airport five arrives. Zero owns four slices out of twelve, and is not chosen this time. The wheel is a bias, not a rule.

Keep going to forty airports. There are seventy-six routes. The busiest airport is number six, with fifteen. The five oldest hold twenty-six percent of all route ends.

This is the Barabási and Albert model, published in Science in nineteen ninety-nine. It has two ingredients: growth, and preferential attachment.

The idea is older. In nineteen seventy-six, Derek de Solla Price described it for citations, and called it cumulative advantage. Success breeds success.

Now grow the network to ten thousand nodes, two links each. That makes nineteen thousand, nine hundred and ninety-six links, and a mean degree of four. The same as the coin-wired network.

Just over half of the nodes have only two links. But the busiest node has three hundred and ninety-seven. That is about a hundred times the mean. In the coin-wired network, the busiest has thirteen.

Fifty-nine nodes have thirty links or more. Eight have a hundred or more.

Who are the hubs? The oldest. The twenty biggest all arrived among the first hundred nodes.

Follow node six. Nineteen links when the network had fifty nodes. A hundred and five at a thousand. Three hundred and ninety-seven at ten thousand.

A late arrival, node five thousand, ends with three links. In the whole younger half of the network, nobody has more than eight.

Now remove nodes, one share at a time, and measure the biggest piece that remains. First at random, like failures. Then the most connected first, like an attack. We do the same to the coin-wired network. Each curve averages five networks.

Failures first. Remove twenty percent at random. The hub-grown network keeps seventy-eight percent of its nodes in one piece. The coin-wired one keeps seventy-six. At this density, the gap is small. At eighty percent removed, three point seven against half a percent.

Now the attack. Remove the most connected first, ranked by their degree in the intact network. At ten percent, fifty-eight percent remain in one piece. At fifteen percent, seven point seven. At twenty percent, four tenths of one percent. The network is dust.

The coin-wired network, under the same attack, keeps eighty-five, seventy-eight, then sixty-nine percent. It falls apart only near thirty-five percent.

Albert, Jeong and Barabási reported this in two thousand, in Nature. Hubs make the network hold up against failures, and hubs are its weak point. As in three point one, fragility comes from position.

The Barabási and Albert paper called these networks scale-free. Their degrees follow a power law: the share of nodes with degree k falls like k to the minus gamma. Their model predicts gamma equal to three.

On log-log axes, a power law is a straight line. So let us look at our degrees. Here is the histogram, one bar per degree. It looks like a fuzzy line.

Fit a line to it, and the slope is minus one point eight six. Not three. Above degree twenty, twenty-four degrees are held by exactly one node. Those counts are noise, and they drag the line flat.

Now two other lists of ten thousand degrees. Not networks, only simulated numbers. One is log-normal. One is a power law with an exponential cutoff. Neither is a power law.

On the same plot, they look like fuzzy lines too. Slopes: minus two point zero zero, and minus one point six seven. A straight-line fit scores zero point nine three for both. For the true power law, it scores zero point eight one, the worst.

So switch to the cumulative view. For each degree k, show the share of nodes with degree k or more. There are no bins, and no counts of one.

The hub network gives a straight line. Its slope is minus one point nine three, so gamma is two point nine three. Close to three.

The log-normal bends: minus one point one seven between degrees three and ten, and minus two point two six between thirty and a hundred. The cutoff curve bends too.

A straight-looking histogram is not evidence. Clauset, Shalizi and Newman showed in two thousand and nine that least-squares fits on histograms can give wrong exponents. And even a right exponent does not show that the data follow a power law.

So how common is a power law in real networks? In twenty nineteen, Broido and Clauset tested nine hundred and twenty-eight real networks: social, biological, technological, transportation and information.

Only four percent showed the strongest evidence of scale-free structure. For eighty-eight percent, a log-normal fit was at least as good as a power law. Social networks were at best weakly scale-free. A few technological and biological ones looked strongly so.

Heavy tails with hubs are common. The exact law is much rarer.

Replies came the same year. Holme argued that much of the dispute is about terminology, and Voitalov and colleagues, with other estimators, found scale-free networks more common.

Whatever the label, the mechanism stands. Growth with a bias toward the connected makes a few huge hubs, and hubs shape how a network breaks.

To sum up. Rich-get-richer growth builds hubs and a crowd of small nodes. Remove hubs and the network falls apart. And never trust a straight line on log-log axes. Look at the cumulative curve.

PLATE 9.45:41

Keep the degrees, shuffle the rest

The configuration model pairs each node's stubs blindly, keeping every degree and nothing else, then erases loops and repeats. On the general relativity collaboration network, the copies keep the two-step paths but have 631 triangles against 48,260 in reality. They also miss assortativity, communities and the size of the giant piece. What the real network shows beyond its copies is structure.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A real network has hubs. How much of what we measure comes from its degrees alone?

To find out, we build a control: a network with exactly the same degrees as the real one, and nothing else. Here is the recipe.

Take nine invented people. Give each one as many loose ends as friends. The degrees are five, three, three, two, two, one, one, one and zero. That makes eighteen loose ends. We call them stubs, or half-links. The person with zero gets none, so they stay alone.

Pin the stubs on a round loom, one peg per stub. Now the weaver picks a free peg at random, then another free peg at random, and joins them with a thread. Again, and again, until no peg is free.

Nine threads for eighteen pegs. Every person ends with exactly the degree they started with. Nobody chose who befriends whom. The pairing is blind.

Look closely. One thread leaves a person and comes back to the same person. That is a self-loop. And two people are joined by two threads, a double link. Blind pairing allows both.

Draw again with the same degrees, and you get another network. Here are three more draws. They have zero, zero and one triangle.

This is the configuration model. Bender and Canfield counted graphs with given degrees in nineteen seventy-eight. In nineteen eighty, Bollobás introduced the pairing of stubs as a way to study them. In two thousand and one, Newman, Strogatz and Watts used it on real networks.

Let us try it on one. Here is the arXiv collaboration network of general relativity, from nineteen ninety-three to two thousand and three. Authors are nodes, and two authors are linked if they wrote a paper together.

It has five thousand two hundred and forty-two authors and fourteen thousand four hundred and eighty-four links. The busiest author has eighty-one co-authors. That makes twenty-eight thousand nine hundred and sixty-eight stubs.

Pair them blindly, a hundred times.

First, an honest count. Each draw has about eight self-loops and about sixty-two repeated links. That is under one percent of the links. But none of the hundred draws was clean: the fewest repeats was forty.

What do people do? They erase. Drop the loops, merge the repeats. After erasing, the copies have fourteen thousand four hundred and fourteen links on average, and a busiest node of seventy-nine instead of eighty-one. A small price.

Will loops and repeats stay rare in bigger networks? It depends on the degrees. Janson showed in two thousand and nine that a blind pairing stays clean with a probability that does not shrink to zero exactly when the sum of squared degrees grows no faster than the number of stubs. With heavy tails, that fails. So we count, and we erase.

Now the question. What is a model that does nothing but keep the degrees good for? It is a witness. Whatever the real network shows beyond its copies is real structure. Whatever the copies reproduce, comes from the degrees alone.

Start with two-step paths. A person with k friends is the middle of k times k minus one over two of them. That depends only on the degrees. The real network has about two hundred and thirty thousand. Copies, before erasing, have exactly the same number.

Next, the giant piece. Molloy and Reed proved in nineteen ninety-five that this model has a giant component when the average of k times k minus two is positive. Here it is eighty-two. Yes, a giant piece. But the copies put ninety-nine percent of authors in it. Reality, seventy-nine.

The typical distance: six point one in the real network, four point four in the copies. So the copies are better connected than reality.

Now triangles. Reality has forty-eight thousand two hundred and sixty. Six hundred and thirty-one in the copies, on average. Seventy-six times fewer. No copy came close.

Transitivity is the share of two-step paths that close into a triangle. It is zero point six three in the real network, against zero point zero zero eight.

Assortativity, from episode seven point three: plus zero point six six, against minus zero point zero zero six. In reality, authors link to authors with similar degrees. Among the copies, degrees do not matter for the pairing.

Last, communities. Louvain finds a modularity of zero point eight six in the real network. In the copies, zero point four one. Even random wiring has groups for Louvain to find, as in episode eight point two. But the real value is twice as high.

Why? Authors of one paper all link to each other. That alone makes triangles and groups. The degrees cannot say it.

So the model keeps the hubs and the two-step paths. It misses the triangles, the assortativity, the communities, and even the size of the giant piece.

You have already used this witness. The chance term of modularity in episode eight point two is this model. The rich club of episode seven point four was divided by copies with the same degrees. Chapter ten turns the witness into a method.

To sum up. Give each node its stubs, pair them blindly, and keep the degrees. Count the loops and repeats, and erase them. What the real network shows beyond the copies is structure.

PLATE 9.55:02

Space costs

Placing nodes on a map and linking only near ones, as in Gilbert's random plane networks, produces clustering and communities for free: about 0.586 on a large map, against 0.005 for a random graph. In the worm, two links in three are shorter than a tenth of the body, and about one in seven spans most of it. Ask what space alone explains.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended on a question. In a brain, a long fibre costs more than a short one. So what does a network look like when distance sets the price?

Every model in this chapter forgot something. They never asked where the nodes are. In a random graph, any two nodes may link, near or far. In a body, that cannot happen.

Here is the nervous system of the worm Caenorhabditis elegans. Two hundred seventy-seven neurons, each with a position along the body. We use the file released with Kaiser and Hilgetag, in two thousand six. Drop the direction of the synapses, and there are one thousand nine hundred eighteen links.

Now measure each link. Call the longest distance between two neurons one body length. The median link is five percent of a body length. The median pair of neurons lies twenty-five percent apart.

Short links dominate. A pair closer than a tenth of the body is linked eight point six percent of the time. Between three tenths and seven tenths of the body, about two percent or less.

But look at the far end. Two hundred eighty-five links are longer than three quarters of the body. They join one end of the worm to the other. So the wiring is mostly cheap, but about one link in seven is expensive.

Gilbert studied the simplest version of this, in nineteen sixty-one. His paper is called Random plane networks. Throw points at random on a map. Give every point the same reach. Link two points when they are within reach of each other. That is all. No coin is tossed for any pair.

Here are forty-five simulated points, from seed ninety-five. Each point stamps a disc, with half the reach as its radius. Two points link exactly when their discs touch.

Grow the reach, and links appear. At a reach of eight hundredths, twenty links. At two tenths, one hundred and one.

Now pick one point in the middle. It has four neighbours, all inside its reach. Any two of them are less than two reaches apart. So they are often within reach of each other too.

Count the pairs. Six pairs of neighbours, four of them linked. Nobody chose those triangles. Space did.

On a big map, the clustering settles at one minus three times the square root of three, over four pi. That is zero point five eight six, derived by Dall and Christensen in two thousand two.

We checked with two thousand simulated points and about ten thousand links. Wrapped around like a torus, the clustering is zero point five nine. On a flat square, zero point six zero. Compare a random graph with the same number of points and links: zero point zero zero five. That is more than a hundred times lower.

Space also makes communities. Louvain, from episode eight point two, cuts our forty-five points into six groups. The modularity is zero point six five. At the same number of links, a random graph gives zero point three eight, on average. Each group is just a neighbourhood, a patch of the map.

Over a hundred draws of two hundred points, the modularity is zero point seven nine, against zero point three four. Even the best random graph reached only zero point three six.

Now the price. Bullmore and Sporns, in two thousand twelve, described brain organisation as a trade-off. Cost on one side. Useful network patterns on the other. Wire length is the cost. Short paths are one useful pattern.

Take a simulated network of sixty points and two hundred eight links. Its wire, added up, is twenty-eight map sides. The typical path is three point seven steps.

Add three random long links. The path drops to three point four. The wire grows by eight percent. Add ten. The path is three point zero. The wire is twenty-six percent longer.

Over two hundred draws, the story is the same. Paths get fifteen percent shorter with three links, and twenty-eight percent with ten. A few long links shorten paths clearly. They also cost wire, link by link.

Back to the worm. Its clustering is zero point two eight. Draw a random graph with the same number of neurons and links, and it gives zero point zero five. It is tempting to call that gap organisation.

But distance alone gave us clustering and communities for free. So before calling a brain network organised, ask what the geometry of the body, or the skull, already explains. Chapter ten builds references that keep the space.

To sum up. Place nodes on a map and link the near ones, and clustering and communities appear for free. Real wiring is mostly short: in the worm, two links in three are shorter than a tenth of the body, and about one in seven spans most of it. Before calling a network organised, ask what space alone explains.
