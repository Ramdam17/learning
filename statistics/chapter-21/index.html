Angles do not average like numbers

Two weather vanes at 350 and 10 degrees average to 180 as plain numbers, south, though both point north. Angles live on a circle, so turn each into a unit arrow, average the arrows, and read the direction and length. The length shows how concentrated the angles are: 1.000 for identical, 0.131 for scattered. It works for hours and days.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Up on the roof stand two weather vanes. One points three hundred and fifty degrees. The other points ten degrees. Both are almost exactly north.

So what is their average heading? Add the two numbers and halve: three hundred and fifty plus ten, over two, makes one hundred and eighty. South. The exact opposite of what both vanes say.

One rule for the whole video. Zero degrees is the top of every circle: north on a compass, midnight on a clock, the first of January on a calendar. Angles are counted clockwise from there.

What went wrong? On the number line, ten and three hundred and fifty are far apart. On the circle, they are neighbours. Angles live on a circle, so their average has to live on a circle too.

Here is the recipe. Turn each angle into an arrow of length one, pointing that way. Every arrow has two shadows: its cosine, along north and south, and its sine, along east and west. Now lay the arrows head to tail.

The first arrow goes north by 0.985, and a little west. The second goes north by the same 0.985, and a little east. The sideways parts cancel exactly. The chain ends straight north, at 1.97. Halve it, and you get the mean arrow. It points at zero degrees, and its length is 0.985. That is the circular mean. This arithmetic is exact, not simulated.

In short: one, each angle becomes an arrow. Two, average the arrows. Three, read the direction and the length of the mean arrow.

Now a messier case, simulated. Forty wind directions, scattered around north. Some a little west, some a little east. Written as plain numbers, 20 of them are small, and 20 are close to three hundred and sixty. So their plain average lands in the middle: 175 degrees. South again.

Run the recipe instead. The mean arrow points at 355 degrees, about five degrees west of north, right where the cloud sits. Its length is 0.924.

You might say: just cut the circle somewhere else. Cut it at south, behind the cloud, and the plain average is 355 degrees. Fine. Cut it at north, through the cloud, and it is 175. The answer depends on where you cut the circle. The mean arrow has no cut.

That length says something too. If all forty winds were identical, it would be one. These cluster tightly, so it is close to one. A longer arrow means a tighter cluster.

Turn one knob: how spread out the angles are. Same forty draws each time, stretched a little further round the circle. All equal, the arrow has length 1.000. With a spread of twenty degrees, it shrinks to 0.947. Fifty degrees, 0.704. Ninety degrees, 0.284. And forty angles drawn all round the circle give 0.131.

Why not exactly zero? Forty arrows never cancel perfectly. And these are single samples. Averaged over 1,000 samples of each kind, the lengths are 1.00, 0.94, 0.69, 0.31 and 0.14. So one number tells you how concentrated the angles are.

Now the strong moment. Think of pigeons released forty at a time. Homing pigeons fly roughly north, with a spread of twenty degrees. Lost pigeons fly every which way. Before the next picture, guess. Repeat the release many times. What does the mean arrow do?

Here are 1,000 releases of each kind, simulated. Each dot is the tip of one mean arrow. For the homing pigeons, all 1,000 tips sit near the rim, never more than 11 degrees from north, and the average length is 0.94. For the lost pigeons, the tips fill a small disc around the centre. The average length is 0.14, and never above 0.40.

Now look at the directions. The first four releases of lost pigeons give 329, 47, 43 and 260 degrees. Only 18.3 percent of the 1,000 land within thirty degrees of north. For the homing pigeons, it is 100 percent.

So the mean and the spread come out of the same object. When the arrow is short, its direction is noise. A direction read off a length of 0.07 means nothing. Circular spread is read from the same length: one minus it. For the wind, one minus 0.924 is 0.076.

The same recipe works for time. A day has twenty-four hours, so one hour is fifteen degrees, and midnight is the top. Take an event at eleven at night and another at one in the morning. The plain average of the clock readings is twelve: noon, the middle of the day. The two arrows say midnight, with length 0.966. Exact arithmetic again.

A year works the same way: three hundred and sixty-five days make three hundred and sixty degrees. Two birthdays, the twenty-ninth of December and the fourth of January. The plain average of the day numbers is one hundred and eighty-three and a half. That is early July. The circular mean is day one, the first of January, with length 0.999. Leap years ignored.

Now simulated arrivals at an emergency room: 60 of them, clustered around midnight. The plain average of the clock readings is thirty-two minutes past noon. That is lunchtime. The circular mean is sixteen minutes before midnight, with length 0.76.

One more place you will meet this. A brain rhythm goes up and down, and its phase is where it stands in the cycle when a stimulus arrives. Zero degrees is the peak. Here are 60 simulated trials. When the phase is locked to the stimulus, the arrows bunch. The mean points at 43 degrees, with length 0.58. When it is not locked, they scatter, and the length is 0.11. In EEG, this length across trials is called inter-trial phase coherence.

One warning. Two opposite clumps, half the winds from the north and half from the south, also give a short arrow: 0.012 in a simulation of forty. A short arrow means no single preferred direction. It does not mean no pattern. Episode 21.2 comes back to it.

Remember: angles live on a circle, so add arrows, not numbers. The direction of the mean arrow is the circular mean, and its length tells you whether that direction means anything.

Is there a preferred direction?

Even a spinner with no favourite direction gives a mean arrow with length: 0.404 for five angles, 0.028 for a thousand. Twenty turtles with length 0.485 beat chance in 746 of 100,000 samples, close to the Rayleigh test. Two opposite clumps cancel, but doubling the angles merges them. Fewer trials bias length upward.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we met a surprise. Forty angles spread all the way round a circle still give a mean arrow that is short, but not zero: about 0.14 on average. Today's question is simple. Is there a preferred direction? Or is that short arrow just chance?

To answer, we need to know what chance does. Picture a hiker lost in a forest, who walks off in any direction. Or, to keep it simple, a spinner. It has no favourite direction. Spin it ten times, and every stop is an angle. Here are three sets of ten spins, simulated. The mean arrows have lengths 0.103, 0.345 and 0.069. Never zero. Never the same twice.

How long does the arrow get when nothing is going on? That depends on how many angles you have. Here are six sample sizes, each repeated 10,000 times with the spinner, simulated. With five angles, the average length is 0.404, and it varies a lot. With ten angles, 0.284. With twenty, 0.198. With fifty, 0.126. With a hundred, 0.089. With a thousand, 0.028. Fewer angles, longer arrows, even when there is no direction at all.

So how long must an arrow be before we stop blaming chance? Look at the line that chance crosses only one time in twenty. For five angles, it is 0.762. For ten, 0.542. For twenty, 0.389. For fifty, 0.245. The more angles, the lower the line. An arrow of 0.4 is ordinary for five angles, and remarkable for fifty.

Does the length shrink like one over the square root of n? Multiply each average by the square root of n. You get 0.90, 0.90, 0.88, 0.89, 0.89 and 0.88. Almost constant, so yes, roughly: the typical length of chance shrinks like one over the square root of n.

Now a real question. Twenty turtles are released on a beach, and we note the heading of each. Simulated. The mean arrow points at 76 degrees, and its length is 0.485. Is that long? Compare it with chance. Here is the curtain of chance: 100,000 samples of twenty spinner angles, each one leaving the length of its arrow. Most are short, because chance rarely lines up twenty headings. Our turtles sit far out in the tail. Only 746 of the 100,000 chance samples are at least as long. So the p-value is 0.0075. You met this idea in chapter 6: the share of chance results at least as extreme as ours.

Statisticians turned this comparison into a formula. It is called the Rayleigh test. It gives 0.0076, in one line, with no simulation. I used the approximation that Berens prints in his 2009 toolbox paper, taken from Zar's textbook. The two p-values differ by one in the fourth decimal. If the turtles had no preferred direction, an arrow this long would be rare. So yes, the turtles probably prefer a direction.

But the same length can mean very different things. Say you record the wind on calm days, and the mean arrow has length 0.30. With ten days, the Rayleigh p-value is 0.4167: nothing. With twenty days, 0.1661. With fifty, 0.0105. With a hundred days, 0.0001. Same arrow length. The number of angles decides.

The Rayleigh test is made for one clump of angles. The usual model of one clump has a name: the von Mises distribution. On the circle, it plays the role that the normal distribution plays on a line. It has a direction, and a concentration. Here are 150 simulated angles for four concentrations, all pointing north. Concentration zero is the spinner: no clump. Raise it, and the bell curve around the circle grows tall and thin. The exact length of the mean arrow rises with it: 0.446, 0.698 and 0.893, for concentrations one, two and five.

Now the trap. An emergency room is busiest around ten in the morning and again around ten at night. Those are twelve hours apart: opposite sides of the dial. Here are 60 simulated arrivals, thirty in each clump. There is a clear pattern: the morning rush and the night shift are easy to see. But thirty arrows point one way, thirty point the opposite way, and the two sides cancel. The mean arrow has length 0.046, and the Rayleigh test says p equals 0.883. Nothing here, although the data are far from uniform. A rhythm locked to two opposite phases would give the same silence. Fisher's book, and the book by Mardia and Jupp, are the standard places to read more about it.

One usual remedy is to double every angle. Ten in the morning is 150 degrees, and it becomes 300. Ten at night is 330 degrees, and it becomes 660, which is 300 again. The two clumps merge into one. The length is now 0.807, and p is below 0.0001.

Now the strong moment. A brain rhythm has a phase at the moment of a stimulus, and we compare two conditions. In both, the truth is exactly the same: a von Mises distribution with concentration 0.3, whose exact arrow length is 0.148. The only difference is the number of trials: 20 in one condition, 200 in the other. Before the next picture, guess. Which condition shows the longer arrow?

Here are 10,000 repetitions of each condition, simulated. With 20 trials, the average length is 0.238. With 200, it is 0.158. About one and a half times longer, with no real difference. Why? A length can never be negative, so noise can only push it up. And fewer trials mean more noise.

So comparing two conditions with different numbers of trials compares their numbers of trials first. Vinck and colleagues described this problem in 2010, for conditions with different numbers of trials, and proposed a bias-free measure: the pairwise phase consistency.

Remember: even when nothing is going on, the mean arrow has a length, and it is longer when there are fewer angles. The Rayleigh test asks what chance gives, but two opposite clumps cancel, and a length is biased upward when trials are few.

Comparing and correlating angles

Every tool has a circular version. An ordinary t-test on phases between 0 and 360 finds nothing, while the Watson-Williams test gives p of about 0.008 and shuffled labels agree. With unequal concentrations both fail. Temperature against hour of day needs a circular-linear coefficient, since cutting the day flips the sign of r. The same shuffling machine works throughout.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked whether one clump of angles points somewhere. Today there are two clumps, and two new questions. Do two groups of angles differ? And does an angle go with another measure? Every tool you know has a circular version.

Start with a brain rhythm. Its phase at the moment of a stimulus is an angle, and we record it in two conditions, thirty trials each. Simulated. In condition one, the mean arrow points at 348 degrees, with length 0.658. In condition two, it points at 26.9 degrees, with length 0.647. The two arrows are 38.9 degrees apart. Difference, or chance?

First, the old trap. Run an ordinary t-test on the plain numbers, degrees from 0 to 360. The averages are 199 and 140, and the test finds nothing: p is 0.106. The numbers cut each clump across the top of the circle.

The circular cousin of the t-test comes from Watson and Williams, in 1956. Pool both groups into one arrow. If the groups point the same way, the pooled arrow stays as long as the separate ones. If they point apart, it shrinks. The test compares those lengths. Here the pooled arrow is 0.615, and the test gives F equal to 7.56. The p-value is about 0.008.

It suits any two clumps of angles: bird headings, arrival hours at two hospitals, birth months in two countries. But it has conditions. Each group should look like one von Mises clump. The two clumps should have a similar concentration. Each group needs at least five angles. And the arrows must not be too short: for groups of eleven or more, the Python port of this test warns when their average length is under 0.45.

When the conditions fail, we go back to the shuffles of chapter 14. Shuffle the condition labels: deal the sixty angles at random into two groups of thirty, and measure the angle between the two mean arrows. Three shuffles give 19.7, 8.2 and 26.9 degrees. Before the next picture, guess. In how many of 100,000 shuffles will the angle reach 38.9 degrees?

Here is the curtain of chance, built from 100,000 shuffles. Most angles are small. Only 617 shuffles reach 38.9 degrees or more. So p is 617 divided by 100,000: about 0.006. The formula said about 0.008. Two approximations, one conclusion.

Now a case where the conditions fail. Migrating birds are released under two skies. Under a clear sky they fly in a tight clump, concentration eight. Under cloud they scatter, concentration one half. The true heading is the same. Here are twenty birds each, simulated, with arrows of length 0.944 and 0.177. Now repeat such a study 500,000 times, with no true difference, and count each test's false alarms at the five percent level.

With similar concentrations, two and two, the formula raises a false alarm about 5 percent of the time, and so do the shuffles. With the birds' concentrations, eight and one half, the formula is wrong 23 percent of the time, and the shuffles, 39. Both fail. With low concentrations, 0.3 and 0.3, the formula fails, 35 percent, while the shuffles hold at 5. Mix unequal concentrations with unequal group sizes, and it is worse: 44 percent, and 66.

So shuffles repair the formula's approximation, but not the assumption underneath. A shuffle says: if the labels did not matter, the two groups would be one population, with the same spread. When the spreads differ, they are not.

Now an angle and an ordinary quantity. Forty body temperatures, simulated, each taken at some hour of the day. An hour is fifteen degrees. Try an ordinary correlation, with the hour as a number from 0 to 24. Cut the day at midnight: r is 0.485. Cut at six: minus 0.192. At noon, minus 0.353. At six in the evening, 0.197. The sign flips with the cut, though the data have not moved. Two readings 35 minutes apart, across midnight, sit at opposite ends of the axis.

The circular-linear coefficient correlates the quantity with the cosine and the sine of the angle, and combines the two. It does not care where you cut. Here it is 0.509. A formula gives p of about 0.0056. Shuffle the temperatures 100,000 times, and 339 shuffles reach 0.509 or more: p is about 0.0034.

The failure can be total. Take the phase of a rhythm at the stimulus against reaction time, 80 trials, simulated. Trials are fastest at phase zero, and slowest at 180 degrees. The ordinary r is 0.147. The circular-linear coefficient is 0.654, and not one of 100,000 shuffles reaches it.

Two angles now: the wind direction, and the heading of a sailing boat, 25 pairs, simulated. The wind arrow points at 319.6 degrees, the heading arrow at 340.2. The coefficient of Jammalamadaka and SenGupta centres each angle on its own mean arrow, then multiplies sines. A constant offset between wind and heading does not matter. Here it is 0.622. The formula gives p of about 0.005, and 111 of 100,000 shuffles of the pairing reach it, in either direction: p is about 0.001.

You need not code any of this. The CircStat toolbox for MATLAB, published by Berens in 2009, has a function for each: the Watson and Williams test, the circular-linear correlation, and the circular-circular one. Python ports exist too: pycircstat, and pycircstat2.

Now the strong moment. The t-test became Watson and Williams. Pearson's correlation became two coefficients. And the shuffles did not change at all: shuffle the labels, or the pairing, and count. Three questions, one machine. Only the statistic is new, and it has to make sense on the circle. Every tool of this series has its circular version.

Remember: compare two groups of angles with Watson and Williams, or with shuffled labels, and link an angle to a number, or to another angle, with a circular coefficient and its shuffles. Shuffles need a statistic that works on the circle, and groups that are interchangeable.
