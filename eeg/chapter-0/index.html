Listening to the brain's electricity

TRANSCRIPTEN

Why E-E-G? Three minutes to fall for it.

Your brain is talking right now. Billions of neurons, sending tiny electrical signals. And a few electrodes resting on your scalp can listen in. That is electroencephalography: E-E-G.

Think of a weather station on a rooftop. It cannot see a single molecule of air. But it reads pressure and wind, minute by minute, and tells you when a storm is coming. E-E-G is a weather station for the brain. It does not see single neurons. It reads the large, synchronized currents of millions of them at once, from the outside, without harming anyone.

Its first great strength is time. E-E-G samples the brain hundreds or thousands of times per second. It can tell you that something changed one tenth of a second after a sound, and then again three tenths of a second later. Few methods follow the brain this closely in time.

Its second strength is reach. An E-E-G system can be carried in a backpack. It works in a hospital, a classroom, a forest, or a living room. It can record a newborn, a sleeping adult, or two people talking to each other at the same time.

And it has a long history. Hans Berger recorded the first human E-E-G in 1924, and published it in 1929. He saw a rhythm, about ten times per second, that grew when people closed their eyes. We still call it alpha.

And it answers real questions. Clinicians use E-E-G to diagnose epilepsy and to score the stages of sleep. Researchers use it to follow attention, memory and language as they unfold. Brain-computer interfaces read it to move a cursor with imagination alone. And with two caps at once, a method called hyperscanning, we can even record two brains in conversation.

But the weather station has limits too. The signal is tiny: a few tens of millionths of a volt. Blinks, muscles and the power line are often much larger. And the skull blurs everything, so knowing exactly where a signal comes from is hard.

That is why this series exists. In fifteen chapters, we will follow the journey of a signal. From the neuron to the scalp. Through the mathematics of waves. Into the lab, to record well. Through the cleaning. And on to the analyses: event-related potentials, rhythms, connectivity, complexity, sources, decoding. And finally, to statistics that hold up, and results that others can verify.

Every episode is short, and focuses on one idea. Every number is sourced. Every simulation is labelled. And at the end, five bonus episodes put it all into Python code.

Each episode starts from an image you already know: a forest in the wind, a swing, a kitchen, a night city, a courtroom. Then it builds the idea, step by step, until you can use it in your own work.

No prior knowledge needed. Just curiosity.
