Simulate it

Four claims of the series are checked in Python, with the seed and versions as part of the result. Means of twenty-person ladles pile up on the pot's mean, with spread 35.1. Ninety-five percent intervals hold the truth 943 times in a thousand, a test's false alarms are 4.81 percent, and power is 54.73 percent. A simulation has its own error bar.

This episode is a bonus, and it teaches nothing new. We take four claims from the series and check each one at the keyboard, with real code. Every line on screen was run, and every number printed under it was printed by that run. You need only the basics of Python.

First, the tools, because a simulation belongs to the versions that ran it. This run used Python three point twelve point twelve, NumPy two point five point five, and SciPy one point eighteen point one. I read the latest releases on the Python package index the day I made this video. NumPy says that its random generator gives no promise of the same stream from one version to the next. So the versions are part of the result, like the seed.

Claim number one comes from episode 1.3. The means of many samples pile up on the mean of the pot, and they spread far less than people do. Here is the sample machine in a few lines. The pot is ten thousand simulated reaction times, the pot of 1.3, built with the same seed, one thousand. Then the machine, a function that takes a seed. It makes a random generator from that seed. A ladle takes twenty people without putting them back, and keeps their mean. The function does that a thousand times.

Run it with seed ninety thousand one hundred. The pot has a mean of 423.4 milliseconds and a spread of 153.1, exactly as in 1.3, because the pot has the same seed. The thousand means average 424.2, with a spread of 35.1. Here is that pile, drawn from the array the code computed. It sits on the pot's mean, and it is more than four times narrower than the people. In 1.3 the means averaged 424.1, with a spread of 33.8. Close, and not equal. Same pot, same ladle, other luck, and that is the question about seeds we answer at the end.

Claim number two comes from 5.4. A ninety-five percent interval holds the truth ninety-five times in a hundred. We can count. A clinic study takes thirty scores from a bell, with a true mean of fifty and a spread of ten. One line makes a thousand studies at once, a table of a thousand rows and thirty columns, with seed ninety thousand one hundred and one. Each row gives a mean and a standard error. The interval is the net of 5.4, the mean plus or minus two standard errors. One test asks whether the truth, fifty, is inside. Before you see the count, guess: out of a thousand intervals, how many will hold it?

Here are the first hundred intervals, and the misses are pink. Each interval either holds the truth or it does not. The ninety-five belongs to the recipe, and the count is how we see it. Across the thousand, 943 hold the truth: 94.3 percent. In 5.4, ten thousand studies gave 94.5. Now use the exact multiplier for thirty people, which SciPy gives us from the t distribution, 2.045. This time 944 intervals hold the truth. The last line prints the Monte Carlo margin: with a thousand studies, a count wanders by about one point and a half. So 94.3 and 94.4 cannot be told apart from 95 here, and 5.4 needed ten thousand studies to see its half point. A simulation has its own error bar.

Claim number three comes from 6.4. If nothing is going on, and the rule is fixed before the data, a test says yes about five times in a hundred. Here is the tea study again. Twenty people drink tea, twenty drink water, and in this world tea does nothing, so we draw all forty from the same pot. Ten thousand studies in one table. SciPy's t-test function compares the two groups of every study at once, and returns one p-value per study.

Guess first: out of ten thousand studies, how many will say yes? Count the p-values below point oh five. 481 of 10,000 studies say yes, a false alarm rate of 4.81 percent. In 6.4 the smoke detector rang 489 times in ten thousand quiet nights, 4.89 percent. Two different worlds, one fixed rule, and each lands close to five. The p-values are flat, as in 6.2: each bar holds about five percent of the studies, and the pink bar, the first, is the false alarms.

Claim number four comes from 6.5. A study has power, the share of real effects it sees. Change one line. Subtract one hundred milliseconds from the tea group, so tea really speeds people up. The seed is the same, so these are the same people. Now 5,473 of 10,000 studies say yes. The power is 54.73 percent. In 6.5, the simulation counted 5,353 with a z test, which takes the spread of the pot as known. Here the t-test must estimate it. A different test, a different seed, a slightly different count. The p-values now pile up near zero, and the pink bar towers over the others.

Now the part that matters most. Run the machine again with seed ninety thousand one hundred. The means average 424.212, with a spread of 35.140. The same, to the last digit. Change the seed to ninety thousand one hundred and four, and the mean moves to 422.024, a shift of about two milliseconds, with a spread of 34.108. The pile has the same shape, in another place.

One last seed. Thirteen hundred is the seed that 1.3 used. The code prints 424.079 and 33.753, which round to 424.1 and 33.8. That is exactly what 1.3 showed on screen. The code did not copy that number. It recomputed it from the seed. This is why the seed is part of the result. A number from a simulation, without its seed and its versions, cannot be checked. Try it yourself. Change the seed, change the size of the ladle, and see which statements of the series still stand.

If you can simulate a study, you can check any claim of this series. Write the world, run it many times, and count. To remember: a simulation is a result with a seed, so keep the seed, the versions, and the margin of error. Shuffle it

A permutation test is written by hand in nine lines and checked against SciPy. Shuffling labels gives p of 0.0567 by hand and 0.0554 with SciPy. Flipping signs gives 0.0176 in both, but one word, independent, changes it to 0.7286. The bootstrap of a median gives 332 to 437.5 twice. Writing it yourself forces you to decide what to shuffle.

This is the second bonus episode, and again it teaches nothing new. Three tools from chapter fourteen, the label shuffle, the sign flip and the bootstrap, written by hand. Then the ready-made versions in SciPy, and a check that both give the same answer. Every line on screen was run, and every number under it was printed by that run. You need the basics of Python, and episode B.1.

First, the tools. These are the versions of B.1: Python three point twelve point twelve, NumPy two point five point five, SciPy one point eighteen point one. I read the Python package index again today: these are the latest NumPy and SciPy. The release notes say that bootstrap arrived in SciPy one point seven, and permutation test in one point eight.

Tool number one comes from episode 14.2: shuffle the labels. Here are the forty people of the tea study, rebuilt from the same pot and the same seed as in 6.2. The first twenty drank tea, and tea is a hundred milliseconds faster. The observed difference is minus 105.05 milliseconds. Episode 14.2 rounded it to minus 105.1.

Now the test, in nine lines. A generator with seed ninety thousand two hundred, ten thousand shuffles, and an array to hold them. In the loop, one line decides everything. It shuffles the forty reaction times, and the first twenty become the tea group again. Then the difference of the two means. After the loop, b counts the shuffles at least as far from zero as ours, on either side. And the p-value is b plus one, over m plus one.

Run it. The code prints b equals 566, and p equals 567 over 10,001: 0.0567. In 14.2, b was 560 and p was 0.0561. They differ, as they should: the seed is different, and 14.2 had shown that ten thousand shuffles wander between 0.056 and 0.063. One more line prints the margin of a count like this one: plus or minus 0.0045.

Here is the curtain, drawn from the array the code computed. Every bar comes from the same forty reaction times. It is centred on zero, with a spread of 55.2 milliseconds, against 55.7 in 14.2.

Now SciPy. The function permutation test takes the two groups, a statistic, and the number of shuffles: the same ten thousand, and the same seed. It prints 0.0554. By hand 0.0567, SciPy 0.0554: a gap of 0.0013, well inside the margin. They cannot match to the last digit. SciPy draws its shuffles its own way, and its two-sided rule is not mine: it doubles the smaller one-sided p-value, where I counted both tails together. It does use the same plus one.

Tool number two comes from 14.3: flip the signs. The ten runners, with the seed of 14.3. Their differences are stored in tenths of a second, as whole numbers, so every tie is exact. The mean saved is 0.95 seconds. Here is the line that matters. This table lists every pattern of signs, 1,024 of them, and one sum for each. We flip signs, and we keep each runner.

Count the patterns at least as far from zero as ours: 18 of 1,024. So p is 18 over 1,024, 0.0176. No plus one this time, because we listed every pattern, our own included. 14.3 showed the same 18, and rounded 0.0176 to 0.018. The curtain of these 1,024 sums is narrow, with a spread of 0.49 seconds, as in 14.3.

SciPy has an argument for this: permutation type, samples. It keeps each pair together, and swaps old and new inside the pair, which is the same as flipping a sign. It is exact too, since 1,024 is fewer than the default number of resamples. It prints 0.0176, the same digits as my loop.

Now change one word. Guess first: same ten runners, same statistic. Will p stay near 0.02? Permutation type independent is the default. It pools all twenty times, and deals them again, ignoring who is who. Same ten runners, same statistic, and p becomes 0.7286: the wrong shuffle of 14.3, with the same 134,620 deals out of 184,756. 0.0176 against 0.7286, and one word apart. By hand, you must write what you shuffle. The ready-made function asks it as an argument, and if you leave it out, it answers independent for you, with no warning.

Tool number three comes from 14.6: the bootstrap of a median. The thirty people of 14.6, with their median of 372.5 milliseconds. Here is the line that matters. It draws thirty people from the sample, with replacement. A list of ten thousand of these medians, then the percentile function, which cuts 2.5 percent on each side.

It prints 332 to 437.5. That is exactly the interval of 14.6, with another seed. This is not magic. The pile holds only 90 different values, so the ends land on the same ones from one seed to the next. The pile itself has a median of 372.5 and a spread of 27.8 milliseconds, against 28.0 in 14.6.

SciPy's bootstrap function, with the percentile method and the same seed, gives 332 to 437.5 again. This time it is the very same pile: with the same seed, its ten thousand medians equal mine, one by one. SciPy's default method is another one, called BCa. It differs in the last step, and here it gives 332 to 437.5 too. On other data it could differ.

So, side by side. The label shuffle: 0.0567 by hand, 0.0554 with SciPy. The sign flip: 0.0176 twice. The bootstrap: 332 to 437.5 twice.

A permutation test fits in nine lines, and the bootstrap in a few more. Remember: writing them yourself forces you to decide what you shuffle or resample, and SciPy gives the same answers when you give it the same answer to that question. This is the fourth and last bonus episode, and it teaches nothing new. We take the eight schools of 16.6, fit them with PyMC, a library that samples a Bayesian model, and check the fit with ArviZ. Three earlier episodes, replayed with real code: 16.3 for the priors, 16.4 for the diagnostics, 16.6 for the model. Every line on screen was run, and every number under it was printed by that run. You need the basics of Python, and episodes B.1 to B.3.

First, the tools. Python three point twelve point twelve and NumPy two point five point five are those of B.1 to B.3. Three are new: PyMC six point four point zero, which samples the model, ArviZ one point three point zero, which reads the samples, and matplotlib three point eleven point two, only needed by PyMC's progress bar. I read the Python package index again today: these are the latest. ArviZ changed a lot between versions, so every function called here is one that this version documents.

Part one. The data are Rubin's eight schools, as in 16.6. Each school ran a coaching programme for the SAT, and estimated its gain in points. School A gained 28, with a standard error of 15. School C seems to have lost 3, with a standard error of 16. Two lists are all the data the model needs: the estimates, y, and the standard errors, se.

Now the model, written the way it is drawn. The first line gives mu, the overall level, a prior. The second gives tau, the spread between schools: a flat prior, up to 80, as in 16.6. The third line says each school's true effect, theta, comes from a curve centred on mu, with width tau. The fourth says each estimate is that true effect, plus noise of known size, and the estimates are the observed data.

Before any sampling, the check of 16.3: what do these priors say before the data? Draw ten thousand sets of the eight school effects from the priors alone. Under the wide priors, nine effects in ten fall between minus 178 and plus 180 points. Take a gain or a loss beyond one hundred points as implausible, on a test whose sections run from 200 to 800. That limit is my choice. Of the wide prior's draws, 35.3 percent are beyond it.

Now priors sized in points: mu centred on zero, give or take 25, and tau half-normal with a scale of 10. Nine effects in ten fall between minus 44 and plus 44, and only 39 draws out of 80,000 pass the limit.

Part two: sampling. The seed is in the call, 90402, and so are the four chains. The first thing to read is not the answer, it is the divergences. A divergence is a step where the sampler fails in a sharply curved part of the posterior, and even a few can bias the answer. Here: 189 divergences out of 4,000 draws.

Now the diagnostics of 16.4. For mu, R-hat is 1.03 and the effective sample size is 485. For tau, R-hat is 1.10, and the effective sample size is 28: four thousand draws are worth 28. On the plot, each dot is a draw, and the pink ones diverged. They sit where tau is small. And not one draw has tau below 0.9: the sampler cannot get into the narrow part. The model we drew does not sample well.

The remedy changes how the model is written, not what it means. Draw a standard normal z for each school, and compute theta as mu plus tau times z. Same model, same data, seed 90403. Divergences: zero out of 4,000. Every R-hat is 1.00. For tau the effective sample size is 1,636, for mu 2,083, and for the eight schools between 2,821 and 4,306. The checks pass. On the same plot, the narrow part fills: 4 percent of the draws have tau below 0.37, where the centred form had none.

Now the summary. The overall level, mu: median 7.8, with a 95 percent interval from minus 2.3 to 18. The spread, tau: median 5.2, from 0.25 to 19. In 16.6, the exact grid gave a median of 5.2, from 0.2 to 20.7. The medians agree. The upper end differs, and the sampler is the reason. On twelve other seeds, the median of tau stayed between 5.0 and 5.6, but its upper end ranged from 19.5 to 44.6. One of those seeds even had 11 divergences, with this same model.

Two numbers for mu may look contradictory: the median is 7.8, the mean is 7.7. 16.6 gave the mean, 7.9, and a different seed moves it by about a third of a point. Running each file a second time, with the same seed, printed the same lines.

The last lines print the posterior mean of each school: 11.4 for school A, 6.2 for school C. Here is the picture of 16.6, drawn from these arrays. Each white diamond is a school's own estimate, each blue dot its posterior mean, and the pink line is the overall level. School A moves from 28 to 11.4, school C from minus 3 to 6.2. All eight schools agree with 16.6 to within 0.2 points.

Now change the prior, as 16.3 asks. Same model, with the weakly informative priors, seed 90404. Divergences: 3. Not zero. The non-centred form is not a cure for everything, and we say so. The median of tau is now 4.1, and its interval runs from 0.24 to 14, against 5.2, and 0.25 to 19, before. School A is at 10.2, instead of 11.4. As 16.6 warned, with eight groups the width leans on its prior, so we report both.

Now the strong moment. Here is the model beside its drawing. Each argument in a line is an arrow. Mu and tau both point to theta: two arrows, one line. Theta and the known standard error both point to y: two more. One line per node, one argument per arrow. The model took four lines, but the work was before it, in the check of the priors, and after it, in the divergences, R-hat and effective sample size.

Remember: write the model the way you draw it. Check the priors before sampling, and read the divergences, R-hat and effective sample size before the answer. A seed makes the run repeatable, and a prior is a hypothesis to report.
