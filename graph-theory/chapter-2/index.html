PLATE 2.15:51

Walks, paths and cycles

Lewis Carroll's word ladder from HEAD to TAIL takes five steps. It becomes a graph when each word is a vertex and words differing by one letter are joined. Walks, paths, cycles and distance follow. The big dictionary gives 3,903 vertices, while a smaller one breaks Carroll's ladder, so choosing the vertices already chooses the answers.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In March 1879, a London magazine printed a strange little puzzle. The magazine was Vanity Fair. The puzzle came from Lewis Carroll, the author of Alice in Wonderland. He had invented it around Christmas 1877, to amuse two young friends. He called it Doublets. Today we call it a word ladder.

Here are the rules. Take two words of the same length. Change one letter at a time, always in the same place. Every step must be a real word. Carroll's own example turns HEAD into TAIL. HEAD, HEAL, TEAL, TELL, TALL, TAIL. Look at each step. One letter changes, and only one. Six words, five steps. There is no shortcut hiding in the rules. HEAD and TAIL differ in all four letters, so no single step joins them. Only the ladder gets you there.

This chapter is about getting around, and this puzzle is where it starts. It does not look like a graph. It looks like a game with a dictionary. But watch what happens when we forget the letters and keep only the structure. Let each word be a vertex. Join two words by an edge when they differ in exactly one letter. The rules have become a drawing. A ladder is now a trip along the edges, from HEAD to TAIL. And there is the hidden graph, peeking out from behind the puzzle.

The real graph is much bigger than the ladder. HEAD has twelve neighbours. BEAD, DEAD, LEAD, MEAD and READ change the first letter. HEAL, HEAP, HEAR, HEAT, HEED, HELD and HERD change one of the others. TAIL has sixteen neighbours. So at every word, the ladder has a choice, and a wrong choice leads away from the goal.

Now let us learn to move on a graph. Every trip in this chapter will be a walk, a path, or a cycle. A delivery round, a message passed along, a signal crossing the brain: they all fit. One thing first. Our edges have no direction, so HEAL leads to HEAD just as HEAD leads to HEAL. A ladder can be read in either direction. The graph we use is a small invented one, a corkboard with seven pegs. A walker starts at P, and unrolls a pink thread from peg to peg. This is a walk: a list of vertices where each one is joined to the next by an edge. A walk is allowed to go back. This one passes through Q twice, and uses the edge from Q to R twice. Its length is the number of edges it uses, which is seven. Now ask for something stricter. A path never visits the same vertex twice. From P to U, the thread goes P, Q, R, U, and never crosses itself. Many paths can join the same two vertices. Here is another, with five edges. The distance between two vertices is the length of the shortest path between them. Between P and U, the distance is three. Notice why a shortest walk is always a path. If a walk visits a vertex twice, cut out the loop in between, and the walk gets shorter. So the walk of seven edges was a long way round.

One more word. A cycle is a path that comes back to its start. Q, R, S, T, and back to Q: four edges, and the thread is tied in a loop. Word graphs are full of cycles. HEAD, DEAD, READ and back to HEAD is a cycle of three. Any two of those words differ only in the first letter, so all three are joined. HEAD, HEAL, HELL, HELD and back to HEAD is a cycle of four. The ladder from Carroll, on the other hand, is a path: six different words, five edges. So the ladder has length five.

What is the shortest ladder between two words? Try LOVE to HATE. LOVE, LAVE, LATE, HATE. So the distance is three. It is not the only way. There are three shortest ladders, and they tie. Now back to HEAD and TAIL. Is Carroll's ladder the shortest possible? With a big dictionary, it is not. A computer finds a ladder of only four steps. Carroll asked for words such as might be used in good society, so we will leave that one off the screen. Carroll's ladder is still a fine one. It is one of thirty-nine ladders with five steps.

Which dictionary did the computer use? The big one is called ENABLE, a public word list made for word games. It holds 172,823 words, and 3,903 of them have four letters. That gives a graph with 3,903 vertices and 21,002 edges. On average, a word has almost eleven neighbours. Some words have no neighbour at all, sixty-five of them, and they float alone. Now try a smaller dictionary. It is the ten thousand most frequent words in Google's web text. Of these, only 1,100 have four letters. The graph shrinks to 1,100 vertices and 2,314 edges. A word now has about four neighbours. And HEAL and TEAL are missing. Carroll's ladder breaks in two places. The shortest ladder from HEAD to TAIL now needs five steps, through other words.

Some ladders disappear altogether. That is the strange part. The words are all still there, but the words between them are gone. LAZY and BUSY are both in the small dictionary. But LAZY sits on an island of two words, and BUSY on an island of five. No ladder joins them. In the big dictionary, a ladder of six steps does. The rule is the same, the game is the same, and the answers are not. Choosing the vertices, and the rule that joins them, is already choosing the answers. We will meet this idea again in chapter fourteen, when data become networks.

To sum up. A walk may wander and revisit. No vertex repeats on a path. A cycle comes back to its start. The distance is the length of the shortest path. And a graph is only as good as the way it was built. Next time, ripples spread from a word, and breadth-first search finds the shortest ladder for us.

PLATE 2.25:59

Ripples: breadth-first search

Breadth-first search spreads like ripples on a pond, using a queue so each vertex gets the number of the ring that reaches it first: its distance. Following who found whom gives a shortest path, and untouched vertices are unreachable. It finds an 11-step way out of a maze, and in the word graph TAIL lies in ring four from HEAD.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the newspaper kiosk, the word ladder is still pinned to the board. Last time we asked for the shortest ladder from HEAD to TAIL, and a computer found one in four steps. But how does it look? It does not try every ladder. It does something much calmer. It drops a pebble.

Picture a pond, seen from above. A few lily pads, joined by little bridges. This pond is invented, and so is its graph. Each pad is a vertex, each bridge an edge. Drop the pebble on the pad called A.

A ring spreads out. The first ring touches the neighbours of A: B, C and D. The second ring touches the neighbours of the neighbours: E, F, G and H. The third ring reaches I and J. The fourth ring reaches K, and the ripples stop. Four rings in all.

Here is the key. Every pad gets the number of the ring that reaches it first. That number is its distance from A, the length of the shortest path. E has the number two, because two bridges separate it from A. K has the number four.

Notice that a bridge inside a ring changes nothing. E and F are joined, but both sit in ring two. A bridge can lead outwards, or sideways. It never leads two rings back.

Now look at L and M. The ripples never reach them. They live on an island, with no bridge to the rest of the pond. A pad that no ring ever touches cannot be reached at all.

A computer cannot watch water, so it keeps a queue. A queue is a waiting line: first in, first out. Write the start in the line, with distance zero. Then repeat one rule. Take the pad at the front of the line. Look at each of its neighbours. A neighbour never seen before gets a distance one larger, remembers who found it, and joins the back of the line.

Step one. Take A. Its neighbours B, C and D are new. They get distance one, and join the line.

Step two. Take B. Its neighbours are A, E and F. A is already known, so only E and F join the line, at distance two.

Step three. Take C. F is already in the line, so only G joins.

Step four. Take D. G is known, and only H is new.

The next seven steps follow the same rule. Pads I and J join, then K. Some steps find nothing new at all, and that is fine. After eleven steps the line is empty, and the search is over.

Why do rings come out? Because a line keeps its order. Everything at distance one waits ahead of everything at distance two, which waits ahead of everything at distance three. No pad is ever taken before a closer pad. That is what breadth means: finish a whole ring before starting the next one.

How much work is this? Little. Each pad enters the line once, and each bridge is looked at from both of its ends, no more. So the work grows only with the number of pads and bridges. Listing every possible ladder, by contrast, quickly becomes hopeless. Going deep first, as far as possible before turning back, is another habit, and it does not give rings.

Each pad remembered who found it. Start from K, and follow those memories backwards. K was found by I, I by E, E by B, and B by A. Read in the other direction, that is a shortest path: A, B, E, I, K. Four bridges. Draw every pad's finder, and you get a tree, the tree of shortest paths from A.

So one run gives three answers. The distance from A to every pad. A shortest path to any pad. And which pads can be reached at all.

Now a place where this really matters. Here is a maze, seen from above, with a door at each end. It is invented, with 40 cells. Make every junction a vertex, every dead end a vertex, and every corridor an edge. A maze is a graph.

Put the pebble down at the entrance. The ripples flood the corridors, ring after ring, and split wherever a corridor splits. The first ripple to reach the exit has taken the shortest way out. Follow the memories back, and the route appears: 11 steps. But look at the cost. By then, the ripples had already flooded 37 of the 40 cells.

Now back to the word ladder. Take the big dictionary, with 3,903 four-letter words. Let the pebble fall on HEAD. Ring one holds 12 words. Then the rings swell: 100, 355, 899. TAIL is in ring four. The widest ring is ring five, with 1,564 words, and the ripples keep going for fourteen rings in all. Ninety-six words are never touched. All of them sit on islands. Run the same search on the small dictionary, and TAIL falls into ring five. The pebble is the same, but the pond is not.

Who invented this? Nobody quite agrees, because it kept being reinvented. The usual histories credit Konrad Zuse, who described it in 1945, in a doctoral thesis on his programming language, Plankalkül. The thesis was rejected, and not published until 1972. In 1959, Edward Moore published it again, to find the shortest path out of a maze. In 1961, Lee turned it into a way to route wires on circuit boards.

And rings are everywhere. The Erdős number counts how far a mathematician stands from Paul Erdős, in the network of co-authors. The Bacon number does the same for actors, around Kevin Bacon. Both are just rings of ripples, around one person.

To sum up. Ripples spread ring by ring. A queue keeps the rings in order. Each pad takes the number of its ring, and that is its distance. The memories give a shortest path, and pads that are never touched are out of reach. But ripples count bridges, not kilometres. Next time, roads get lengths, and a fire has to do the work.

PLATE 2.37:42

When links have lengths: Dijkstra

When roads have lengths, counting steps fails: a route of three roads and eight kilometres beats a direct nine. Dijkstra's algorithm imitates a fire, always settling the closest unsettled town and updating its neighbours. It works because lengths only add, so negative weights break it, and strengths must first become distances. One over the weight and one minus the weight can disagree.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the newspaper kiosk, next to the word puzzles, hangs a road map. Unfold it. Towns, roads, and on every road a small number: its length in kilometres. The question is the oldest one of any journey. What is the shortest way from here to there?

You already know how to see the graph. Each town is a vertex. Each road is an edge. And each edge carries its length as a weight. This map is invented, but the problem is real. A much bigger version of it is solved every time a phone gives you directions.

Last time, ripples found shortest paths. The first ring reached the neighbours, the second ring the neighbours of neighbours. But ripples count edges, not kilometres. Look at the town on the far right, T. One ring is enough to reach it, because a road goes there directly. That road is nine kilometres long. Now take the long way round: two, then four, then two. Three roads, eight kilometres. The path with more edges is shorter. Counting steps is no longer enough.

So let us change the picture. Light a fire in the starting town, S. Let it burn along every road at the same steady speed, one kilometre per second. The fire does not choose. It runs down every road at once. When a road forks, the flames split. When two flames meet on a road, they stop. Watch which town catches fire, and when.

A catches fire at two seconds. B at five, even though a road leads straight from S to B. That road is six kilometres long, and the flame that went through A got there first. C burns at six. D at seven. And T at eight, by the long way round, a full second before the flame on the direct road could arrive. E is last, at nine.

Here is the key. Each town catches fire at the exact moment equal to its distance from S. The fire is a perfect shortest path machine, because the first flame to arrive is the one that took the shortest route. And the roads used by the first flames form a tree: the tree of shortest paths from S.

A computer cannot watch flames. But it can follow the fire in its head. At each moment, it only needs to know one thing: which town will burn next. That is the idea of an algorithm published in 1959 by a young Dutch programmer, Edsger Dijkstra.

The algorithm sorts the towns into three groups. Towns that have already burnt are settled: their distance is final. We draw them in ink. Towns reached by a road from a settled town form the frontier. Each one has a provisional distance, the best found so far. We draw them in yellow. All the other towns are not reached yet. We draw them pale.

Then the algorithm repeats a single rule. Among the frontier towns, settle the one with the smallest provisional distance. Then look along its roads, and update its neighbours. If going through the newly settled town is shorter than what a neighbour had, write down the shorter distance.

Let us run it. Step one. S is settled at zero. Its neighbours enter the frontier: A at two, B at six, and T at nine.

Step two. The smallest is A, at two. Settle it. Through A, B costs two plus three, which is five. That beats six, so B is updated. C enters the frontier at six.

Step three. Settle B, at five. Through B, D enters at seven. C through B would cost seven, which is worse than six, so nothing changes.

Step four. Settle C, at six. Through C, T costs six plus two, which is eight. That beats nine. T is updated, and the direct road is dropped.

Step five. Settle D, at seven. E enters the frontier at nine.

Step six. Settle T, at eight. Through T, E would cost twelve, so nine stays.

Step seven. Settle E, at nine. The frontier is empty, and every distance is final.

Look at the order: two, five, six, seven, eight, nine. It is exactly the order in which the towns caught fire. The algorithm is the fire, jumping from town to town instead of crawling along every road. And the highlighted roads form the same tree of shortest paths.

Why can a settled distance never change? Take the frontier town with the smallest provisional distance. Any other route to it would have to leave the settled towns through some other frontier town, which is already at least as far away. From there, the remaining roads can only add length. They can never take any away. So nothing can beat it. Remember that word, add. It will matter in a minute.

The story of this algorithm is famous. In 1956, Dijkstra was shopping in Amsterdam with his fiancée. Tired, they sat down on a café terrace for a cup of coffee. There, without pencil or paper, he designed the algorithm. He later called it a twenty minute invention. He needed it for the public demonstration of a new computer, and he wanted a question anyone could understand, such as the shortest way to travel from Rotterdam to Groningen. He published it three years later, in a paper of three pages. The same short paper also builds the cheapest network that connects every town, which we will meet at the end of this chapter.

Now the catch. The proof used one fact: going further along a road can only add distance. Every weight must be a length, and a length is never negative. What happens if one weight is negative, like the anticorrelation between two brain regions that we met with weighted graphs?

Here is a tiny map of one-way roads. From S, a road of two leads to X, and a road of three leads to Y. From Y to X, the road has a weight of minus two. Dijkstra settles X first, at two, because two is the smallest. Then Y, at three. But the route through Y reaches X at three minus two, which is one. X was already settled, and the algorithm never looks back. It answers two, when the truth is one. And nothing in the algorithm checks, so it gives no warning at all. Negative weights need other algorithms, such as Bellman and Ford's.

There is a subtler trap. In many networks, a weight is not a length at all. It is a strength: how often two people talk, or how strongly two brain regions are correlated. A bigger number means closer, not further. Feed strengths to Dijkstra, and it will avoid your strongest links as if they were long detours. The weights must first be turned into distances.

One simple way is to take one over the weight. Another is one minus the weight. Both make strong links short. But they do not agree. Take a direct link of strength one half, and a detour through two links of strength eight tenths. With one over the weight, the direct link wins: two, against two and a half. With one minus the weight, the detour wins: one half, against four tenths. Same network, same algorithm, different shortest path.

This matters well beyond road maps. In chapter six, several measures of importance are built on shortest paths. Change the transformation, and you change which nodes look central. So the choice must be stated, and justified, every time.

To sum up. When links have lengths, count kilometres, not steps. Dijkstra's algorithm follows an imaginary fire. It always settles the closest town that is not settled yet, then updates its neighbours. It is correct because lengths only add up. And that is also its limit. Weights must never be negative, and strengths must become distances first.

Next time, a simpler question. Forget the shortest route: can you get there at all? Some towns live on islands.

PLATE 2.45:55

Islands: connected components

A connected component is a group of vertices that can reach one another, found with one search from each unvisited vertex. The 3,903-word graph has 78 components, one giant holding 3,807 words. With arrows, weak and strong connection differ. Keeping only worm links of at least five synapses leaves 57 pieces, so count components before measuring anything else.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we followed a fire along the roads. Today, a simpler question. Forget how far. Can you get there at all?

Pinned beside the newspapers, there is a ferry chart of an archipelago. Ferries sail between some islands, and not between others. Pick any island, and ride every ferry you can. Then ride the ferries from the islands you reached, and keep going. You end up on a group of islands. But never on all of them.

Each island is a vertex, and each ferry line is an edge. This archipelago is invented, but the question is real. A group like the one you reached has a name: a connected component. Inside it, any two vertices are joined by a path. And no edge leaves it.

Here are four components: seven islands, five, three, and one island alone. Every vertex lives in exactly one component. No island belongs to two groups. And an island with no ferry at all is a component of one.

How does a computer find them? It reuses the search from episode two point two. Take the islands in alphabetical order. Island A has no lantern yet, so start a search from A. The boat visits B, D, C, E, F and G, and lights a lantern on every island it touches. No ferry leads further. That is component one, with seven islands.

The next island without a lantern is H. Search from H: five islands. Then M: three islands. Then P. No ferry at all, so component four is a single island. Four searches, four components. Each island was visited once, and each ferry looked at once. So the whole count takes time proportional to the vertices plus the edges. It is the textbook method, described by Hopcroft and Tarjan in 1973.

Now add one ferry, from G to H. Two groups merge into one group of twelve islands. Cut that ferry, and they fall apart again. One line can be all that holds two groups together.

Now a real graph: the four-letter words of episode two point one. Two words are joined when they differ in exactly one letter. The big dictionary, ENABLE, has three thousand nine hundred and three words. It falls into seventy-eight components.

One of them is huge. It holds three thousand eight hundred and seven words, ninety-seven and a half percent of the dictionary. The other seventy-seven are tiny. Sixty-five are single words with no neighbour at all: kiwi, lynx, void, once. Change any one letter, and you never reach another word in the list.

Some islands hold two words, like onyx and oryx. One holds three: icon, ikon and iron. The biggest of the small islands holds five: guru, judo, kudo, kudu and kuru.

The huge piece has a name too: the giant component. Many real networks look like this, one giant piece, with a scatter of small islands around it. Why the giant piece appears is the question of chapter nine.

The smaller dictionary, the ten thousand most frequent words, has eleven hundred four-letter words. That makes one hundred and fifty-six components, and its giant piece holds seventy-nine and a half percent. Same rule, different dictionary, different islands. Choosing the vertices is already choosing the answer.

Now add arrows. On a one-way ferry chart, connected can mean two things. Weakly connected means that if you forget the arrows, everything is joined. Strongly connected means that from every island you can reach every other, and come back.

Here, three islands sail round a loop, and three more sail round a second loop. One one-way ferry leads from the first loop into the second, and another leads to a dead end. Forget the arrows, and this is one weak component of seven islands. Respect them, and it breaks into three strong components: two loops, and the dead end alone. You can go from the first loop to the second. You can never come back. Tarjan found a linear-time method for strong components in 1972.

The worm from episode one point two shows both. Its chemical synapses join two hundred and seventy-nine neurons. Forget the direction, and every neuron sits in one piece. Respect it, and a strong core of two hundred and thirty-seven neurons remains, eighty-five percent. The other forty-two neurons make forty-one tiny strong components: forty single neurons, and one pair.

Now a trap. In chapter fourteen, we will build networks from tables of numbers by keeping only the strongest links. Watch what that does. Take the worm, and keep only the connections with at least five synapses. The worm is a wiring diagram, not a correlation table, but the mechanism is the same.

Three hundred and eighty-two connections out of two thousand one hundred and ninety-four survive. The network falls into fifty-seven pieces. The biggest holds two hundred and nineteen neurons. Fifty-two neurons stand alone. Raise the threshold to ten synapses, and there are one hundred and seventy-eight pieces, and the biggest holds only forty-nine neurons.

Why does this matter? Between two components, the distance is infinite. The average distance of episode two point three then means nothing, and neither do many measures built on it. So before you measure anything else, count the components.

To sum up. A connected component is a group of vertices that can reach each other, with no edge leaving it. One search from a vertex without a lantern finds one component, and one sweep finds them all. With arrows, weak ignores the direction, and strong respects it. Real networks often have one giant piece and a scatter of islands. Count them first.

Next time, the leanest connected graph there is: one without a single cycle. A tree.

PLATE 2.55:12

Trees

A tree is a connected graph with no cycle, and with n vertices it always has n minus one edges. Between any two vertices lies exactly one path, cutting an edge splits it, and adding one closes a loop. Labelled trees number n to the power n minus two. A family tree is a tree only until two branches meet.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended on a promise. The leanest connected graph there is. Today we keep it.

On the kiosk shelf hangs a paper mobile. Beads on threads, each one tied to the bead above. This one is invented, so we can take it apart.

Count the beads. Seven. Count the threads. Six. Every bead hangs from the top by some chain of threads, so the mobile is connected. And no thread loops back on itself, so it has no cycle. A connected graph with no cycle is called a tree.

Seven beads, six threads. Is that luck? No. Grow the mobile from a single bead. Each new bead has to be tied on, or it floats alone. One thread is enough. A second thread would close a loop. So every new bead brings exactly one thread. A tree with n vertices always has n minus one edges.

You can also run it backwards. Look at the mobile again. Some beads hang by a single thread: D, F and G. Those are the leaves. Every tree with at least two beads has a leaf. Snip it off, and what is left is still a tree, with one bead and one thread fewer. Peel leaf after leaf, and you end with a single bead and no thread at all.

Now pick two beads, D and G. Walk from D to G. D, B, E, G. Three threads. There is no other way. In a tree, between any two vertices there is one path, and only one. Two different paths would make a cycle.

Take the scissors. Snip any thread, and the mobile falls in two pieces. We tried all six threads. Always two pieces.

Now tie a new thread between E and F. A loop closes: E, B, A, C, F, and back to E. Five threads in the loop. Of the fifteen threads that were missing, every one closes exactly one loop.

So a tree is perfectly balanced. Take one edge away and it breaks. Add one and it loops. Any one of these facts is enough on its own. Connected, with n minus one edges. Or no cycle, with n minus one edges. Or exactly one path between any two vertices.

You have already drawn a tree without saying so. Remember the search of episode two point two? Each vertex remembers who found it. Here is a graph with seven vertices and nine edges. Search from A.

Every new vertex is found through one edge. Six vertices are found, so six edges are kept. The other three were never needed. The search wove a tree out of a tangle. Every connected graph hides one.

Trees are everywhere. A folder on a computer: each folder sits inside exactly one other, so every file has one route from the top. A company chart, where each person has one boss. An evolutionary tree, where in the simplest picture branches split and never rejoin. In each, between two items there is one route.

How many trees are there? Take four labelled beads, A, B, C and D. List every tree on them. Choose three of the six possible threads, and keep the choices with no loop. There are sixteen trees. Twelve are chains of four. Four are stars, one centre and three arms.

Sixteen is four to the power four minus two. In general, n labelled vertices make n to the power n minus two trees. Five vertices, one hundred and twenty-five trees. Ten vertices, one hundred million. That is how many ways there are to wire ten points with no loop at all. The formula carries the name of Cayley, who published it in eighteen eighty-nine. But Borchardt had already proved it in eighteen sixty.

Now a trap. The family tree. Put yourself at the bottom. Two parents above you, four grandparents, eight great-grandparents. Each generation doubles. Ten generations back, that is one thousand and twenty-four slots. Thirty generations back, more than a billion. Drawn like this, with every slot its own vertex, it really is a tree. Fifteen vertices, fourteen edges.

But people share ancestors. Suppose your parents are first cousins. Then two of your great-grandparents sit in two slots each. Draw every person only once, and slide the copies together.

Count again. Thirteen people, but still fourteen edges. A tree has one edge fewer than vertices. Thirteen vertices and fourteen edges cannot be a tree. Two cycles have closed. You have six great-grandparents, not eight.

This is no curiosity. Alfonso the Twelfth of Spain had four great-grandparents, where most people have eight. Genealogists call it pedigree collapse.

The name family tree hides the real graph. It is a graph with cycles. And that is the thread of this whole series: recognise the graph that hides behind a name.

To sum up. A tree is a connected graph with no cycle. With n vertices, it has n minus one edges. Between two vertices there is one path. Cut an edge and it splits. Tie one more and a loop closes. And a family tree is a tree only until two branches meet.

Next time, the cheapest network that joins every town. It will be a tree.

PLATE 2.66:51

The cheapest network: spanning trees

The cheapest network connecting every town is a tree, the minimum spanning tree. Kruskal's rule finds it: take lines from cheapest, skipping any that close a cycle. On nine Moravian towns it needs 170 kilometres of cable instead of 574. It is not the tree of shortest routes from one town, which needs 240, and brain networks use it as a backbone.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we met the tree. The leanest connected graph there is. Today we put a price on it.

On the kiosk wall hangs a folded road map. Unfold it. Nine towns of Moravia. You must bring electricity to every one of them. A cable between two towns costs money, and the longer it is, the more it costs. Which cables do you pick, so that every town is connected and the bill is as small as it can be?

This does not look like a graph problem. It is one. Towns are vertices. Possible cables are edges. Every edge carries a price.

That was the question of Otakar Borůvka, a young mathematician in Brno, in 1926. A friend at the West Moravian Power Plants had told him about it. Borůvka solved it, and published it twice. Once in a Czech mathematics journal, once in plain terms, for electrical engineers. His own example had forty towns.

We cannot use his numbers, so today's map is a stand-in. Nine real towns, and the price of a cable is the straight-line distance, in kilometres. Illustrative costs, on a real map.

Now draw every cable we allow. Nineteen lines, between towns that are neighbours on the map. Each gets a price tag. Laying all nineteen would take about 574 kilometres of cable. Which ones can we leave out?

Look at three towns that are all joined to each other: Náměšť, Třebíč and Velké Meziříčí. Remove the dearest of the three lines, the one between Náměšť and Třebíč. The three towns are still connected, through the third. We saved twenty kilometres, and lost nothing.

That works every time. A line that closes a cycle connects nothing new. So the cheapest network that connects everything has no cycle at all. It is connected, and it has no cycle. It is a tree. With nine towns, it has eight lines.

Among all the trees that touch every town, we want the cheapest. It is called the minimum spanning tree. Spanning, because it reaches every vertex. There are far too many trees to compare. Yet there is a rule so simple that it feels like cheating.

Here it is. Sort the lines from the cheapest to the dearest. Go down the list. Take a line, unless it closes a cycle. That is all. Joseph Kruskal published it in 1956.

Step one. The cheapest line joins Velké Meziříčí to Třebíč, eighteen kilometres. No cycle yet. Keep it. Step two. Třebíč to Moravské Budějovice. Keep. Step three. Náměšť to Velké Meziříčí. Keep. Steps four and five. Brno to Ivančice, then Ivančice to Náměšť. Keep both.

Step six. Náměšť to Třebíč, twenty kilometres. But these two towns are already joined, through Velké Meziříčí. This line would close a cycle. Reject. The rule asks one question: are the two ends already connected? If they are, the line is useless.

Steps seven and eight add Tišnov and Žďár. Steps nine and ten close cycles. Step eleven adds Znojmo. That makes eight lines, and every town is connected. The eight tickets left can add nothing. We stop. The bill is 170 kilometres of cable, instead of 574. About thirty per cent.

Greedy rules usually fail. Here, it can be proved that this one never does.

The story is older than Kruskal. Borůvka solved it in 1926. In 1930, Vojtěch Jarník, in the same Brno journal, described another method: grow one tree from one town, always adding the cheapest line to a new town. Kruskal came in 1956. In 1957, Robert Prim found Jarník's method again, on his own, in the Bell System Technical Journal. And in 1959, Edsger Dijkstra solved this problem in the same three-page note as his shortest path from episode 2.3. Graham and Hell told this history in 1985.

Now a trap. Imagine the power station stands in Brno. Give every town its shortest route from Brno. We know how, it is Dijkstra from episode 2.3. It also gives a tree, a tree of shortest routes.

Put the two trees on the same map. Pink is the cheapest tree. Blue is the tree of shortest routes from Brno. Only three lines are shared. Five belong to the cheapest tree alone, and five to the shortest-route tree alone.

They are not the same tree. The shortest-route tree needs 240 kilometres of cable. The cheapest needs 170. That is seventy more. Why? The shortest-route tree wants every town close to Brno. The cheapest tree does not care about Brno at all. Take Znojmo. In the shortest-route tree it is 56 kilometres from Brno. Along the cheapest tree, the current would travel 124. Cheapest to build, or fastest from here. Two questions, two trees.

The same tree turns up in neuroscience. In a brain network, regions are vertices and links are edges. In 2014, Stam and colleagues proposed the minimum spanning tree as a backbone of a brain network. In 2015, Tewarie and colleagues described it as an unbiased method for brain network analysis.

Why a tree? Because it always has n minus one edges. Here are two simulated subjects, eight regions each. At the same correlation threshold, one network has twelve links, the other twenty-one. Hard to compare. Keep only the tree, and both have seven edges. The comparison no longer depends on density.

One detail. Our cables had costs, and small was good. Correlations are strengths, and big is good. So the useful tree keeps the strongest links. That is a maximum spanning tree. Or equivalently, turn each correlation r into a distance, one minus r, and take the minimum spanning tree. Episode 1.3 asked: distance, or strength? The tree comes out the same either way. Chapter 14 weighs what this method gains, and what it throws away.

To sum up. The cheapest network that connects everything is a tree. The cheapest one is the minimum spanning tree. Kruskal's rule finds it: take the cheapest line, unless it closes a cycle. And it is not the tree of shortest routes.
