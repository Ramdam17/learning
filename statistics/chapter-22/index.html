PLATE 22.17:09

The garden of forking paths, and preregistration

With no true effect, four defensible choices, outliers, log, covariates and window, make twenty-four paths, and three give p below 0.05. Freeing all choices makes 22.7 percent of empty studies significant. Even one test is risky if the path depends on the data, as in Borges's garden. Preregistration writes one path down, restoring 5.1 percent; exploratory findings need new data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the hedge maze. In chapter 15 we ran twenty tests on data where nothing happens, and about two studies in three found a false alarm. Today we run just one test. The danger is still there, and it hides in the choices made before the test.

Here is a study I invented, simulated, with no true effect at all. Forty people, in two groups of twenty. For each person, the alpha power of an EEG signal in two time windows, early and late. We also know each person's age and sex. Do the groups differ?

Before the test, the analyst makes four choices, and every one is defensible. Keep everyone, or drop the outliers beyond two and a half standard deviations. Analyse the raw power, or its logarithm, because power is skewed. Adjust for nothing, for age, or for age and sex. Use the early window, or the late one.

Count the paths. Two times two times three times two: twenty-four. Every path ends in a p-value.

Here is one simulated study, walked down all twenty-four paths. Guess before you see: how many paths find p below 0.05, when nothing is going on? Three of the twenty-four. The best has p equal to 0.033. I chose this study out of ten thousand because it has some. A paper could report that path, and tell a story about the late window. Look at the three that fired: all drop the outliers, take the log, and read the late window. Neighbouring paths agree, because they share the same forty people.

Now guess again. On ten thousand empty studies, how often does at least one of the twenty-four paths reach 0.05? One time in ten? One in four? One in two? We free the choices one at a time. One path, fixed in advance: 5.1 percent, the rate we promised. Free the window: 9.2 percent. Free the covariates too: 13.4. Free the exclusions: 17.6. Free the transformation as well: 22.7 percent. Nearly one empty study in four has a road to a significant result.

Each path alone is an honest test, about five percent. The union of paths is not. It is not 70.8 percent either, the figure chapter 15 gives for twenty-four independent tests, because every path re-analyses the same forty people, so the paths agree with each other a lot.

In 2011, Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn simulated this in Psychological Science. Their table, from 15,000 simulated samples of twenty people per group: two outcome variables, 9.5 percent. Ten more people per group after a first look, 7.7. Controlling for gender, 11.7. Dropping one of three conditions, 12.6. All four together: 60.7 percent, which the text rounds to 61. Their number is higher than ours because their four freedoms are different ones, in a different design. The two numbers need not match. They agree on the message: far above five.

You may say: I never tried twenty-four paths. I ran one test. Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken answered that this is not enough. Their working paper is from 2013, and a shorter version appeared in American Scientist in 2014. If the path you take depends on the data, so that with different data you would have done a different, equally reasonable analysis, then you are in the garden of forking paths, even if you walk only one. The name comes from a story by Borges, from 1941.

Nobody here is cheating: each choice feels natural once the data are on the screen. We simulate such an analyst. One test per study. The rules look at the data, never at a p-value. Take the window where the groups look further apart on the plot. Take the log if the histogram looks skewed. Drop a point that looks like an outlier. Adjust for age if the groups look unbalanced. Then run a single test. On ten thousand empty studies, 6.2 percent come out significant. Only a little above five, because our analyst has just four small habits. But it is above five, with one test per study. The multiple-testing problem of chapter 15 exists even when only one test is run.

The remedy is to close the maze before entering. Preregistration means writing down the research questions and the analysis plan before observing the outcomes, and then keeping to it. Nosek and colleagues described it in 2018 in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Here is the card. The question. The groups, the sample size and when we stop. The exclusion rule, with its number. The transformation. The covariates. The window. The test. In our simulation the card says: keep everyone, log power, age and sex, early window. One path. 5.1 percent. The promise is back.

Preregistration does not forbid exploring. It asks you to say which is which. Nosek and colleagues write that discovery science is vitally important, when it is clearly labelled. Here is an honest report for our study, illustrative. Planned test: p equal to 0.70, no difference. Exploratory, found after looking: the late window, outliers dropped, log power, p equal to 0.033, to be tested in new data. And that is the right next step. We took the best exploratory path of each of the 2,266 empty studies that had one, and ran it on fresh data. Only 119 were significant: 5.3 percent. A path found by exploring, once tested on new data, is just one more planned path. The new data are what turn a lead into a result.

Five more forks, in other fields, all illustrative. A reaction-time lab can cut trials beyond two, two and a half or three standard deviations: the card writes one cutoff. An observational study can adjust for age, income, smoking, or any mix: the card lists the covariates. A clinical trial can find that a drug works in women under fifty and not overall: the card names the subgroups, and every other one is exploratory. A lab can test after every ten participants and stop when p dips below 0.05: the card writes the sample size or the stopping rule. An imaging study can choose the region, or the electrode, where the effect looks biggest: the card names it before the data.

What if careful analysts, each with their own path, all analysed the same data? That is the next episode.

Remember: a p-value is valid for the path you would have taken whatever the data said, so write the path down before you look, and label everything else exploratory. Next time: many analysts, one dataset.

PLATE 22.27:06

Many analysts, one dataset

Twenty-nine teams analysed one soccer dataset on red cards and dark skin tone, with odds ratios from 0.89 to 2.93. In a simulated EEG multiverse of sixteen paths, estimates run from 0.51 to 0.94 and thirteen give p below 0.05. The error bar describes new people, not a new analyst, so report the multiverse, not one path.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the hedge maze. Last time, one analyst walked many paths through one dataset. Today there are many analysts, and each one chooses a path. Do they arrive at the same place?

In 2018, Silberzahn and colleagues gave one dataset to twenty-nine teams, sixty-one analysts in all. It held 2,053 soccer players and 146,028 pairs of a player and a referee, with the red cards given. The question: do referees give more red cards to dark-skin-toned players? Each team reported an odds ratio. One means no difference. Above one means more red cards for darker skin.

Guess before you see: how far apart do twenty-nine careful teams land? Here is Table 3 of the paper, one dot per team, with its ninety-five percent interval. The lowest is 0.89. The highest is 2.93, with an interval from 0.11 to 78.66, which says almost nothing. The median is 1.31. Twenty teams found a significant positive effect. Nine did not. The teams used regressions of every kind, from simple linear models to multilevel and Bayesian ones. The twenty-nine analyses used twenty-one different sets of covariates. Same data, same question, and the verdict depends on the team.

Brain imaging has even more choices. In 2020, Botvinik-Nezer and colleagues gave one fMRI dataset, from 108 people making risky gambles, to seventy teams. Each team tested nine hypotheses, each about activity in one brain region, and answered yes or no. No two teams used the same workflow. For hypothesis five, a drop in activity with losses, 84.3 percent of the teams said yes. For hypothesis one, a rise in activity with gains, in the same region, 37.1 percent said yes. For three other hypotheses, 5.7 percent. On average, twenty percent of the teams disagreed with the majority. And for every hypothesis, at least four different pipelines, all used in practice, gave a significant result.

What can one analyst do? In 2016, Steegen and colleagues proposed to run every reasonable analysis, not just one. They called it a multiverse analysis, over the ways of excluding, transforming and coding the data. Let us build one, in a simulated EEG study. Sixty people, thirty per group. Alpha power in an early and a late time window. Age and sex. This time a small true effect exists: 0.4 standard deviations on the log scale. We know it, because we built it.

Four defensible choices. Keep everyone, or drop the outliers. Raw power, or its logarithm. No covariates, or age and sex. The early window, or the late one. Two times two times two times two: sixteen paths.

Here is the multiverse of one dataset, sorted. Each row is a path: an estimate, in standard deviations, and its ninety-five percent interval. The estimates run from 0.51 to 0.94, one point eight times higher. Thirteen paths give a p-value below 0.05, as low as 0.0007. Three do not: 0.052, 0.053 and 0.057. A paper that shows only the top row says a clear yes. A paper that shows only the bottom row says almost. Both ran an honest analysis of the same sixty people. Why do the paths differ at all? One drops two people, one rescales, one adjusts, one reads another window.

Guess again: the sixteen estimates cover a stretch of the axis. Compared with the width of one path's error bar, is that stretch small, equal, or large? Now measure. Five thousand simulated datasets, the same true effect, sixteen paths each. The usual standard error of one path is 0.26. It says how far the estimate moves with new people. The standard deviation of the sixteen estimates, with the same people, is 0.16. Choices move the estimate sixty percent as much as sampling does. On average the sixteen span 0.51, about half the width of one error bar, 1.05. The error bar describes new people. It says nothing about a new analyst.

What about the verdict? In only three percent of the studies do all sixteen paths say yes. In thirty-eight percent, all say no. In fifty-nine percent, the verdict depends on the path. And in six percent, exactly one path out of sixteen says yes. A result that holds on one path only is a result about that path.

Which choice moves the estimate most? Averaged over the datasets, switching the window shifts it by 0.22, the most, because early and late are two different measurements. Exclusions: 0.11. Covariates: 0.10. Transformation: 0.09. Now turn one knob. Make the raw power more skewed, from a log spread of 0.3 to 1.5. Exclusions rise from 0.09 to 0.15. Transformation rises from 0.06 to 0.17. Covariates fall from 0.17 to 0.05. The weight moves between choices, and the total stays between sixty and sixty-nine percent of the standard error.

Four more forks, all illustrative. A reaction-time study can cut trials at two, two and a half or three standard deviations, use raw or log times, and summarise by the mean or the median: twelve paths. An EEG study can filter at 0.1 or 1 hertz, correct a baseline or not, and pick one of three windows: twelve paths. A clinical trial can adjust for four different sets of covariates, in an intention-to-treat or a per-protocol analysis: eight paths. A survey can code a five-point answer as a number or as agree and disagree, and treat don't know as missing or as the middle: four paths.

Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson call this display a specification curve, and add a joint test across all the specifications. Their 2020 abstract says analytical decisions certainly involve variability not reflected by standard errors.

So do not report one path. Report the multiverse, and say which choices move the answer. The bonus episodes redo this series at the keyboard, starting with B.1: Simulate it.

Remember: the error bar describes new people, not a new analyst, so a result that holds on one path alone is a result about that path. Next time: bonus B.1, Simulate it.
