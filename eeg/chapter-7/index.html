Why preprocess · and what it costs

Preprocessing resembles hopping between islands: each cleaning step brings the analysis closer but charges a toll in signal. High-pass filtering, trial rejection and component removal all risk removing or distorting brain activity. A 2023 study found that only filtering and bad-channel interpolation reliably helped its quality metric, so every step must earn its place, with before-and-after checks and a pipeline fixed in advance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter seven, the longest of the series: cleaning up. Before we pick up a single tool, one question deserves an episode of its own. Why preprocess at all, and what does it cost?

Picture an archipelago of small islands. Each island is a cleaning step: filtering, removing line noise, repairing channels, independent component analysis. To reach the treasure on the far side, the analysis, you hop from island to island. Every hop takes you closer, but every hop also costs a little: a toll, paid in signal.

Raw EEG contains the brain activity you want, mixed with things you do not want: blinks, eye movements, muscle, heartbeats, line noise, slow drifts, electrode problems. Preprocessing tries to reduce these, so that the effect you care about becomes easier to see and to test.

But every step can also remove or distort brain signal. A high pass filter removes drifts, and also part of the slow brain activity. Rejecting noisy trials removes artifacts, and also reduces the number of trials, and so statistical power. Independent component analysis removes blink components, and if a component also carries some brain activity, that goes too. An aggressive filter can even create waves that were not there, as episode seven point seven will show.

So cleaning is a trade off between the noise that remains and the signal that is lost. More cleaning is not automatically better.

In 2023, Arnaud Delorme tested this directly, in a paper titled "EEG is better left alone". He measured data quality as the percentage of channels showing a significant difference between two experimental conditions, shortly after the stimulus, on several public datasets. High pass filtering clearly helped. Interpolating bad channels helped too. But many other automated steps did not improve this measure, or even made it worse: re referencing and advanced baseline methods, rejecting segments or trials, and automatic removal of eye and muscle components did not reliably help.

This result has been discussed, and it does not mean that every step is useless. It depends on the metric, the data and the question. Artifact removal can be essential for connectivity, for frequency bands where muscle dominates, for single trial decoding, or for noisy recordings from children or patients. But it is a strong reminder: each step must earn its place.

How do you decide whether a step earns its place? Three habits help.

First, know what each step removes. Ask: which artifact does it target, what part of the signal does it touch, and what does it assume? The coming episodes answer these questions one by one.

Second, look before and after. Plot the raw data, the spectrum and the average response before and after each step. If you cannot see what a step changed, you do not know what it did.

Third, decide the pipeline in advance, and apply it identically to all participants and conditions. Tuning cleaning steps until an effect appears is a hidden form of analytic flexibility. Preregister the pipeline, or at least document every choice.

And remember: the best cleaning is good recording. Nothing in this chapter can bring back a saturated channel, a missing trigger, or a participant who fell asleep.

Here is the map of the chapter. First, the artifact zoo, physiological and technical, and how to read raw EEG with your own eyes. Then filters, line noise, bad channels and re referencing. Then independent component analysis in three episodes, followed by artifact subspace reconstruction, epoching and autoreject, other tools, and finally one pipeline in the right order.

Let's recap. Preprocessing reduces noise, and also costs signal. Cleaning is a trade off, not a race to remove as much as possible. Delorme's 2023 study found that high pass filtering and bad channel interpolation helped, while many automated steps did not reliably improve his metric. So know what each step removes, look before and after, decide the pipeline in advance, and record well in the first place.

Next time: the artifact zoo, part one, the physiological species.

PLATE 7.24:25

The artifact zoo I · Physiological

Physiological artifacts are species in a field guide, each identified by topography, spectrum and time course. Blinks are large smooth frontal bumps, saccades produce opposite-polarity steps at the sides of the eyes, and muscle gives fast broadband bursts at temporal sites. The heart gives sharp peaks about once per second, while pulse, sweat, tongue and body movement have their own signatures.

TRANSCRIPTEN

To clean EEG, you first need to recognise what you are cleaning. Today, we visit the first half of the artifact zoo: the physiological species, produced by the participant's own body.

Imagine a naturalist's field guide. Each animal has its own page, with three ways to identify it: where it lives, what it sounds like, and how it moves. Artifacts are the same. Each species has a typical topography, where it lives on the head; a typical spectrum, what it sounds like; and a typical time course, how it moves.

Our first species: the blink. The eye is an electrical dipole, positive at the front, the cornea, and negative at the back, the retina. When the eyelid closes, it slides over the cornea and changes the potential around the eyes. On the EEG, a blink is a large, smooth deflection lasting a few hundred milliseconds, strongest at the frontal pole electrodes, F P one and F P two, and decreasing towards the back of the head. Its amplitude can be many times larger than the brain signal.

Second: saccades, the rapid eye movements. When the eyes turn to the right, the cornea gets closer to the right side of the head. Electrodes near the right eye become more positive, and electrodes near the left eye more negative. So horizontal saccades show up as box like steps of opposite polarity at F seven and F eight, or at electrodes beside the eyes. At their onset, there is also a brief spike from the eye muscles, the saccadic spike potential, which we met in episode four point six.

Third: muscle. Contractions of the jaw, the forehead, the neck and the scalp produce fast, irregular activity with a broad spectrum, rising towards high frequencies, from about twenty hertz to several hundred. It is strongest at temporal electrodes over the chewing muscles, at frontal electrodes when frowning, and at the back of the head when the neck is tense.

Fourth: the heart. The electrical activity of the heart spreads through the body and can reach the scalp. It appears as small, sharp, regular peaks, about once per second, often clearer on one side, near the mastoids or the neck. A good clue: these peaks line up with the heartbeat if you record an electrocardiogram channel.

Fifth: pulse artifact. When an electrode sits over a small artery, each heartbeat moves it slightly. This produces a slow, rhythmic wave on that single channel, following each heartbeat with a short delay. Moving the electrode a little usually solves it.

Sixth: sweat. Sweat changes the electrical properties of the skin and creates very slow drifts, often over many channels, especially in a warm room or with an anxious participant. These live below one hertz, in the infraslow and delta range, as episode four point nine described.

Seventh: the tongue. The tip of the tongue is negative relative to its base, so moving it, while speaking, swallowing or licking the lips, produces slow potentials, the glossokinetic artifact, largest at frontal and temporal sites. Speech tasks are full of it.

And finally, movement of the whole body or head, which pulls cables and shifts electrodes, producing large, slow swings across many channels.

Notice the pattern. Each species has a home, a sound and a way of moving. Blinks: frontal, slow, single bumps. Saccades: lateral frontal, steps with opposite polarity. Muscle: temporal and frontal, high frequency, bursts. Heart: regular and sharp, about once per second. Pulse: one channel, rhythmic, after each beat. Sweat: slow drifts. Tongue: slow, frontal and temporal, during speech.

Recognising the species tells you which tool to use, as later episodes in this chapter will show, and also when not to bother: a few blinks outside your time window may not matter at all.

Let's recap. Physiological artifacts come from the participant's body: blinks, saccades, muscle, heart, pulse, sweat, tongue and movement. Identify each by three clues: topography, spectrum and time course. And remember that some species, like blinks and saccades, are huge compared with the brain signal.

Next time: the artifact zoo, part two, the technical species.

PLATE 7.34:31

The artifact zoo II · Technical

Technical artifacts come from equipment and the room, like a hum, a bumped cable and an unplugged microphone at a recorded concert. Line noise is a narrow spectral peak with harmonics, pops are steps on one channel, and flat or saturated channels carry no signal. Bridged electrodes share gel and look identical, creating fake connectivity, and a survey found them in over half of sessions.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Not all artifacts come from the body. Some come from the equipment and the environment. Today, we visit the second half of the zoo: the technical species.

Think of a concert recorded in an old hall. Besides the music, you hear the hum of the lights, the creak of a chair, a microphone cable being bumped, and one microphone that is not even plugged in. None of these come from the musicians, and each needs its own fix.

Our first technical species: line noise. Power lines and devices around the participant radiate at fifty hertz in Europe and many other countries, or sixty hertz in North America. The EEG picks this up, especially on electrodes with poor or unequal contact. In the spectrum, line noise appears as a sharp, narrow peak at fifty or sixty hertz, often with harmonics at twice and three times that frequency. In the raw signal, it looks like a thick, fuzzy band. Episode seven point eight compares the ways to remove it.

Second: electrode pops. When the contact between electrode and skin changes suddenly, for example as gel dries or the electrode moves, the voltage on that channel jumps abruptly, then slowly recovers. A pop is a step or a spike on a single channel, with no matching activity on its neighbours. That isolation is the giveaway: brain activity is never confined to one electrode.

Third: flat or dead channels. A broken wire, an unplugged electrode or a saturated amplifier input gives a channel with almost no variation, or a constant value. Saturation, which we met in episode one point seven, is especially treacherous: the signal clips at the edge of the range, and nothing can recover it.

Fourth: bridged electrodes. When too much gel spreads between two neighbouring electrodes, it connects them electrically. The two channels then record nearly identical signals. This is hard to see by eye, because both look like normal EEG. But it has real consequences: topographies are smoothed, and between the two bridged channels, connectivity measures will report a perfect, and completely fake, coupling.

How common is it? In 2014, Daniel Alschuler, Jürgen Kayser and colleagues screened public EEG datasets with a measure called the electrical distance: the variance of the difference between two channels. Bridged channels have a difference close to zero. In their survey, more than half of the recording sessions contained at least one bridge. The lesson: use only the gel each electrode needs, and screen for bridges. In Python, M N E includes a function to compute bridged electrodes from electrical distances.

Fifth: cable movement. When the cables between the electrodes and the amplifier swing or are touched, they can induce slow, large deflections on several channels at once. Active electrodes and short, fixed cables reduce the problem, but walking, gestures and even breathing can move cables.

Sixth: other devices. Monitors, phones, lifts, air conditioning and medical equipment can all produce interference at their own frequencies, sometimes intermittent. A spectrum of a short recording with the participant sitting still, or even with the cap on a dummy head, tells you what your room sounds like.

And a seventh, less visible species: timing problems, triggers that are missing, duplicated or delayed, which we covered in episode six point three. They do not distort the EEG itself, but they place your epochs in the wrong place.

As with the physiological species, each technical artifact has its own signature. Line noise: a sharp spectral peak and harmonics. Pops: steps on one channel. Flat channels: no variance. Bridges: two channels that are too similar. Cable movement: slow swings on several channels. Other devices: unexpected peaks, sometimes intermittent.

Let's recap. Technical artifacts come from equipment and environment: line noise, electrode pops, flat or saturated channels, bridged electrodes, cable movement and other devices. Bridges are common and invisible by eye, so screen for them with electrical distances. And a recording of the empty room tells you what your environment sounds like.

PLATE 7.44:07

Reading raw EEG with your eyes

Trained eyes remain the best quality check, like a lifeguard learning to scan a beach. In the MNE browser, a fixed view and a fixed scan order go from the whole picture to the front, sides, back, single channels, pairs and the full recording. Blinks, saccades, muscle, alpha, pops, flat channels and possible bridges are named, and bad channels and segments are annotated.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Algorithms are powerful, but the best quality check in EEG is still a pair of trained eyes. Today, we practise reading raw EEG, the way you would in the M N E data browser.

Think of a lifeguard watching a beach. At first, the sea looks like a confusing mass of movement. With practice, the lifeguard learns to scan systematically, recognise normal patterns, and spot the one swimmer in trouble within seconds. Reading EEG is the same skill: scan, recognise, name.

First, set up the view. In M N E, you open the browser with raw dot plot. Choose a sensible scale, for example around fifty microvolts per channel spacing, so that normal activity fills the space without overlapping. Show about ten seconds at a time. Order the channels from front to back, so that spatial patterns are easy to see. And apply only a gentle filter for viewing, for example a high pass at half a hertz, so slow drifts do not push traces off the screen.

Then, scan in a fixed order, every time.

Start with the overall picture. Is the signal roughly the same size on all channels? Is any channel much larger, flatter, or fuzzier than its neighbours?

Next, look at the front. Large, smooth bumps at F P one and F P two that fade towards the back: blinks. Steps of opposite polarity at F seven and F eight: horizontal saccades.

Then the sides. Bursts of fast, irregular activity at temporal channels: muscle, from the jaw. If they come and go with talking or swallowing, you have found the cause.

Then the back. With the eyes closed, you should see alpha: regular waves around ten hertz, waxing and waning, strongest at occipital and parietal sites. Alpha that disappears when the eyes open is a sign that the recording is basically healthy.

Then single channels. A sudden step that recovers slowly, on one channel only: an electrode pop. A channel with almost no variation: flat. A thick band of fuzz on one or two channels: line noise from poor contact.

Then pairs. Two neighbouring channels that look exactly identical, wiggle for wiggle: a possible bridge.

Finally, look at the whole recording at low resolution. Zoom out to a few minutes. Slow drifts, sudden changes in noise level, a period of large movements, or a moment where alpha keeps appearing in an eyes open task, which may mean drowsiness.

Now, a small exercise. Imagine a ten second page on the screen. Pause the video when the page appears, and try to find three blinks and one electrode pop before the answers are highlighted.

Did you find them? The blinks are the three large bumps at the frontal pole channels, at about two, five and eight seconds. The pop is the sudden step on channel T eight, at about six seconds, with nothing on its neighbours.

While you browse, you can mark things. In the M N E browser, you can click a channel name to mark it as bad, and add annotations, such as "bad underscore muscle", to segments you want to exclude or inspect later. These marks travel with the data and can be saved, so your inspection becomes part of the documented pipeline.

How much should you read by eye? At minimum, browse every recording quickly before any automated cleaning, and again after cleaning, to check what changed. For large datasets, browse a random sample in more detail, and every recording flagged as unusual by automated quality metrics.

Let's recap. Reading raw EEG is a skill: set a good view, scan in a fixed order, from the overall picture to the front, the sides, the back, single channels, pairs, and the whole recording. Name what you see. Mark bad channels and annotate bad segments in the browser. And always look before and after automated cleaning.

PLATE 7.53:39

Filters I · What a filter does

A filter is a sieve for frequencies, with low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and band-stop shapes. Real filters cannot be perfect walls: they have a passband, a transition band and a stopband with ripples and finite attenuation. A cutoff number means different things in different tools, a passband edge for MNE's FIR filter but the minus-three-decibel point for IIR, so the frequency response should be plotted.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Filters, part one. Before we choose a filter, we need to know what a filter actually does.

A filter is a sieve for frequencies. Some frequencies pass through, others are held back. The frequency response tells you how much of each frequency survives.

Picture it as a curve. On the horizontal axis, frequency. On the vertical axis, gain: one means the frequency passes untouched, zero means it is removed.

There are four basic shapes. A low-pass filter keeps the slow frequencies and removes the fast ones. A high-pass filter does the opposite, and removes slow drifts. A band-pass filter keeps a window in the middle. And a band-stop, or notch, filter removes one narrow window, for example the power line at fifty or sixty hertz.

Now, the ideal filter would be a perfect wall: everything on one side passes, everything on the other side disappears. Real filters cannot do that. Between the part that passes and the part that is stopped, there is always a slope.

This slope has a name: the transition band. On one side is the passband, where the gain stays close to one. On the other side is the stopband, where the gain stays close to zero. The narrower the transition band, the steeper the wall, and the longer the filter has to be in time.

Inside the passband, the gain is not perfectly flat either. It can wobble slightly above and below one. These are called ripples. And in the stopband, the frequencies are not erased, only attenuated, by a certain number of decibels.

A quick word on decibels. Minus six decibels means half the amplitude. Minus three decibels means half the power, which is about seventy per cent of the amplitude. Minus twenty decibels means one tenth of the amplitude.

So what does "a filter at one hertz" really mean? It depends on the convention, and conventions differ between tools.

In M N E, for the default F-I-R filter, the number you give is the edge of the passband. For a one hertz high-pass, the automatic transition band is one hertz wide. So the gain is already close to one at one hertz, falls to one half at zero point five hertz, and reaches the stopband near zero.

For an I-I-R filter, like a Butterworth, the number you give is the cutoff, usually the minus three decibel point. At that frequency, about thirty per cent of the amplitude is already gone, and frequencies just above it are also slightly attenuated.

Either way, the cut is never clean. A filter at one hertz touches frequencies on both sides of one hertz. Half a hertz is not entirely removed, and two hertz is not always entirely spared.

That is why you should always look at the frequency response of your filter, not just its name. In M N E, the function create filter, together with plot filter, draws exactly this curve.

And the filter does not only act on frequencies. It also acts in time. What happens to a sharp edge, or to the moment just before a stimulus, is the subject of the next episode.

To recap. A filter keeps some frequencies and attenuates others. Low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and band-stop are the four basic shapes. Every real filter has a passband, a transition band and a stopband, with ripples and a finite attenuation. And the number on the filter means different things in different tools, so check the curve.

PLATE 7.63:54

Filters II · FIR, IIR, phase and causality

Filters act on time as well as frequency. FIR filters are weighted moving averages that can have linear phase, while IIR filters are short but bend phase unless run forwards and backwards. Zero-phase filtering is non-causal and can leak activity before an onset, so latency questions favour causal filters; filtering continuous data avoids edge distortion, and steep filters ring like a bell.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Filters, part two. Last time we looked at what a filter does to frequencies. Today we look at what it does to time.

There are two big families of digital filters. F-I-R stands for finite impulse response. I-I-R stands for infinite impulse response.

An F-I-R filter is a weighted moving average. Each output sample is a sum of the input samples around it, each multiplied by a fixed weight. When the input is a single click, the output is just the list of weights, and then it stops. That is why the response is finite.

An I-I-R filter also reuses its own past outputs. A single click goes round and round inside the filter, and the response fades out slowly but never exactly reaches zero. I-I-R filters, like the Butterworth, are short and cheap, but they are less predictable.

Now, every filter takes some time to respond. Different frequencies can be delayed by different amounts. This delay is the phase response.

A symmetric F-I-R filter delays all frequencies by the same amount: half its length. That is called linear phase. Because the delay is the same for every frequency, it can be removed simply by shifting the output back in time. M N E does this by default.

An I-I-R filter delays different frequencies by different amounts. The shape of a waveform can be bent. The usual fix is to run the filter twice: once forwards, then once backwards. The two delays cancel out. This is zero-phase filtering, and in Python it is called filt filt. The price is that the filter is applied twice, so its attenuation is doubled.

But here is the catch. A zero-phase filter uses samples from the future to compute the present. It is non-causal.

Imagine an E-R-P whose response starts at one hundred milliseconds. After a zero-phase filter, part of that response is smeared backwards in time. A small wiggle can appear before the real onset, sometimes even before the stimulus itself. An effect that looks early may only be the filter leaking.

A causal filter only uses the past and the present. Nothing can appear before it actually happens. But the output is delayed, and the waveform can be distorted.

So which one should you use? Widmann, Schröger and Maess, in 2015, give practical advice. For most analyses, a zero-phase, linear-phase F-I-R filter is a good default. If your question is about onset latencies, a causal filter is the safer choice, as Rousselet pointed out in 2012. And in every case, use the gentlest filter that does the job.

There is one more trap: the edges. A filter needs samples on both sides to work. At the beginning and at the end of a recording, those samples do not exist. The filter invents them, and the edges get distorted.

The fix is simple. Filter the continuous recording, before cutting it into epochs. And if you must filter short segments, keep a margin around them and throw the margin away afterwards. M N E also pads the signal at the edges for you.

One last point: ringing. A very steep filter, with a narrow transition band, rings like a bell after a sharp event. Small oscillations appear around blinks or jumps, and they can look like real brain rhythms.

To recap. F-I-R filters have a finite response and can have linear phase. I-I-R filters are short but bend the phase, unless they are applied forwards and backwards. Zero-phase filters are non-causal and can move activity before an onset. Filter the continuous data to avoid edge effects. And steep filters ring.

Filters III · High-pass filters and ERPs

High-pass filters remove drifts but also shrink slow ERP components, because what remains must average to zero. A simulated bump develops opposite-polarity dips, some before the true onset, above about 0.3 hertz, and a language experiment showed fake effects appearing before real ones. For ERPs, about 0.1 hertz or lower is advised, while ICA is fitted on a copy filtered near one hertz.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Filters, part three. High-pass filters, and what they can do to an E-R-P.

A high-pass filter removes slow drifts: sweat, electrode settling, slow movements. That is useful. Drifts add noise, and less noise means more statistical power.

But many E-R-P components are slow too. The P300, the N400 and the P600 last for several hundred milliseconds. To a high-pass filter, a slow component and a slow drift look alike.

Let us take one simulated, positive component, a bump around six hundred milliseconds, and filter it with stronger and stronger high-pass filters.

At zero point zero one hertz, and at zero point one hertz, the bump looks almost exactly like the original.

From about zero point three hertz upwards, two things happen. The peak gets smaller. And small negative dips appear on both sides of it, including one before the real component.

At one hertz, the original bump is much smaller, and the dips are clearly visible.

Why? A high-pass filter removes the slow part of the waveform. What is left has to average out to roughly zero. So a positive bump must be paid for by negative areas around it. And with a zero-phase filter, some of that payment lands before the bump.

This is not just a simulation. Tanner, Morgan-Short and Luck, in 2015, recorded a classic language experiment. Without filtering, they found the expected effects: an N400 for semantic violations, and a P600 for syntactic violations.

With high-pass filters at zero point three hertz and above, artifactual effects of opposite polarity appeared before the true effects. A significant N400 appeared before the P600 in the syntactic condition. And a P2 effect appeared before the N400 in the semantic condition.

So a filter setting can change which components you think your manipulation affects. That is a different conclusion, not just a smaller effect.

What do they recommend? For most cognitive E-R-Ps, a high-pass cutoff of about zero point one hertz or lower. It removes much of the drift without distorting the components.

Not everyone agrees on every detail. Maess, Schröger and Widmann replied that their test data contained little low-frequency noise, and that in noisier data, a slightly stronger high-pass can help. Tanner and colleagues answered with more data. The shared lesson is simple: try your filter on your data, and check that it does not create peaks.

Now, there is one situation where a strong high-pass filter is welcome: independent component analysis. I-C-A works better when slow drifts are removed, so a high-pass of one or two hertz often gives cleaner components, as shown by Winkler and colleagues in 2015.

The trick is to use two copies of the data. On one copy, filter at one hertz and fit the I-C-A. Then apply the solution, the unmixing matrix, to the original data, filtered gently at zero point one hertz. You get clean components, and your slow E-R-Ps stay intact. We will do exactly this in the I-C-A episodes.

And a last practical tip. Report your filters fully: the type, the cutoff, the transition band or the order, and whether the filter was causal or zero-phase. Without that, nobody can know what your waveforms went through.

To recap. High-pass filters remove drifts, but also shrink slow components. Above about zero point three hertz, they can create fake peaks of opposite polarity, even before the real effect. For E-R-Ps, stay around zero point one hertz or below. For I-C-A, filter a copy more strongly. And always report your filter settings.

Next time: line noise. The notch filter, CleanLine, and ZapLine.

PLATE 7.84:24

Line noise · Notch, CleanLine, ZapLine

Line noise is a sharp spectral peak at fifty or sixty hertz with harmonics. A notch filter removes it but leaves a hole in the spectrum, sine fitting as in CleanLine avoids the hole but lags when the hum fluctuates, and ZapLine removes the spatial components dominated by the line frequency, like finding a fridge's voice across many microphones. ZapLine-plus automates the settings.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Line noise. The hum at fifty or sixty hertz that sneaks into almost every EEG recording. Today we compare three ways to remove it.

First, a look at the problem. In the spectrum, line noise is a sharp, narrow peak at fifty hertz in Europe, or sixty hertz in North America. Often there are smaller peaks at the harmonics: twice, three times, four times that frequency. Its amplitude can change over time, and from one channel to the next.

Method one: the notch filter. A band-stop filter with a very narrow stop band, centred on the line frequency, and one more for each harmonic.

It is simple, fast, and available everywhere. In M N E, it is raw dot notch filter.

But it removes everything in that narrow band, brain activity included. In the spectrum, it leaves a hole. If you care about activity in the gamma range, around fifty or sixty hertz, that hole sits right in the middle of your data. And like any steep filter, a narrow notch can ring in time around sharp events.

Method two: fit the sine and subtract it. This is the idea behind CleanLine, written by Tim Mullen for EEGLAB. Instead of removing a band, it estimates the sinusoid at the line frequency, its amplitude and its phase, in short sliding windows, and subtracts only that sinusoid.

If the noise really is a clean sine wave, the brain activity at nearby frequencies is left alone, and there is no hole. In M N E, the notch filter function offers a similar approach, with the method called spectrum fit.

The weakness: line noise is not always a clean, stable sine wave. When its amplitude or frequency drifts quickly, the fit lags behind, and some noise remains.

Method three: ZapLine, introduced by Alain de Cheveigné in 2020. ZapLine adds a new idea: space.

Line noise comes from a few sources outside the head, so it reaches all the electrodes with a fixed spatial pattern. ZapLine uses this. It looks for a few spatial components, combinations of channels, that are dominated by power at the line frequency. It removes those components, and keeps everything else.

Picture a busy café recorded with many microphones, and one fridge humming in the corner. Rather than muting a frequency on every microphone, you find the fridge's own voice across all the microphones, and remove just that.

ZapLine combines spectral and spatial filtering. It removes the noise without digging a hole in the spectrum, and it handles the harmonics too.

It has two choices to make: the noise frequency, and how many components to remove. Remove too few, and noise remains. Remove too many, and you start to remove brain activity.

ZapLine-plus, by Marius Klug and Niels Kloosterman in 2022, automates these choices. It cuts the recording into chunks where the noise is spatially stable, finds the exact noise peak in each chunk, chooses the number of components with an outlier detection rule, and checks the spectrum afterwards.

Now, the three spectra side by side. After the notch filter: the peak is gone, but there is a hole. After the sine fit: the peak is mostly gone, but some noise may remain where the hum fluctuates. After ZapLine: the peak is gone, and the spectrum around it stays smooth.

In Python, ZapLine is available in the meegkit package, and there are Python implementations of ZapLine-plus too.

A few practical points. Do it early, on the continuous data, before epoching. Check the spectrum before and after, on every recording. And remember that strong line noise on one channel is often a sign of a poor contact: that channel may be better marked as bad.

To recap. Line noise is a narrow peak at fifty or sixty hertz, with harmonics. A notch filter removes it, but leaves a hole. Fitting and subtracting the sine avoids the hole, but struggles when the noise fluctuates. ZapLine removes the noise's spatial components, and ZapLine-plus chooses the settings automatically. Always check the spectrum before and after.

Next time: bad channels. How to find them, and how to replace them.

PLATE 7.94:57

Bad channels · Find, then interpolate

A bad channel no longer reflects the brain beneath it and can spoil references, ICA and topographies. The PREP criteria flag flat channels, extreme amplitude, low correlation with others, high-frequency noise, and RANSAC unpredictability from interpolated neighbours. Spherical spline interpolation, like reading a missing value off an elastic surface, replaces them, but adds no information, so each interpolated channel lowers the rank by one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Bad channels. Some electrodes simply do not do their job. Today: how to find them, and what to do with them.

A bad channel is one whose signal no longer reflects the brain under it: a loose electrode, a bridge, a broken wire, constant line noise, or huge drifts. One bad channel can spoil the average reference, the I-C-A, and every topography you draw. So we find them first.

Your eyes are the first detector, as we saw in episode seven point four. But with many channels and many participants, it helps to have criteria that a computer can apply the same way every time.

The PREP pipeline, published by Nima Bigdely-Shamlo and colleagues in 2015, gathered a set of such criteria. In Python, they are available in the pyprep package. Let's go through them.

Criterion one: flat channels. A channel with almost no variation, or with missing values, is dead.

Criterion two: extreme amplitude. Compare the spread of each channel with the spread of all the others, using robust statistics: the median, and a robust standard deviation. A channel more than five robust standard deviations away from the group is flagged.

Criterion three: low correlation with the others. Brain activity is spatially smooth, so every channel should correlate with at least some of its neighbours. PREP cuts the data into one-second windows, and finds each channel's highest correlation with any other channel. If that correlation falls below zero point four in too many windows, the channel is flagged.

Criterion four: high-frequency noise. Compare the power above fifty hertz with the power below it. A channel where this ratio is unusually high, again more than five robust standard deviations, is probably picking up muscle or electrical noise.

Criterion five: RANSAC, which stands for random sample consensus. Pick a random quarter of the channels, and use them to predict every other channel by interpolation. Repeat this fifty times, and take the median prediction. If a channel's real signal correlates with its prediction by less than zero point seven five, in more than forty per cent of the windows, it is flagged as unpredictable.

Each criterion catches a different kind of failure. Together, they are much more reliable than any single one. And they work best in a loop: remove the bad channels, recompute the reference, and test again, because one very bad channel can make the others look odd.

Still, check the result by eye. Automatic criteria can be fooled, for example by a participant with genuinely large alpha at the back.

Now, what do we do with a bad channel? Two options: drop it, or interpolate it.

Interpolation estimates the missing channel from its neighbours. The usual method is spherical spline interpolation, described by Perrin and colleagues in 1989. Imagine a smooth elastic surface stretched over the scalp, passing through all the good electrodes. The value at the missing electrode is read off that surface.

In M N E, you mark the channel as bad, and call interpolate bads.

Interpolation keeps the same set of channels for every participant, which makes group analyses and topographies much easier.

But here is the key point. An interpolated channel brings no new information. It is just a weighted sum of the other channels. So the data now has one fewer independent dimension than it has channels. We say its rank has dropped by one.

Why does that matter? Because some methods, like I-C-A, need to know the true number of independent dimensions. If you ask I-C-A for as many components as channels, while the rank is lower, it can fail or produce strange, duplicated components. The next episode is all about rank.

That is also why the order matters. A common recipe: detect bad channels, leave them out while you compute the reference and the I-C-A, and interpolate them at the end.

And a limit: if too many channels are bad, or if bad channels sit next to each other, interpolation becomes a guess. Many labs set a maximum, for example ten per cent of channels, beyond which the recording is excluded. Decide that threshold before you look at the results.

To recap. Find bad channels with clear criteria: flat, extreme amplitude, low correlation, high-frequency noise, and RANSAC, as gathered in PREP and pyprep. Confirm by eye. Interpolate with spherical splines, ideally at the end of the pipeline. Remember that each interpolated channel lowers the rank by one. And set your exclusion rule in advance.

Re-referencing in practice and data rank

Average referencing makes channels sum to zero, so one channel is computable from the others and rank drops by one, like a group photo containing a mirror reflection. Interpolation and bridges lower rank further. ICA cannot find more components than the rank, and forcing it yields ghost components with normal-looking maps but noise time courses, so rank is checked and bad channels are interpolated last.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Re-referencing in practice, and the rank of your data. A short episode about a number that quietly decides whether your I-C-A works.

In chapter three, we saw that every EEG channel is a difference: the voltage at one electrode minus the voltage at a reference. Today, the practical side: when to re-reference, and what it does to your data.

The most common choice is the average reference. At every moment, we subtract the mean of all channels from each channel. After that, the channels always add up to zero.

And that is the important part. If the channels always add up to zero, then any one channel can be computed from all the others. It carries no new information.

This leads us to rank. The rank of your data is the number of truly independent channels. Picture a group photo in which one person is just a reflection in a mirror: you see sixty-four faces, but only sixty-three people.

Raw data recorded against one reference electrode, with sixty-four active channels, usually has a rank of sixty-four. After an average reference, it has a rank of sixty-three. Each interpolated channel removes one more, as we saw in the last episode. And a bridge between two electrodes can make two channels so similar that they behave almost like one.

Why does this matter? Because many methods need to invert a matrix built from the data, and a matrix of rank sixty-three, with sixty-four rows, cannot be properly inverted.

I-C-A is the classic victim. I-C-A can find at most as many independent components as the rank of the data. If you ask it for sixty-four components from rank sixty-three data, it can fail to converge, or produce components that look like real topographies but are just noise.

Hyeonseok Kim, Makoto Miyakoshi and colleagues, in 2023, called these ghost components. They showed that ghost components have white-noise time courses and spectra, yet surprisingly normal-looking scalp maps, so they are easy to miss.

There is a subtle twist: effective rank. After some operations, such as spline interpolation, the data are not exactly rank deficient: the smallest eigenvalue is not zero, just tiny. A simple rank check can then say the data are full rank, while numerically, the I-C-A behaves as if they were not. Kim and colleagues suggested treating eigenvalues below a very small threshold as zero.

Their paper also started a debate. Arnaud Delorme and Scott Makeig replied that this is not a bug in I-C-A, but a known requirement: rank-deficient data must be reduced before I-C-A. Both sides agree on the practical lesson: know your rank, and tell the algorithm.

So how do we keep track of it, in practice?

First, remove or mark the bad channels before re-referencing, so that one noisy electrode does not leak into the average of all the others.

Second, in M N E, the average reference can be added as a projector, with set E-E-G reference, average, and projection equals true. M N E then knows that one dimension is gone.

Third, before I-C-A, check the rank. M N E has a function, compute rank. Then ask I-C-A for no more components than that, for example by setting the number of components a little below the rank. M N E's I-C-A first reduces the data with a principal component analysis, which handles this neatly.

Fourth, the order. A common sequence is: bad channels out, average reference, I-C-A, then interpolation of the bad channels, and if you want, a final average reference again. Interpolating at the end means the I-C-A never sees the duplicated dimensions.

And fifth, write the rank in your log at every step. If a number goes down when you did not expect it, something happened that you should know about.

To recap. The average reference makes channels sum to zero, so the rank drops by one. Interpolation and bridges lower it further. I-C-A cannot find more components than the rank, and forcing it creates ghost components. Remove bad channels before referencing, check the rank with compute rank, choose the number of components accordingly, and interpolate at the end.

ICA I · The cocktail party

EEG electrodes are microphones at a cocktail party, each recording a weighted mixture of sources such as neurons, eyes, muscles and heart. ICA finds an unmixing matrix by seeking outputs that are statistically independent and as non-Gaussian as possible. It assumes linear instantaneous mixing and stable sources, returns a time course and scalp map per component, and leaves their identification to the analyst.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Independent component analysis, part one. Today, no code. Just the idea, told with a party.

Imagine a cocktail party. Several people are talking at the same time, and several microphones hang from the ceiling. Each microphone records a mixture of all the voices: a bit more of the people nearby, a bit less of the people far away.

Your task: from the microphone recordings alone, recover each voice separately. You do not know where the people stand, and you do not know what they said.

This is the cocktail party problem. And it is very close to the situation in EEG. The electrodes are the microphones. The voices are the sources: groups of neurons, but also the eyes, the muscles, the heart. Each electrode records a weighted mixture of all of them.

Let's write it simply. The recordings equal a mixing matrix times the sources. The mixing matrix holds the weights: how strongly each source reaches each electrode. Independent component analysis, I-C-A, tries to find an unmixing matrix, so that the unmixing matrix times the recordings gives back the sources.

But there are infinitely many ways to unmix. Which one is right? I-C-A makes one bet: the true sources are statistically independent. Knowing what one source does at a given moment tells you nothing about what the others are doing.

Independence is stronger than being uncorrelated. Two signals can be uncorrelated and still depend on each other. Independence asks that no relationship at all can be found between them.

How does I-C-A search for independence in practice? There is a beautiful trick, thanks to the central limit theorem. When you add independent signals together, the mixture looks more Gaussian, more bell-shaped, than the signals you started with.

So I-C-A turns the problem around. It looks for combinations of the channels that are as far from Gaussian as possible. The least Gaussian combinations are the best candidates for the original, unmixed sources.

Picture the histogram of each signal. A mixture of voices gives a smooth bell. A single voice gives a pointier shape, with long tails: mostly quiet, with occasional loud bursts. I-C-A turns the knobs of the unmixing matrix until the histograms of its outputs look as pointy as possible.

Different algorithms measure non-Gaussianity or independence in different ways. Infomax, introduced by Bell and Sejnowski in 1995, maximises information flow through a network. FastICA maximises a measure of non-Gaussianity. Picard is a faster way of solving the same kind of problem. Makeig and colleagues first applied I-C-A to EEG in 1996.

Now, the assumptions. I-C-A works when four conditions hold, at least roughly.

One: the mixing is linear and instantaneous. Each electrode is a weighted sum of the sources, with no delay. For EEG, this holds well: at the frequencies we record, electrical fields spread through the head almost instantly, and they add up linearly.

Two: the sources are statistically independent. Blinks, heartbeats, muscle bursts and brain rhythms do have quite separate lives. This is roughly true, though not perfectly.

Three: the sources are not Gaussian, with at most one exception. Most EEG sources, and especially artifacts, are far from Gaussian. A blink is a perfect example: silent most of the time, then huge.

Four: there are no more sources than channels, and the mixing stays the same over the recording. This is where EEG cheats. The brain has far more than sixty-four sources. In practice, I-C-A finds the largest, most independent ones, and lumps many small ones together. And if the cap slides, or the participant moves, the mixing changes, and I-C-A suffers.

A few things I-C-A cannot tell you. It cannot recover the true size or the sign of a source, only its shape. It does not order the components by importance. And it does not say what the components are. A component is just a time course plus a map across the scalp. Deciding whether it is a blink, a heartbeat or brain activity is our job, and the topic of episode seven point thirteen.

That map is very useful. Each component comes with a topography, a column of the mixing matrix, showing how strongly it reaches each electrode. A blink component has a map concentrated at the front. A component from the heart often has a broad gradient across the head.

And here is why I-C-A is so popular for cleaning. Once you have the components, you can remove one, the blink, and mix the rest back together. You get the data without blinks, and everything else stays in place.

To recap. EEG electrodes record linear mixtures of sources, like microphones at a party. I-C-A finds an unmixing matrix by looking for outputs that are as independent, and as non-Gaussian, as possible. It assumes linear, instantaneous, stable mixing, independent non-Gaussian sources, and no more sources than channels. EEG roughly fits. And each component comes with a time course and a scalp map.

Independent component analysis, part two. Last time, the idea. Today, how to actually run it on your data, step by step, with M N E.

Think of I-C-A as a sorting machine at a recycling centre. You feed it a mixed load. It sorts everything into bins. Then you look into each bin, decide which ones are waste, throw those away, and put the rest back together. The machine sorts. You decide.

Step one: prepare a training copy. I-C-A is sensitive to slow drifts, which break its assumptions. So we make a copy of the data, high-pass filtered at one hertz, as we saw in episode seven point seven. We also leave out the bad channels, and the segments we marked as bad, so that a few huge artifacts do not take over the decomposition.

Step two: make sure there is enough data. I-C-A has to estimate a full unmixing matrix: with sixty-four channels, that is more than four thousand numbers. A common rule of thumb from the EEGLAB community is to have at least about twenty to thirty times the number of channels squared, in samples. For sixty-four channels at two hundred and fifty hertz, that is roughly five to eight minutes of clean data. The rule is empirical, not a law, and more data is better. Fewer channels need much less.

Step three: choose the number of components. As we saw in episode seven point ten, it cannot be more than the rank of the data. M N E first reduces the data with a principal component analysis, and you can ask for a number of components, or for the share of variance to keep.

Step four: choose an algorithm. In M N E, the I-C-A object offers fastica, infomax, and picard. Extended infomax can handle sources with both pointy and flat histograms. Picard is fast and reaches similar solutions. Whatever you choose, fix the random seed, so that you get the same result every time you run it.

Step five: fit. In M N E: create the I-C-A object, then call fit on the one hertz copy.

Step six: inspect each component. For every component, look at three things, just like in the artifact zoo. Its scalp map: where does it live? Its spectrum: what does it sound like? Its time course: how does it move?

A blink component: a map at the very front, a spectrum dominated by low frequencies, and a time course with big, isolated bumps that match the blinks in the raw data.

A horizontal eye movement component: a map with opposite poles on the left and the right of the forehead, and square steps in time.

A heart component: a broad map, and a time course with small, regular spikes, about once per second.

A muscle component: a very focal map at the edge of the head, and a spectrum that rises at high frequencies.

A brain component: a smooth, dipole-like map, a spectrum with a clear peak, for example alpha, and a time course without isolated jumps.

M N E has plotting functions for each view: plot components for the maps, plot sources for the time courses, and plot properties for everything about one component at once. It can also help find eye and heart components by correlating them with E-O-G or E-C-G channels.

Step seven: apply. List the components to remove in exclude, then apply the I-C-A solution to the original data, the copy filtered gently at zero point one hertz. The unmixing learned on the one hertz copy is valid for the original, because the channels and their mixing are the same.

Now, the moment of truth. Remove the blink component, and look at the data before and after. The blinks are gone. Everything else, the alpha, the evoked responses, stays exactly where it was. This is the power of I-C-A.

But watch what happens when we remove a brain component by mistake. In this illustrative example, the evoked response at the back gets smaller, and the difference between conditions shrinks. Nothing warns you. The data look cleaner, but you have thrown away part of the effect you were looking for.

So be conservative. Remove the components you are confident about, typically blinks, eye movements and the heart. When in doubt, keep. And write down which components you removed, and why.

A few more tips. Fit I-C-A on continuous data, or on long epochs, not on short averages. Check the result on the raw data, scrolling before and after. And if the recording is very long, you can downsample the training copy to speed things up.

To recap. Fit I-C-A on a copy high-pass filtered at one hertz, without bad channels and bad segments. Have enough data, and no more components than the rank. Fix the random seed. Inspect each component by its map, spectrum and time course. Apply the solution to the gently filtered original. And remove only what you are sure of.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Independent component analysis, part three. Classifying components automatically, with ICLabel.

After I-C-A, you have a pile of components to judge. With sixty-four channels and thirty participants, that can mean almost two thousand components. Judging them all by eye takes a long time, and two people will not always agree. This is where an automatic classifier helps.

ICLabel was published in 2019 by Luca Pion-Tonachini, Ken Kreutz-Delgado and Scott Makeig. It is a neural network that looks at a component and gives a probability for each of seven categories: brain, muscle, eye, heart, line noise, channel noise, and other.

Think of it as a second opinion from an experienced colleague. A very fast one, who has seen a lot of components.

How did it learn? The team gathered features from over two hundred thousand components, taken from more than six thousand EEG recordings. Then they built a website where volunteers could learn to recognise components, and label them. Thousands of these labels were combined into probabilities, and used to train the network.

What does ICLabel look at? The same three clues we use by eye. The scalp map of the component. Its power spectrum. And its autocorrelation, which describes how its time course repeats itself.

For each component, the output is seven numbers that add up to one. For example: brain, zero point zero five. Eye, zero point nine two. The rest close to zero. A confident blink.

But many components are not so clear. Brain, zero point four five. Muscle, zero point three. Other, zero point two. What do we do with that?

This is the most important part of the episode. Decide your rule before you look at the results.

A rule might be: remove a component if its probability of being eye or heart is above zero point eight. Or: keep a component unless its brain probability is below a threshold. Different rules suit different questions. A study of muscle activity, or of gamma oscillations, will not use the same rule as a study of slow E-R-Ps.

Why before? Because if you choose the threshold after seeing which one gives the nicest effect, you are no longer cleaning your data. You are shaping your result. Write the rule in your pre-registration, or at least in your analysis plan.

Then, check the borderline cases by eye. Look at the components whose top probability is modest, or whose label surprises you. Look at the map, the spectrum and the time course, as in episode seven point twelve. And keep a record of every decision you overrode, and why.

A few cautions. ICLabel was trained on data with an average reference, filtered between one and one hundred hertz, decomposed with extended infomax. It can run on other data, but its accuracy may change. The other category is a catch-all: it often contains mixtures that I-C-A could not separate, and removing them can remove brain activity too. And with few channels, components are less clean, so the labels are less reliable.

In Python, ICLabel is available in the mne-icalabel package. You fit your I-C-A with M N E, then call label components, with the method iclabel. It returns a label and a probability for each component, and you decide what to exclude.

The classifier helps. It does not decide. Its strength is consistency: the same component always gets the same label, across participants, studies and labs. Your strength is judgment, especially for the strange cases.

To recap. ICLabel gives each component a probability for seven categories, learned from a large crowd-labelled dataset. It looks at the scalp map, the spectrum and the autocorrelation. Choose your rule and threshold before looking. Check borderline cases by eye. Keep in mind the conditions it was trained on. And report your rule and how many components you removed.

ASR · Artifact Subspace Reconstruction

Artifact subspace reconstruction repairs rare, large bursts such as coughs or cable touches, like a guard who learns a quiet morning and then steps in only for the unusual part. It learns covariance from clean data, scans short windows, and reconstructs directions whose variance exceeds a cutoff. A low cutoff removes brain activity too; twenty to thirty is a tested balance, applied before ICA.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Artifact subspace reconstruction, or A-S-R. A method that repairs short bursts of noise automatically, even in real time.

I-C-A is great for artifacts that repeat with a stable pattern, like blinks. But some artifacts are rare, large and messy: a cough, a head shake, someone touching the cables. I-C-A struggles with these. A-S-R was designed for them.

A-S-R was developed by Christian Kothe and colleagues, and described in a 2015 paper by Tim Mullen and colleagues on wearable dry EEG. It is available in EEGLAB through the clean raw data plugin, and in Python through the asrpy package.

Think of a security guard who first spends a quiet morning learning what a normal day looks like. After that, whenever something unusually loud happens, the guard steps in, but only for that moment, and only for the part that is unusual.

Step one: calibration. A-S-R needs a stretch of clean data. You can give it a resting recording, or let it find the cleanest windows in your data automatically. From this clean data, it learns how the channels normally vary together: their covariance.

Step two: sliding windows. A-S-R moves through the recording in short windows, about half a second long by default. In each window, it runs a principal component analysis. This finds the directions, combinations of channels, that carry the most variance at that moment.

Step three: compare. For each direction, A-S-R asks: is the variance here much larger than in the clean calibration data? The threshold is a number of standard deviations, called k, or the cutoff.

Step four: reconstruct. The directions above the threshold are treated as artifact. They are removed, and replaced by an estimate based on the remaining, normal directions and on how the channels varied together during calibration. The rest of the window is left untouched.

So A-S-R does not throw windows away. It repairs them. The output has the same length as the input, and quiet periods pass through unchanged.

Now, the slider: the cutoff k. This is the most important choice.

With a low k, A-S-R is very sensitive. It steps in often. It removes artifacts, but also strong, genuine brain activity, like a burst of alpha or a large evoked response.

With a high k, A-S-R is relaxed. It only acts on huge events. Brain activity survives, but moderate artifacts pass through.

Chang and colleagues, in 2020, evaluated A-S-R on twenty EEG recordings, using I-C-A and ICLabel to measure what was removed. They concluded that a cutoff between twenty and thirty gave the best balance: A-S-R removed more eye and muscle activity than brain activity, and it improved the I-C-A that followed. The early default in EEGLAB was five, which is much more aggressive. asrpy uses twenty by default.

Twenty standard deviations sounds enormous. But the scale is measured on clean calibration windows, not on raw voltages, and in practice it still catches the large bursts.

Where does A-S-R fit in a pipeline? Usually after high-pass filtering and bad channel removal, and before I-C-A. A-S-R takes care of the big, rare events, so that I-C-A can concentrate on the stable sources, like blinks and the heart.

A few cautions. The calibration data must really be clean: if it contains artifacts, A-S-R learns that they are normal. A-S-R works best with many channels. If your effect of interest is itself a large, brief event, check that A-S-R does not shrink it, by comparing your results with and without it. And as always, report the cutoff, and how much of the data was modified.

To recap. A-S-R learns normal covariance from clean data, then scans short windows. Directions whose variance exceeds k standard deviations are removed and reconstructed. Low k cleans more but removes brain signal. High k keeps more but lets artifacts through. A cutoff of twenty to thirty is a common, tested choice. In Python, use asrpy.

Epoching, baseline and autoreject

Epoching cuts continuous data into clips around events, like a film editor, and baseline correction subtracts a quiet pre-event mean chosen in advance. A fixed rejection threshold is blunt: it discards good epochs from high-amplitude participants and misses artifacts in low-amplitude ones. Autoreject learns per-channel thresholds by cross-validation and repairs or rejects each epoch.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Epoching, baseline correction, and autoreject. The moment the continuous recording becomes trials.

So far, we cleaned the continuous data. But most analyses look at what happens around events: a stimulus, a response, a beat of music. So we cut the recording into pieces, one per event. These pieces are called epochs.

Think of a film editor. The full footage runs for an hour. The editor cuts out a short clip around each moment that matters, lines up all the clips on the same starting frame, and throws away the ones where someone walked in front of the camera.

Step one: cut. For each event marker, keep a window from a little before to a little after it. For a typical E-R-P, perhaps from two hundred milliseconds before the stimulus to eight hundred after. In M N E, you create an Epochs object from the raw data and the events, with t min and t max.

Choose the window with your later analyses in mind. For time-frequency analysis, add extra margins on both sides, because wavelets need room, and the edges get distorted. You can crop them away later.

Step two: baseline correction. Every epoch starts at a slightly different voltage, because of slow drifts that remain. To compare epochs, we subtract, from each channel in each epoch, the mean voltage during a reference period before the event, often the whole pre-stimulus interval.

After that, every epoch starts around zero, and what you see after the event is the change relative to just before it.

The baseline should be quiet. If the participant is already preparing a response, or if the previous trial's response overlaps the baseline, the correction will move that activity into your post-stimulus window. Choose the baseline period before looking at the effect, and report it.

Step three: reject bad epochs. Even after filtering, I-C-A and A-S-R, some epochs remain contaminated: a sudden movement, a sneeze, an electrode that popped for a second.

The classic approach is a fixed threshold. If any channel exceeds, say, plus or minus one hundred microvolts in an epoch, reject that epoch. It is simple and widely used.

But one threshold for everyone is a blunt tool. Here are two participants. The first has naturally large EEG, for example strong alpha: lots of clean epochs exceed one hundred microvolts, and we lose half of their trials for nothing. The second has low amplitude EEG: an epoch with a real artifact stays below one hundred microvolts, and slips through.

Autoreject, published by Mainak Jas and colleagues in 2017, learns the thresholds from the data instead.

The first idea: choose the threshold by cross-validation. Split the epochs into parts. For each candidate threshold, reject the epochs above it in one part, average the rest, and see how well that average matches a robust estimate, the median, from the other part. Too low a threshold throws away good data and makes the average noisy. Too high a threshold lets artifacts distort it. The best threshold is in between.

The second idea: do it per channel. Each channel gets its own threshold. Then, for each epoch, autoreject counts how many channels are bad.

If only a few channels are bad in an epoch, they are repaired by interpolation, just for that epoch. If too many are bad, the whole epoch is rejected. How many is a few, and how many is too many? Those two numbers are also chosen by cross-validation.

The result: more trials kept, and fewer artifacts. And the same procedure is applied to every participant, with thresholds adapted to each of them.

In Python, autoreject works directly with M N E epochs. You create an AutoReject object, call fit transform on your epochs, and you can plot a summary of which channels were interpolated and which epochs were rejected.

A few tips. Run autoreject after I-C-A, or run it once before I-C-A to exclude the worst epochs from the I-C-A fit, and again afterwards. Check how many trials each participant has left, and per condition: a big imbalance between conditions can bias the comparison. And decide in advance the minimum number of trials needed to keep a participant.

To recap. Epochs are windows cut around events; add margins for time-frequency analyses. Baseline correction subtracts the pre-event mean; keep the baseline quiet and decide it in advance. A fixed rejection threshold is simple, but rejects too much in some participants and too little in others. Autoreject learns per-channel thresholds by cross-validation, and repairs or rejects each epoch. Report the trials left per condition.

Other tools in the box

Four further cleaning tools each carry their own assumption. EOG regression subtracts a scaled eye signal but may remove frontal brain activity, signal space projection removes fixed artifact topographies, denoising source separation finds components maximising a chosen property, and wavelet denoising shrinks large coefficients channel by channel. TRANSCRIPTEN

Other tools in the box. I-C-A, A-S-R and autoreject are the big names, but they are not the only ways to clean EEG. Today, four more tools, what each one assumes, and when to pick it.

Think of a toolbox. A hammer is wonderful, but not for every job. A good craftsperson knows each tool's purpose, even the ones they rarely use.

Tool one: regression of the E-O-G. This is the oldest approach, popularised by Gratton, Coles and Donchin in 1983. You record the eye activity with electrodes around the eyes. For each EEG channel, you estimate how much of the eye signal leaks into it: a single weight, the propagation factor. Then you subtract the eye signal, times that weight, from the channel.

It is simple, transparent and fast, and it does not need many channels. Its main assumption: the E-O-G channels contain only eye activity. In reality, they also pick up some frontal brain activity, so a bit of brain gets subtracted too. In M N E, this is available as E-O-G regression.

Choose it when you have good E-O-G channels, few EEG channels, or not enough data for a stable I-C-A.

Tool two: signal space projection, or S-S-P. Very common in M E G, and available for EEG. First, you collect examples of an artifact, for example epochs around blinks or heartbeats. A principal component analysis finds the spatial patterns, the topographies, that best describe the artifact. Then you project the data onto the space orthogonal to those patterns, removing everything that looks like them.

Its assumption: the artifact has a fixed spatial pattern, and the brain activity you care about does not share it. If a brain source has a similar topography, it will be weakened too. In M N E, functions such as compute proj E-O-G build these projectors, and you can switch them on and off.

Choose it when the artifact is stable and well sampled, and you want a quick, reversible fix.

Tool three: denoising source separation, or D-S-S. D-S-S is a family of methods that find spatial components which maximise a property you define. You decide what counts as interesting: for example, what repeats across trials, or what is strongest at one frequency. D-S-S then finds the combinations of channels that best show it.

ZapLine, from episode seven point eight, is in fact a D-S-S, tuned for line noise. Another variant finds the components most reproducible across trials, which is a way to enhance evoked responses. The assumption is your choice of bias: D-S-S will find what you ask for, so ask carefully. In Python, D-S-S is in the meegkit package.

Choose it when you can describe your signal or your noise precisely: a frequency, a repetition, a time window.

Tool four: wavelet denoising. Here the idea is not spatial but spectral and temporal. You decompose each channel with a wavelet transform. Artifacts like spikes and jumps produce a few very large coefficients. You shrink or zero those coefficients above a threshold, and transform back.

It works on single channels, so it can help when you only have a few electrodes. Its assumption: artifacts and brain activity differ in their wavelet coefficients. Choosing the wavelet and the threshold matters a lot, and a careless threshold can distort real transient brain activity. In Python, the PyWavelets package provides the building blocks.

Choose it for single or low channel recordings, or as a targeted repair for spikes.

How do they compare? Regression and S-S-P remove a known artifact with a fixed pattern. D-S-S finds components that match a property you specify. Wavelets work channel by channel, in time and frequency. I-C-A, by comparison, needs no template, but needs enough data and channels. And A-S-R handles rare, large bursts.

There is no universally best tool. What matters is that you know each tool's assumptions, check them on your data, and say clearly in your methods which tool you used and why.

To recap. E-O-G regression subtracts a scaled eye signal, but can remove some frontal brain activity. S-S-P projects out fixed artifact topographies. D-S-S finds components that maximise a property you choose; ZapLine is one. Wavelet denoising shrinks large coefficients, channel by channel. Pick tools by their assumptions, not by habit.

One pipeline, in the right order

A cleaning pipeline is a factory line where each station expects the right input. A sensible order runs from inspection, line noise, bad channels, filtering, referencing and ICA to interpolation, epochs, autoreject and a quality report; swapping steps creates ghost components, spreads noise or distorts edges. Many teams analysing one dataset made different choices and found different effects, so every choice must be documented.

TRANSCRIPTEN

One pipeline, in the right order. The last episode of chapter seven puts all the tools on a single assembly line.

Each tool we met does one job well. But the order in which you use them matters as much as the tools themselves. Swap two steps, and the result can change.

Picture a small factory. Raw material comes in at one end. It passes through a series of stations. Each station expects its input in a certain state. If a station receives the wrong input, it does a poor job, and every station after it inherits the problem.

Here is one sensible order. It is not the only one, but each step has a reason.

Station one: import and inspect. Load the raw data, from BIDS if you can, check the events and the channel positions, and browse the recording by eye, as in episode seven point four.

Station two: line noise. Remove the fifty or sixty hertz hum with ZapLine or a notch filter, on the continuous data, as in episode seven point eight. Line noise can make good channels look bad, so we remove it before judging channels.

Station three: bad channels. Detect them with clear criteria, for example with pyprep, as in episode seven point nine. Mark them, but do not interpolate yet.

Station four: filtering. A gentle high-pass, around zero point one hertz, for the data you will analyse. And a separate copy, filtered at one hertz, for I-C-A, as in episodes seven point five to seven point seven.

Station five: re-reference. Average reference, computed without the bad channels, so that one noisy electrode does not contaminate all the others. Track the rank, as in episode seven point ten.

Optionally, A-S-R here, to repair rare, large bursts before I-C-A, as in episode seven point fourteen.

Station six: I-C-A. Fit on the one hertz copy, with no more components than the rank. Classify the components with ICLabel and your eyes, apply a rule decided in advance, and apply the solution to the gently filtered data, as in episodes seven point eleven to seven point thirteen.

Station seven: interpolation. Now that I-C-A is done, rebuild the bad channels with spherical splines, so that every participant has the same channels.

Station eight: epochs and baseline, as in episode seven point fifteen.

Station nine: autoreject, to repair or reject the epochs that are still contaminated.

And finally, a quality report: how many channels were interpolated, how many components removed, how many epochs kept per condition, and the spectrum before and after.

Now, let's swap two stations, and watch what happens.

Swap one: interpolate the bad channels before I-C-A. The interpolated channels add no new information, but I-C-A does not know that. With the wrong number of components, we risk ghost components and a poorer decomposition.

Swap two: compute the average reference before removing bad channels. One channel with huge noise is now spread into every other channel. Every channel looks a little worse, and the bad channel detection becomes harder.

Swap three: epoch first, and filter the short epochs. The filter has no room at the edges, and the beginning and end of every epoch get distorted, as we saw in episode seven point six.

So the order is not a detail. Each station prepares the ground for the next one.

But even with a sensible order, there are many reasonable choices at each station. How much do they matter? The EEGManyPipelines project tested this directly. One hundred and sixty-eight teams of analysts, nearly four hundred researchers from thirty-seven countries, analysed the same EEG dataset to test the same hypotheses. No two submitted pipelines were identical. And the reported effects varied from team to team.

That does not mean that EEG results are arbitrary. It means that choices matter, and that they must be visible.

So here is the most important rule of the chapter. Document every choice, and justify it. Decide the main parameters before you look at your effects. Write them in a pre-registration or an analysis plan. Share your code. And if a result depends strongly on one choice, say so, or test a few reasonable alternatives and report them all.

And remember episode seven point one: every cleaning step costs something. Sometimes the best pipeline is the shortest one that does the job.

To recap. A sensible order: inspect, line noise, bad channels, filtering, reference, I-C-A, interpolation, epochs, autoreject, then a quality report. Swapping steps can create ghost components, spread noise, or distort edges. Many teams analysing the same data make different choices and get different effects. Document and justify every choice, decide in advance, and share your code.
