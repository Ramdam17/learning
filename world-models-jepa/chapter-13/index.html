Generative vs predictive

Two benches debate where a model's loss should sit: on pixels or on a description of them. Latent prediction argues a loss should not pay for unknowable detail, citing feature targets beating pixel targets, MuZero and V-JEPA 2; generation argues dropped details may be what the agent needed. Latent generators and dense latent prediction show the two already meet in the middle.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The lights go down on the shadow theatre, and the stage fills with benches. Welcome to the agora, the square where chapter thirteen takes place. Two benches face each other. In the middle stands a ladder of evidence, and LENS keeps it up to date. Today's question is where a model should be judged: on the pixels, or on a description of them. Each bench gets the floor for the same time, and at the end there is no verdict.

First, what both benches agree on. Every model in this debate does the same three things. It reads one piece of the world, it guesses another piece, and a loss says how wrong the guess was. The only thing that differs is where the loss sits. Picture a railway line with three stations: pixels, then a necklace, then pixels again. Each bench plants a judge's flag on the station where it wants the error measured.

Episode 6.5 set the scene. When a leaf could land in five places, squared error on pixels rewards the average, and the average is a blur. And every pixel gets a share of the loss, even the shimmer that nobody could have foreseen. Hold on to that, because both benches will use it.

The first bench speaks for prediction in latent space. That is JEPA, and also MuZero. Its first argument is that a loss should not pay for what cannot be known. Yann LeCun made that case in his 2022 position paper, the one we opened in episode 11.1. A model that predicts a description is free to drop the unpredictable details. The flag goes on the necklace station.

Its best evidence is a controlled comparison. In February 2024, Bardes and colleagues posted V-JEPA. In one ablation, they trained the same video model twice. Once the target was the pixels, and once it was the features of a second encoder. With the encoder frozen, features scored higher on all three tests. On Kinetics, seventy-three point seven against sixty-eight point six. On ImageNet, seventy-four point eight against seventy-three point three. On Something-Something the two were within two tenths of a point. This is one model size, on recognition tasks, and the authors say so.

The first bench has a second argument, and it is about acting. To pick a move, an agent needs the reward, the value of where it stands, and the next move. It does not need the color of every leaf. MuZero, posted in November 2019 by Schrittwieser and colleagues, learns a model that predicts exactly those three quantities. Without being told the rules, it matched AlphaZero at Go, chess and shogi, and it set a new best on fifty-seven Atari games.

Then, in June 2025, V-JEPA 2. A frozen encoder was pretrained on over one million hours of video and images. A predictor conditioned on actions was added, trained on less than sixty-two hours of robot video. On robot arms in two different labs, it picked and placed objects by planning toward goal images, with no data collected in those labs.

Now the floor goes to the second bench, which speaks for generation: video models and autoregressive models. Its first argument is that nobody knows in advance which details matter. The DreamerV3 paper, which we met in episode 10.3, ran an ablation. Its authors write that performance predominantly rests on the unsupervised reconstruction loss of the world model. Take the pixel loss away, and the agent suffers. The flag stays on the pixel station, because that is where the information is.

A second paper makes the same case in its title. Diffusion for World Modeling: Visual Details Matter in Atari, by Alonso and colleagues, at NeurIPS in 2024. Its argument is that the compact discrete latents of earlier world models can throw away details that matter to the agent. Trained entirely inside a diffusion world model, the agent DIAMOND reached a mean human-normalized score of one point four six on Atari one hundred thousand. The authors report it as a new best for agents trained entirely within a world model.

The second bench also argues from breadth. In February 2024, the Genie paper learned playable environments from unlabeled internet video, and it found its own action codes without a single action label. In September 2025, a team at Google DeepMind reported that Veo 3 solves tasks it was never trained for, from segmentation to simple mazes. They suggest that video models are on a path to becoming generalist vision models, the way language models did.

Now the moment that surprises both benches. They are closer than the shouting suggests. Look at the generators. Latent diffusion, by Rombach and colleagues, first posted in December 2021, trains the diffusion model not on pixels but in the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder, to save computation. NVIDIA's Cosmos platform, in January 2025, compresses video with tokenizers, and both its diffusion models and its autoregressive models work on those tokens. Genie too predicts the tokens of a video tokenizer. So many generators already put their loss on a compressed description, and paint the pixels afterwards. Their flag stands on the middle station.

And the movement also goes the other way. V-JEPA 2.1 was posted on the fifteenth of March 2026 by Mur-Labadia, Muckley, Bar, Assran, Sinha, Rabbat, LeCun, Ballas and Bardes. Its title is Unlocking Dense Features in Video Self-Supervised Learning. V-JEPA 2 put its loss only on the hidden tokens. The new version also puts a loss on the visible ones, weighted more strongly near the hidden regions. That is what dense means here: every token is asked to predict, not only the masked ones. The authors explain that visible tokens had no reason to keep local detail.

Look at what did not change. The loss is still measured between features, and never between pixels. And look at what it cost. Adding the loss on visible tokens raised segmentation on ADE20K from twenty-two point two to about thirty-four, and cut the depth error on the NYU depth benchmark from point six eight to point four seven. But it lowered accuracy on action recognition and on ImageNet, and a second ingredient, a loss applied at several layers of the encoder, was needed to win most of that back. So the detail that was once left out of the necklace is now asked for, inside the necklace.

What is left of the disagreement? Two questions. The first is where to put the loss: on frames, on tokens, or on features that no decoder ever reads. The second is what one must be able to predict in order to act. The first bench points to MuZero and V-JEPA 2: reward, value, or a latent, are enough to plan. The second bench points to Dreamer and DIAMOND: drop the pixels, and you may drop what the agent needed. Both have measured results, on different tasks. Atari with a hundred thousand steps is not a robot arm with a goal image, so these results cannot sit on the same rung of the ladder.

Each bench also owes something to the other. In January 2025, the Physics-IQ benchmark tested video generators, Sora among them. The authors found that physical understanding was limited, and that it did not follow visual realism. In their words, visual realism does not imply physical understanding. They also note that some cases were solved, and that learning some physics from observation may be possible. That is the rung the first bench cites. The second bench cites the zero-shot results of Veo 3. And the V-JEPA 2.1 numbers show that a loss on hidden tokens alone left fine detail on the table.

To sum up. Both benches choose where the loss goes: on the pixels, or on a description of them. The prediction bench has feature targets that beat pixel targets in a controlled comparison, and models that plan with MuZero and V-JEPA 2. The generation bench has Dreamer and DIAMOND, where details mattered, and generators that learn many tasks. They already meet in the middle, with latent generators and dense latent prediction. Do language models have world models?

Four rungs separate claims that a language model has a world model: it predicts well, the structure can be read by a probe, the network uses it when edited, and it holds when the task changes. Othello networks climb all four in a small closed world, while taxi-route and chess models stall higher up. A probe shows what is readable, not what is used.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The lights come up on the agora. Two benches face each other across the square, and in the middle stands the ladder of evidence. LENS keeps it up to date. Today's question sounds short: do language models have world models? Each bench gets the floor for the same time, with its best arguments and the studies it cites. At the end, there is no verdict.

Let's fix the words first, as in episode ten point one. A world model can be something you run, or a picture of how the world is put together. Today's debate is mostly about the second. When a network only reads text, does it end up holding the structure of what the text is about?

LENS sets four rungs on the ladder, because the word "knows" hides four different claims. Rung one: the model predicts well. Rung two: the structure can be read from inside the network, with a probe. Rung three: the network uses it, so that editing it changes the output. Rung four: it still holds when the task changes. Each study we meet gets a flag on the highest rung it reaches.

The first bench says that predicting text can force a model of the world. Ilya Sutskever put it this way in an interview on the twenty-seventh of March, twenty twenty-three. To predict the next word well, a model has to understand the reality that produced the words. Text is a trace of the world. To compress the trace well, you rebuild what left it. The bench's best evidence comes from a game.

Othello is played on an eight-by-eight board. Kenneth Li and colleagues trained a GPT-style network on lists of moves only. Each move is one of sixty squares. The network never saw the rules, and never saw a board. The training set was twenty million games of random legal moves. The paper was posted in October twenty twenty-two, and appeared at ICLR in twenty twenty-three.

On games it had never seen, the network's top choice was illegal in one hundredth of one percent of the cases. An untrained network of the same size was wrong ninety-three percent of the time. Could it just have memorised the games? The authors removed one of the four possible openings from training, which is a quarter of all games. The error rate stayed at two hundredths of a percent. So that is rung one.

Then they looked inside. A probe reads one square from the network's activations, and says empty, black or white. A straight-line probe never did better than twenty percent error. A small two-layer probe reached one point seven percent at best. Rung two. Then rung three: the authors edited the activations until the probe's board changed by a single square. The moves the network then allowed matched the edited board. On a thousand positions reachable by legal play, there were point one two wrong moves per position, against two point six eight if nothing was edited. On a thousand positions that legal play can never reach, point zero six, against two point five nine.

A follow-up came in September twenty twenty-three, from Neel Nanda, Andrew Lee and Martin Wattenberg. Their idea was a change of coordinates. The network does not store the board as black and white. It stores it as mine and theirs, from the point of view of the player about to move. In those terms, a straight-line probe reads the board with ninety-nine point six percent accuracy at its best layer. In black and white, the same kind of probe reaches about seventy-five. And adding one direction to the activations steers the moves: point one zero wrong moves, against two point seven two.

Remember episode four point three. A probe that fails does not say the information is missing. The straight line failed because it was asked in the wrong coordinates.

One more study for this bench leaves the game board. In October twenty twenty-three, Wes Gurnee and Max Tegmark probed Llama two models with names of places and dates of events. Straight-line probes recovered where a place is and when an event happened. They are careful: basic ingredients of a world model, they write, and further investigation is needed. That flag sits on rung two.

Now the floor goes to the second bench, for the same time. Its position is that text is not enough. Jacob Browning and Yann LeCun made the case in an essay in August twenty twenty-two. Language is a thin, compressed channel, and much of what we know is not linguistic. A system trained on language alone, they argue, will not reach human understanding, however much text it reads.

LeCun added numbers in March twenty twenty-four, and they are his own rough estimates. A language model trains on about ten to the thirteen tokens, about two times ten to the thirteen bytes. A four-year-old has been awake for about sixteen thousand hours, and by his estimate, about ten to the fifteen bytes have reached the visual cortex. His conclusion: learn from observation and action. Build a model that predicts how the world changes when you act, in the space of representations, as in episode eleven point one.

This bench also has studies. In June twenty twenty-four, Keyon Vafa and colleagues asked what rung one hides. They trained transformers from scratch on taxi rides in Manhattan. The city has four thousand five hundred and eighty intersections and nine thousand eight hundred and forty-six street segments. The model gets an origin and a destination, and writes the turns.

It does very well. Nearly every turn is valid. The model trained on shortest paths found the true shortest route ninety-seven percent of the time, and a probe recovered its current intersection ninety-one percent of the time. Rungs one and two. Then the authors tested the map itself. Two routes that reach the same place should allow the same continuations. For that model, the score was point one zero, where the true map scores one. And a street map rebuilt from its own directions had impossible orientations, and flyovers over other streets.

That matters for rung four. When one move in a hundred was replaced by a random valid move, the share of valid routes fell from ninety-nine percent to sixty-nine. At ten percent of moves replaced, it fell to eight percent.

The second study is from February twenty twenty-six, by András Balogh and Márk Jelasity, at ICLR. They trained many chess models, then built legal games meant to push a model into an illegal move. No model passed. They also tested the probes. The board states extracted from the networks usually played no causal role in the next move. So here is a flag on rung two with nothing above it.

That is the limit LENS wants carved on the side of the ladder. A probe shows what is readable in a network, not what the network uses. We said so in episode four point three, and again in bonus one. Only an edit reaches rung three, and only a change of task reaches rung four. In Othello, the edits worked. In chess, they mostly did not. Different games, different training.

Each bench now gets one reply. The first points to what the papers themselves report: the training data matters. Vafa and colleagues also ran their tests on the Othello networks of Li and colleagues. The one trained on synthetic games recovers the true rules, and plays near-perfect games under detours. The one trained on championship games fails at once. That puts a flag on rung four for the first bench. And in taxi routes, a model trained on random walks, not shortest paths, stayed valid when ten percent of moves were replaced, where the shortest-path models fell to eight. So incoherence may be a fact about a recipe, not about text.

The second bench answers that a game with fixed rules is a small world. Li and colleagues list natural language as future work. In the taxi city, most models stalled below the top rung, and in chess, every model tested did. And in a world whose rules nobody gives, the question stays open.

Look at the ladder as it stands, as of November twenty twenty-six. A flag on rung two in every study. Rung three reached in Othello, and mostly missed in chess. Rung four: reached by the synthetic Othello network and the random-walk taxi model, missed by the other taxi models and every chess model. We have not said who is right. We have said where each claim stands.

To sum up. First, "does it have a world model" hides four claims: it predicts, it can be read, it is used, it holds. Second, some networks climb all four rungs in a small, closed world, while others stall on the upper ones. Third, probes show what is readable, not what is used. What does « understanding the world » mean?

Understanding hides three claims, like three stations at an editing desk: predict what happens, predict what would happen if one acted, and say what would have happened otherwise. A violation-of-expectation test turns a video model's prediction error into surprise at impossible events. V-JEPA showed more surprise than pixel-prediction and language models, which supports only the first claim, with benchmark caveats.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The lights come up on the agora. Two benches face each other, and in the middle stands the ladder of evidence that LENS keeps up to date. Today the benches share one word: understanding. People say a model understands the world, and the word hides several different claims. We will sort the claims out, then look at a test that borrows its method from infant research and gives it to networks.

Last time, the ladder asked how well a claim is supported. Today we ask which claim is being made. For that, LENS wheels in an editing desk, the kind you use to cut a film. The film is the world, as a model sees it. The desk has three stations, and each station is one meaning of understanding.

At the first station, you watch the film and guess what comes next. A ball rolls toward a screen, so you expect it to come out on the other side. That is the first definition: predict what will happen. Judea Pearl, whose three levels of causal questions we borrow here, calls this level seeing, or association. Networks trained on video can get very good at it.

At the second station, you put a hand on a lever. Now the question is: what would happen if I did this? If I nudge the ball, where does it go? That is the second definition: predict what would happen if you acted. Pearl calls it intervention, or doing. In a 2019 article in Communications of the ACM, he argues that interventional questions cannot be answered from passive observation alone, however big the data.

At the third station, you rewind. The ball rolled behind the screen and never came back. What would have happened if the screen had not been there? You cut the film at one frame, change a single thing, and splice a new ending next to the old one. That is the third definition: say what would have happened otherwise. Pearl calls it counterfactual, or imagining. Bareinboim and colleagues proved a theorem about these levels: data from one level almost always leaves the next level undetermined. So the three stations are three different claims.

Where do the models of this series stand? V-JEPA, the video model of today's test, sits at the first station. Its authors say it predicts the future only as an observer. V-JEPA 2 adds a second station. In June 2025, a predictor conditioned on actions was trained on less than sixty-two hours of robot video. It then planned pick and place motions toward goal images, on robot arms in two labs it had never seen. That is a claim about acting. For the third station, none of the studies we have met in this chapter tests it. It is simply not measured here.

Now, how do you test the first station without training for the test? Developmental psychology found a way, told in full in episode fourteen point five. Show an infant two scenes that are identical except for one impossible event, and see which one holds the gaze longer. Longer looking is read as surprise, and surprise as a failed expectation. This is the violation-of-expectation paradigm. How to read looking times is still debated.

For a network, the same logic becomes a computation. Take a pair of videos, matched in everything but one event. In one, a ball rolls behind a screen and never comes out. The model reads the first frames and predicts the representation of the frames that follow. Then it compares that prediction with the representation of what really happened. The distance is its surprise, frame by frame. The video with the larger surprise is the one the model calls impossible. Nobody trains the model for this task.

The paper is by Garrido and colleagues, from Meta and two French institutions, and it was posted on arXiv in February 2025. It is a preprint. The authors pretrain V-JEPA on unlabeled video, by predicting hidden blocks in representation space.

They compare it with three other models. VideoMAE version two predicts masked parts in pixel space. Qwen and Gemini one point five Pro are multimodal language models, asked in words which video of a pair is impossible. All of them face three datasets. IntPhys has about three hundred and sixty synthetic videos in its development set. GRASP has about four thousand, synthetic too, and ten properties. InfLevel-lab has about four thousand, filmed in a lab. Chance is fifty percent, and untrained networks are the control.

The headline. On pairs, V-JEPA picks the impossible video ninety-eight percent of the time on IntPhys, sixty-six on GRASP and sixty-two on InfLevel. Pixel prediction and the language models land only marginally above untrained networks.

Property by property, the picture is uneven. Here a smaller model was trained on HowTo100M. On IntPhys, object permanence and continuity both reach about eighty-six percent, against fifty-one for untrained networks. But on GRASP, for color constancy, solidity and collision, there is no significant gain over untrained networks.

The ablations show how little it takes. Hide random pixels instead of blocks, and accuracy on IntPhys drops by about five points, where video classification drops by about twenty. Keep only one tenth of one percent of HowTo100M, which is one hundred and twenty-eight hours of video, and every property stays above seventy percent. A model of one hundred and fifteen million parameters still passes eighty-five. But train on Something-Something alone, a dataset of short clips about motion, and accuracy falls close to chance.

On IntPhys's private test set, with thirty-six hundred videos per property, the huge model's pairwise error is under one percent, against twelve to thirty percent for human participants. Judging a single video is harder. There the huge model's error, measured as one minus AUROC, runs from nineteen to thirty percent, close to the humans' twenty-four to thirty-eight. The smaller model sits near forty percent or worse.

So what does it prove? A network trained only to predict hidden parts of video in representation space, with no labels and no access to these benchmarks, shows more prediction error on the impossible video of a pair, far more often than chance. That holds for object permanence, continuity and shape constancy in these scenes. It is a mechanism shown, for the first station, and it survives changes of masking, size and data.

Now what it does not prove. First, it is not the second or third station. The authors state that V-JEPA cannot condition its predictions on an action, and that object interactions stay close to chance.

Second, the comparison is not tidy. The models differ in data, size and objective, and the language models answer in words, not by surprise. The authors write that this does not mean pixel or language models cannot learn intuitive physics.

Third, benchmarks carry shortcuts. On GRASP, untrained networks can latch onto spurious features. On a relabeled InfLevel test, untrained networks improve too.

Fourth, these are best cases. The context length was chosen for each property to maximize accuracy, and with one fixed length, performance falls somewhat. And those hundred and twenty-eight hours are seen over and over, to match thirty years of viewing.

Fifth, a later benchmark. IntPhys 2, in June 2025, reports most models tested at chance, against near-perfect humans. And a December 2025 workshop paper trained V-JEPA on child headcam video, and found no significant gain on it.

None of this says the network expects the way an infant does. A higher error is a distance between two vectors. Calling it surprise is a reading borrowed from infants. As in chapter fourteen: mechanism shown for the test, analogy for the mind.

To sum up. First, understanding hides three claims: predict, predict under action, and say what would have happened otherwise. Second, a violation-of-expectation test turns prediction error into surprise. In 2025, V-JEPA showed more surprise for impossible videos, on pairs and in synthetic scenes, where pixel prediction and language models did not. Third, that is evidence for the first claim only, with the limits of its benchmarks. Next time: episode 13.4, benchmarks, how world models are tested.

Benchmarks: how world models are tested

A benchmark is a measuring instrument with errors, and four families of tests cover intuitive physics, action anticipation, robot planning, and probes of physical quantities. Six pitfalls make scores mislead: test data already seen, settings chosen after the fact, weak baselines, few trials, best-of-N attempts, and flaws in the test itself. Six questions let a reader judge any announcement.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the agora. LENS has a stack of score sheets on the table, and today the job is to read them. A benchmark is a measuring instrument, and every instrument has errors. So we will walk through four families of tests, then six ways a score can mislead. Every number comes with its date and its number of trials.

Picture a shooting range, with one lane for each family and a target board at the end of each lane. Lane one is intuitive physics. IntPhys, posted in March 2018, shows pairs of game-engine videos, one possible and one impossible, and asks which is which. In episode 13.3, V-JEPA scored ninety-eight percent on its development set of about three hundred and sixty videos.

IntPhys 2, from June 2025, has a main set of one thousand and twelve videos, covering permanence, immutability, continuity and solidity. In its Table 2, eight models land between forty-nine and fifty-eight percent overall, where chance is fifty. Humans reach ninety-six. The models differ between the two tests, so this is not a like-for-like drop, but it shows why a harder test is a finer ruler. Physics-IQ, from January 2025, is the same lane for video generators. Episode 10.5 gave its numbers: three hundred and ninety-six real videos, and a best score of twenty-nine point five.

Lane two is action anticipation. Epic-Kitchens-100, from June 2020, holds a hundred hours of cooking filmed from the head, with ninety thousand actions in seven hundred videos. The model sees video that stops one second before an action begins, and must name it. The score is class-mean top-five recall: the right action only has to be among five guesses, and every class weighs the same, rare or common. In June 2025, V-JEPA 2 reached thirty-nine point seven on the validation split, which has nine thousand six hundred and sixty-eight action segments. The best of three earlier models in that table had twenty-seven point six.

Lane three is robot planning. In the V-JEPA 2-AC study, each cell of Table 2 is ten trials, with the objects and starting poses varied from one trial to the next, in two labs. Picking and placing a cup in the first lab worked eight times out of ten. The baseline, Octo, managed two.

Lane four is probes of physical quantities. A linear readout on frozen features predicts a quantity such as mass or friction. CALIPER, a preprint of September 2026, tests eight representations, V-JEPA 2 among them, on two thousand simulated episodes. The question is how far an object of unknown mass and friction will slide.

Now the six pitfalls, one at each target. Number one: the test was already seen. In May 2024, a team wrote twelve hundred and five fresh problems, called GSM1k, to match a popular school maths test. Some language models scored up to eight percent lower on the fresh set. For video world models, this series has found no published overlap measurement, and that is not proof that there is none. One defence is a held-out set. IntPhys 2 keeps three hundred and forty-four videos, released without their metadata, and scores them through a leaderboard.

Number two: choices made after the fact. In the V-JEPA study of episode 13.3, the context length is chosen, for each property, as the one that maximizes performance. With one fixed length, the paper says performance falls. In IntPhys 2, each model reports its best run for each column, except the held-out set, which was run once. If a setting is picked by looking at the very scores you report, they are flattered. That is the double dipping of EEG episode 15.8, and the garden of forking paths of EEG episode 15.9. CALIPER finds the same thing in probes: changing how frames are aggregated moves a probe more than pretraining does.

Number three: the baseline. In the Quantum series, episodes 15.5 and 15.6, the habit is an honest baseline. In World-in-World, an ICLR 2026 paper, a vision-language policy without any world model solves fifty point three percent of five hundred and fifty-one recognition episodes. With the Runway Gen4 world model, it solves sixty-four point eight. That reference is fair: the same agent, minus the model. Now look at Table 3 of V-JEPA 2. Cosmos gets eighty samples per action, and V-JEPA 2-AC gets eight hundred, taking four minutes against sixteen seconds. The paper prints these budgets, and the reader has to weigh them.

Number four: few trials. Take eight successes out of ten. The exact ninety-five percent interval runs from forty-four to ninety-seven percent, computed with the kit's Python. Octo's two out of ten runs from two and a half to fifty-six. The intervals overlap, yet an exact test of eight against two gives p equals zero point zero two three. So the ranking is probably real, and the size of the gap is not known. With five hundred and fifty-one episodes, the interval shrinks to about four points on each side. The Quantum series adds the rest: fix the number of trials, and the analysis, before the run. That is preregistration.

Number five: best of N. On Physics-IQ Verified, Cosmos 3 Super, image to video, scored forty-two point seven in early September 2026 with no help. With rewritten prompts, forty-eight point six on the first of October. Add the best of eight attempts, and fifty-five point one on the twelfth of October. The selector is WMReward, a V-JEPA 2 surprise score from a January 2026 paper. The model has not changed, only the number of tries. Entries come from their own authors. And Sora 2 scores twenty-six point five on Verified, against forty-two point three on the original board, so never mix the two scales.

Number six: the instrument itself. Physics-IQ Verified revised fifty-seven point six percent of the original samples, and the rankings of six models moved, with a Kendall tau of zero point four six. CALIPER shows the same for probes. In a clean, fixed-camera scene, all eight representations land within zero point zero two R-squared of the ceiling. Resample camera, lighting and clutter, and the same eight spread over zero point five. A clean test cannot rank them.

So, six questions for any announcement. Had the model seen the test? Who chose the settings, and when? Compared with what, at what budget? How many trials, and what interval? How many attempts hide behind the one score? And has the test itself been checked?

To sum up. First, four families of tests: intuitive physics, anticipation, robot planning, and probes. Second, each score has a date, a number of trials, and a way to mislead. Third, six questions let you read an announcement critically. Next time: episode 13.5, evidence to date.

Evidence to date

Each claim about world models gets a stamp card with four boxes: proposed, shown in simulation, shown on a lab robot, and shown at scale, and it sits at the highest level a named paper actually shows. Representations are shown at scale, planning on lab robots, hierarchy in simulation, and video physics and language-model world models only in part. An empty box means not found.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The lights come up on the agora, for the last time in this chapter. Over this series, many claims were made about world models, and each came with a date and a caveat. Today they all go into one table, dated as of November twenty twenty-six. The rule is strict: a claim sits at the highest level that a named paper actually shows, and never higher.

Picture a stamp card for each claim, with four boxes. A rubber stamp prints a round mark in a box, and the mark carries the date of the paper that earns it. No paper, no stamp.

The four boxes are four levels. First, proposed: the idea is written down, with no results. Second, shown in simulation: results in a simulated world, or on a benchmark. Third, shown on a lab robot: real trials on a physical robot, in a lab. Fourth, shown at scale. That last phrase needs a rule, and the rule is this series' own convention, not a standard: many tasks or environments, with results from more than one group, or use outside a lab.

A full stamp means shown. A half stamp means shown in part, or with mixed results. An empty box means that nothing was found. That is different from shown not to work.

First card: JEPA learns useful representations. Proposed in June twenty twenty-two, in LeCun's position paper. Then benchmarks: I-JEPA in twenty twenty-three, and V-JEPA 2 in June twenty twenty-five. For the fourth box, V-JEPA 2 reports motion understanding, action anticipation and video question answering. In May twenty twenty-six, a study by authors who are not on the V-JEPA papers compared four video encoders across five robustness axes, and found a consistent profile for the latent predictors. So this row is stamped three times, with one caveat: useful is not best. Other methods still lead on some tasks.

Second card: JEPA enables planning. Proposed in the same position paper, twenty twenty-two. In simulation, LeWorldModel, March twenty twenty-six, plans on simulated control benchmarks. On a robot, V-JEPA 2-AC, June twenty twenty-five: two Franka arms in two labs, goal pictures, short tasks, and ten trials per cell, in Table 2. Later, RoboJEPA, in October, reports sixty-seven percent of grasps and twenty-seven percent of pick-and-place on a real arm, in its Table 3. So the third box is full. The fourth is empty: short tasks and a few arms do not meet the convention. The latest found is RoboJEPA, October twenty twenty-six.

Third card: H-JEPA, the stack of JEPAs at several time scales. Proposed in June twenty twenty-two, in section four point six of the position paper. In simulation, H-JEPA, posted in October twenty twenty-six, raises success on a simulated ant maze from eighteen to seventy-three percent. In November, WARP-JEPA, from another group, reports planning up to eighteen point eight times faster on frozen H-JEPA models.

The robot box gets a half stamp. HWM, in April twenty twenty-six, plans at two time scales on a real Franka arm: thirty-five successes out of fifty for a cup, thirty out of fifty for a box, against none for the flat planner, in its Table 1. But its levels share one space, where H-JEPA gives each level its own. H-JEPA's own robot result is offline. The fourth box is empty.

Fourth card: video models simulate physics. Proposed in February twenty twenty-four, in OpenAI's report on Sora: the company's claim. For the second box, a half stamp. On Physics-IQ, January twenty twenty-five, the best model scored twenty-nine point five out of one hundred, in Table 1. The latest benchmark found, October twenty twenty-six, gives the best of eight models fifty-seven point seven six, on its own scale. None reaches one hundred.

The robot box also gets a half stamp. In December twenty twenty-five, the Gemini Robotics team used a Veo-based model to rank eight robot policies on five tasks, and checked the ranking against more than sixteen hundred real trials. That shows a use: evaluating policies. It does not show that the physics is right. The fourth box is empty.

Last card: language models have a world model. Proposed, and disputed: Sutskever argued for it in twenty twenty-three, Browning and LeCun against in twenty twenty-two. In the second box, a half stamp. The Othello network of Li and colleagues has a board that can be read and edited. But the chess study of Balogh and Jelasity, twenty twenty-six, found no fully sound model, and in most of them the probed board had no causal role. No robot result was found. The fourth box is empty. The latest found, April twenty twenty-six, tested four commercial models on mazes, and its authors conclude that they build no robust spatial world model.

Now read the table across. Every row has its first box. Almost none has its last. Two cautions. An empty box means not found, and its date says when the search stopped. It does not mean shown not to work. And a stamp does not travel: a robot stamp for planning gives nothing to the hierarchy.

These questions are open, and named. Does latent planning hold on long horizons and many tasks? Can a hierarchy with separate levels plan on a real robot, in closed loop? Do video models reach physical consistency? Do language models use the world models that probes find, when the task changes? And which parts of the architecture of episode twelve point three, the configurator, the memory, the hard-wired cost, have any published system?

To sum up. As of November twenty twenty-six, representations are shown at scale, useful but not always best. Planning is shown on lab robots, not at scale. The hierarchy is shown in simulation, and in part on a robot. Video physics and language models are each shown in part. Empty means not found.
