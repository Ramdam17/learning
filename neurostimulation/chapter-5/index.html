Sound you can focus

Focused ultrasound is a pressure wave of a few hundred kilohertz that a bowl, lens or phased array makes arrive in step at one spot, like bats ringing bells with timed delays. The focus is a few millimetres wide and a few centimetres long, and can sit deep. Low-intensity pulses modulate with little heating, while high-intensity beams ablate.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Most bats find their way with calls pitched above the range of human hearing. A belfry at dusk is full of such calls, and we hear none of them. This chapter is about that kind of sound, ultrasound, and about a trick no bat needs. We can aim it, so that its energy lands on one small spot deep inside a head.

First, what is the wave? Sound is neither electric nor magnetic. It is a wave of pressure. As it passes, the tissue is squeezed, then stretched, then squeezed again. Ultrasound is simply sound above about twenty kilohertz. The ultrasound used to stimulate the brain sits much higher. Transducers for the brain typically work between two hundred fifty and one thousand kilohertz, and many studies use five hundred. In soft tissue, sound travels at roughly fifteen hundred metres per second. So at five hundred kilohertz, one wavelength is about three millimetres.

That wavelength matters, because it sets the smallest spot you can make. Here is the picture for this episode. Under the belfry vault hangs a choir of bats, each with a small bell. If they all ring at the same instant, the sound spreads out and fades. But if each bat rings at its own carefully chosen delay, every ring reaches one chosen spot at the same moment. There the rings add up. Everywhere else they arrive off the beat, and they partly cancel.

A real transducer does the same thing. The simplest one is a bowl. Every part of its curved surface is the same distance from one point, so the waves arrive there in step. A lens can do the job too. Other devices use many small elements, called a phased array, and fire each element with its own tiny delay. Those delays are the bats' timing. Change them, and the focus moves, without moving the device.

Now, the shape of the spot. The waves do not meet at a perfect point. They meet in a small ellipsoid, narrow across the beam and long along it. The ITRUSST consortium's practical guide gives simple rules. The width grows with the wavelength and with the ratio of focal distance to the width of the transducer. The length grows with the same ratio squared. At five hundred kilohertz, depending on the study, the focus is about three to five millimetres wide, and about eighteen to thirty millimetres long. In a 2014 human study, Legon and colleagues measured four point nine millimetres across and eighteen along. Raise the frequency and the spot shrinks. But the skull will make you pay for it, as we will see.

What is stimulated? Whatever tissue sits in that spot. Neurons, certainly. Glial cells and blood vessels sit there too, and whether they take part is still open. The guide adds a warning. The half-maximum outline describes the beam. It does not mark the region where neurons respond, and that region is not yet known. Still, the appeal is clear. TMS and transcranial currents are strongest near the surface. A focus can sit deep, with a width of a few millimetres. In 2018, Legon and colleagues aimed one at the human thalamus.

Now the parameters. A protocol is defined by the frequency, the pressure or intensity at the focus, the pulse duration, how often pulses repeat, the duty cycle, which is the fraction of time the sound is on, and the total duration. Here is one real recipe. In 2018, Legon and colleagues stimulated the human motor cortex at five hundred kilohertz. Each pulse lasted about a third of a millisecond. Pulses repeated a thousand times per second, so the duty cycle was thirty-six percent, and the whole train lasted half a second. The spatial-peak pulse-average intensity, measured in a water tank, was seventeen watts per square centimetre. The mechanical index was zero point nine. Using the guide's formula, that is a peak pressure of roughly zero point seven megapascals in water. It is our own calculation, and it is only the tank. The skull lets just part of it through.

Two reference points come from diagnostic ultrasound. A mechanical index of one point nine, and an intensity of one hundred ninety watts per square centimetre. The 2025 ITRUSST consensus, led by Aubry, builds on them. It calls the mechanical risk non-significant when the mechanical index is at most one point nine. For heat, it accepts a temperature rise of at most two degrees Celsius, or a thermal dose of at most two equivalent minutes at forty-three degrees in the brain. The authors say plainly that these are not safety limits. They are levels below which significant harm is not expected, for people with no contraindication.

That brings us to the two regimes. The physics is the same. The dose is not. Low-intensity ultrasound, called TUS or LIFU, is pulsed, so that tissue barely warms. In a 2010 mouse study, Tufail and colleagues measured a rise of less than one hundredth of a degree. High-intensity focused ultrasound deposits so much energy that it heats the tissue until it is destroyed. That is ablation. The consensus we just read explicitly leaves it out. Episode five point four returns to it.

What do we know? In mice, Tufail and colleagues evoked movements and hippocampal activity in 2010. In macaques, Folloni and colleagues sonicated the amygdala and the anterior cingulate cortex in 2019. The coupling of each target with the rest of the brain dropped, and only for the target. In humans, Legon and colleagues reported in 2014 that sound aimed at the somatosensory cortex reduced the amplitude of sensory evoked potentials. The effect vanished when the beam moved one centimetre away. In 2018, in forty participants, sound aimed at the thalamus reduced an evoked component linked to that nucleus, compared with sham. What is debated is how it works, how long the effect lasts, and what part the ear plays. Episode five point three takes those up. We make no clinical claim here.

The skull is the hard part, and episode five point two is about it. Bone slows and distorts the wave unevenly, like bats whose rings arrive late. In three skulls measured at five hundred kilohertz, Riis and colleagues found that only thirteen to thirty-one percent got through, depending on the region. Thicker bone passed less and distorted more. And a tilt of only three degrees moves the focus about three millimetres at six centimetres depth, as much as the focus is wide.

Here is the classic trap. Headlines say focused ultrasound for two very different things. One is a pulse that nudges neurons. The other is a beam that burns a small hole. Ask for the intensity, the duty cycle, the temperature rise, and whether a number was measured in water or in the head.

To sum up. Ultrasound is a pressure wave, a few hundred kilohertz. A bowl, a lens or a phased array makes the rings arrive together. The spot is a few millimetres wide and a few centimetres long. Low-intensity pulses modulate. High-intensity beams ablate. And a number is only meaningful if you know where it was measured.

PLATE 5.28:27

Getting through the skull

Bone is fast, lossy and uneven: it shifts the wave's phase, drops the pressure and heats, like light through an uneven window pane, so the dose in the brain differs from that in a water tank. A CT scan, or a pseudo-CT made from an MRI, maps the bone, and a simulation predicts the focus and pressure, only as good as its skull properties.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the skull showed up as a few bats whose bells ring late. This episode opens that box. What does bone do to a sound wave? How do we find out, for one particular head? And what does it change about the dose?

Here is the picture. In the belfry, the morning sun comes through an old window pane and lands as a bright spot on the floor. The pane is uneven, thick on one side and thin on the other. Light slows down inside glass, so the spot lands away from where a ruler says it should. It is dimmer, because the glass reflects and absorbs some of the light. And the glass itself warms. Light is not sound, but three effects carry over: a shift, a loss, and heat. Now suppose you hold a thickness map of the pane. You could trace the beam through it, and know where the spot will fall before the sun comes up. That map is what this episode is about.

First, the bone. A skull is not a uniform shell. It has two hard plates around a spongy layer. Sound travels at about fifteen hundred metres per second in water. In bone it is much faster. Riis and colleagues measured three human skulls at five hundred kilohertz. Averaged over each skull, the speed was between twenty-four hundred and twenty-nine hundred metres per second. So the wave comes out of the bone early, by about one to two and a half microseconds, depending on the region. One cycle lasts two microseconds. That is a phase shift of about two thirds of a cycle in the parietal bone, and more than a whole cycle in the frontal and occipital bone.

Bone also takes energy. Some is reflected, some is scattered, and some is absorbed and turned into heat. In the same skulls, only thirteen percent of the pressure got through the frontal bone, sixteen percent through the occipital bone, and thirty-one percent through the parietal bone. Thicker bone let less through. Other studies give other numbers, depending on the frequency and the skulls. At two hundred seventy kilohertz, Albelda Gimeno and colleagues measured three skulls at six places. The loss ranged from six point six to thirteen point five decibels. On average, ten decibels means ninety percent less intensity. So the pressure in the brain is not the pressure in the water tank.

The focus can also move. In those six places, it shifted in total by between one and a half and ten millimetres. At that low frequency the focus is thirty millimetres long, so the authors judged the shifts small. But a time shift counts for more cycles as the frequency rises, and the focus gets shorter. At five hundred kilohertz and above, the same bone matters more.

So how do we get the map of the bone? With a CT scan. Bone density on CT is related to sound speed and to attenuation. Pichardo and colleagues showed it in 2011 on seven excised skulls. And Aubry and colleagues showed in 2003 that properties deduced from CT allow a computed focus through an excised skull, checked by experiment.

But many volunteers have an MRI and no CT, and an MRI shows bone poorly. So the field builds a pseudo-CT: a neural network turns the MRI into a CT-like image. In 2023, Yaakub and colleagues did it from T1-weighted images. In thirty-seven healthy people, the simulated focal pressure was point five zero megapascals with the pseudo-CT, and point four eight with the real CT. A simple binary skull mask gave only point two eight. Miscouridou and colleagues used a zero-echo-time sequence, made to see bone. Their focal pressure was within about six percent of the CT result, and the focus within point six millimetres.

Then comes the simulation. A computer takes the head, with a speed, a density and an absorption for each voxel, and solves the wave equation through it. The result is a pressure map: where the focus lands, and how strong it is. Can we trust the codes? In 2022, the ITRUSST group, led by Aubry, compared eleven simulation tools on nine benchmarks, from a flat slab of bone to a whole skull. The median difference in focal pressure was under ten percent, and in focal position one millimetre or less. But every tool was given the same skull. That shows the codes agree with each other. It does not show that the skull properties are right. A 2026 preprint, not yet peer reviewed, compared five skull models with measurements on seven real skulls. Errors in peak pressure were twenty to thirty-one percent, depending on the model. That is why the ITRUSST consensus asks for models validated on real skulls, and for the uncertainty to be reported.

So what does an individual simulation buy? It tells you the dose in this head. Across six places, the pressure loss varied by more than a factor of two. And ignoring the bone structure, as the binary mask did, underestimated the pressure by about forty percent. It tells you where the focus lands. And it shows where the heat goes. This is the acoustic twin of the field models of chapter seven. Build the head from an image, solve a physical law on it, and expect no two heads alike.

Now the safety indices. Both come from diagnostic ultrasound. The mechanical index is the peak negative pressure in megapascals, divided by the square root of the frequency in megahertz. It tracks the risk of cavitation, where tiny bubbles form and collapse. At five hundred kilohertz, an index of one point nine is a peak negative pressure of about one point three megapascals. That is our own calculation. The diagnostic recipe ignores the skull, so it overestimates what reaches the brain. ITRUSST therefore proposes a transcranial version, MItc, computed with the pressure estimated inside the head. The consensus calls the mechanical risk non-significant at or below one point nine.

The thermal side uses the cranial thermal index, TIC. It assumes the bone sits right at the transducer face and absorbs everything there, a deliberately conservative picture. It is computed from the output power and the width of the beam where it meets the skull. The higher the index, the shorter the allowed exposure: eighty minutes at an index of two, a hundred sixty seconds at four, ten seconds at six. For temperature itself, the consensus gives non-significant levels: a rise of at most two degrees, or thirty-nine degrees absolute. Or a thermal dose of at most two equivalent minutes at forty-three degrees in brain, sixteen in bone, and twenty-one in skin. Simulation matters here too, because most of the heat is deposited at the outer surface of the skull. The hottest place may not be the target.

Where do the mechanical numbers come from? The FDA guidance of 2023, for diagnostic scanners, expects a mechanical index of at most one point nine, or a pulse-average intensity of at most one hundred ninety watts per square centimetre. It adds a time-average limit of seven hundred twenty milliwatts per square centimetre. The community uses these as reference points. The consensus leaves out the time-average one, because the thermal index and the dose describe heating better. And the authors say plainly: these are levels where significant harm is not expected, for people with no contraindication. They are not safety limits.

Here is the classic trap. A simulation is not a measurement. It is a prediction, only as good as the properties we give it. One skull number for everybody is no model at all. So ask four things. Which skull model? Built from which image? Validated how? And with what uncertainty?

To sum up. Bone is fast, lossy and uneven. It shifts the phase, drops the pressure, and heats. A CT, or a pseudo-CT made from an MRI, gives the map. A simulation turns the map into a predicted focus and a predicted pressure. The safety indices only mean something with the pressure inside the head.

PLATE 5.37:12

Mechanisms still debated

What focused ultrasound does in the brain is a line-up of four suspects. Stretch-sensitive channels are plausible from cell studies, the intramembrane bubble is a hypothesis, and warmth explains some setups. The ear is real: pulsed ultrasound can be heard, and hearing alone drove one reported motor effect, so masking, control sites and acoustic shams must rule it out first.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At dusk, a bell rings in the belfry. Nobody saw who pulled the rope. So the keeper does what any keeper would do. He lines up the suspects, and he asks each one for an alibi.

Focused ultrasound has the same problem. Episode five point one showed how the beam reaches a spot inside the head. This episode asks what the beam does when it gets there. Four suspects are standing in the line-up. For each one, we will ask the same question. What did the controls show?

Suspect number one is stretch. Sound is a pressure wave, so it pulls and pushes on the membrane of every cell it crosses. Some ion channels open when their membrane is stretched. They are called mechanosensitive channels. In 2008, Tyler and colleagues applied ultrasound at about four hundred to seven hundred kilohertz to mouse brain slices. They reported that neurons fired, through sodium and calcium channels.

Since then, most of the evidence has come from cells. In 2021, Sorum and colleagues showed that ultrasound opens a stretch-sensitive potassium channel called TRAAK. It worked even when the channel was rebuilt in a bare lipid membrane, with no other cell parts. In one test they used pulses at five megahertz, which is much higher than the frequencies used in people. In 2022, Yoo and colleagues stimulated mouse cortical neurons in a dish at three hundred kilohertz. Calcium entered through mechanosensitive channels, other channels amplified it, and blocking those channels reduced the response. A 2019 mouse study, by Oh and colleagues, pointed to another channel, TRPA1, on astrocytes. Mice without it moved less.

So this suspect is plausible, and it has been seen in cells. It has not been shown in a living human brain.

Suspect number two is the bubble. Not a big bubble. A tiny gap that would open for an instant between the two layers of the membrane, as the pressure swings. Krasovitski and colleagues proposed this idea in 2011, and called it intramembrane cavitation. In 2014, Plaksin and colleagues turned it into a model of a neuron. Its predictions matched published data on mouse motor cortex stimulation.

But notice what kind of evidence this is. It is a calculation that fits data. In the dish experiments of Yoo and colleagues, the authors reported no role for cavitation. In the studies we checked, the gap itself has not been observed in a living neuron. This suspect is a hypothesis, and it is open.

Suspect number three is warmth. Sound energy is absorbed, so tissue warms. With low duty cycles the rise is small, as we saw in episode five point one. But it is not small in every setup. In 2019, Darrow and colleagues sonicated the thalamus of fifteen rats, at three point two megahertz. The evoked potentials dropped, and the focus warmed by about two degrees. To test heat alone, they delivered the same heat with a laser, through an optical fibre, to the same spot. The suppression looked similar. They concluded that the effect was thermal.

Other groups disagreed. In 2020, Spivak, Schafer and Bystritsky replied that inhibition has been seen without such a temperature change. So, depending on the study, heat can explain an effect. It depends on the frequency, and on how long the sound is on. Where the warming is negligible, the question stays open.

Now, suspect number four. The one nobody expected. The ear.

Ultrasound is silent. But pulsed ultrasound repeats, and the repetition can fall in the range we can hear. In 2018, two papers appeared together in Neuron. Guo and colleagues worked in guinea pigs, at two hundred twenty kilohertz. Ultrasound lit up many cortical and subcortical regions. Then they cut the auditory nerves, or removed the fluid of the cochlea, and the activity disappeared. Their idea was that the beam vibrates the cerebrospinal fluid, which connects to the fluid of the ear.

Sato, Shapiro and Tsao worked in mice, with wide-field imaging of the cortex. The activity looked like the response to an audible sound. Chemical deafening reduced it, and it also reduced the startle-like movements.

The story is not one-sided. In 2019, Mohammadjavadi and colleagues smoothed the sharp edges of each pulse. In mice, that removed the auditory response, and the motor response stayed. Two strains of genetically deaf mice still moved. So an auditory route is real in some setups. And a direct effect has not been excluded in others.

What about people? In 2020, Braun and colleagues tested eighteen volunteers. They could tell stimulation from no stimulation. The EEG showed an auditory response. A masking sound brought detection down to chance. And in a skull outside the body, sound was conducted through the bone at the pulse rate. In 2021, Johnstone and colleagues found that many pulses in common use are clearly audible. Ramping and masking helped some participants, but the effects did not simply add up.

Then, in 2024, Kop and colleagues took a protocol pulsed at one thousand hertz, which had been reported to reduce motor cortex excitability. They ran four experiments at three institutions, with twelve, twenty-seven, sixteen and twelve participants. They added a control site, a sound-only control, and masking. The inhibition appeared at the control sites too, and it followed the sound. In their experiments, it was driven by hearing. Episode nine point one covered how a sham can leak. Here, the sham that matters is an acoustic one.

Not every study ends there. In 2022, Butler and colleagues stimulated the visual motion area, with a mask and a control site. Detection improved and reaction times shortened, and an EEG response changed. So, depending on the study, an effect survives the controls, or it does not.

Let us sort the suspects. Established: pulsed ultrasound can be heard, and the ear can drive the brain. Plausible: stretch-sensitive channels, shown in cells, and warmth in some setups. Open: whether a direct effect is present in a human brain at the doses most often used, and by which route.

Here is the classic trap. An effect is not a mechanism. A response to ultrasound does not tell you who rang the bell. So ask four questions. Was the sound masked? Was there a control site, and an acoustic sham? What was the temperature rise? And was the frequency the one used in people, or several times higher?

To sum up. Four suspects, four sets of controls. Stretch is plausible. The bubble is a hypothesis. Warmth depends on the setup. The ear is real, and it must be ruled out first.

PLATE 5.48:10

Beyond modulation: ablation and opening the barrier

The same beam does three things depending on dose. A gentle pulse modulates, a hot focus near fifty-five degrees ablates a small thalamic target permanently to relieve tremor, and a pulse with injected microbubbles opens the blood-brain barrier for about a day, which remains investigational. Only the first is neurostimulation, and heat, injection and reversibility tell the three apart.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The belfry bell is the same bell all day. Tap it softly, and it hums. Strike it hard, and the whole tower shakes. Nothing about the bell has changed. Only the dose.

Focused ultrasound works like that. In the last three episodes, the beam was gentle. It was a pulse meant to nudge neurons, with almost no warming. In episode five point one, the heating at the focus was less than a hundredth of a degree in mice, and the ITRUSST consensus accepts a rise of at most two degrees in people. This episode is about the same beam, with the dial turned up, or with something injected into the blood. These uses are not neurostimulation in the sense of this series. But they use the same physics, and they often share the same headlines. So we should know them.

Here is the picture. Imagine a jar of raw egg white in the belfry. It is clear. Warm it a little, and it stays clear. Warm it past a certain point, and it turns white, and it stays white. You cannot un-cook it. Now focus a beam inside the jar. The white sets only at the focus, and the rest of the jar stays clear. That is ablation.

In the head, the target is a small clump of thalamic cells, called the ventral intermediate nucleus. It sits in a loop that is involved in tremor. The beam is guided by an MRI scanner, which can also measure temperature, almost live. The team first fires a few weak sonications, reaching about forty to forty-five degrees. That is too cool to make a lesion, but warm enough to see on the scan, and to check that the hot spot lies where intended. The patient is awake, so they can report what they feel. Then the power is raised, step by step, until the target reaches roughly fifty-five to sixty degrees Celsius, depending on the study. In a review of two hundred fifty treatments, a peak above fifty-four degrees was reported to ablate tissue. The skull is a challenge for this beam too, and episode five point two covers it.

What does it do? In 2016, Elias and colleagues published a randomized, sham-controlled trial in the New England Journal of Medicine. Seventy-six people with moderate to severe essential tremor, who had not responded to at least two medications, were assigned three to one to a treatment on one side, or to a sham procedure. Neurologists who did not know the assignments rated videos of the tremor. Hand tremor, on a thirty-two-point scale, went from eighteen point one to nine point six at three months with treatment. After the sham, it went from sixteen to fifteen point eight. The difference between groups was eight point three points. At twelve months, the improvement remained.

There were costs. Gait disturbance appeared in thirty-six percent of the treated patients, and numbness or tingling in thirty-eight percent. Twelve months later, nine percent still had the gait problem, and fourteen percent still had the numbness. This is the price of destroying tissue. The lesion is permanent, so the side effects can be permanent too.

The same approach was tested in Parkinson's disease, in people whose main symptom was tremor. In 2017, Bond and colleagues randomized twenty-seven patients, two to one, to treatment or sham. On medication, the median hand tremor score improved by sixty-two percent after treatment, against twenty-two percent after sham. That sham number is large, and it is a useful reminder of how strong a placebo response can be. Early in that trial, two patients had mild weakness, because heat reached a nearby fibre bundle, the internal capsule. After that, the team added another axis of monitoring.

Now, what is the regulatory status? Here is the stamp, as of November 2026. In the United States, the FDA approved the system in July 2016, for a one-sided thalamotomy in essential tremor that no longer responds to medication. In December 2018, it added tremor-dominant Parkinson's disease. In 2021, it added a one-sided pallidotomy for advanced Parkinson's disease with motor complications. In December 2022, it allowed a second side to be treated, at least nine months after the first, in essential tremor. In 2025, it added a related pallidothalamic tractotomy, for Parkinson's disease. In Canada, Health Canada has an active licence, and the manufacturer announced it for essential tremor. We found no licensed Parkinson's indication in Canada. The Canadian database lists the licence, but not the indication, so the indication comes from the manufacturer. In Europe, the manufacturer reports a CE mark for essential tremor, including the second side since 2023, for tremor-dominant Parkinson's disease, and for neuropathic pain.

Now the second use, which is gentler, and it needs something in the blood. The brain is protected by a barrier. The wall of its small vessels is a lining of cells joined tightly, so most drugs, especially large ones like antibodies, cannot get across. Picture a ring of dancers holding hands around the dance floor. Nobody gets through the ring, except the guests the dancers let in.

Now add microbubbles. These are tiny gas bubbles, a few micrometres across, which are injected into a vein. When the sound reaches them, each one swells and shrinks with every pressure swing, and pushes on the vessel wall. At the right dose, the dancers' hands are tugged apart for a while. In 2001, Hynynen and colleagues did this in eighteen rabbits. They found that the barrier could be opened at the target, and that the lowest powers did it without damage to the neurons around.

Here, the heat is not the point. In the first trial in people with Alzheimer's disease, Lipsman and colleagues, in 2018, used two hundred twenty kilohertz. Each spot was sonicated for fifty seconds, in bursts of three hundred milliseconds. The duty cycle was less than one percent. The power was set at about half of the level where the bubbles began to cavitate, which was found by a ramp test. Five patients took part. The barrier opened within seconds, in the target, in every patient. The scan showed it closed again within about twenty-four hours. There was no serious adverse event, and no clinically significant worsening on cognitive scores at three months. This was a phase one safety trial. It did not test whether anyone got better.

In 2024, Rezai and colleagues went one step further, in three people. Each month for six months, they paired the ultrasound with an infusion of an antibody against amyloid. The amyloid fell numerically more in the treated areas than in the matching untouched areas on the other side. Three people is a proof of concept, not a treatment effect.

So here is the second stamp. As of November 2026, opening the barrier is investigational. We found no licensed indication in Canada, in the United States, or in Europe. In the United States, the trials we found run under investigational device exemptions.

Here is the classic trap. A headline says ultrasound in the brain, and you cannot tell which of three things it means. Ask three questions. How much heat? Under two degrees, or about fifty-five. Was anything injected? Bubbles, or nothing. And can it be undone? A gentle pulse is meant to leave no lasting change, a barrier closes in a day, and a lesion stays.

To sum up. Same beam, three doses. A gentle pulse modulates. A hot focus ablates, for tremor, and the approval is real but narrow. A pulse with bubbles opens the barrier for about a day, and that is still investigational. Only the first is neurostimulation.
