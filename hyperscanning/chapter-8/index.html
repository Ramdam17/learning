The dyad is the unit

A study of twenty couples has twenty independent units, not forty people, a thousand channel pairs or thousands of windows, since a dyad's members share session, room and each other. Counting people inflates false positives to about one in six, and counting windows to over two in three. The remedy is to average within dyads or use mixed models.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the ball. Twenty couples are on the floor tonight. Before we test anything about them, we need to answer a question that sounds too simple. How many of them are there?

Let's count the way a spreadsheet might. Twenty couples means forty dancers. Each dancer wears a cap with thirty-two channels. Between two caps, that makes one thousand and twenty-four channel pairs. Cut each recording into short windows, and you can easily reach tens of thousands of rows. Every one of these numbers has been used, at some point, as a sample size.

Only one of them is right. The study has twenty independent units. Twenty dyads.

Why not forty people? Because the two members of a dyad are not independent. They share the same session, the same room, the same task, and above all, each other. Whatever happened between them shapes both recordings at once. A measure of synchrony makes this explicit. It is computed from both people together, and when it is symmetric, it gives one value per dyad.

This idea has a long history outside neuroscience. Researchers who study couples, families or teams call it nonindependence. A classic reference is the book Dyadic Data Analysis, by Kenny, Kashy and Cook, published in 2006.

What goes wrong if we count the wrong unit? Let's simulate it. Twenty dyads, and no real effect at all. We run the same test thousands of times, and count how often it says significant at five percent.

Counted as dyads, the test is wrong about one time in twenty. That is exactly what five percent promises. Counted as forty people, with two almost identical values per dyad, it is wrong about one time in six. Counted as windows, fifty per dyad, it is wrong in more than two cases out of three. The data did not change. Only the count did.

The reason is simple. Every extra copy of the same information makes the test more confident, without adding any new evidence. Statisticians call this pseudo-replication. In hyperscanning, it hides in three places: people, channels and time.

So how do we keep the dyad as the unit, and still use all our data? Three habits.

First, summarise inside the dyad before you compare dyads. Average over windows and over trials within each dyad, then run the group test on one value per dyad and per condition.

Second, when you need the windows or the trials, use a model that knows where they come from. A mixed model with a random effect for each dyad lets the data inside a dyad be correlated. We build one in the next episode.

Third, build the null at the level of dyads too. With twenty dyads, each A can be paired with the nineteen B's who were not their partner. That gives three hundred and eighty pseudo-pairs. The real dyads are compared with that distribution, dyad by dyad, or as a group mean against the mean of shuffled pairings.

One more distinction matters. Some dyads have two different roles: parent and child, teacher and student, leader and follower. Their members are distinguishable, and the analysis can keep track of who is who. Other dyads are two strangers in identical roles. Their members are indistinguishable, and any analysis that depends on calling one of them A is arbitrary. Decide which kind you have before you collect data.

And for groups larger than two, the same logic climbs one level. A class, a band or a team becomes the unit, and the people inside it are no longer independent.

What does this mean for planning? The number that sets your power is the number of dyads. Recording longer, adding channels, or cutting more windows can make each dyad's value more precise. It does not give you more dyads. We will compute power in dyads in episode eight point four.

Let's recap. Count dyads, not dancers. Two members of a dyad share too much to be independent. Average inside the dyad, or let a mixed model handle it. Build the null from pseudo-pairs, at the same level. Decide early whether your dyads have roles.

Next, we give each dancer two badges, and build a mixed model for dyads.

PLATE 8.25:13

Mixed models for dyads

A mixed model gives each dyad a random intercept, so people within a dyad may resemble each other. The actor-partner model asks whether my rating depends on my own trait, my partner's, and the dyad's synchrony. Treating people as independent inflates false positives, and because pseudo-pairs reuse people, their random effects must be crossed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the ball. Last time we counted couples, not dancers. Tonight every guest wears two badges. One names the dyad. The other names the person.

That is exactly what a mixed model needs. Picture the guest list as a table, with one row per person. Each row has a dyad badge, a person badge, and a score, say how engaged that person felt. Two rows always share a dyad badge. And rows that share a badge look more alike than rows that do not.

A mixed model has two kinds of terms. Fixed effects are what you want to estimate, the same for every dyad: a slope, or a difference between conditions. A random effect is a deviation that belongs to a badge. Give each dyad its own random intercept, and the model learns how much dyads differ from each other, and how much people differ inside a dyad. In our simulation of thirty dyads, fifty-four percent of the variance sits between dyads. That is the intraclass correlation. It is the reason a person count would mislead us.

Now let's link behaviour to the pair. Here is an illustrative example, entirely simulated. Thirty dyads of strangers. Each person has a trait score, and an engagement rating. Does my engagement depend on my own trait, on my partner's trait, or on both? This is the question of the actor partner interdependence model, or APIM, described in the book by Kenny, Kashy and Cook, and in a short 2005 paper by Cook and Kenny.

We build it step by step. Step one: my own trait predicts my own rating. That is the actor effect. Step two: my partner's trait predicts my rating too. That is the partner effect. For each row, the partner's trait is simply the other row of the dyad, copied into a new column. Step three: add the dyad's synchrony. It is one value per dyad, shared by both rows. Step four: add the random intercept for the dyad. In formula notation, rating, tilde, own trait plus partner trait plus synchrony plus, one given dyad.

What comes out? The actor effect is zero point two eight. The partner effect is zero point two six. Both intervals just clear zero. The synchrony effect is zero point three, but its interval runs from minus zero point zero six to zero point six five, so we cannot say. We know the planted values: actor zero point four, partner zero point two five, synchrony zero point three. A single study of thirty dyads wanders around them.

Why not simply treat sixty people as sixty observations? Because synchrony belongs to the dyad. Treated as independent, its standard error is zero point one four. In the mixed model it is zero point one eight. We repeated the study a thousand times with no synchrony effect at all. Treating people as independent called it significant eight percent of the time. The mixed model, five and a half percent.

Two limits. Our strangers are interchangeable, so one actor effect and one partner effect serve everyone. With roles, such as parent and child, each role gets its own pair. And a random intercept can only describe members who resemble each other. If one member's high goes with the other's low, as with a leader and a follower, this model cannot represent it.

Now the second badge plays a different part. Remember the pseudo-pairs, each A matched with every B who was not their partner. With twenty people on each side, that gives three hundred and eighty pseudo-pairs. They are not independent either, because each person appears in nineteen of them. Someone who syncs easily with everybody raises nineteen values at once. A dyad badge cannot help here, since a person belongs to many pairs, not to one. We need random effects that are crossed: one intercept for each A person, and one for each B person, estimated together. Baayen, Davidson and Bates described this crossed structure in 2008.

In our simulation, the mean synchrony of pseudo-pairs is zero point three one. Treating the three hundred and eighty as independent gives a standard error of zero point zero zero five. The crossed model gives zero point zero two two, about four times larger. Over three hundred simulated studies, the naive interval caught the true mean thirty-six percent of the time. The crossed model, ninety-two percent.

One last caution. Mixed models estimate variances, and variances are hard to pin down with few dyads. Plain Wald tests tend to be too optimistic in small samples. Luke showed in 2017 that the Kenward Roger and Satterthwaite corrections keep errors near five percent. Report your variance components, and say which correction you used.

Let's recap. Give each dyad a badge, and let it be a random effect. The actor effect is my trait on my behaviour. The partner effect is theirs on mine. Synchrony enters at the dyad level. When pseudo-pairs reuse people, cross the random effects.

Next, permutations and multiple comparisons.

PLATE 8.36:06

Permutations and multiple comparisons

Shuffling partners builds a null for synchrony, and the p-value is one over the number of shuffles plus one, never zero. Across 1,024 channel pairs, false positives are guaranteed without correction. Bonferroni is harsh, false discovery rate allows a small share of false findings, and cluster permutation exploits patchy effects, but testing a selected cell on the same data is circular.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the ball. Tonight there is one rule. At every round, the couples split up, and everyone takes a new partner at random.

DICE runs the shuffle. Why shuffle? Because we want to know what synchrony looks like when partners do not belong together.

Take one pair of channels, one on her cap and one on his, in twenty simulated dyads. In the real pairing, the group average, in Fisher z units, is zero point zero seven two. Is that large?

Round one: shuffle the partners, and compute the same average. Round two. Round three. Each round adds one number to the card. After twelve rounds, the card is already filling in. After five thousand rounds, the null band is smooth. It is centred near zero, with a spread of about zero point zero one four.

Now, where does the real value land? Not one shuffle out of five thousand reached it. So the p value is one, divided by five thousand and one. That is zero point zero zero zero two, and never zero. Phipson and Smyth explained why in 2010: the real pairing is itself one of the possible arrangements.

That was one pair of channels. A real study has a whole matrix.

Each cap has thirty-two channels. Between two brains, that makes thirty-two times thirty-two, one thousand and twenty-four cells. Test every cell at five percent, and even with no synchrony anywhere, about fifty-one cells should light up. In our simulated null study, exactly fifty-one did. Each one was wrong.

And this is the small version. Add five frequency bands and twenty time windows, and you have one hundred and two thousand, four hundred tests. At five percent, five thousand, one hundred and twenty false alarms are expected. Even one hundred independent tests give a ninety-nine point four percent chance of a false alarm. Without a correction, false positives are guaranteed.

We repeated the null study three hundred times. Every single one had at least one false positive. On average, forty-eight cells.

There are several answers. The oldest is the Bonferroni correction: divide five percent by the number of tests. Across our three hundred null studies, only five reported anything. But it is harsh. In a study with a real effect, it finds just nine cells.

The second answer changes the question. Instead of never being wrong, accept that a small share of the discoveries are false, and control that share. Benjamini and Hochberg proposed this false discovery rate in 1995. Sort the p values, and keep those below a rising line. In the study with a real effect, it finds nineteen cells, all inside the planted block. In the null studies, it reported something seven times out of three hundred.

The third answer uses the shape of the effect. A real effect is rarely a single cell. It is a patch of neighbouring cell pairs. Maris and Oostenveld described cluster permutation tests in 2007. First, threshold every cell. Second, join neighbouring cells into clusters. Third, give each cluster a mass: the sum of its t values. Fourth, in every shuffle, keep only the largest cluster mass. One comparison then covers the whole matrix.

For channel pairs, neighbours need a new definition. Two cells touch when her channels are neighbours and his channels are neighbours. Ayrolles and colleagues described this for inter-brain links in 2021.

Here, the largest real cluster has a mass of one hundred and sixty-seven, and a cluster p value of zero point zero zero six. It covers forty-eight cells: thirty-seven inside the planted block, and eleven just outside. In the null study, no cluster survived. Across the three hundred null studies, fifteen reported one. That is five percent, as designed.

A cluster test says an effect exists in the cluster, not where its exact edges are. Sassenhagen and Draschkow made this point in 2019.

A fourth tool is to test less. Fix a region of interest before you see the data, from theory or from earlier work. Average its cells for each dyad, and run one test. Here, one test gives one p value, zero point zero zero zero two.

The last trap is subtle. Look at the whole matrix, pick the cell with the strongest synchrony, and then test that same cell on the same data. In our null study, the strongest cell has a t value of three point zero, and a p value of zero point zero zero three seven. It looks significant.

We ran this select-then-test routine on two thousand studies with no effect at all. It came out significant two thousand times out of two thousand. Now select the cell on ten dyads, and test it on the other ten. That is significant one hundred and eleven times out of two thousand, five point six percent, close to the five percent we promised.

Kriegeskorte and colleagues called this circular analysis, or double dipping, in 2009. In their survey of one hundred and thirty-four functional MRI papers, forty-two percent contained at least one such analysis. The remedy: choose on one part of the data, test on another, or fix the choice in advance.

In code, the recipe is short. MNE offers cluster permutation tests with an adjacency matrix. For channel pairs, you build that matrix yourself. The false discovery rate is one function call in the Python statistics libraries.

Let's recap. Test a thousand cells, and false positives are guaranteed. Permute the partners to build the null. Control the family of errors, or the share of false discoveries. Use clusters when effects are patches. Fix the regions in advance. And never test the cells you picked on the same data.

PLATE 8.45:22

How many dyads? Power analysis

Power is the share of simulated studies that reach significance when a known effect is planted, and the sample size that matters is the number of dyads. Halving the effect needs about four times as many dyads. Published effects are inflated by small samples and selective reporting, so plan on a smaller, realistic effect and write it into the preregistration.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the ball. Tonight the organiser has one question, and it must be answered before a single invitation goes out. How many couples should she invite? Too few, and a real effect can slip by unseen. Too many, and months of recordings are wasted.

The answer is called power. Power is the chance that a study finds an effect, when the effect is really there. A common target is eighty percent, a convention described by Cohen in 1992.

There are formulas for power. But a dyad study comes with its own pipeline, and a formula needs assumptions that rarely fit it. So the organiser rehearses instead. She plants an effect of a known size in DyadSim. She runs the whole study. Then she runs it again, a thousand times, and counts the evenings where the test came out significant. That proportion is the power.

Here is the rehearsal. Each simulated dyad gives one number: its phase locking value in the alpha band, minus the value it gets with a shuffled partner. One number per dyad, because the dyad is the unit. The group test is a one-sided t test at five percent. And the size of the effect is Cohen's d: the mean of those numbers, divided by their spread across dyads.

First, a sanity check. Plant nothing. With fifteen dyads, four point one percent of the thousand rehearsals came out significant. With thirty dyads, five point five percent. That is about five percent, as promised.

Now plant an effect. A large one, with a d of about one. Seven dyads are enough to reach eighty percent power. A medium one, with a d of zero point seven four. Twelve dyads. A small one, with a d of about zero point four. Thirty-eight dyads. The smaller the effect, the more dyads you need, and faster than you might expect. Cut the effect by half, and you need about four times as many dyads.

So which effect should the organiser plant? The tempting answer is the one published in the literature. That is where the trouble starts.

Published effects are often inflated. Small studies have low power, and only the significant ones tend to be reported. In 2013, Button and colleagues showed that the average power of neuroscience studies is very low, and that this leads to overestimated effects. In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration replicated a hundred psychology studies. The replication effects were, on average, half the size of the originals. And in 2014, Gelman and Carlin gave the exaggeration a name: a type M error, for magnitude.

Let's watch it happen. The true effect is small, with a d of zero point four. Each study has fifteen dyads. We run a thousand studies. Four hundred and twenty-two reach significance. Those are the studies that get written up.

Their average observed d is zero point six six. That is about sixty-five percent too large. Across all thousand studies, the average is right, zero point four two. The studies that missed stay in the drawer, and the ones on the notice board all exaggerate. This is the winner's curse.

Now see what it does to planning. Suppose the organiser reads a paper reporting a d of about one. She plans seven dyads, for eighty percent power. If the truth is the small effect, her real power is twenty-two percent. She will probably find nothing. And a non-significant result from such a study says very little. Episode eight point six comes back to this.

So for a main hypothesis, start from a realistic effect, smaller than the published mean. Shrink the published value, or use the smallest effect that would still matter to you. With the small effect, fifteen dyads succeed four hundred and twenty-two times out of a thousand. Forty dyads succeed eight hundred and thirteen times. Plan around that second number, and add a few extra dyads, because some will be excluded for artefacts.

What do hyperscanning studies actually use? We looked in published reviews and meta-analyses for a median number of dyads, by modality. As of November 2026, we found means and ranges, but no median we could verify, so we do not quote one. We can give two anchors. A 2022 meta-analysis of fNIRS cooperation studies pooled thirteen studies and eight hundred and ninety participants. And a 2022 analysis of a public fNIRS dataset of twenty-four dyads concluded that a large sample would be needed to detect significant coupling.

The code is short. For each candidate number of dyads, draw that many dyads from your simulated pool, run the test, and record whether it passed. Repeat a thousand times, and take the mean. Then write the assumed effect, and how you chose it, in your preregistration.

Let's recap. Power is the proportion of successes over many rehearsals. The sample size is the number of dyads. Halve the effect, and you need about four times as many. Published effects are inflated by small samples and selection. Plant a smaller, realistic effect for your main hypothesis.

Next, the multiverse of hyperscanning.

PLATE 8.55:49

The multiverse of hyperscanning

Every analysis choice, such as band, reference, epoch length, measure and rejection, splits the path, and five choices of three options give 243 analyses of one recording. A specification curve on simulated data with a planted effect shows ninety-eight pipelines finding it, one hundred and thirteen finding nothing and thirty-two reversed. Preregister one main pipeline and report the full curve beside it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the ball. Tonight we climb to the balcony and look down. Every dance floor you can see is a different way to analyse the very same recording.

Think about the last time you analysed a pair of recordings. You filtered the signals. You picked a reference. You cut the data into windows. You chose a measure of synchrony. You decided which noisy stretches to throw away. Each step had several reasonable answers, and none of them was wrong. We met these choices before: windows in episode five point five, references in seven point two, and the biases of phase measures in seven point four.

Now picture the ball. Each choice is a landing on the grand staircase, and at every landing the staircase splits in three. Five choices, three options each, and you reach the bottom with two hundred and forty-three doors. Behind every door is a dance floor: one complete analysis of your data. Together, they are your multiverse.

Statisticians call this the garden of forking paths. In 2016, Steegen and colleagues proposed a simple remedy in Perspectives on Psychological Science. Run every reasonable path, not just one, and show them all. They named it a multiverse analysis. In 2020, Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson, in Nature Human Behaviour, gave the idea a picture and a procedure: the specification curve. Three steps. List the defensible specifications. Plot the result of each one. Then test them all together.

Does this matter for EEG? It does. Šoškić and colleagues, in Psychophysiology in 2024, analysed one N400 dataset with fourteen pipelines taken from the literature. The high-pass filter, the artifact removal, the reference and the amplitude measure all changed some outcomes. Only the low-pass filter did not. Kessler, Enge and Skeide, in Communications Biology in 2025, varied the preprocessing of seven public experiments. It changed decoding performance considerably.

And in hyperscanning? We searched for a published multiverse or specification curve of hyperscanning EEG. We could not verify one, and we say so plainly. The closest work is Zimmermann, Schultz-Nielsen, Dumas and Konvalinka, in Imaging Neuroscience in 2024. They varied epoch length and the formula, on eighteen real dyads and on simulated ones. Epochs of one second or less could inflate synchrony when there was no strong interaction. That is a warning about arbitrary choices. It is not a count of how many conclusions flip. So the curve you are about to see is simulated.

Here is the simulated study. Twenty dyads, two conditions. In the first, the partners are coupled twice as strongly as in the second. We planted a real effect. The five choices are the frequency band, the reference, the epoch length, the synchrony measure, and how many noisy epochs are rejected. Three options each, so two hundred and forty-three analyses. Every one ends with the same test across twenty dyads, one value per dyad and condition.

Now we line the floors up. Each dot is one pipeline. Its height is the difference between the two conditions, sorted from lowest to highest. Below, a column of dots shows which options built that pipeline. This is the specification curve.

Ninety-eight pipelines find the effect, significant at five percent in the expected direction. One hundred and thirteen find nothing. And thirty-two are significant the other way round. Same data, same dyads, same planted effect.

Look at the dots below. Two choices decide almost everything. With the bipolar reference, no pipeline finds the effect. Neither does any pipeline that uses amplitude envelope correlation. The band, the epoch length and the rejection barely matter here: each of their options gives between thirty-seven and forty-four percent. The average reference gives fifty-four percent, and the single channel sixty-seven. This is one simulated world. In real data other choices may dominate, and the curve is how you find out which.

Now the warning. We repeated the whole multiverse on sixty studies with no true effect at all. On average, five and a half pipelines out of two hundred and forty-three came out significant. But in thirty-eight of the sixty studies, at least one pipeline did. Walk through the doors until a lamp lights up, and you will usually find one.

So what do we do? First, before the ball, choose one main pipeline and write it down. Justify each choice with the literature, not with the result. Then preregister it, as episode twelve point one shows. Our main pipeline was eight to twelve hertz, average reference, two second epochs, phase locking value, and the ten percent largest epochs rejected. Its p value was about zero point zero zero three.

Second, report the multiverse beside it: the curve, the share of significant pipelines, and the choices that move the result. Only include pipelines you can defend. Do not pick the best dot afterwards. The dots are not independent tests, so a joint test across them is needed, and that is what the specification curve provides.

Let's recap. Every analysis choice opens a new floor. Plot every path you can defend. Say plainly what you could not verify. Choose and preregister one main pipeline, and report the curve beside it.

PLATE 8.65:47

Evidence for no synchrony

A non-significant result is not evidence of no synchrony. With the same zero true effect, twelve dyads and one hundred and twenty dyads both give a non-significant test, but only the larger study can show equivalence within a pre-set smallest effect of interest. The equivalence test and the Bayes factor, which depends on its prior, support the conclusion: no synchrony beyond so much.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the ball. Tonight one couple dances at opposite ends of the floor. A judge has to decide. Are they really dancing apart, or was the music simply too quiet to tell?

That is the situation after a null result. You ran the test, and the p value is above five percent. What may you write? Not that there is no synchrony. A non-significant test only says the data are compatible with no effect. They may be just as compatible with an effect that matters. Altman and Bland said it in the British Medical Journal in nineteen ninety-five: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Let's watch it happen. Everything here is simulated. Each dyad gives one number: its synchrony minus the average of its own pseudo-pairs, on the Fisher z scale. Zero means no more synchrony than shuffled partners. We plant no synchrony at all.

Study one has twelve dyads. The mean is minus zero point zero four, and the p value is zero point five two. Not significant. But the ninety-five percent interval runs from minus zero point one nine to plus zero point one zero. It still contains effects we might care about.

Study two has one hundred and twenty dyads. The mean is zero point zero one, and the p value is zero point eight four. Also not significant. The interval is much tighter: minus zero point zero five to plus zero point zero six. The t test gives the same verdict twice. Yet these two null results do not say the same thing.

So the first step comes before any data. Decide the smallest effect size of interest. Below which value would you say that synchrony no longer matters for your question? It may come from theory, from the smallest change that would alter a conclusion, or from earlier work. Here, as an illustration, we choose plus or minus zero point one. Fix it in advance, and write it down. A band chosen after seeing the data is worth nothing.

Now the equivalence test, called TOST, for two one-sided tests. It was first used to compare drugs, and Lakens brought it to psychology in twenty seventeen, with a tutorial by Lakens and colleagues in twenty eighteen. We flip the question. Test one asks: is the effect above minus zero point one? Test two asks: is it below plus zero point one? If both say yes at five percent, we conclude that the effect lies inside the band. In practice, the ninety percent interval must sit entirely inside the band.

Study one fails. Its ninety percent interval runs from minus zero point one six to plus zero point zero eight, and spills out on the left. The test gives p equals zero point two one. The judge says: I cannot tell.

Study two passes. Its interval runs from minus zero point zero four to plus zero point zero five, entirely inside the band, and p is below zero point zero one. The judge says: no synchrony beyond a tenth.

Was that luck? We repeat each study four thousand times, still with no synchrony. With twelve dyads, ninety-four percent of studies are non-significant, but fewer than one percent show equivalence. With one hundred and twenty dyads, ninety-five percent are non-significant, and ninety-five percent show equivalence. Being non-significant is easy. Showing equivalence has to be earned with enough dyads.

And the test is honest in both directions. With one hundred and twenty dyads and a small true effect of zero point zero five, still inside the band, equivalence is shown in fifty-eight percent of studies. With a true effect of zero point two, outside the band, it is never shown. Planning the number of dyads for this test is the power analysis of episode eight point four, aimed at the band. Here, about eighty dyads give an eighty percent chance of showing equivalence.

A second tool is the Bayes factor. Rouder and colleagues described Bayesian t tests for accepting the null in two thousand nine, and Dienes applied the idea to non-significant results in twenty fourteen. Picture two advocates in front of the judge. One says the effect is exactly zero. The other says there is a small positive effect, about a tenth. Each advocate predicts where the data will land. The Bayes factor is how much better the first predicted what we saw.

A common convention reads a factor above three as evidence for no effect, and below one third as evidence for an effect. In between, the data are insensitive. Study one gives two point seven: insensitive. Study two gives three point one, just over the line. Across the four thousand repeats, twelve dyads reach evidence for no effect in thirteen percent of studies. One hundred and twenty dyads reach it in sixty percent. The number depends on the prior, so report the prior, and show how the answer moves when it changes.

What goes in the paper? State the smallest effect before the data. Report the interval. Report the equivalence test or the Bayes factor, with its prior. Write no synchrony beyond a tenth, not no synchrony. And when the answer is inconclusive, write inconclusive.

Let's recap. A non-significant test does not show absence. Fix the smallest effect of interest in advance. The equivalence test asks whether the interval sits inside the band. The Bayes factor compares two advocates, and depends on its prior. Conclude no synchrony beyond so much, never no synchrony.
