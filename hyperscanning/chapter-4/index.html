Why milliseconds matter

A timing error becomes a phase error in proportion to frequency: ten milliseconds is a third of a degree at 0.1 hertz but 144 degrees at 40 hertz. In simulation, jitter and clock drift hide real coupling at high frequencies, as a miss and never a false alarm. EEG needs millisecond timing checked over the session; fNIRS and fMRI forgive more.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the film set. Two cameras, two microphones, and one clapper board. Ava and Otto are about to be recorded, each by their own amplifier. This episode asks a question that sounds like plumbing: how well do the two recordings have to agree about time?

Every recording system keeps time with a small clock, usually a quartz crystal. Cheap crystals are specified to about twenty parts per million. Two clocks that disagree by even that much will never tick in perfect step. Remember this number, we will use it in a minute.

Now, why would a few milliseconds matter? Think of the spinning arrow from the EEG series. A rhythm is an arrow that turns, and its angle is the phase. Shift one recording by a time dt, and you rotate its arrow. The angle you add is three hundred and sixty degrees, times the frequency, times dt.

Take a timing error of ten milliseconds. At a tenth of a hertz, the slow rhythm of fNIRS, that is a third of a degree. At ten hertz, the alpha and mu range, it is thirty-six degrees. At forty hertz, in the gamma range, it is one hundred and forty-four degrees. The same clock error is invisible in one signal and enormous in another. So the demand on timing depends on the frequency you study.

Does a fixed shift ruin everything? Not for every measure. The phase locking value ignores which angle the two signals keep, so a constant offset leaves it alone. But measures built on lag, and on who leads, read that shift as real. And an offset that wanders is worse, because it blurs the angle itself.

Here is a simulation. Two signals are truly locked, and the phase locking value is about 0.88. Now add a random timing error to one of them, a different small error at every sample. At ten hertz, an error of ten milliseconds brings the value down to 0.72. Twenty milliseconds, 0.40. At forty hertz, five milliseconds is already enough to drop it to 0.40, and ten milliseconds leaves almost nothing, 0.04. At a tenth of a hertz, even thirty milliseconds changes nothing. The synchrony was there. The clock erased it.

So the sloppier your timing, the more you lose at high frequencies. And you never get a false alarm from this. You get a miss. A real coupling hidden behind a bad clock looks exactly like no coupling.

Then there is the slow enemy, drift. Suppose Ava's clock runs twenty parts per million fast, and Otto's runs twenty parts per million slow. Together they separate by forty parts per million. That is one hundred and forty-four milliseconds in one hour.

What does that do? At ten hertz, the offset turns by one and a half cycles over the hour. At forty hertz, by almost six cycles. Over the whole hour, the simulated phase locking value of our locked pair falls from 0.88 to 0.19 at ten hertz, and to 0.04 at forty hertz. Nothing was wrong at the start. By the end, the two brains are no longer on the same clock.

Now the slow signals. In one fNIRS hyperscanning study, Cui and colleagues recorded at ten samples per second, and found coherence between two brains at periods of about three to thirteen seconds. At those speeds, even that same hour of drift adds only about five degrees at a tenth of a hertz. fNIRS and fMRI tolerate far more than EEG. But tolerate does not mean forget. Left alone for a whole day, the same drift would reach several seconds, and then it matters even for slow signals.

And the EEG side? Dumas and colleagues recorded pairs of people imitating hand movements, and reported inter-brain synchrony in the alpha and mu band, near ten hertz. Rhythms like that are exactly where a clock error of tens of milliseconds starts to hurt.

Let's recap. A timing error becomes a phase error, in proportion to frequency. Random jitter hides real coupling. Drift hides it more, the longer you record. EEG needs milliseconds, and checks them over the whole session. fNIRS and fMRI forgive more, but not an unchecked clock.

PLATE 4.24:47

Triggers, LSL and shared timestamps

Two amplifiers are like two cameras with separate clocks, so the recordings need a shared clap. A wired pulse is the reference but marks only the moments it is sent, while software markers add latency and jitter. Lab Streaming Layer aligns computer clocks but cannot see delays inside devices, so a photodiode on a screen flash measures the real delay and spread.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the film set. Two cameras are shooting the same scene, and each one has its own clock. Back in the editing room, how do you line up the two reels? You look for one moment that both of them saw. The clap.

Two amplifiers are those two cameras. Each one stamps its samples with its own clock, and the two clocks were started at different moments. Nothing in the files says how the two timelines fit together. This episode is about the clap: how to make one, how to keep it going all session, and how to check that it worked.

Start with the hardware clap. The stimulus computer sends one electrical pulse, split to a trigger input on each amplifier. Each amplifier writes the pulse into its recording, in the same timeline as the brain signal. The same physical event is now marked in both files. Wired pulses are fast and steady, and that is why they are still the reference.

But a pulse only marks the moments when you send one. Between claps, the two reels slowly slide apart. So one clap at the start is not enough. And a pulse needs a free input on each device, which a lot of equipment does not have.

The other option is a software marker. The stimulus program sends a message that says, event twelve just happened. That message travels through the operating system, a driver, a cable, and a buffer before anyone writes it down. It arrives late, and not always by the same amount. Two words matter here. Latency is how late it is, on average. Jitter is how much that lateness changes from one event to the next. A constant latency can be measured and subtracted. Jitter is noise that stays in your data.

This is where Lab Streaming Layer, or LSL, comes in. In the film set, it is the script supervisor. Every device publishes a stream. Each sample carries a timestamp, taken from the clock of the computer that acquired it, together with a description of the stream. One recorder collects all the streams, from both amplifiers and the marker stream, into a single file.

But the computers' clocks still disagree. So the recorder keeps asking each machine what time it is. It sends a short message, and the answer comes back. The round trip is measured. The best guess is that the answer was written halfway through. If the two ways took different times, the guess is slightly off, but never by more than half of the round trip. So the probe with the shortest round trip is kept. In the 2025 paper that describes the toolbox, the offset is assumed to be well under one millisecond on most network hardware. The exchange repeats every few seconds, and all the offsets are saved in the file.

When you load the file, the reader applies those offsets and smooths the timestamps of regularly sampled streams. Now the reels slide back into line, all session long.

Here is the catch. Lab Streaming Layer aligns the clocks of the computers. It cannot see what happens inside a device before a sample reaches the computer. A stimulus is drawn on a screen a little after the marker leaves. An amplifier buffers its data before it hands them over. The authors of the toolbox say plainly that these delays are not measured or corrected, and that they typically amount to a few tens of milliseconds. So a good clap in the software is not the end of the story.

The only way to know is to test. This is the truth test. A small light sensor, a photodiode, is stuck on the corner of the screen. The screen flashes, and the sensor sends its signal to the analog input of both systems. One physical event, seen by two recordings. For every flash, subtract the time on one system from the time on the other.

Here is a simulated run, with sixty flashes. The median difference is eight milliseconds. That is a fixed delay inside one device, invisible to the clocks. The spread is about half a millisecond. We remove the median, and the result is a small spread: ninety-five percent of the flashes now agree within about one millisecond. Both numbers are the answer. The delay says what to correct. The spread says what you cannot.

Do this before the study, again whenever the setup changes, and once more at the end of a session. Then report it. Which hardware, which software, how many flashes, the median delay and the spread. Episode twelve point two comes back to this, when we write the methods section.

So, three habits. Mark the same event in both recordings. Let a shared clock stamp everything in between. And measure the error you really have, instead of the one you hope for.

PLATE 4.34:45

EEG: one amplifier or two?

With one amplifier and two caps, timing is perfect but the two people may share a ground or reference, so noise leaks into both recordings. In simulation, a shared reference gives independent brains a correlation of 0.26, removed by re-referencing each person to their own electrodes. Recording two mannequin heads reveals any leak, since correlation without brains is artefact.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back on the film set. Behind the cameras there is a cart full of cables and power strips. Today we plug two people into it. The question looks like a purchase order. One amplifier, or two? It is really a question about what the two heads share.

An EEG electrode never measures a voltage on its own. It measures a difference, between the electrode and a reference. There is also a ground wire, which keeps the person and the amplifier at a stable level. Both wires belong to one body, in one circuit.

So here is the first setup. One amplifier, with two caps plugged into it. The timing is perfect, because there is a single clock and a single file. But the two people may now share a ground, or even a reference. Whatever leaks into that shared wire lands in both recordings, at the same moment.

The classic leak is the electrical grid. Mains hum is fifty hertz in Europe and sixty hertz in North America. A notch filter removes the hum itself. It does not remove the broadband noise that rides on the same shared wire.

Now the second setup. Two amplifiers, each with its own cap, its own ground, and its own reference. Each cart runs on its own battery. The price is timing, which was the job of the two previous episodes. The gain is that nothing electrical runs between the two heads.

What does the literature say? In a 2019 paper in MethodsX, Barraza and colleagues describe hyperscanning setups for several amplifier families, and state that individual ground and reference is obtained in all of them. They also flag one case. A single amplifier that was not designed for hyperscanning, with one ground shared by everyone, was called a possible confounding factor.

A 2023 paper in MethodsX, by Douglas, Tremblay and Newman, did record two people on a single amplifier. They split the ground with a distributor cable. Then they re-referenced each person offline to their own electrodes. They also relayed a vendor warning that this works best when the noise levels of the two people are about equal.

What do manufacturers offer? BioSemi's hyperscanning flyer says each box keeps its own reference, the boxes are linked only by optical fibre, and the subjects are galvanically isolated from each other. The Brain Products and ANT Neuro pages we read describe synchronisation, triggers and a single file. They say nothing about ground. So ask the vendor, in writing, how the grounds are wired.

Now the key point. Suppose each person has a true signal, a and b, independent. Both recordings subtract the same reference noise, r. The measured signals are a minus r, and b minus r. The term r appears in both, so the two recordings now correlate, and nothing happened between the two people.

This is not one of the four sources of synchrony. It is not the stimulus, not rhythm, not mutual prediction, and not the body. It is a measurement artefact, and it can sit on top of any of them.

Here it is in a simulation, with fixed random numbers. Two independent brains, eight channels each. With separate references, the mean correlation between the two people is zero. With a shared reference noise, it is zero point two six. If each person is re-referenced to the average of their own electrodes, it falls back to zero. That works here because the leak is identical on every channel. A real leak is not always that tidy.

How do you check your own setup? Record with no brains. Put the caps on two mannequin heads, with saline or gel as for a real person, and run the exact setup, with the same cables, the same room and the same analysis. In the simulation, the empty recording correlates at zero point nine seven with a shared reference, and at zero with separate ones. With no brains, any correlation is a leak. I did not find a published standard protocol for this test, so treat it as a sensible control, not as a rule.

A different problem is caught elsewhere. In 2013, in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, Burgess showed that people recorded separately, under the same conditions, can still look synchronised. That is shared conditions, not shared wires, and we come back to it with the pseudo-pair test.

So before the first participant, ask three things. Is there a shared ground or reference? What does an empty recording say? And is the analysis done with each person re-referenced on their own?

fNIRS for two

A fixed budget of fNIRS channels must give both heads the same regions, so every channel has a partner. Short channels, about eight millimetres apart, sample only the scalp, and each head needs its own. In simulation, shared heartbeat and breathing give long channels a correlation of 0.49 between people, falling to 0.26 after short-channel regression.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back on the film set. This time the crew has one job: record two foreheads with light. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy looks like the easy modality for two. But three choices are hidden inside it. How many channels each person gets. Where those channels sit. And what the long channels really measure.

Picture a lighting rig. Each head wears a grid of small lamps. Some are sources, which send light into the head. Others are detectors, which catch what comes back. One source and one detector make one channel.

There are two ways to light two heads. In the first, one device drives both headsets. In the second, each person has a device of their own. With one device, the channels of a single system are divided between the two heads. A 2020 review describes an eye contact study built that way. With two devices, each head keeps its own budget, but the two recordings must share one clock. That is the problem of episode 4.2. Channel counts depend on the system, so read your own manual.

Now the trade-off. The budget is fixed. Every lamp you give to AVA is a lamp OTTO does not get. Here is an illustrative budget of twelve channels. Split six and six, and each head covers three regions, with two channels each. Give AVA eight, and OTTO is left with four. One of his regions goes dark. And the pair goes dark with it, because a dyad measure needs the same region on both heads.

So cover the same regions on both heads, with the same layout, so that every channel has a partner. A 2013 review found that five of the first seven fNIRS hyperscanning studies had recorded only the left side of the head. It recommended recording both hemispheres.

Now a lamp that does something different. A short channel puts the source and the detector very close together. Its light barely reaches the brain. It samples only the scalp, and the blood flowing through it. A 2015 simulation study tested this. With a five percent criterion on brain sensitivity, the optimum distance was 8.4 millimetres in a typical adult. The 2021 best-practice guidelines say about eight millimetres, and describe short-separation regression. You subtract what the short channel sees from the long channel. Each head needs its own short channels, because each scalp has its own blood.

Why does this matter even more for two people? Because that scalp signal is not only noise. Heart rate, breathing, blood pressure and slow Mayer waves all come from the body. A 2016 opinion article warned that blood responses that are not neurovascular can be misread as brain activity. And the 2021 guidelines note that breathing and Mayer waves sit in the same frequency range as the hemodynamic response. A filter cannot remove them.

Now put two people in the same room, doing the same task. Their heartbeat and breathing can fall into step. A 2013 review describes a study where coherence between two people rose in the heart rate band, and in a slow band from 0.06 to 0.2 hertz. If those rhythms are in both long channels, the two brain signals look synchronized. That is not brain synchrony. It is route four, body coupling.

Here is a simulation, so we know the truth. Two people with mostly independent brain signals, and a slow body rhythm that they largely share. Across 500 simulated dyads, the mean correlation between their long channels is 0.49. Regress each person's own short channel out, and it falls to 0.26. Pseudo-pairs sit at zero, before and after. What remains is the small shared brain component we built in.

One more job for the rig. Knowing where the lamps really are. A cap never places a lamp exactly where the diagram says. So measure the optode positions on each person, with a three-D digitizer, a neuronavigation system, or photogrammetry. The 2021 guidelines list these, and ask for the spread of the positions. Then register them to a common brain space, as a 2014 review explains. Do it for both heads, so that channel five on AVA and channel five on OTTO look at the same region.

So, three habits. Split the budget so that both heads cover the same regions. Put short channels on both heads. And measure where the lamps really are. A synchrony between two long channels is a question about the body as well as the brain. Episode 5.4 comes back to cleaning it.

fMRI and MEG for two

Linking two fMRI or MEG scanners puts a communication link between the partners, and its delay becomes part of the design. In simulation, a known 130 millisecond delay can be subtracted from the measured lag, but jitter cannot, and it lowers the peak correlation. The slow blood response also blurs quick exchanges, while shared-magnet coils and wearable MEG bring people closer together.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two scanners, two huge magnets, and two people who need to interact. This episode is about the biggest machines in the lab, and how to make them work for a pair.

Let's go back to the film set. Picture two soundproof booths. In each one, a participant lies in an fMRI scanner. Between the booths runs a talkback line. In 2002, Montague and colleagues published the first system that did this. They called it Hyperscan. It links magnetic resonance scanners through the internet, so two people can interact while both brains are imaged. Their example was a simple game of deception between pairs of subjects.

Inside a scanner, nobody can turn to the other person. The interaction has to pass through something. A screen, a microphone, or a button under the hand. And that channel has a delay. The delay is not a nuisance to hide. It is part of the paradigm. Montague's abstract says that people may interact asymmetrically and asynchronously, so both the design and the analysis must accommodate it.

The same idea works with MEG. In 2012, a team at Aalto University in Finland, with Baess as first author, connected two MEG systems with a real-time audio link. The two participants sat in two laboratories, five kilometres apart. Both recordings were synchronised for joint analysis. In 2015, Zhdanov and colleagues added video, and replaced the telephone line with an internet connection. The one-way latency was about 130 milliseconds. Nine pairs mirrored each other's hand movements, and their average synchronisation accuracy was 215 milliseconds.

So why does the delay matter for the measure? Here is a simulation. Partner A acts. Partner B's brain responds 200 milliseconds later, but B only sees the action after the link delay. With a fixed delay of 130 milliseconds, the measured lag is 330 milliseconds. That is the true 200, plus the link. Because the delay is known, we can subtract it. Now give the link a jitter of 50 milliseconds. The average lag does not change, but on average over 40 simulated runs the peak correlation falls from 0.30 to 0.21. Nobody can subtract a jitter. So measure the link, and report its variability.

fMRI adds a second blur. The blood-flow response peaks about five seconds after an event. Suppose A acts at time zero, and B answers one second later. In the simulation, the two responses add up to a single hump. A typical scanner takes one image every two seconds, so a one-second exchange is half of one image. The order inside a quick exchange is hard to read from the images alone. It has to come from the behaviour record.

Can both people share one magnet? In 2012, Lee, Dai and Jones, at Princeton, built a pair of head coils that scanned two subjects' brains simultaneously in one scanner. In 2020, Renvall and colleagues used a custom two-helmet coil, with sixteen channels in total, and scanned ten pairs. The partners were face to face. They took turns tapping each other's lip. Sensorimotor areas responded in turn, and the coils covered the whole brain. The price was a lower signal-to-noise ratio than a standard head coil.

So here is the balance sheet. On the plus side, fMRI covers the whole brain, including deep structures. MEG follows the cortex at the scale of milliseconds. On the minus side, people lie in a scanner. As a 2023 paper puts it, scanners can be claustrophobic, and interactions go via video. Even a shared magnet is not a café table.

Now the booths shrink into helmets. In 2018, Boto and colleagues at Nottingham described a MEG system that can be worn like a helmet. It uses quantum sensors that do not need superconducting technology, with a system that cancels the background magnetic field. Subjects nodded, stretched, drank and played a ball game, and the data stayed good even with large head movements. In 2023, Holmes and colleagues used wearable MEG for hyperscanning. Two people did an interactive touching task, and a ball game. Despite large and unpredictable motion, the signals were clear, and the two brains' oscillation envelopes were correlated.

Whatever the machine, the usual question stays. A shared screen or voice is an input, so the first source, stimulus, shared input, stays open until the design closes it. Rhythm and movement, mutual prediction, and body coupling wait their turn.

Record the behaviour too

A study that records only brains cannot say where synchrony comes from, so behaviour is recorded as control and outcome: video, audio, movement, gaze, heartbeat and breathing, one stream per person on the same clock. In simulation, an uncoupled dyad shows a correlation of 0.44 that falls to 0.01 once movement is removed, while a truly coupled dyad keeps 0.55.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the film set. So far, every lamp and every lens has been aimed at the brains. In this episode we turn the cameras around, and point them at Ava and Otto themselves. Why? Because a study that records only brains cannot say where its synchrony comes from.

Remember the four sources of synchrony. One, stimulus. Two, rhythm and movement. Three, mutual prediction. Four, body coupling. Most studies hope to find the third. The other three are suspects, and the evidence about them is not in the brain recordings. It is in what the two people did, heard, saw, and how their bodies behaved.

So behaviour has two jobs. It is a control, for routes one, two and four. And it is often the variable of interest: how well the pair coordinated, who led, who looked at whom. Either way, it has to be recorded, one stream per person, in the same clock as the brains.

Let's walk into the control room, where every stream gets its own monitor. Video: one camera per person, framing the face and the hands. Audio: one microphone per person, not one for the whole room, so you can tell who spoke when, even when voices overlap. Movement: motion capture, or small accelerometers on the head and hands. Eye tracking: where each person looks, and when two gazes meet. And the body: heartbeat and breathing, the signals of route four.

These streams run at very different speeds. A video camera at thirty frames per second gives one image every thirty-three milliseconds. An EEG amplifier at one thousand samples per second gives one sample every millisecond. So what must match is not the rate, it is the clock. Everything goes onto one shared timeline, with the tools of the earlier episodes of this chapter.

Here is a real example. In 2013, Müller and colleagues recorded EEG from pairs of guitarists improvising together. The sound of each guitar was fed into extra EEG channels, so the notes and the brain signals shared one clock. The microphone and video recordings were then used to place event markers afterwards.

Now let's see why the control matters. This data is simulated, so the truth is known. Two dyads, each recorded with one movement channel per person. In both, the partners tap along with each other, and the movement leaks a little into the brain signal, through muscles, head motion or cable swing.

In the first dyad, the two brains are not coupled at all. Yet the correlation between the two signals is zero point four four. Pseudo-pairs, people who never met, reach at most zero point three four, at their ninety-fifth percentile. So this dyad stands out, and it looks like synchrony.

Now remove each person's recorded movement from that person's own signal. The correlation drops to zero point zero one, inside the null. Nothing was left. The movement did it.

In the second dyad, the brains really are coupled. The raw correlation is zero point seven three. After removing the movement, it is still zero point five five, above the null limit of zero point four. This dyad keeps something. And without the movement channels, you could never have told these two dyads apart.

A word of caution. Removing the movement removes everything that moves linearly with it. If the movement also drives real brain activity, in the motor cortex for example, that real signal goes too. So report the value with and without the removal, and say exactly what you removed. Movement gets its own treatment in chapter five.

The second job of behaviour is to be the thing you explain. In 2010, Dumas and colleagues recorded pairs with two cameras and two EEG systems, while the partners spontaneously imitated each other's hand movements. Periods of interactional synchrony, scored from the behaviour, coincided with synchronisation between the brains, in the alpha-mu band, over right centroparietal regions. Without the video, there would be no periods to compare.

In 2020, Dravida and colleagues combined fNIRS hyperscanning with eye tracking. They counted each pair's eye contacts, and linked that count to the coupling between their brains. And in 2021, Hamilton argued in Neuron that hyperscanning becomes stronger when it is combined with behavioural data and models of mutual prediction.

One practical sentence. A video of a face, or a recording of a voice, can identify a person. So these files are stored apart from the brain data, with restricted access, and linked to it only through a participant code.

Let's recap. Behaviour is the control for three of the four routes, and often the outcome you want to explain. Record one stream per person: video, voice, movement, gaze, heartbeat and breathing. Put them all in the clock of the brain recordings. Report your result with and without removing the behaviour. And store the videos like what they are: identifiable.

Hyperscanning in the wild

Recording in classrooms, concerts and gyms gains validity and loses the cable, so clocks drift and wireless packets go missing. In simulation, naive appending cuts the phase locking value from 0.45 to 0.11. Sample counters reveal gaps, which are marked missing, drift is fitted with a straight line, and the gaps are reported like any exclusion, which restores the synchrony.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the film set. Today the crew packs the cameras, the microphones and the clapperboard into a location van, and drives out of the lab. The van makes three stops. A classroom. A concert. A gym.

First stop, the classroom. In 2017, Dikker and colleagues recorded portable EEG from twelve high school students, across a semester of ordinary lessons. The more in step the group's brain activity was, the better it predicted how engaged the students were, and how they got along.

Second stop, the concert. In 2021, Czepiel and colleagues did not record brains. They recorded the bodies of the audience at live string quintet concerts: heart rate, breathing, skin conductance and facial muscles. Moments of high synchrony across listeners showed the signature of physiological arousal. One hundred and twenty-nine people attended. Data from thirty-one were lost to technical problems, so ninety-eight were analysed.

Third stop, the gym. People run, jump and sweat, so the choice of device matters. In 2020, Pinti and colleagues reviewed fNIRS for cognitive neuroscience. A well-fitted fNIRS cap keeps giving good signals while a participant walks. Outdoors, though, stray sunlight can saturate the detectors.

Wireless EEG pays a price too. In 2012, Debener and colleagues took a low-cost wireless EEG for a walk, with a standard electrode cap. A single-trial P300 could still be classified. But the accuracy was seventy-seven percent indoors, and sixty-nine percent outdoors.

So what do you gain? Validity. The behaviour is the real one, in the real place, with the real audience. Dikker's team took this further. With art institutions, they recorded thousands of museum and festival visitors, in ten-minute face-to-face interactions.

And what do you lose? The cable. With it go two guarantees: the clock, and the packets.

First, the clock. Each device keeps its own time, and each runs a little fast or a little slow. Second, the radio link. A wireless device sends its samples in small packets, and some packets never arrive.

Lab Streaming Layer already helps. It measures the clock offsets every few seconds. The recording stores them, and the importer fits a straight line through them. The importer also splits a stream wherever data was lost.

Let's see what these two problems do. This data is simulated. One dyad, truly coupled in the alpha band, recorded for ninety seconds. The clock of device B runs two tenths of a percent slow, and it loses packets in short bursts. Both effects are exaggerated, to make them visible.

First, the naive way. Append what arrives, and trust the clock. Every lost packet pulls the rest of the recording earlier. By the end, B is off by about one point four seconds.

Measure the phase locking value over the whole recording, and average it over thirty simulated dyads. With a perfect link, it is zero point four five. With the naive alignment, it falls to zero point one one. The synchrony is gone.

Now the careful way. Every packet carries a sample counter, a number that goes up by one each time. If the counter jumps from one hundred and twelve to one hundred and sixteen, three samples are missing. So compare the samples you expected with the samples you received, or look for jumps in the timestamps. This is general practice, not a published method.

Then put each sample where its counter says. Mark the missing ones as missing, never as zeros. Estimate the drift with a straight line through the arrival times. Here it finds two tenths of a percent. And leave out the samples next to each gap.

In the first dyad, that meant fourteen gaps, one point four percent of the samples lost, and about one point two seconds in total. Across the dyads, eighty-eight percent of the samples were kept. The phase locking value comes back to zero point four five.

Last step: document the gaps. For each participant, report how many gaps there were, how long the longest was, how much time was lost, and what share of the data you left out. Report them like any other exclusion. And fix your rule before you look at the result.

One more caution. In a classroom, route one, the shared stimulus, is everywhere. So in the wild, the controls of the previous episode matter even more.

Let's recap. The wild gives validity, and costs the cable. Without it, clocks drift and packets are lost. Counters reveal the gaps. Mark them, correct the drift, and report both. In the simulation, this recovered the synchrony that the naive alignment erased.

One dataset, several people

A recording of two people is useless a year later unless pairs can be found. Each recording needs a tag saying who, where, role, condition, synchronization method and measured latency. BIDS has no entity, field or file for a pair, and its workarounds are not machine-checked, so pairs are encoded in documented participant and recording columns plus a plain text note.

TRANSCRIPTEN

At the end of every shooting day, the clapboard goes back in its box. It said which scene, which take, which day. Without it, an editor faces hours of footage and no way to tell what belongs to what.

A recording of two people has the same problem, a year later. Somebody opens the folder. Two files, two participants. Which two go together? Who was seated where? Who had which role? What was the condition? How were the clocks tied, and how late was the second signal? Today's question is simple. Can another lab find the pairs?

Here is the picture for this episode. The clapboard turns into a luggage tag. Each person's recording is a suitcase, and the data folder is a baggage claim. A suitcase with no tag circles the belt forever. And two suitcases that belong together find each other only if both tags say so.

The tag has six lines. Who: the participant, and the pair. Where: which seat. Role: for example, the one who describes and the one who listens. Condition: what task they were doing. Synchronization: which method tied the two clocks together. And the measured latency, the number from the truth test of episode 4.2.

What does BIDS offer for this? BIDS is the Brain Imaging Data Structure, a way to name and arrange files so that people and software can read them. I checked its current specification and its official sources in November 2026. Here is what I can say, and nothing more.

First, what already works. Each person is a participant, with a folder of their own. One table lists all participants. The specification lets it carry extra columns, and recommends a companion file that describes them. So pair, seat and role can live there. A second table lists each recording, with its acquisition time. It also allows additional fields, which it asks you to document. That is a good place for the synchronization method and the measured latency. The condition goes in the task label and in the events table.

Look at the dotted outline. These extra columns are our own convention. The specification allows them, but nothing checks that they mean a pair.

Now, what is missing. The specification has no entity, no field and no file for a pair or a group. The word hyperscanning does not appear in it. The frequently asked questions page offers two workarounds. Give both people the same session label. Or give both recordings the same acquisition label, such as dyad zero zero one. Both work. Neither is machine-checked as a pair. And the session trick clashes with studies where the same people come back on another day.

Is anyone working on it? A question about this was opened on the specification's GitHub in January 2020. It is still open. In September 2026, a maintainer filed a summary of all the threads on recordings of several subjects at once, hyperscanning among them. It lists six options, from a simple pair column to a new entity, and suggests a small first step. I found no extension proposal on hyperscanning. The official list has none, and that summary says the same. It adds that the near-infrared extension, published in Scientific Data in 2025, left the topic aside on purpose, and so did the extensions that followed.

Two more gaps matter to us. A video that shows both people has no home in the standard. The workaround discussed is the source data folder. And the recordings table gives each file a start time, but no duration, so you cannot compute which files overlapped. A proposal to add it is open, not merged.

So the advice is modest. Today, make the pairs findable in three places. One: a pair column in the participants table, described in its companion file. Two: the synchronization method and the measured latency, in documented columns of the recordings table. Three: a short plain text note at the top of the dataset, saying how pairs are encoded and how the clocks were checked. When the standard decides, a labelled column is easy to convert.

Back at the baggage claim. Six lines on the tag. Who, where, role, condition, synchronization, latency. BIDS gives you the suitcase, and most of the shelves. It does not yet give you the pair. Until it does, write it down, and say so.
