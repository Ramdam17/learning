What a bit means · and what it doesn't

An estimated number of bits is the output of a procedure, not a fixed measurement: five labs analysing the same 300 samples report between 0.04 and 0.76 bits. Normalising by an entropy does not remove the dependence on binning or the bias, and is meaningless for continuous variables. Values from different studies are comparable only when every setting is reported and the procedures match.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the games room, the world of chapter 6. Here we shuffle cards, cut decks, and roll dice, because this chapter is about what an information value allows you to conclude. We start with the most basic question of all. When a paper reports 0.3 bits, what does that number mean? And what does it not mean?

Start with intuition. A bit sounds like a universal unit, and in one sense it is. One bit is the information in one fair yes-or-no answer, whatever the question. But an estimated number of bits is not a measurement of a fixed physical quantity, like a temperature read from a thermometer. It is the output of a procedure, a choice of variables, a preprocessing, a discretisation, a sample size, and an estimator. Change any of these and the number changes on the very same data.

Here is the calculation in one picture. One dataset, 300 samples of a simulated pair of signals with a correlation of 0.5. Its true mutual information is about 0.21 bits. Now imagine several labs, each analysing these same 300 samples with a reasonable method.

The first lab cuts each signal into four bins of equal width and uses the plug-in estimator, about 0.10 bits. A second lab uses eight bins with equal counts, about 0.28 bits. A third, more ambitious uses 16 equal count bins, about 0.76 bits. A fourth lab uses the Gaussian copula estimator, about 0.15. A fifth uses KSG, about 0.04 on this particular sample.

Same data. Answers from 0.04 to 0.76 bits, a factor of almost 20. No lab made an error. Each made choices and each choice moved the number. The 16 bin value is inflated by the plug-in bias of episode 5.2. The 4 bin value is deflated by coarse binning. The KSG value is low because on only 300 samples its spread is several hundredths of a bit and this sample fell on the low side.

So a number of bits is not a universal effect size. Its meaning depends on the estimator, its settings, the sample size, the preprocessing and the discretisation. All of them must be reported or the number cannot be interpreted, let alone compared with another study. There is also a statistical side. Even with every choice fixed, a single estimate on 300 samples has a spread, and that spread belongs next to the number.

A common response is to normalise. Divide the mutual information by the entropy of one variable, or by the smaller of the two entropies, or by their geometric mean, so that the result lies between 0 and 1, like a correlation. Normalisation can help, but it has its own traps.

First trap, the entropy in the denominator is estimated with the same choices as the numerator. With four equal count bins, each entropy is exactly two bits, with 16 bins four bits. In our example, dividing by the smaller entropy gives about 0.07 with 4 bins and about 0.19 with 16. Normalisation did not remove the dependence on binning. It changed its shape.

Second trap, normalisation does not remove bias. Take the same kind of data with 8 equal count bins. With 100 samples, the plug-in mutual information averages about 0.59 bits, and its normalised version about 0.20. With 1000 samples, 0.22 bits, and 0.07 normalised. The normalised value still depends strongly on the number of samples, because the bias of the numerator has not gone anywhere.

Third trap, for continuous variables the entropy is not a finite, well-defined number, as episode 2.6 showed. A differential entropy can be negative and depends on units, so dividing by it is meaningless.

For continuous variables there is a more principled rescaling, proposed by Edward Linfoot in 1957, the information coefficient of correlation. It converts a mutual information in nats into the correlation that a Gaussian pair would need to carry the same information, the square root of 1-e to the minus twice the information. For a truly Gaussian pair, it returns the ordinary correlation. For our dataset whose true correlation is 0.5, the Gaussian copula estimate converts to about 0.43 and the low KSG estimate to about 0.22. It puts information on a familiar scale, but it inherits every error of the estimate it starts from. Other normalisations exist, such as the symmetric uncertainty used in machine learning, twice the information divided by the sum of the two entropies. Each comes with the same caveat, it rescales an estimate it does not repair it.

What is the meaning of a bit sensitive to then? To the estimator, to its settings, to the number of samples, to preprocessing such as filtering, re-referencing or normalisation, and to how continuous signals were discretised if they were. And also to the question, the information between an EEG channel and a stimulus depends on the set of stimuli, as episode 4.8 showed with channels and capacity.

Here is the classic pitfall. A review article compiles mutual information values from 20 studies of the same brain–behaviour relationship and ranks them, or averages them, or uses the spread as evidence of heterogeneity across populations. But the studies used different estimators, bin counts, sample sizes, and stimulus sets. Much of the spread may come from the procedures, not from the brains. Information values from different studies are comparable only if the procedures are the same, or if each value is accompanied by enough detail, and ideally code, to recompute it under a common procedure.

What should you report? The estimator and every setting. The number of bins and how they were placed. The bandwidth, k, the network and training protocol. The units bits or nats. The number of samples and whether they are independent. The pre-processing. A null distribution from surrogates which the next three episodes build. And ideally the code. Episode 6.9 turns this into a checklist. The list is long and it is meant to be. It is what makes a number of bits mean something.

The companion notebook analyses one simulated dataset with several estimators and settings, computes the normalised versions and their dependence on binning and sample size, and converts estimates into the information coefficient of correlation.

To recap, a bit is a universal unit, but an estimated number of bits is the output of a procedure. On the same data, reasonable choices gave values from 0.04 to 0.76 bits. Normalising by an entropy does not remove the dependence on binning or the bias, and it is meaningless for continuous variables. Linfoot's information coefficient of correlation offers a familiar scale with the errors of its input. Report everything and compare only like with like.

Surrogates I · Shuffling and the null you actually test

Shuffling one signal's samples builds a null distribution for an information value, and the p-value counts how many shuffled values reach the real one. The test quietly assumes every ordering of samples is equally plausible, which fails for autocorrelated signals: shuffling erases their memory, so independent slow signals are declared coupled, from 5% false positives without memory to 83% with strong memory.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room where the first thing anyone does with a deck is shuffle it. Shuffling is also the first thing most analyses do to decide whether an information value is real. Today we build that test, see why it is so popular, and discover the hidden assumption that can make it say yes to almost anything.

Start with intuition. You have two signals, X and Y, and an estimate of their mutual information, say 0.04 bits. Is that more than noise? Every estimator gives positive values on independent data, as chapter 5 showed. So the question is not whether the value is above zero. It is whether it is above what the same estimator on the same amount of data would give if X and Y were unrelated.

Shuffling builds that reference. Keep X as it is. Shuffle the order of the samples of Y randomly. Now each X is paired with a random Y, so any real relationship between them is destroyed, but everything else is kept. The same number of samples, the same values, the same histograms. Compute the mutual information again. Repeat a few hundred times. The values form a null distribution, what the estimator produces when the pairing carries no information.

Then compare. If the real value is larger than almost all the shuffled values, the pairing matters. The p-value is the fraction of shuffled values at least as large as the real one. One detail, count the real value itself among the shuffles, adding one to the count and one to the total. Belinda Phipson and Gordon Smyth explained in 2010 why this avoids reporting a p-value of exactly zero, which a finite number of shuffles can never justify.

This test is popular for good reasons. It works with any estimator, however complicated. It automatically includes the estimator's bias because the shuffled values carry the same bias. And it makes no assumption about the shape of the distributions.

But it does make one assumption, and it is a strong one. When you shuffle, you treat every ordering of the samples as equally plausible under the null hypothesis. Statisticians call this exchangeability. For independent samples, drawn one by one like candies from a jar, it holds. For time series, it does not. Neighbouring samples of an EEG are near copies of each other, as episode 1.7 showed. Shuffling scatters them and creates a signal with no memory at all.

So the null hypothesis that shuffling actually tests is not X and Y are independent. It is X and Y are independent, and the samples of Y are independent of each other. If your signals are autocorrelated, the second part is false before you start, and the test can reject the null for that reason alone.

Here is the calculation in one picture. We simulate pairs of signals that are completely independent of each other, but each autocorrelated with a memory set by a single number, phi, each sample is phi times the previous one plus fresh noise. 1000 samples per signal, the Gaussian copula estimator, 200 shuffles per test, 200 independent pairs. Every rejection is a false positive.

With no memory, phi equal to zero, the test rejects in about 5.5% of pairs, close to the 5% it promises. With phi equal to 0.5, a mild memory about 13%, with 0.9 about 47%, with 0.99 typical of a slow signal sampled fast about 83%. Most completely unrelated pairs are declared significantly coupled.

Look at one of those pairs. The two signals wander slowly, each on its own. Over a thousand samples, two independent slow wanderings often drift in the same direction for a while, purely by chance, and the estimated information is about 0.04 bits. Shuffle one of them and the slow wandering becomes white noise. The shuffled estimates stay below about 0.003 bits. The real value is more than 10 times the shuffled threshold. The test says the coupling is overwhelming. There is no coupling at all. And the estimate is not wrong. Two slow, independent signals really do agree by chance over a finite recording, the question is how often, and only a null that keeps their slowness can answer it.

Why? Because slow signals have few independent samples, as episode 1.7 explained with the effective sample size. A thousand samples of a signal with phi equal to 0.99 are worth only a handful of independent observations, and chance agreement between two such signals is large. The shuffled null, built from a thousand truly independent samples, is far too narrow. It describes a much easier world than the one you are in.

What is the shuffling test sensitive to? First, autocorrelation, as we have seen, the stronger the memory, the more false positives. That covers almost every brain signal, EEG, fNIRS, BOLD, heart rate, pupil size. Second, any other structure that shuffling destroys, trends, slow drifts, and non-stationarity, where the statistics of the signal change over time. Third, the number of shuffles, which limits the smallest p-value you can claim, with 200 shuffles, nothing below about 1 in 200. Use at least several hundred, and more when correcting for many tests. And fourth, what you shuffle. Shuffling trials in an experiment where trials are independent is fine. Shuffling samples within a continuous recording usually is not. A useful check before trusting any surrogate test. Run it on pairs you know are unrelated, such as recordings from two different participants. If those come out significant, the test is broken.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study computes mutual information between EEG channels or between brain and heart signals. For many pairs, tests each against shuffled surrogates, and finds that nearly every pair is significantly coupled. The conclusion is a densely connected network, but every signal involved is autocorrelated, and the shuffled null tests the wrong hypothesis. The density of the network says more about the slowness of the signals than about their coupling.

The fix is to shuffle in a way that keeps the memory of each signal, while breaking only the relationship between them. That is the subject of the next episode.

The companion notebook implements the shuffling test with the Phipson–Smyth p-value, measures its false positive rate on independent autocorrelated signals for several values of phi, and plots one example pair with its null distribution.

To recap, shuffling one signal destroys the pairing and builds a null distribution that includes the estimator's bias. The p-value counts how many shuffled values reach the real one, plus one, but shuffling assumes exchangeable samples. For autocorrelated signals, it tests independence and absence of memory at once, and it rejects far too often. In our simulation, from 5% without memory to 83% with strong memory on signals that are completely unrelated.

Next time, shuffles that keep the memory. Circular shift and block shuffles.

Surrogates II · Circular shifts and block shuffles

Circular shifts and block shuffles break only the alignment between two signals while each keeps its own memory, like cutting a deck of cards instead of shuffling card by card. On independent autocorrelated signals they bring false positives from 55% back to about 6 or 7% and keep most of the power. Short recordings and shared slow drifts still make them too permissive.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room. Last time, shuffling a deck card by card tore apart the runs of glued cards, and the test said yes to almost everything. Today, two ways of shuffling that keep the glue, cutting the deck and shuffling whole blocks of cards. They are called circular shifts and block shuffles, and they are the workhorses of surrogate testing for time series.

Start with intuition. We want a null distribution in which X and Y are unrelated, but in which each signal keeps its own memory, its own slowness, its own rhythm. Shuffling individual samples destroyed the memory. So we need to break only one thing, the alignment between the two signals in time.

The first idea is the circular shift. Take signal Y and slide it along the time axis by a random amount, wrapping the end of the recording around to the beginning, like cutting a deck of cards. Every sample of Y keeps its neighbours, except at the single seam. The autocorrelation of Y is preserved almost perfectly. But Y is now misaligned with X. Whatever relationship existed at the right timing is gone. Repeat with many random shifts, and the values form the null distribution. One rule, avoid very small shifts, which leave the signals nearly aligned. A minimum shift longer than the memory of the signals is the usual choice.

The second idea is the block shuffle. Cut Y into blocks, say 100 samples long, and shuffle the order of the blocks. Inside each block, the memory is intact. Only the junctions between blocks are broken. Blocks should be longer than the memory of the signal, so that most of the autocorrelation survives.

For experiments organised in trials, there is a third, natural version. Shuffle the trials. Pair the X of trial 3 with the Y of trial 17. Each trial keeps its full internal structure, and only the trial-by-trial correspondence is destroyed. This is valid when trials are exchangeable, for example when their order was randomised by design.

Which one to choose? For continuous recordings such as resting state, circular shifts are the simplest and preserve the most structure. Block shuffles are useful when the recording has several natural segments, or when the seam of a circular shift is a concern. Whenever the data come in trials, trial shuffles are usually the most natural null, because they match how the experiment was built.

Here is the calculation in one picture. We return to the independent but autocorrelated signals of the last episode, with 1,000 samples each and the Gaussian copula estimator. 150 independent pairs, 200 surrogates per test. Every rejection is a false positive.

With a memory of phi equal to 0.9, shuffling individual samples rejected in about 55% of pairs. Circular shifts reject in about 7%. Block shuffles in about 6%. Close to the 5% promised. The phantom connectivity disappears. Nothing about the estimator changed, only the surrogates did.

Look at one pair with phi equal to 0.99. The estimated information is about 0.008 bits. Against shuffled samples the p-value is below 1%, significant. Against circular shifts the p-value is about 0.55. Against block shuffles about 0.66. Nothing there which is the truth.

Now the limits. With phi equal to 0.99, circular shifts still reject in about 16% of pairs and block shuffles in about 10. Far better than 77% with sample shuffling, but not 5. Why? With a memory of about 100 samples, a recording of 1,000 samples contains only about 10 independent stretches. There are only about 10 genuinely different ways to shift or cut it, and the null distribution is estimated from very little independent information. When the recording is short compared with the memory of the signal, no surrogate can create the independent data you do not have. It is the effective sample size of episode 1.7 again, now limiting the test itself.

And the other side, power. Surrogates that preserve memory are more conservative, so do they still detect a real coupling? We add a weak true coupling to the pairs at phi equal to 0.9. Shuffled samples detected in every pair, but they also flagged unrelated pairs half the time, so that is meaningless. Circular shifts and block shuffles detected in about 85% of pairs. They keep most of the power and only lose what was never earned.

What are these surrogates sensitive to? First, the length of the recording relative to the memory. The shorter it is, the fewer independent surrogates exist. Second, the minimum shift and the block length, too short and the memory leaks across, too long and too few different surrogates remain. A block length of a few times the autocorrelation time is a reasonable start. Third, non-stationarity. A circular shift moves the beginning of one signal opposite the end of the other. If both signals drift slowly in the same way because electrodes dry or attention fades over the session, the surrogates break that shared drift while the real data keep it. And the test can still find a coupling that is only a common trend. Detrending or comparing within shorter stationary segments helps. Fourth, the seam itself. Wrapping the end onto the beginning creates a jump if the signals start and end differ. And fifth, for trial shuffles, the exchangeability of trials. If trials change systematically across the session, shuffling them mixes early and late trials and changes the null.

Here is the classic pitfall in a corrected form. A study learns from the last episode, switches from sample shuffling to circular shifts, and declares the problem solved. But its recordings last 30 seconds of a signal with a slow component of several seconds, so only a handful of independent shifts exist, and its signals share a slow drift across the session. The test is better but still too permissive. The safeguard is the one from last time, run the full pipeline on pairs that are known to be unrelated, such as recordings from different participants or different sessions, and check that the false positive rate is close to the nominal level.

The companion notebook implements circular-shift, block-shuffle and trial-shuffle surrogates, measures their false positive rates on independent autocorrelated signals at two memory lengths, measures their power on a weakly coupled pair, and reproduces the example pair.

To recap, circular shifts and block shuffles break the alignment between two signals while keeping each signal's memory. Trial shuffles do the same for experiments organised in trials. On independent autocorrelated signals, they bring the false positive rate from 55% back to about six or seven, and they keep most of the power to detect real coupling. They still struggle when the recording is short compared with the signal's memory, and when signals share slow drifts. Check every test on pairs you know are unrelated.

Surrogates III · Phase randomization, AAFT, IAAFT

Phase randomisation keeps each signal's power spectrum exactly, like a choir singing the same notes with each singer starting at a random moment; AAFT and IAAFT also keep the histogram. Randomising one signal tests independence with memory preserved, randomising both tests whether dependence exceeds a linear Gaussian coupling. Rejecting that null does not prove nonlinear coupling.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room, for the most sophisticated shuffle of the chapter, circular shifts kept each signal's memory approximately. Today's surrogates keep it exactly. They preserve the full power spectrum of each signal, frequency by frequency, and randomise everything else. They are called phase-randomised surrogates and their refinements AAFT and IAAFT. They are powerful and they test a very specific null hypothesis, which is easy to misread.

Start with intuition. Any signal can be written as a sum of sine waves, one per frequency, each with an amplitude and a phase. The amplitudes make up the power spectrum, and the power spectrum carries all of the signal's autocorrelation. The phases say how the waves line up in time. Keep the amplitudes, replace every phase by a random angle, and add the waves back together. The result has exactly the same spectrum, and therefore the same autocorrelation as the original. But any structure beyond the spectrum is gone. Think of a choir singing the same notes at the same loudness, each singer starting at a random moment. The chord survives, the melody does not.

This idea was introduced as a test for nonlinearity by James Theiler and colleagues in 1992. The surrogate is what the signal would look like if it were a linear Gaussian process with that spectrum, filtered Gaussian noise. If a statistic computed on the real signal is far outside its surrogate distribution, the signal is not compatible with that model.

There is one problem. Phase-randomised surrogates are Gaussian, whatever the original's distribution. If the real signal is skewed, say a power envelope that is always positive, the surrogates have a different histogram, and any statistics sensitive to amplitudes may differ for that reason alone. Theiler and colleagues proposed a fix in the same paper, the amplitude-adjusted Fourier transform, or AAFT. Rescale the signal to a Gaussian, randomise its phases, and rescale back to the original values. In 1996, Thomas Schreiber and Andreas Schmitz improved it with an iterative version, IAAFT, which alternates between imposing the original spectrum and imposing the original histogram until both are matched closely. In practice, IAAFT converges within a few dozen iterations, our notebook stops after 50. A small mismatch remains in the spectrum or in the histogram, and for long recordings with sharp spectral peaks it is worth checking.

So, for one signal, IAAFT gives surrogates with the same values, the same spectrum, and nothing else. The null hypothesis becomes a linear Gaussian process observed through a fixed, possibly nonlinear measurement function.

How does this serve mutual information between two signals? There are two different uses, and they test two different things.

The first use randomise the phases of Y alone, independently of X. Each surrogate of Y keeps its spectrum, but loses any alignment with X. This is a memory-preserving null for independence, like the circular shift, but with exact spectra. Here is the calculation in one picture. Independent pairs of skewed, autocorrelated signals, the exponential of a slow random process, with a binned estimator of mutual information. 100 pairs, 100 surrogates per test. Shuffling individual samples rejects in about 20% of pairs. Phase randomisation rejects in about 3%, AAFT in about 2, and IAAFT in about 3. All three keep the false positive rate at or below the nominal 5%. The exact rates depend on the estimator and on the signals, but the ranking is the same as in the last two episodes. Surrogates that keep the memory respect the null and sample shuffles do not.

The second use is subtler. Randomise the phases of X and Y together, adding the same random phase to both at each frequency. This preserves each spectrum and also the cross-spectrum, all the linear relationship between the two signals, including their correlation at every lag. What is destroyed is any dependence beyond the linear one. This bivariate version was described by Dean Prichard and James Theiler in 1994. Comparing mutual information against these surrogates asks, is there more dependence than a linear Gaussian coupling would produce?

Two cases. First, a linear coupling, Y is a mix of X and its own noise. The KSG estimate is about 0.19 nats, and the surrogate threshold, the 95th percentile, is about 0.24. Not significant correctly, the coupling is linear and the surrogates reproduce it. Their distribution also shows how much information a linear coupling alone would produce, a useful baseline in its own right. Across 40 pairs, the test rejects in about 10%, near the nominal level given the small number of pairs. Second, a nonlinear coupling, Y is the square of X plus noise. The linear relationship is zero, so the bivariate surrogates contain almost no dependence. The estimate is about 0.55 nats against a threshold of about 0.06. Rejected in every pair.

Now the crucial point. Rejecting the null of a linear Gaussian process does not prove a nonlinear coupling. It says only that the data are not compatible with that particular model. Many things other than nonlinear coupling can cause the rejection, non-stationarity, because the surrogates assume a single spectrum for the whole recording, a non-Gaussian distribution that the measurement function cannot explain, discontinuities between the start and end of the recording, or simply a different kind of linear process than the one assumed. A rejection is a reason to look further, not a conclusion.

In practice, a sensible order is the following. First, test for any coupling with memory-preserving surrogates, circular shifts, or phase randomisation of one signal. Only if a coupling is found, and only if the question is about its form, use the bivariate surrogates to ask whether a linear model explains it. And before that second step, check stationarity, for example by repeating the test in shorter segments.

What are these surrogates sensitive to? First, stationarity. Phase randomisation assumes the spectrum is the same throughout. A recording whose rhythm changes, alpha strong at first and weak later, is not reproduced, and the test may reject for that reason. Second, the edges. The Fourier transform treats the recording as periodic. If the last sample differs from the first, the jump leaks power across frequencies, trimming the recording so that the end matches the start in value and slope reduces the problem. Third, the histogram. Plain phase randomisation changes it. AAFT and IAAFT preserve it with small residual mismatches in the spectrum. Fourth, the number of surrogates as for every test. And fifth, the question, the univariate version tests independence. The bivariate version tests linearity of the coupling. Mixing them up changes what the result means.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study compares the mutual information between two EEG channels with bivariate IAAFT surrogates, finds it significantly larger, and concludes that the two regions interact nonlinearly. But the recording includes eyes-open and eyes-closed periods, and the alpha rhythm changes between them. Non-stationarity alone can make the data incompatible with a single linear Gaussian model. The rejection says, not a stationary linear Gaussian process. It does not say nonlinear interaction. Testing within stationary segments and considering every alternative to the null comes before any claim of nonlinearity. Another check is to simulate data from a linear model fitted to the recording with the same change of rhythm and to see how often the test rejects when the null is true.

The companion notebook implements phase randomisation, AAFT and IAAFT, checks that they preserve the spectrum and the histogram, measures false positive rates for independence, and runs the bivariate test on linear and nonlinear couplings.

To recap, phase randomisation keeps each signal's power spectrum exactly and randomises everything else. AAFT and IAAFT also keep the histogram. Randomising one signal tests independence with memory preserved. Randomising both together tests whether the dependence exceeds a linear Gaussian coupling. The null is a stationary linear Gaussian process, possibly seen through a static nonlinear lens. Rejecting it does not prove nonlinear coupling. Non-stationarity, edges, and other departures can cause it too.

Bias and sample size

Information estimates are biased upward, and the bias shrinks as samples grow, so conditions with different trial counts give different estimates even when the true information is identical. In a simulation, 60 versus 200 trials produced a p-value below one in 100 million with no real difference. Subsampling the larger condition to equal size, many times, makes the difference vanish.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room, at a table where two players are compared on very different numbers of games. One played 60 rounds, the other 200. Their win rates are not equally reliable, and for information estimates the problem is worse than reliability. The number of samples changes the average answer itself. Today, the most common way to fool yourself with information, a difference between conditions that is only a difference in sample size.

Start with intuition. Chapter 5 showed that the plug-in estimator and many others are biased upward and that the bias shrinks as the number of samples grows. With few samples the estimator invents information, with many it invents less. So two conditions with exactly the same true information but different numbers of trials will give different estimates. The condition with fewer trials will look more informative. This is not a small effect hidden in the decimals. At the sample sizes of a typical experiment, the bias can be larger than the information itself.

Here is the calculation in one picture. A simulated pair of signals with a weak correlation of 0.3, a true mutual information of about 0.07 bits. We estimate it with the plug-in estimator on six equal-count bins per variable, 300 times at each sample size.

With 30 samples, the estimate averages about 0.82 bits, more than 10 times the truth. With 60, about 0.43. With 100, 0.26. With 200, 0.15. With 500, 0.09, with 1,000 0.08, with 5,000 about 0.06, slightly below the truth because six bins lose a little information. The curve falls steeply, roughly as 1 over n, exactly as the Treves-Panzeri formula of episode 5.2 predicts. The spread shrinks too, from about 15 hundredths of a bit at 30 samples to under one hundredth at five thousand. Small samples are both inflated and noisy. Both properties matter for comparisons.

Now the experiment. 20 simulated participants, two conditions. In condition A, each participant contributes 60 trials. In condition B, 200 trials because, say, condition A was rarer or more trials were rejected for artefacts. The true information is identical in both conditions, by construction.

Condition A averages about 0.39 bits. Condition B about 0.15. A paired t-test across the 20 participants gives a p-value below 1 in 100 million. A spectacular difference between conditions, with no difference at all in the brain. The statistics are impeccable, it is the quantity being tested that is wrong.

Now equalise. For each participant, draw 60 trials at random from the 200 of condition B, estimate the information, and repeat 50 times to average out the luck of the draw. The subsampled condition B averages about 0.43 bits. Compared with condition A, the p-value is now about 0.16. The difference has vanished. It was never there.

That is the rule, compare conditions at equal sample sizes. When trial counts differ, subsample the larger condition down to the size of the smaller one, many times, and average. The cost is some statistical power in the larger condition. The benefit is that the comparison means something.

Three details make subsampling work. Draw whole trials, not individual samples, so that each trial keeps its internal structure. Use the same indices for both variables so that every pair stays paired. And repeat the draw many times, then average. A single subsample adds its own luck to the comparison. The repeated subsamples are not independent of each other, so their spread is not a confidence interval. It only tells you how much the draw matters. 50 draws, as here, are usually enough.

There are alternatives. Bias-corrected estimators reduce the problem, but, as episodes 5.3 and 5.4 showed, never remove it completely, especially at small n. Surrogate distributions computed separately for each condition at each condition's own sample size can be used to compare excess information above each condition's own null. And estimators with small bias such as KSG or the Gaussian copula reduce the gap, though not to zero. Equalising the sample size is the simplest and most robust.

What is this sensitive to? First, the size of the difference in sample size. A ratio of 3 as here is enough to dominate. Second, the estimator. The more biased, the larger the artefact and the plug-in estimator with many bins is the worst. Third, the dimension. Every added variable multiplies the cells and the bias. Fourth, hidden differences in sample size. Artefact rejection, different trial durations, different numbers of events, or different effective sample sizes because one condition has slower, more autocorrelated signals. Two conditions with the same number of samples can still differ in effective N, which is the subject of the next episode. Fifth, the settings. Changing the number of bins or neighbours changes the size of the artefact, so a difference that appears with one setting and vanishes with another deserves suspicion.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports more information between brain activity and the stimulus for rare deviant tones than for frequent standard tones. Deviants are rare by design, perhaps one trial in five. So the deviant condition has far fewer trials than the standard condition, and its estimates are inflated by bias. Unless the analysis equalised trial counts, the result may be a property of the design, not of the brain. The same trap appears when comparing correct and error trials, attended and unattended stimuli, or patients and controls with different numbers of clean trials. In hyperscanning, it appears when real pairs and pseudo-pairs are built from different amounts of data.

The companion notebook draws the curve of estimated information against sample size, simulates the two-condition experiment with 60 and 200 trials, and shows the difference disappearing after subsampling.

A simple diagnostic belongs in every comparison. Plot the estimate against the number of trials for each condition by subsampling at several sizes. If the two curves still separate when compared at the same number of trials, the difference may be real. If they lie on top of each other at equal n, it was the sample size all along. Either way you will know.

To recap, information estimates are biased and the bias depends on the number of samples. Two conditions with the same true information but different trial counts give different estimates and the smaller condition looks more informative. In our simulation, a ratio of 60 to 200 trials produced a difference with a p-value below 1 in 100 million, which vanished after subsampling. Always compare at equal sample sizes, watch for hidden differences in N, and report the trial counts of every condition.

Next time, when the number of samples is the same, but the number of independent samples is not. Autocorrelation, effective N and the Theiler window.

PLATE 6.67:53

Autocorrelation, effective N and the Theiler window

Nearest neighbour estimators on smooth, oversampled signals find neighbours just before and after each sample in time, so they measure smoothness and report phantom dependence. The Theiler window excludes temporal neighbours but alone overshoots into negative values. Combining it with a larger k or with downsampling recovers the truth, and the window should be chosen from the autocorrelation time, longer after filtering.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room, at the table where we learn that a slow deck has glued cards. Two episodes ago, the surrogates were fooled by autocorrelation. Today, the estimator itself is fooled. When a signal is sampled much faster than it changes, each sample's nearest neighbours are simply the samples just before and after it in time. Nearest neighbour estimators then measure smoothness instead of dependence. The classic remedy is the Theiler window, introduced by James Theiler in 1986. Today we see what it fixes and what it does not fix on its own.

Start with intuition. Recall episode 1.7. A signal with memory has fewer independent samples than its number of samples. Its effective sample size is smaller, roughly the number of samples divided by the time it takes the autocorrelation to die out. For the smooth simulated signals of this episode, that time is about 35 samples. 2,000 samples are worth only about 57 independent ones.

Now recall how KSG works from episode 5.7. For each sample, it looks for the nearest samples in the joint space of X and Y. For a smooth signal, the nearest samples are its neighbours in time. One sample later, X has barely moved and neither has Y. So every point finds its k neighbours in its own short stretch of trajectory, very close in both X and Y. The estimator reads that as strong dependence.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Two independent smooth signals, 2,000 samples each. KSG with k equal to 3, 20 repetitions. The true mutual information is 0. KSG reports about 0.37 nats. A strong, entirely phantom dependence, produced by smoothness alone.

Theiler's remedy, when searching for neighbours, excludes samples that are too close in time. Choose a window, say 30 samples on either side, and pretend those samples do not exist when looking for neighbours, both in the joint space and in the marginal counts. Now neighbours must come from other moments of the recording.

With a Theiler window of 30 samples, the estimate on the independent signals drops to about minus 0.15 nats. The phantom information has melted and gone too far. The estimate is now clearly negative.

Why negative? Think about the trajectory. With the temporal neighbours excluded, the nearest neighbours in the joint space now come from another moment when the trajectory passed close by. But a smooth trajectory passes by slowly, so the k nearest neighbours are often consecutive samples of one single passage. The joint neighbourhood is set by that one passage and is very small. Along each marginal axis, however, the strip of the same width catches runs of samples from many passages. The marginal counts are large compared with k, and the estimator reports negative information.

There are two practical fixes and they work. The first is to increase k beyond the length of a passage, so that the joint neighbourhood is set by several passages rather than one. With the Theiler window and k equal to 20, the independent signals give 0.00 nats on average. The second is to downsample. Keep one sample in 20 so that consecutive samples are closer to independent. That also gives about 0.03 nats with a larger spread because only 100 samples remain, about eight hundredths of a nat against three for the window with a larger k.

Now check that the fixes do not destroy real information. Two smooth signals with a true coupling, a true mutual information of about 0.22 nats. Without any correction, KSG reports about 0.56, inflated by smoothness. With the Theiler window alone, about 0.12 too low. With the Theiler window and k equal to 20, about 0.21. With downsampling about 0.22, again with a larger spread. Both fixes recover the truth.

So the Theiler window is necessary, but not sufficient on its own. It removes temporal neighbours. The choice of k or a coarser sampling must ensure that the remaining neighbours come from genuinely different moments. This is worth stating plainly because many implementations offer the Theiler window as a single setting, and it is tempting to switch it on and move on. Check what the estimate does as k grows, and compare it with a downsampled version. If they disagree, the neighbours are still describing the trajectory, not the dependence.

How should you choose the window? Use the autocorrelation function of each signal. The window should be at least as long as the time over which the autocorrelation stays clearly positive, the autocorrelation time. Here, about 35 samples, so a window of 30 is on the short side, and a window of 50 would be safer. When in doubt, try several windows and check that the estimate stabilises.

What is this sensitive to? First, the sampling rate relative to the signal's frequency content. EEG sampled at 1000 Hz and low-pass filtered at 40 Hz is massively oversampled for this purpose. Second, filtering. Every filter lengthens the autocorrelation, so a band-pass filtered signal needs a longer window than a raw one. Third, k, as we have just seen. Fourth, the estimator. Binned and Gaussian copula estimators are not fooled in the same way because they do not search for neighbours, but their surrogate tests still need the effective sample size. And fifth, the same issue appears in every quantity built on neighbour searches. Transfer entropy, active information storage, sample entropy, recurrence analysis. Each needs its own window.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study estimates KSG mutual information between two EEG channels, recorded at 1000 Hz and band-pass filtered between 8 and 12 Hz, and finds a large value in every participant. The filtered signals are smooth over tens of milliseconds. Neighbours are found within the same oscillation cycle. Most of the reported information is smoothness, a Theiler window of at least one alpha cycle, a value of k larger than the samples per passage or a downsampling, and surrogates that preserve the autocorrelation are all needed before the number means anything. A useful habit, report the autocorrelation time of each signal next to the window you chose.

The companion notebook simulates smooth, independent, and coupled signals, estimates their autocorrelation time and effective sample size, implements KSG with a Theiler window, and compares no correction, the window alone, the window with a larger k, and downsampling.

To recap, oversampled, smooth signals fool nearest neighbour estimators. Neighbours in time masquerade as dependence, about 0.37 nats from nothing here. The Theiler window excludes temporal neighbours, but alone it can overshoot into negative values because the remaining neighbours come from single slow passages. Combine it with a larger k or downsample, and the estimate returns to the truth, both without and with a real coupling. Choose the window from the autocorrelation time, longer after filtering.

PLATE 6.79:12

Multiple comparisons in information networks

Testing every pair of 64 electrodes means thousands of tests, so hundreds of false links appear by chance: 114 in a network of pure noise. The maximum statistic, the false discovery rate and cluster methods control different errors and support different claims. With too few permutations, the smallest attainable p-value blinds false discovery control and true links vanish, while the maximum statistic still finds them.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room where one roll of the dice can come up six by luck, and a thousand rolls certainly will. Today's problem is not a new estimator or a new surrogate. It is arithmetic. When you test every pair of electrodes for information, you run thousands of tests, and a few hundred will pass by chance alone. Information networks are built from those tests.

Start with intuition. A test at the 5% level promises that, when there is nothing to find, it will wrongly reject 5 times in 100. That promise is about 1 test. Run 100 tests on pure noise and you expect about 5 false positives. Run 4000 and you expect about 200.

Now count. A cap with 64 electrodes has 64 times 63 over 2 pairs, 2,016 undirected pairs for mutual information. For a directed measure such as transfer entropy, each pair counts twice, once in each direction, 4,032 directed pairs. At 5% each, pure noise produces about 100 undirected links, or about 200 directed ones.

Here is the calculation in one picture. 64 channels of simulated, completely independent noise, 1,000 samples each. We compute the Gaussian copula mutual information for every pair, and test each against 1,000 surrogates that shuffle each channel independently, which is valid here because the samples are independent. Without any correction, 114 pairs are significant at 5%, close to the 100 expected by chance. Drawn as a network, it looks like a rich web of connections. Every single link is false. Nothing in the picture warns you. It is simply what honest arithmetic predicts.

So we must correct. There are three main families of corrections, and they control different things.

The first controls the family-wise error rate, the probability of making even one false discovery among all the tests. The classic version is Bonferroni. Divide the threshold by the number of tests. With 2,016 tests, each must reach a p-value below about 2.5 in 100,000. A more powerful version for permutation tests is the maximum statistic. At each permutation, record only the largest value across all pairs. That builds the null distribution of the maximum. Each real pair is then compared with that distribution. A pair is significant only if it beats what the best of all pairs achieves by chance. On our noise network, the maximum statistic finds zero significant links. Correct.

The second family controls the false discovery rate, as proposed by Yoav Benjamini and Yosef Hochberg in 1995, the expected proportion of false discoveries among the links you declare. It is less strict than the family-wise error rate and more powerful when many true effects exist. On the noise network, it also finds zero links.

The third family uses structure. In EEG, real effects often extend over neighbouring channels, times, and frequencies. Cluster-based permutation tests, popularised in neuroscience by Eric Maris and Robert Oostenveld in 2007, group neighbouring suprathreshold results into clusters and test the size of each cluster against its permutation distribution. They gain power by assuming effects are spatially or temporally contiguous, and they make claims about clusters, not about individual links. The price is precision. A significant cluster says that an effect exists somewhere in it, not that every channel or time point inside it is involved.

Now plant 10 true links in the noise, 10 pairs of channels with a modest real dependence. Without correction, all 10 are found, together with 111 false ones. A network that is 90% noise. With the maximum statistic, all 10 are found and no false link at all.

And with the false discovery rate, zero. Not one true link survives. Why? This is a trap worth knowing. With 1000 permutations, the smallest p-value a permutation test can produce is 1 in 1001. The false discovery procedure ranks the p-values and compares the k-th smallest with k times 5% divided by the number of tests. With 2,016 tests, the first comparison demands a p-value below about 2.5 in 100,000, and the procedure only reaches 1 in 1,000 at the 41st rank. Our 10 true links all share the smallest possible p-value, 1 in 1,001, but 10 is not 41. None passes. With p-values computed from too few permutations, Bonferroni and false discovery procedures become blind, however strong the effects. The maximum statistic does not have this problem because it compares the real values directly with the permutation distribution of the maximum. If you need p-values for thousands of tests, you need tens of thousands of permutations or a method designed for it. A quick rule, to make a Bonferroni threshold reachable at all, the number of permutations must exceed the number of tests divided by the significance level. For 2,000 tests at 5%, that is more than 40,000 permutations.

For directed networks with transfer entropy, there is a further level of structure. The IDTxl toolbox, described by Leonardo Novelli and colleagues in 2019, infers each target's sources step by step, adding a source only if it brings significant information given those already selected, and tests at several levels with maximum statistics to control false positives across the whole network. Chapter 8 returns to it. In group studies there is one more layer. The correction belongs to the group statistic after combining participants, not to each participant separately.

What is this sensitive to? First, the number of tests. Every added channel, time window, or frequency band multiplies it. Decide in advance which tests you need. Second, what the correction controls. Family-wise error, false discovery rate, or clusters. Each supports a different claim, and the claim must match. Third, the resolution of permutation p-values as we just saw. Fourth, dependence between tests. Neighbouring electrodes share signal through volume conduction, so tests are not independent. The maximum statistic and cluster methods handle that naturally. Bonferroni is conservative under it. And fifth, the surrogates themselves. A correction for multiple comparisons cannot fix a test that is invalid in the first place, such as sample shuffling on autocorrelated signals.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study computes mutual information between all pairs of 64 electrodes, thresholds each at p below 0.05, and reports graph measures of the resulting network, hubs, clustering, small-worldness. With about 100 false links expected among 2,000 pairs, those graph measures describe chance as much as the brain. And if the surrogates were sample shuffles on EEG, even more links pass, as episode 6.2 showed. Correct for multiple comparisons, use valid surrogates, and report how many links would be expected by chance.

What should be reported then? The number of tests and what defines them. Channels, directions, time windows, frequency bands. The correction and what it controls. The number of permutations and the smallest p-value it allows, and the expected number of false links under the null. With those four numbers, a reader can judge a network at a glance. Without them, a figure full of links says nothing about how many of them should be there.

The companion notebook simulates a 64-channel noise network, tests every pair with permutations, applies the uncorrected threshold, the maximum statistic and the false discovery rate, plants 10 true links and shows the resolution trap.

To recap, thousands of tests at 5% produce hundreds of false links, 114 in our noise network. The maximum statistic controls the chance of any false link and works directly with permutations. The false discovery rate controls the proportion of false links but needs p-values fine enough to pass its thresholds. Cluster methods gain power by assuming contiguous effects. Choose the correction that matches your claim, use enough permutations, and never correct an invalid test.

PLATE 6.89:18

Preprocessing breaks information

Preprocessing changes the timing of signals before information is measured, so it can alter directed measures such as transfer entropy in any direction. Filtering one channel created a strong direction from signals with none, downsampling erased a real interaction, zero-phase filtering produced a backward flow, and unequal noise hid a real driver. Test the pipeline on simulations with known ground truth.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room, where the house can change the rules between rounds without telling you. In a real analysis, the house is your preprocessing pipeline. Filtering, downsampling, rereferencing, artefact removal. Every step changes the signals before any information is measured. Episode 4.5 told us that processing can never create information about the stimulus. Today we see that, for measures between signals and especially for directed measures like transfer entropy, preprocessing can change the answer in every direction, including creating a direction that does not exist.

Start with intuition. Transfer entropy, which chapter 8 builds properly, asks whether the past of one signal helps predict the present of another, beyond what the second signal's own past already tells. Its answer depends on timing, what counts as past, what counts as present. Any operation that shifts, smears, or removes timing information can change it. A filter delays and spreads signals in time. Downsampling throws away the fast timescales where an interaction may live. A zero-phase filter, applied forwards and backwards, mixes the future of a signal into its present. None of these operations is wrong. Each is a choice, and each has consequences for timing.

Here, to isolate the preprocessing, we use a linear Gaussian version of transfer entropy, computed with 5 samples of history on 5,000 samples per signal, repeated 30 times. Values are in nats.

Here is the calculation in one picture, starting with the most striking case. Two signals that share a common source at exactly the same moment, each with its own noise. There is no direction in this system, neither signal drives the other. Transfer entropy is small and symmetric, about 0.012 nats in each direction.

Now filter only one of them, the second, with an ordinary causal low-pass filter, the kind built into many amplifiers. A causal filter delays the signal a little. Suddenly, the unfiltered signal seems to anticipate the filtered one. Transfer entropy from the first to the second jumps to about 0.34 nats. In the other direction, about 0.001. A strong, perfectly clear direction, created entirely by a filter applied to one channel. Filter both channels the same way and the asymmetry disappears again, about 0.005 in each direction. Identical filters delay both signals by the same amount, so neither appears to lead. It is the difference in delay that manufactures a direction.

This is not exotic. Channels recorded with different hardware, different reference electrodes, or different preprocessing, such as EEG and ECG in a brain heart study or two amplifiers in a hyperscanning setup, can easily be filtered differently.

Second case, a real direction. The first signal drives the second with a delay of one sample. On the raw data, transfer entropy from the driver to the follower is about 0.135 nats, and the reverse is essentially 0. Correct.

Now downsample both signals by a factor of 4, keeping one sample in 4. The interaction happened within one sample of the original rate. After downsampling, it falls between samples. Transfer entropy from driver to follower drops to about 0.014, almost 10 times smaller. The interaction has not disappeared from the system, it has disappeared from the data. No estimator can recover it afterwards.

Now band-pass filter both signals with a zero-phase filter, the default in many toolboxes because it does not shift peaks in time. Transfer entropy from driver to follower stays at about 0.14. But the reverse direction, which should be 0, rises to about 0.08. A zero-phase filter uses future samples to compute each filtered value, so the follower's filtered present contains a smear of its own future, which reflects the driver's present. A spurious backward flow appears. With a causal band-pass filter instead, the reverse direction is smaller, about 0.044, but still clearly above zero, because the filter lengthens each signal's memory and makes the follower's own past a worse summary of its history.

Third case unequal noise. Add strong measurement noise to the driver only. The forward transfer entropy falls from about 0.135 to about 0.033 nats, four times smaller, on the same interaction. A noisier recording of the driver hides its influence. In other settings, as episode 8.10 will show, unequal noise can even make the cleaner signal appear to drive the noisier one. A direction of information flow is only as reliable as the equality of the recordings.

These effects have been studied in detail. Lionel Barnett and Anil Seth showed in 2011 that Granger causality, the linear cousin of transfer entropy, is theoretically unchanged by invertible filters applied identically to both signals, but that in practice filtering raises the model order needed and degrades estimation. Weber and colleagues in 2017 documented how filtering and downsampling distort transfer entropy estimates. The theory says some operations should not matter, finite data and estimators say otherwise.

Undirected measures are not immune either. Mutual information between two filtered signals depends on which frequencies the filter keeps, because different rhythms can be coupled differently, and filtering lengthens the autocorrelation, which shrinks the effective sample size and inflates estimates and surrogate thresholds alike, as episodes 6.2 and 6.6 showed.

What is this sensitive to? First, whether both signals are processed identically. Different filters on different channels are the most dangerous situation, because they create asymmetries in timing. Second, the type of filter. Zero-phase filters mix future into present, and causal filters add delays and lengthen memory. Third, the sampling rate relative to the interaction delay. Downsample below the timescale of the interaction and it vanishes. Fourth, the history length used by the measure. A filter lengthens memory so the history must be longer, which costs data. Fifth, rereferencing and spatial filters which mix channels together and can create shared instantaneous components. Chapter 8 returns to that with volume conduction. And sixth, noise levels that differ between channels. Seventh, artefact removal methods such as independent component analysis, which recombine channels and can move information between them.

Here is the classic pitfall. A brain-heart study finds strong transfer entropy from the ECG to an EEG channel and concludes that the heart drives the brain. The ECG was recorded with a different amplifier and its own hardware filter. The EEG was band-pass filtered offline with a zero-phase filter. Both were downsampled for convenience. Every one of these steps could create, remove, or reverse a direction. Before interpreting a direction, run the identical pipeline on simulated signals with a known direction or with none and check that it is preserved.

A few practical rules follow. Record at a sampling rate well above the fastest interaction you care about and downsample only with that timescale in mind. For directed measures, prefer causal filters or estimate on minimally filtered data with longer histories. Apply exactly the same filters to every channel you compare and correct for known hardware delays. And write the entire pipeline into the methods. In a game where the house can change the rules, the rules must be on the table.

To recap, preprocessing cannot add information about a stimulus, but it can change information between signals in any direction. A causal filter on one channel created a direction of about 0.34 nats from nothing. Downsampling erased a true interaction. A zero-phase filter created a backward flow of about 0.08 nats. Unequal noise hid a real driver. Process all signals identically, choose filters with care, keep the sampling rate above the interaction timescale and test the full pipeline on simulations with known ground truth.

PLATE 6.96:47

Report it so others can redo it

An information value is reproducible only if every rule behind it is written down: data, preprocessing, estimator settings, units, statistics, code, seeds, and a check against ground truth. Fixing the seed makes two runs identical, while changing it moves the p-value because surrogates are random. Reproduced means the same number from the same data and code; replicated, the same effect in new data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the games room for the last episode of the chapter. Over eight episodes, we found that an information value depends on the estimator and its settings, on the number and independence of samples, on the surrogates, on the number of tests, and on every preprocessing step. That is not a reason for despair. It is a list. And a list can be written down. Today, we turn it into a checklist so that another lab can take your report, rerun your analysis, and get the same number.

Start with intuition. A board game can be replayed by strangers only if the rules are written in the box. If one table plays with house rules nobody wrote down, their scores cannot be compared with anyone else's. An information analysis is the same. The number is reproducible only if every rule that produced it is written down, and ideally if the game itself, the code, is in the box.

Here is the checklist in six parts.

1. The data. Where they come from with the dataset identifier and version and the licence. How many participants, trials, and samples, and how many were excluded with the reasons. The sampling rate.

2. Preprocessing. Every filter with its type, order, and cutoff frequencies, and whether it was causal or zero-phase. Any downsampling. The reference. How artefacts were handled. Whether every channel went through exactly the same steps.

3. The estimator. Its name and every setting, the number of bins and how they were placed, the bandwidth. k. The embedding dimension and delay, the Theiler window, for neural estimators, the architecture, optimiser, learning rate, batch size, number of steps, stopping rule, and the split between training and evaluation data. And the units, bits or nats.

4. The statistics. The type of surrogate and why it matches the null hypothesis, the number of surrogates and the smallest p-value they allow, the number of tests and the correction for multiple comparisons, whether conditions were compared at equal numbers of trials, and for every reported value, its uncertainty.

5. The code. A public repository with the exact version used, the software environment with library versions, and the random seeds.

Six, a check against ground truth. The same pipeline run on simulated data with a known answer, ideally one with no dependence and one with a known dependence, reported alongside the real result.

Here is the calculation in one picture. A simulated pair of signals with a weak coupling analysed with KSG k equal to 4 on standardised signals with 200 circular shift surrogates. We fix the random seed at 2026 and run the analysis twice, as two labs would. Both runs give the same estimate, about 0.04 nats and the same p-value 0.050 to every digit. The data file carries a fingerprint, a short hash, so that the second lab can check it has the same input.

Now run the same analysis five more times with different random seeds. The estimate itself does not change because KSG is deterministic on fixed data. But the p-value moves from run to run because the surrogates are random. With 5 other seeds, it comes out at about 0.06, 0.07, 0.050 twice, and 0.055. The same data and the same analysis are significant or not, depending on the seed. With only 200 surrogates, that variation can move a result across the threshold of 0.05. That is why the seed belongs in the report, and why the number of surrogates should be large enough that the conclusion does not depend on the draw.

When a second lab takes the notebook, the data fingerprint, and the seed and obtains the same number, the result is reproduced. The analysis does what the report says it does. That is not the same as replicated, which would mean the same effect found again in new data by a new experiment. Reproduction is the minimum. Some journals and funders now ask for it explicitly, and a notebook that runs from raw data to final figure is the simplest way to provide it. Replication is the goal. But a result that cannot even be reproduced cannot be replicated meaningfully, because nobody knows exactly what was measured.

What makes reproduction fail in practice? First, unreported choices, a default bin count or bandwidth that changed between library versions. Second, randomness without seeds, surrogates, subsampling, jitter added to break ties, neural network initialisation. Third, data that are not available or not identified by version. Fourth, preprocessing done by hand outside the code so that nobody can redo it exactly. And fifth, environments that drift. The same code a year later with different library versions can give a different number. Recording the environment or packaging it in a container prevents that.

Here is the classic pitfall. A method section says, mutual information was computed with a nearest neighbour estimator and tested with surrogates, p below 0.05. Which estimator, which k, which normalisation of the signals, which surrogates, how many, which correction, which seed, which code. Every one of these choices could change the result, as this chapter showed. The sentence sounds like a description. It is closer to an advertisement. The checklist turns it back into a method.

The companion notebook runs the example analysis with a fixed seed twice, prints the data fingerprint and the environment, reruns it with several seeds to show the variation of the p-value, and ends with the full checklist as a template you can copy into your own methods section.

To recap, an information value is reproducible only if every rule behind it is written down. Data, preprocessing, estimator and settings, units, statistics, code with versions and seeds, and a check against ground truth. Fixing the seed made two runs identical, changing it moved the p-value because surrogates are random. Reproduced means the same number from the same data and code. Replicated means the same effect in new data. Reproduction comes first.
