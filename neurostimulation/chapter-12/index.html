How to read a clinical status

A clinical status is a document read field by field: regulator stamps say what may be sold and for what, while evidence grades say what trials show. The US doors (approval, clearance, De Novo, humanitarian exemption), Canadian licence classes and the European CE mark answer different questions. A grid of where, which door, what exactly and when keeps approved and effective apart.

TRANSCRIPTEN

First, one card. Educational content, not medical advice. This episode reads documents. It does not rank treatments, and it never tells a viewer what to choose.

Welcome to the border post. Every technique in this chapter reaches the counter with papers. A clinical status is one of those papers, and like a visa, it can be read line by line. The goal is a reading grid, not a list of treatments.

Two kinds of paper sit on this counter. On one side, the stamps of regulators. On the other, a guidebook written by learned societies, with star ratings. They answer different questions. A stamp says what may be sold, and for what. The stars say what the trials show. Keep them apart.

In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration has four main doors. The first is premarket approval, PMA, for the highest-risk devices, class three. The agency approves one only when the file holds sufficient valid scientific evidence that the device is safe and effective for its intended use.

The second door is the 510(k). Here the word is clearance, not approval. The maker shows substantial equivalence to a predicate: a legally marketed device with the same intended use, and no new questions of safety or effectiveness. The question is not, does it work? It is, is it like one already on sale?

The third is De Novo, for a novel device with no predicate. The agency classifies it into class one or class two.

The fourth is the Humanitarian Device Exemption, HDE, for conditions affecting not more than 8,000 people a year in the United States. It is exempt from the effectiveness requirements of the law. Sales for profit are capped by an annual distribution number: the devices needed to treat one person for a year, times 8,000.

In Canada, Health Canada sorts devices into four classes by risk. Class one needs no licence. Classes two, three and four cannot be sold without a medical device licence. For classes three and four, the application must contain evidence of clinical effectiveness. The public listing, MDALL, shows licensed devices of classes two to four, with the licence holder, licence number, device name and class. Its documented fields do not include the indication. That has to be read elsewhere.

In Europe, the mark is the CE mark, under Regulation 2017/745, the MDR. When a notified body is involved, the CE mark is followed by that body's identification number. Notified bodies and their certificates have a module in EUDAMED, the European database on devices. That module has been mandatory since the 28th of May, 2026.

Now, one real stamp, read line by line. It is a record in the FDA 510(k) database, number K260396, decided on the 6th of September, 2026. Status as of November 2026.

Line one, the door. A 510(k), decision: substantially equivalent. Line two, the device. A repetitive TMS system, class two, listed under 21 CFR 882.5805. Line three, the predicate. An earlier clearance, K230735, from the 13th of September, 2023.

Line four, the indication. Treatment of major depressive disorder in adult patients who have failed to receive satisfactory improvement from prior antidepressant medication in the current episode.

Line five, the protocol. The summary compares the settings with the predicate. 10 hertz. A hundred and twenty percent of motor threshold. 3,000 pulses a session. Five sessions a week, for six weeks, over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Same as the predicate.

Now read what is not on the stamp. No size of effect. No other condition. And nothing about Canada or Europe, which keep their own records.

Next, the guidebook. In 2020, Lefaucheur and a European expert panel graded the evidence for repetitive TMS, in Clinical Neurophysiology. Level A means definite efficacy. Level B, probable. Level C, possible. The grades rest on real-versus-sham differences, replicated in enough independent studies. High-frequency stimulation of the motor cortex for neuropathic pain reached Level A.

A level belongs to a condition and a protocol. It is read next to a stamp for that same condition, never instead of one. And it moves with the trials. The 2017 guideline on tDCS, by Lefaucheur and colleagues, gave no Level A to any indication. In 2021, Fregni and colleagues added a secondary meta-analysis, and placed depression at Level A. So read the edition and the year.

Here is the moment that matters. Approved and effective do not mean the same. An authorisation covers one indication, one device and one protocol, and it says a regulatory standard was met: equivalence, or reasonable evidence, depending on the door. An evidence level is read in trials and meta-analyses, and can change with the next paper. The two can disagree, in either direction. Neither is wrong. They measure different things. Meta-analyses and their biases are chapter thirteen.

So here is the grid. One: where? Which jurisdiction. Two: which door? Approval, clearance, De Novo, exemption, licence class, or CE mark with its notified body. Three: what exactly? Indication, device and protocol. Four: when? The date, and the edition. Five: what do the trials say? Read apart, in the guideline and its year.

To sum up. A status is a document with fields. Read them one by one, and keep the stamp and the stars apart.

Depression

Six depression treatments are read as separate records, each pairing a regulatory status with its sham-controlled trial: repetitive TMS, theta burst, accelerated imaging-guided TMS, electroconvulsive therapy, vagus nerve stimulation and tDCS, with deep brain stimulation still experimental. Effects range from modest to large, and a few came back close to the decoy. Trials differ in patients and scales, so the carts cannot be ranked.

TRANSCRIPTEN

First, one card. Educational content, not medical advice. This episode reads records. It does not compare treatments for anyone, and it never says what a person should choose.

Back at the border post, the longest queue is for depression. Six travellers wait at the counter. Each pulls a luggage cart, and each cart is weighed against a decoy twin of the same size. The decoy is the sham. Every status below is as of November 2026.

One warning before the first traveller. The six carts come from different trials, with different patients and different scales. Each one can be read alone. They cannot be lined up against each other.

Part one. Magnetic stimulation.

The first traveller is repetitive TMS over the left prefrontal cortex. In the United States, a first device came through the De Novo door in October 2008. Later 510(k) clearances read the same way: adults with major depressive disorder who have not improved enough on antidepressant medication. In Canada, the public listing shows class three licences for TMS therapy systems, for example from 2013 and 2019. It does not show the indication. For Europe, not checked.

Now the cart. In 2007, O'Reardon and colleagues randomised three hundred and one medication-free patients. Active stimulation beat sham on the main scale at week four, after a post hoc correction for an imbalance at baseline. Remission at week six was about twice as frequent, on two of the three scales used. In 2010, George and colleagues tested a carefully masked sham in a hundred and ninety patients. Remission reached fourteen point one percent with active stimulation, and five point one with sham. The number needed to treat was twelve. A real effect, and a modest one.

The second traveller is theta burst. A session lasts about three minutes, instead of thirty-seven and a half. In 2018, the THREE-D trial in Canada randomised four hundred and fourteen patients with treatment-resistant depression, to ten hertz stimulation or to intermittent theta burst. Scores on the seventeen-item Hamilton scale fell from about twenty-three and a half to thirteen and a half in both groups. Theta burst was non-inferior. Note what this is. It compares two active treatments. It is not a comparison with sham. In the United States, a theta burst system was cleared in August 2018, for the same adult indication.

The third traveller is the accelerated protocol, guided by imaging. Ten theta burst sessions a day, for five days. The target is chosen on each patient's resting-state MRI: the spot in the left prefrontal cortex most anticorrelated with the subgenual cingulate. In the United States, a 510(k) clearance came on the 1st of September, 2022, for adults with major depressive disorder who have not improved on prior antidepressant medication in the current episode. In Canada, no listing was found under that device name.

The sham-controlled trial, by Cole and colleagues, was published in 2022. At its planned interim analysis, fourteen patients had active treatment and fifteen had sham. Four weeks later, the depression score had dropped by fifty-two point five percent with active treatment, and eleven point one percent with sham. That is a small trial. A second sham-controlled trial of the same protocol, published in 2026, randomised forty-eight people. Remission at one month was fifty percent with active treatment, against twenty point eight with sham.

Part two. Implants, currents, and surgery.

The fourth traveller is electroconvulsive therapy, ECT. On the 26th of December, 2018, the FDA moved the ECT device from class three to class two, for one group of uses: catatonia, or a severe major depressive episode in major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder, in patients thirteen and older who are treatment-resistant or who need a rapid response. For every other use, a premarket approval is still required. Canada and Europe: not checked.

The cart is old, and heavy. In 2003, the UK ECT Review Group pooled six trials, with two hundred and fifty-six patients. Real ECT beat simulated ECT, with a standardised effect size of minus zero point nine one. Against drugs, eighteen trials gave minus zero point eight.

The fifth traveller is vagus nerve stimulation, VNS. In the United States, a premarket approval supplement was granted on the 15th of July, 2005. The indication is adjunctive, long-term treatment of chronic or recurrent depression, in adults in a major depressive episode, with an inadequate response to four or more adequate antidepressant treatments. In Canada, the listing shows a class four licence for a VNS therapy system, held since 1998. It does not show the indication.

Its cart has two parts. In the acute sham-controlled trial, in 2005, with two hundred and thirty-five patients, response at ten weeks was fifteen point two percent against ten percent, not significant. In 2017, a five-year registry followed seven hundred and ninety-five patients, without randomisation. Cumulative response was sixty-seven point six percent with VNS, against forty point nine with usual treatment. Without randomisation, that gap cannot prove cause. Since February 2019, Medicare covers VNS for depression only inside an approved randomised trial. That trial, RECOVER, is still recruiting.

The sixth traveller is transcranial direct current stimulation, tDCS. On the 8th of December, 2025, the FDA granted a premarket approval for a tDCS device for moderate to severe major depressive disorder in the current episode, alone or as an add-on, in adults not considered treatment refractory to medication. In Canada, tDCS stimulators are licensed, but the listing does not show an indication.

In 2017, the ELECT trial in Brazil randomised two hundred and forty-five patients. The Hamilton score fell by nine points with tDCS, and five point eight with sham. The difference was three point two points. The drug comparator did better, and tDCS was not shown to be non-inferior to it. In 2024, a fully remote ten-week trial, in a hundred and seventy-four patients, found nine point four points of improvement with active tDCS and seven point one with sham. It was a phase two trial.

One more traveller stands behind the line, without papers: deep brain stimulation for depression. Its status is experimental. Two sham-controlled trials were run. At the capsule and striatum target, thirty patients: response of twenty percent with active stimulation, fourteen point three with sham, no significant difference. At the subcallosal cingulate, ninety patients: twenty percent against seventeen, and the trial stopped at a futility analysis. Studies continue, and several are recruiting now.

Now the moment that matters, the guidebooks. In the Canadian guideline, CANMAT 2023, neuromodulation is generally considered after medication and psychotherapy have failed. TMS is usually recommended for treatment-resistant depression, and ECT for difficult-to-treat depression. ECT can come first in severe illness, for example with psychotic or catatonic features. tDCS was downgraded to third line, citing limited information on parameters and negative recent trials. VNS is third line. DBS is investigational.

In the United Kingdom, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence judged the evidence for TMS adequate in 2015. For tDCS, also in 2015, it asked for special arrangements. For VNS, in 2020, it asked for sham-controlled research. This is what the documents say. It is not a ranking, and it is not advice.

What stays open? First, who will respond. In 2026, an EEG study of the accelerated protocol suggested that beta power in the left anterior cingulate, measured before treatment, might predict benefit. One study, one hypothesis. Second, how long the effect lasts. In a naturalistic study of two hundred and fifty-seven patients after TMS, sixty-two point five percent of the hundred and twenty who first responded still did at one year. Third, maintenance. In that same study, more than a third of all patients received extra TMS sessions. How often, and with what, is still being tested.

To sum up. Six travellers, six stamps, six carts. A stamp says what may be sold, and for whom. A cart says what the trials found against sham. Some carts are heavy, some light, and a few came back about the same as the decoy. The stamp and the cart are read apart. And three questions are still open: who responds, for how long, and how to keep it.

OCD and addiction

Deep TMS holds a US authorisation for obsessive-compulsive disorder and a clearance for short-term smoking cessation, and implanted stimulation holds a humanitarian exemption for severe OCD. Trials show modest average gains over sham, and other addictions carry no stamp at all. A stamp states what a regulator allowed, not the size of the benefit.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Educational content, not medical advice. This chapter reads clinical statuses. It never recommends a treatment, and it never compares treatments for a viewer.

Welcome back to the border post. Two passports lie on the counter today. The first belongs to a machine that stimulates the brain from outside the head: deep TMS, a helmet built around an H-shaped coil. The second belongs to an implanted system: deep brain stimulation, or DBS. Both carry stamps for obsessive-compulsive disorder, and the first also carries one for smoking.

A word on how to read a page. A stamp states a regulatory status and nothing else: which regulator, which kind of decision, which exact indication, and when. How well a treatment works is written elsewhere, in the trials. Here, the two stay apart. Every status below is as of November 2026.

First page: deep TMS for OCD. In the United States, the FDA granted a De Novo authorisation in August 2018, as an adjunct for the treatment of adult patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder. In Canada, Health Canada lists an active licence whose title names OCD, first issued in May 2021. The database shows the title, not the licensed indication. In Europe, the manufacturer states that the system carries a CE mark covering OCD. No certificate was checked.

Now the trial behind that page. In 2019, Carmi and colleagues randomised ninety-nine adults with OCD, at eleven centres, to active or sham deep TMS. Sessions were daily for six weeks, each after a brief symptom-provocation. The Yale-Brown score fell by six points with active stimulation, and by three point three with sham. A response, a drop of at least thirty percent, was reached by thirty-eight percent of the active group and eleven percent of the sham group. One month later, the figures were forty-five and eighteen percent. So the average gain is modest, and most participants did not reach the response threshold at the end of treatment.

Second page: deep TMS for smoking cessation. This is a 510(k) clearance, K200957, dated the twenty-first of August 2020, for use as an aid in short-term smoking cessation for adults. The words short-term belong to the stamp. Health Canada lists an active licence with a matching title, first issued in October 2022. In Europe, the manufacturer lists a CE mark for smoking cessation too, with no certificate checked.

The trial was reported by Zangen and colleagues in 2021. It included two hundred sixty-two smokers: three weeks of daily sessions, then weekly sessions for three weeks, each after a craving cue. The main outcome was four consecutive weeks of abstinence, confirmed by urine cotinine. In the intention-to-treat set of two hundred thirty-four people, nineteen point four percent quit with active stimulation, and eight point seven percent with sham. The FDA review used all two hundred sixty-two participants, with a four-month window, and reports seventeen point one against seven point nine. Two analyses, two sets of people, the same direction.

Third page, a different kind of document. In the United States, DBS for OCD is a Humanitarian Device Exemption, number H050003, granted in February 2009. The indication is narrow. Bilateral stimulation of the anterior limb of the internal capsule, as an adjunct to medication and as an alternative to anterior capsulotomy, for chronic, severe, treatment-resistant OCD in adults who have failed at least three SSRIs. An exemption of this kind rests on probable benefit, not on demonstrated effectiveness, and the manufacturer's labelling says so. The record is still active, with supplements decided as recently as 2026.

In Canada, no licence titled for OCD was found. The database lists DBS systems without their indications, so the Canadian status is not determined. In Europe, the manufacturer announced a CE mark for the OCD indication in July 2009. The current certificate was not checked.

Now the page that matters most. Turn the deep TMS passport past OCD and smoking. The next pages are other addictions: alcohol, cocaine, methamphetamine, gambling. They carry no stamp. The same machine is authorised for one indication, and remains experimental for its neighbour. Among the FDA records searched, smoking is the only addiction with a transcranial magnetic stimulation entry, and no other addiction was found as of November 2026.

What do the studies say? For alcohol, a 2025 meta-analysis of twenty randomised trials found that brain stimulation lowered craving slightly more than sham. The standardised difference was minus point two one, with an interval from minus point three eight to minus point zero four. Only the direct-current subgroup was significant on its own. For cocaine and amphetamines, a 2019 meta-analysis of twelve studies reported a large pooled effect, with only high-frequency magnetic stimulation reaching significance. A pooled estimate from twelve studies is fragile. For gambling, a 2020 review of eleven studies, six of them controlled, could draw no firm conclusion. These are craving scores in research settings. A queue of trials is not an authorisation.

The classic trap is reading a stamp as a verdict. A stamp says what a regulator allowed, for whom, and when. It does not give the size of the benefit, and it says nothing about the next page of the passport.

To sum up. Deep TMS holds a De Novo authorisation for OCD from 2018, and a 510(k) clearance for short-term smoking cessation from 2020. DBS for severe, treatment-resistant OCD is a Humanitarian Device Exemption from 2009. Other addictions are experimental. Status in one place, evidence in another.

PLATE 12.410:00

Movement disorders

Deep brain stimulation for movement disorders has the longest record, from 1997, and is read like a mixing desk with one slider per symptom. Tremor, slowness, dyskinesia and drug dose stay improved for years, while posture, speech and freezing of gait decline, and cognition needs watching. Focused ultrasound lesions the same thalamic target without an implant, and each stamp covers one narrow indication.

TRANSCRIPTEN

First, one card. Educational content, not medical advice. This episode reads records. It does not compare treatments for any viewer.

Welcome to the border post. Movement disorders are the field where invasive stimulation has the longest record. The first United States approval for deep brain stimulation, DBS, dates from the 31st of July, 1997. That is almost thirty years, and it allows a precise question. Which symptoms improve, and which do not? Picture a mixing desk, with one slider for each symptom. Stimulation raises some sliders. It leaves others where they were.

First, where the electrode goes. Three targets, all deep, drawn here in profile and labelled illustrative. For tremor, the thalamus, at its ventral intermediate nucleus, VIM. For Parkinson's disease, the subthalamic nucleus, STN, or the internal globus pallidus, GPi. For dystonia, the GPi.

Now the stamps of the United States. They all belong to one record, premarket approval P960009, and each change is a supplement with its own date. In 1997: unilateral thalamic stimulation to suppress tremor of the upper extremity, in essential tremor or parkinsonian tremor not adequately controlled by medication.

On the 14th of January, 2002, supplement S007: bilateral stimulation of the GPi or the STN, as adjunctive therapy for some symptoms of advanced, levodopa-responsive Parkinson's disease. On the 17th of November, 2015, supplement S229 rewrote the wording: disease of at least four years' duration. On the 20th of February, 2025, supplement S478 added an optional feature, adaptive DBS. The system senses brain activity and adjusts the stimulation. The wording now names motor complications of recent onset, from four months to three years, or of longer standing.

Dystonia has its own papers. A humanitarian device exemption, H020007, was granted on the 15th of April, 2003, for long-term primary dystonia that does not respond to drug therapy, in patients at least seven years old. On the 22nd of November, 2025, supplement S482 added a premarket approval. Bilateral GPi stimulation, for chronic, intractable primary dystonia. In adults: generalized dystonia, segmental dystonia of the head and neck, and cervical dystonia. In children: generalized dystonia, aged twelve or above.

In Canada, the public listing shows DBS neurostimulators as class four devices with active licences. One example is licence 81337, first issued on the 27th of November, 2009. The listing does not show the indication, so none is stated here. For Europe, no record was checked.

Now, what the trials found. In 2006, Deuschl and colleagues compared STN stimulation with medication alone, in 156 patients under 75 with severe motor complications. At six months, stimulation had improved the motor score more than medication alone, by 19.6 points on average. Serious adverse events were more common with stimulation, 13 percent against 4 percent, and they included a fatal brain haemorrhage.

In 2009, Weaver and colleagues studied 255 patients. Stimulation gained 4.6 hours a day of good motor function without troubling dyskinesia. Medication alone gained none. At least one serious adverse event occurred in 49 stimulated patients, against 15. One patient died of a cerebral haemorrhage, and some tests of information processing showed small decrements.

In 2013, the EARLYSTIM trial, by Schuepbach and colleagues, enrolled 251 patients with motor complications of recent onset, mean age 52. At two years, quality of life improved by 7.8 points with stimulation, and worsened by 0.2 points with medication. Serious adverse events: 54.8 percent against 44.1 percent.

Now the long view, the moment that matters. Krack and colleagues followed the first 49 patients treated with STN stimulation for five years. Off medication, motor scores were 54 percent better than before surgery, and daily-living scores 49 percent better. Speech was the only motor function that did not improve off medication. Dyskinesia, and the dose of dopaminergic drugs, went down.

But on medication, akinesia, speech, postural stability and freezing of gait got worse between the first year and the fifth. There was no control group, and the authors read this worsening as consistent with the natural history of the disease. Cognitive scores were unchanged on average, yet dementia developed in three patients after the third year. One patient had a large brain haemorrhage. One died by suicide.

At ten years, Castrioto and colleagues rated the video of 18 patients, blind to the condition. Stimulation still improved the total motor score, tremor and bradykinesia, and the dose of levodopa stayed lower. The axial signs, posture and gait, declined the most. This is class three evidence, with 18 patients.

So on the mixing desk, some sliders stay up for years: tremor, slowness, dyskinesia, the drug dose. Others slide down over time: posture, speech, freezing of gait. And cognition is a slider to watch.

A pooled review in 2021 covered 39 STN studies and 2,035 patients, with data at six to twelve months. The motor score was 50.5 percent better. The most common surgery-related events were infection, 5.1 percent, and intracranial haemorrhage, 3.1 percent. Across studies, response to levodopa before surgery was highly predictive of the motor outcome.

Dystonia shows the same pattern. In a sham-controlled trial of 40 patients, Kupsch and colleagues found movement scores improved by 15.8 points with stimulation, against 1.4 with sham. All movement symptoms improved except speech and swallowing. There were 22 adverse events in 19 patients, including four infections. The most frequent was dysarthria. An earlier study of 22 patients, by Vidailhet and colleagues, saw the movement score fall from 46.3 to 21.0 at twelve months.

For essential tremor, a systematic review in 2010 found 430 patients treated with thalamic stimulation. Most adverse events were mild and could be handled by changing the settings. The evidence was level four, and the authors concluded that it is possibly safe and effective.

Now focused ultrasound, which makes a lesion in the same thalamic target, with no implant. The technique is episode 5.4. Here are the records. In 2016, Elias and colleagues randomised 76 patients with medication-refractory essential tremor, three to one, to thalamotomy or sham. Hand tremor scores went from 18.1 to 9.6 at three months with thalamotomy, and from 16.0 to 15.8 with sham. The difference was 8.3 points. At twelve months, the change from baseline was 7.2 points. Gait disturbance occurred in 36 percent of patients, and numbness or paresthesia in 38 percent. At twelve months, these persisted in 9 and 14 percent.

The stamps belong to premarket approval P150038. On the 11th of July, 2016: unilateral thalamotomy for medication-refractory essential tremor, age 22 or above. On the 16th of December, 2018, supplement S006: tremor-dominant Parkinson's disease with medication-refractory tremor, age 30 or above. On the 29th of October, 2021, supplement S014: unilateral pallidotomy for advanced Parkinson's disease with medication-refractory, moderate to severe motor complications.

On the 8th of December, 2022, supplement S022: the second side in essential tremor, at least nine months after the first. And on the 3rd of July, 2025, supplement S037: unilateral pallidothalamic tractotomy in advanced Parkinson's disease, with a staged second side at least six months later.

In Canada, licence 96969, class three, was first issued on the 17th of May, 2016. The listing does not show the indication. In Europe, the maker announced a CE mark in December 2012. No certificate record was checked.

To sum up. The record is long, from 1997 to 2026. Some sliders stay up for years. Others slide down, and they are the ones to read carefully. And each stamp is narrow: one record, one indication, one date.

PLATE 12.57:51

Epilepsy

Three implanted treatments exist for drug-resistant epilepsy: vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation and deep brain stimulation of the thalamus. Long-term series show high responder rates, meaning at least half fewer seizures, but much lower seizure-free shares, around a sixth to a fifth. Like notes peeled off a wall calendar, seizures are reduced and rarely brought to zero, in open-label series.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Educational content, not medical advice. This chapter reads clinical statuses. It never recommends a treatment, and it never compares treatments for a viewer.

Back at the border post, today's topic is epilepsy that medicines have not controlled. The International League Against Epilepsy defined it in 2010: failure of adequate trials of two tolerated, appropriately chosen anti-seizure drug schedules to achieve sustained seizure freedom. For some of these patients, three implanted approaches exist. Vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, and deep brain stimulation of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus. Before the three stamps, one question about the word success.

Picture a wall calendar, with one sticky note for every seizure in a month. This picture is illustrative, not patient data. A treatment peels some notes off the wall. Trials often draw a blue tape line at half. Fall below the line, a reduction of at least fifty percent, and the patient counts as a responder. The other finish line is a bare wall. No seizures at all, over a stated period. That is seizure freedom. These are two different questions, and a treatment can answer the first far more often than the second.

First, vagus nerve stimulation. In the United States, the FDA approved the system in July 1997, under PMA P970003. The January 1998 supplement in the FDA record states the indication: adjunctive therapy to reduce seizure frequency in adults and adolescents over twelve with partial-onset seizures that resist antiepileptic medications. Supplement S207, in June 2017, lowered the age to four years and older. In August 2026, supplement S249 changed the wording to focal-onset seizures. In Canada, Health Canada lists an active licence titled as a VNS system, first issued in August 1998. The database does not show indications. In Europe, the manufacturer reports CE marking for drug-resistant epilepsy, most recently announced in 2018. No certificate was checked.

Now the trial. In 1998, Handforth and colleagues compared high stimulation with low stimulation, in a double-blind study of one hundred ninety-six patients. After three months, seizures fell by an average of twenty-eight percent with high stimulation, and by fifteen percent with low. The difference was significant, with a p-value of zero point zero four. The control here was a low dose, not a sham.

Years of practice add a wider view. In 2011, Englot and colleagues pooled seventy-four studies, with three thousand three hundred twenty-one patients. Seizures fell by an average of forty-five percent. At the last follow-up, about half of the patients had a reduction of fifty percent or more. The authors add that complete seizure freedom is rarely achieved, and that a quarter of patients get no benefit.

Second, responsive neurostimulation. Episode six point four covered the device. The FDA approved it on the fourteenth of November 2013, under PMA P100026. The indication is adjunctive therapy for adults eighteen and older with partial-onset seizures, from no more than two foci, refractory to two or more medications, and frequent and disabling. In Canada, no licence was found under the names searched. In Europe, the status was not checked.

The numbers from episode six point four, in brief. In the blinded phase of the 2011 trial by Morrell, seizures fell by thirty-seven point nine percent with stimulation, and seventeen point three percent with sham. After nine years of open-label follow-up, Nair and colleagues reported a median reduction of seventy-five percent, and a responder rate of seventy-three percent. Eighteen point four percent, forty-seven of two hundred fifty-six, had at least one year without seizures. Of those forty-seven, twenty-nine were seizure-free at their last follow-up.

Third, deep brain stimulation of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus. In the United States, the FDA record shows supplement S219 to PMA P960009, dated the twenty-seventh of April 2018. It adds epilepsy as an indication: bilateral stimulation as an adjunctive therapy for adults eighteen and older with partial-onset seizures, with or without secondary generalization, refractory to three or more medications. The record states that effectiveness was shown in patients averaging six or more seizures a month. In Europe, the manufacturer announced a CE mark in September 2010, and no certificate was checked. In Canada, the licence records list active deep brain stimulation systems from this manufacturer. They show no indications, so the epilepsy indication is not determined.

The trial is called SANTE. In 2010, Fisher and colleagues randomised one hundred ten adults to stimulation or no stimulation, for three months, double-blind. Median seizure frequency fell by forty point four percent in the stimulated group, and by fourteen point five percent in the control group. Then everyone received stimulation. By two years, the median reduction was fifty-six percent. Fifty-four percent were responders, and fourteen patients had been seizure-free for at least six months.

In 2015, Salanova and colleagues reported five years. The median reduction was sixty-nine percent. The responder rate at five years was sixty-eight percent. And sixteen percent had been seizure-free for at least six months at some point in the five years.

Now the moment that matters. Put the numbers side by side, and one pattern appears in every long-term series. The responder rate is high. The seizure-free share is far lower. After nine years of responsive stimulation, the responder rate was seventy-three percent, while eighteen percent had a year free of seizures. After five years of thalamic stimulation, the responder rate was sixty-eight percent, while sixteen percent had six months free. These treatments reduce seizure frequency in a share of patients, rarely to zero.

A caution about these lines. The studies differ in design, definitions and patients. Six months free is not one year free. Nothing here is a ranking, and it is not a reading for any viewer. The long-term series are also open-label, with no control group, and their own authors classify them as class four evidence.

So here is the classic trap. A headline says that most patients respond. A responder still has seizures. Respond means at least half fewer. It does not mean none.

To sum up. Drug-resistant epilepsy has three implanted approaches with US statuses: VNS since July 1997, responsive neurostimulation since November 2013, and thalamic DBS since April 2018. A responder is a patient with at least fifty percent fewer seizures. Seizure freedom is no seizures over a stated period. These treatments lower seizure frequency in a share of patients, and rarely bring it to zero. Status in one place, evidence in another.

Pain, stroke and the experimental frontier

Pain, stroke, migraine and experimental indications are read as solid stamps or dotted outlines. Paired vagus nerve stimulation has an approval for stroke arm rehabilitation, several devices hold narrow migraine clearances, and motor cortex TMS for pain has the top guideline grade but no US stamp found. Welcome back to the border post. Today the counter is quiet, and the notice board is full. It holds applications that are still under review. A solid stamp means that a regulator decided something. A dotted outline means that no decision was found. Every status below is as of November 2026.

The board has four columns. Chronic pain. Stroke. Migraine. And the experimental frontier.

First column: chronic pain. Start with repetitive TMS of the motor cortex, for neuropathic pain. In 2020, Lefaucheur and a European expert panel graded high-frequency stimulation of the motor cortex as Level A, definite efficacy. That is a level of evidence, not a stamp. Four years earlier, the European Academy of Neurology guideline, by Cruccu and colleagues, gave only a weak recommendation, resting on evidence it called poor to moderate. Guidelines move with the trials.

Then came a multicentre trial. In 2021, Attal and colleagues randomised one hundred fifty-two adults with peripheral neuropathic pain, in four French clinics. Treatment was fifteen sessions of ten-hertz stimulation, over twenty-two weeks. Stimulation of the motor cortex reduced pain intensity more than sham, with a p value of point oh one. Stimulation of the prefrontal cortex did not, with a p value of point nine. Headache was the most common side effect, and it occurred in all three groups.

Now the stamps. In the United States, no record was found of a clearance for magnetic stimulation of the cortex for pain. One clearance does exist for a magnetic stimulator: five-ten-K, K250286, from July 2025. Its indication is to stimulate peripheral nerves, for relief of chronic intractable, post-traumatic and post-surgical pain, in adults. Same family of machine, different target. For implanted motor cortex stimulation, no approval for pain was found either, and clinical sources describe it as off-label. Canada and Europe were not checked for this column.

Second column: stroke. One item here carries a solid stamp. In the United States, a premarket approval, P210007, dated the twenty-seventh of August 2021. It covers an implanted vagus nerve stimulator, used during rehabilitation, to reduce upper-extremity motor deficits and improve motor function in patients with chronic ischemic stroke and moderate to severe arm impairment. In Canada, no licence with a matching title was found. Europe was not checked.

The trial behind it is called VNS-REHAB. Dawson and colleagues, in The Lancet in 2021, enrolled one hundred eight people, at nineteen services in the United Kingdom and the United States. All had weak arms, at least nine months after an ischaemic stroke, and all were implanted. Half received pulses paired with movement therapy. The other half received pulses of zero milliamps. After six weeks of therapy, the Fugl-Meyer arm score rose by five points with stimulation, and by two point four with sham. The difference was two point six points, with an interval from one to four point two. Ninety days later, a clinically meaningful response was seen in forty-seven percent, against twenty-four percent.

Next to it, two items without a stamp. First, tDCS in rehabilitation. The Cochrane review by Elsner and colleagues, in 2020, pooled sixty-seven studies and seventeen hundred twenty-nine people. For daily living, a small pooled effect appeared, of point two eight. It did not persist when only trials with proper allocation concealment were kept. For arm function, no effect was found. In the FDA records searched by name, no entry for tDCS in stroke rehabilitation was found.

Second, repetitive TMS. In 2018, Harvey and colleagues gave one-hertz, navigated stimulation to the uninjured motor cortex, before each of eighteen therapy sessions. Of one hundred ninety-nine people enrolled, sixty-seven percent of the active group and sixty-five percent of the sham group gained five points or more at six months, with a p value of point seven six. Rehabilitation helped both groups. The stimulation added nothing measurable. No FDA record was found for TMS in stroke rehabilitation.

Third column: migraine. One might expect dotted outlines here. The records say otherwise. This column holds solid stamps, each for a narrow indication.

Single-pulse TMS at the back of the head: a De Novo, DEN130022, December 2013, for the acute treatment of pain associated with migraine with aura. Then a five-ten-K, K162797, June 2017, for the acute and prophylactic treatment of migraine headache.

Non-invasive stimulation of the vagus nerve, on the neck: a De Novo, DEN150048, April 2017, for the acute treatment of pain associated with episodic cluster headache in adults. Then a five-ten-K, K173442, January 2018, adding migraine headache. Health Canada lists active licences for that device family, the first issued in August 2012. The database shows titles, not indications.

And remote electrical neuromodulation, a stimulator worn on a limb: a De Novo, DEN180059, May 2019, for the acute treatment of migraine with or without aura, in adults who do not have chronic migraine. No Canadian licence with a matching title was found for the TMS device or the limb stimulator. Europe was not checked.

Fourth column: the experimental frontier. Every outline here is dotted. Start with tinnitus. A 2020 meta-analysis pooled twenty-nine randomised studies, with twelve hundred twenty-eight people. Compared with sham, magnetic stimulation lowered the tinnitus handicap score by about eight and a half points at one month. The authors asked for larger multicentre trials. One arrived. In 2017, Landgrebe and colleagues compared one-hertz stimulation over the left temporal cortex with sham, in one hundred sixty-three patients across several centres. The adjusted difference on the tinnitus questionnaire was minus one point, with an interval from minus three point two to plus one point two. Real stimulation was not better than sham. In the FDA records, no entry for magnetic stimulation in tinnitus was found. One De Novo exists for a different technique: sound combined with electrical stimulation of the tongue, DEN210033, March 2023, for adults with at least moderate tinnitus.

Auditory hallucinations. A 2021 meta-analysis pooled twenty-seven magnetic stimulation studies, with nine hundred sixty patients. Compared with sham, no significant effect was found, with a p value of point two one. The authors concluded that efficacy cannot be established. No authorisation was found.

Cognitive disorders. A 2024 meta-analysis of twenty-five randomised trials, in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease, reported large pooled gains on the Mini-Mental State Examination, a standardised difference of point eight, with an interval from point two six to one point three three. Large pooled effects from small trials are a hypothesis. The best-known pivotal test did not confirm it. A system combining TMS with cognitive training for Alzheimer's went to an FDA advisory panel on the twenty-first of March 2019. The pivotal study, in one hundred six patients, did not demonstrate effectiveness, and the panel voted fourteen to zero against approval. No FDA record and no Canadian licence with a matching title were found. Europe was not checked.

Other indications wait in the same tray.

This is the pattern of the whole column. A promising small trial. A pooled estimate. Then a confirmatory trial that is missing, or that disagrees. Why early effects so often shrink is the subject of the next episode. Here, the rule is simpler: a dotted stamp, and its date.

The classic trap is reading a guideline level, a pooled effect or a promising pilot as an authorisation. A level, a pooled estimate and a stamp answer three different questions.

To sum up. Pain: Level A in one guideline, and no cortical stamp found in the United States. Stroke: one approval, for paired vagus nerve stimulation. Migraine: several narrow stamps. The frontier: dotted, and dated November 2026.

From pilot to approval: why most trials fall short

Promising stimulation results fade on the road from small open-label pilot to multicentre sham-controlled trial, like a shadow shortening as the sun climbs. Small samples, selection of strong pilots and imperfect blinding explain it. Deep brain stimulation, vagus nerve stimulation and TMS for tinnitus each show a large pilot effect followed by a small or null pivotal one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Educational content, not medical advice. This chapter reads clinical statuses. It never recommends a treatment, and it never compares treatments for a viewer.

Back at the border post, one last question for this chapter. Why do so many promising stimulation results fade? A small trial reports a large benefit. A larger trial reports a small one. This episode follows an indication along its road, and asks what shrinks at each stop.

Picture a post in a garden, with its shadow. The post is the effect that is really there. The shadow is what a study measures. At dawn, the sun is low, and the shadow is long. As the sun climbs, the shadow shortens, but the post never changes. This picture is illustrative, not data. Early studies stand at dawn. Better designed studies stand higher in the sky.

The road has four stops. First, a small open-label trial. A few patients, everyone knows they receive stimulation, and there is no control group. Second, a randomised sham-controlled trial in a single centre. Third, a multicentre randomised sham-controlled trial, large enough to be decisive. Fourth, the decisions: authorisation by a regulator, and a place in the guidelines.

In the United States, the regulator's own vocabulary follows the same road. An early feasibility study enrols a small number of subjects, often with a device that has not reached its final design. A traditional feasibility study captures preliminary safety and effectiveness data, again in a small number of subjects, often to prepare the next step. A pivotal study is designed to collect definitive evidence for a specified intended use, in a statistically justified number of subjects. For a significant-risk device, the FDA must approve the investigational device exemption, the I-D-E, before the study can begin. Pivotal results then support a marketing application.

Why does the shadow shorten? The first reason is small samples, and the winner's curse. Small studies scatter widely around the truth. The ones that cross the significance line, and get followed up, are the ones that happened to land high. In 2008, Ioannidis showed that when a discovery is claimed at a significance threshold and the study is underpowered, the observed effect is expected to be inflated. In 2013, Button and colleagues estimated the median statistical power of neuroscience studies at twenty-one percent, across forty-nine meta-analyses. A 2017 reanalysis found that power varies between subfields, and is extremely low in some of them.

Medical trials show the same pattern. In 2013, Dechartres and colleagues compared trials inside ninety-three meta-analyses, seven hundred thirty-five trials in all. Compared with the largest quarter, the smallest quarter showed treatment effects thirty-two percent larger. Trials of fewer than fifty patients showed effects forty-eight percent larger than trials of a thousand patients or more. In 2011, the same group found that single-centre trials show larger effects than multicentre trials, with a ratio of odds ratios of zero point seven three. And in 2012, Pereira and colleagues looked at very large effects. Ninety percent of those seen in a first trial became smaller once other trials were added. The median odds ratio fell from eleven point nine to four point two.

The second reason is selection. A pilot that looks weak is often dropped. A pilot that looks strong moves on, and its effect size is then used to plan the next trial. In 2006, Kraemer and colleagues cautioned that the most likely outcomes are that worthwhile studies are aborted, and that the others are underpowered.

The third reason is blinding. When patients and raters know who receives stimulation, ratings drift toward the hoped-for result. In 2008, Wood and colleagues pooled one hundred forty-six meta-analyses. For subjective outcomes, lack of blinding exaggerated effects, with a ratio of odds ratios of zero point seven five. For objective outcomes, the ratio was one point zero one.

Now, three stimulation stories. Each controlled trial is paired with the pilot that came before it. First, deep brain stimulation of the subcallosal cingulate, for treatment-resistant depression. In 2005, Mayberg and colleagues reported remission in four of six patients, in an open-label study. In 2008, Lozano and colleagues followed twenty patients. Six months after surgery, sixty percent were responders. Then came the randomised, sham-controlled trial, published in 2017 by Holtzheimer and colleagues. Of ninety participants, twelve of sixty, twenty percent, responded with stimulation, and five of thirty, seventeen percent, with sham. The difference was not significant. The response definitions differ a little between the studies, and the sham group improved too.

Second, vagus nerve stimulation for depression. In 2000, Rush and colleagues reported an open trial of thirty patients, with ten weeks of stimulation. Response rates were forty percent on the Hamilton scale, forty percent on the global impression, and fifty percent on the Montgomery-Asberg scale. In 2005, the randomised trial enrolled two hundred thirty-five patients. After ten weeks, the Hamilton response was fifteen point two percent with active stimulation, and ten percent with sham, with a p-value of zero point two five one. One secondary self-rating did favour stimulation, with seventeen percent against seven point three. The authors concluded that the trial did not yield definitive evidence of short-term efficacy.

Third, repetitive magnetic stimulation for chronic tinnitus. In 2005, Kleinjung and colleagues ran a crossover study of fourteen patients, with a sham coil. They found a highly significant improvement after five days of real stimulation, and none after sham. In 2017, Landgrebe and colleagues randomised one hundred sixty-three patients in a multicentre trial. After ten sessions, the tinnitus questionnaire scores of the two groups differed by one point, with a confidence interval from minus three point two to plus one point two. The authors noted the contrast with smaller studies.

Shrinking does not mean zero. Ioannidis noted that discovered effects are not always inflated. A negative pivotal trial does not prove that no patient benefits. It shows that the average difference against sham was too small to detect in that design. Each stop on the road answers a different question, and none replaces the others.

So a result can be read along the road. How many patients? Was there a control, and a sham? Who rated the outcome, and did they know the group? Has another centre found the same? Chapter thirteen goes further. Replications and meta-analyses pool the evidence, and publication bias and the placebo response distort it.

To sum up. Pilot effects are often larger than confirmatory effects, for three reasons: small samples, selection, and imperfect blinding. The road runs from open-label pilots to multicentre sham-controlled trials, then to authorisation and guidelines. At each stop, part of the early promise can shrink, and what remains is the estimate that counts.
