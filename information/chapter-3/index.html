KL divergence · The cost of the wrong map

The KL divergence is the average number of extra bits paid for using a code built for the wrong distribution, a wrong map. It is never negative, zero only for identical distributions, and independent of units. Mutual information is itself a KL divergence, Tort's modulation index borrows it, zeros in the map make it infinite and small samples bias it upward.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the Cartographer's Cabinet. This is Chapter 3 and our new home is a workshop full of maps. Some maps are accurate, some are old, some were drawn for a different country altogether. Today we measure what it costs to navigate with the wrong map. That cost is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and it sits underneath mutual information, classifiers, and many brain measures.

Start with intuition. Remember the telegraph codes of Episode 2.3. A good code gives short codewords to frequent symbols. But a code is built for a particular distribution. It is a map of which symbols are common and which are rare.

Now suppose the map is wrong. You built your code for one distribution, call it Q, but the messages actually come from another distribution, P. Frequent symbols may get long codewords, rare ones short codewords. On average, your messages will be longer than necessary.

How much longer? That is exactly the Kullback-Leibler divergence of P from Q, written KL of P given Q, or D of P parallel Q. It is the average number of extra bits you pay, per symbol, for using a code optimized for Q when the truth is P.

Here is the calculation in one picture. Take the telegraph source from Chapter 2, 1 half 1 quarter 1 eighth 1 eighth. Its entropy is 1.75 bits, and the ideal code reaches it. Now use the wrong map, a code designed for four equally likely symbols, two bits each. On the true source, every message costs two bits per symbol. The extra cost is 2 minus 1.75, 0.25 bits per symbol. That is the KL divergence of the true source from the uniform map.

The formula behind it. For each outcome, the ideal codeword under the true distribution has length log of 1 over p. Under the wrong map, log of 1 over q. The penalty for that outcome is the difference, log of p over q. Average the penalty over the true distribution and you get the divergence, the sum over outcomes of p times log of p over q.

The quantity was introduced by Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler in 1951, in a paper on information and sufficiency in statistics. With the natural logarithm, it is measured in nats, with log base 2 in bits like every quantity in this series.

Read it as an expected surprise difference. For each outcome, how much more surprised is someone holding the wrong map compared with someone holding the right one, averaged over what really happens?

Now the most important property. The KL divergence is never negative. It is zero only when the two distributions are identical. This is called Gibbs' inequality, after the physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs. It is the foundation of everything in this chapter. In coding terms, it says something you would hope for. No wrong map can beat the right one on average. You cannot save bits by believing something false.

The proof is short, and it comes from the shape of the logarithm. The log is a concave function, so the average of a log is at most the log of an average. Apply that to q over p, averaged under p, and the divergence comes out greater than or equal to zero, with equality only when q equals p everywhere.

For continuous variables, the sum becomes an integral of p times log of p over q, with densities in place of probabilities. And here comes good news after the traps of the last chapter. Because the divergence is built from a ratio of two densities, a change of unit stretches both densities by the same factor, and the factor cancels. Microvolts or volts, the divergence between two distributions of the same signal is the same. Unlike differential entropy, the KL divergence does not depend on the ruler. The same holds for any smooth, invertible transformation applied to both distributions.

Let's bring it to the lab. Suppose alpha power in short windows is classified as low, medium, or high. With eyes closed, an illustrative distribution is 10, 30, and 60%. With eyes open 60, 30, and 10. The KL divergence of the eyes closed distribution from the eyes open one is about 1.29 bits. Each window recorded with eyes closed is, on average, about 1.3 bits more surprising to an observer who expects eyes open.

And here is a measure you may already use without knowing it. The modulation index of Adriano Tort and colleagues, introduced in 2010 for phase-amplitude coupling, works like this. Cut the phase of a slow rhythm into bins, 18 bins of 20 degrees in their paper. In each bin, average the amplitude of a fast rhythm. Normalize those averages so they sum to 1, like a distribution. If there is no coupling, the amplitude is the same in every phase bin, and this distribution is flat. The modulation index is the KL divergence of the observed distribution from the flat one, divided by the log of the number of bins, so that it lies between 0 and 1.

Tort and colleagues point out one subtlety themselves. This normalized amplitude profile has the properties of a distribution, but it is not the distribution of a random variable. It borrows the divergence as a measure of how far a profile is from flat.

What is the KL divergence sensitive to?

First its direction. KL of P from Q is in general not equal to KL of Q from P. Which distribution plays truth and which plays map changes the number, sometimes dramatically. That is the subject of the next episode.

Second zeros. If the map Q assigns zero probability to something that actually happens under P, the penalty for that outcome is infinite, and so is the divergence. A map that declares a road impossible, when the traveller actually takes it, is infinitely wrong. This is the same explosion we met with surprise in episode 2.1, now averaged over a whole distribution. Conversely, if P is zero somewhere, that outcome contributes nothing, whatever Q says.

Third estimation. Computed from histograms, the divergence inherits all the problems of chapter 1. With few samples, empty bins in the estimate of q create spurious zeros and therefore huge or infinite values. And even without zeros, the plug-in estimate of the divergence between two samples from the same distribution comes out positive, on average. Finite samples always look a little different from each other.

Here is the classic pitfall. Two conditions are compared with a KL divergence or with a modulation index computed from different numbers of trials or windows. The condition with fewer samples has noisier histograms and therefore a larger divergence from flat even with no real difference. This is why the modulation index is usually compared to surrogate data, built by shifting the amplitude relative to the phase and why sample sizes should be matched. Chapter 6 returns to surrogates in detail.

One more connection, which will matter for the whole series. Mutual information is itself a KL divergence, the divergence of the true joint distribution from the product of the marginals. It is the cost of using a map that assumes independence when the variables are in fact dependent. We will build on that in chapter 4.

The companion notebook computes the telegraph example, the alpha example, and a modulation index on simulated phase-amplitude coupling, and shows how the plug-in divergence between two samples of the same distribution shrinks with the number of samples, but never quite reaches zero.

To recap, the Kullback-Leibler divergence of P from Q is the sum of P times log of P over Q, the average extra cost, in bits, of using a map built for Q when the truth is P. It is never negative and zero only when P equals Q, Gibbs' inequality. It explodes when the map assigns zero to something real. Tort's modulation index is a normalized divergence from the flat distribution. And mutual information is the divergence of a joint distribution from independence.

KL is not a distance

The KL divergence is a divergence, not a distance: it is not symmetric and it breaks the triangle inequality, as two coins and a detour through a third coin show. The direction chosen decides the result. Fitting one Gaussian to a two-bump distribution covers both bumps in the forward direction and locks onto a single bump in the reverse.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cartographer's cabinet, with a map and a territory that refused to be swapped. Last time, the Kullback-Leibler divergence measured the cost of using the wrong map. It is often called a distance between distributions. Today we see why that word is misleading, and why you must always say in which direction you computed it.

Start with intuition. A distance between two cities is the same in both directions. Paris to Lyon equals Lyon to Paris. And going through a third city can never be shorter than going directly. Those two properties, symmetry and the triangle inequality, are what make a distance a distance. The KL divergence has neither.

First symmetry. Here is the calculation in one picture. Take a fair coin 50-50 and a biased coin 90-10. The divergence of the fair coin from the biased one is about 0.74 bits. The divergence of the biased coin from the fair one is about 0.53 bits. Same two coins, two different numbers. Convert to nats multiplying by the natural log of 2, and both numbers shrink by the same factor. The asymmetry does not depend on the unit. It is built into the definition.

Why? Because the two directions ask different questions. KL of P from Q averages the penalty over P. It asks how badly the map Q serves a traveller who really follows P. If the truth is the fair coin, tails happens half the time and the biased map says tails is rare. Every tail is a costly surprise. If the truth is the biased coin, tails is rare, so the map's mistakes about tails rarely matter.

Second, the triangle inequality. Take three coins, 50-50, 75-25, and 90-10. Going directly from the first to the last costs about 0.74 bits. Going through the middle coin, the two steps add up to only about 0.34 bits. The detour is shorter than the direct road. No distance behaves like that.

In the lab, the asymmetry appears as soon as one condition serves as the reference. Take the alpha distribution from last time, low, medium, or high power with illustrative values. At rest 10, 30, and 60%. During a task 50, 40, and 10 percent. The divergence of the rest distribution from the task one is about 1.19 bits. The divergence of the task distribution from the rest one is about 1.07 bits. Neither is the true value. They are two different questions, how poorly the task describes rest and how poorly rest describes the task.

So the KL divergence is a divergence, not a distance. It measures a directed mismatch, how wrong one distribution is as a description of another.

The direction matters most when we fit a simple model to a complicated distribution. Here is the classic illustration. The true distribution P has two bumps, one around minus 2 and one around plus 2. We want to approximate it with a single Gaussian Q, and we choose Q by minimizing a KL divergence. Which direction?

Minimize KL of P from Q, the forward direction, and the penalty is averaged over the truth. Wherever P has mass and Q does not, the cost is large. So Q is forced to cover both bumps. The best Gaussian sits in the middle, wide enough to span both, with a standard deviation of about 2.1. It puts most of its mass in the valley between the bumps, where the truth has almost none. This direction is called mass-covering.

Minimize KL of Q from P, the reverse direction, and the penalty is averaged over the model. Now the cost is large wherever Q puts mass and P does not, such as the valley. So Q avoids the valley and locks onto one of the bumps, narrow and well-fitted, ignoring the other bump entirely. This direction is called mode-seeking.

Same family, same truth, two very different answers. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions. Methods in machine learning and in models of the brain often choose one direction implicitly. Variational inference, for instance, typically minimizes the reverse direction, with the model's approximation in the first slot. That choice shapes what the approximation captures and what it misses.

What is this sensitive to? To direction as we have seen. And to supports. The forward divergence is infinite whenever the model gives zero probability to something that happens. The reverse divergence is infinite whenever the model gives probability to something impossible. The two directions break in opposite situations.

Here is the classic pitfall. A paper reports a KL divergence between two conditions, say rest and task, without saying which distribution sat in which slot. A reader recomputes it the other way, gets a different number, and cannot reproduce the result. Or a matrix of divergences between many conditions is fed into a clustering method that expects distances, and the method quietly assumes a symmetry that is not there.

The fixes are simple. Always write the divergence with its direction, KL of task from rest, for example, with the true or reference distribution stated. If a symmetric measure is genuinely needed, choose one explicitly. You can add the two directions together, which gives the Jeffreys divergence. Or you can use the Jensen-Shannon divergence which is symmetric, bounded, and whose square root is a true metric. That is the subject of episode 3.4.

The companion notebook computes both directions for the coins, checks the triangle inequality failure, and fits a single Gaussian to two bumps by minimizing each direction, so you can watch the mass-covering and mode-seeking solutions appear.

To recap, a distance is symmetric and obeys the triangle inequality. The KL divergence does neither. KL of P from Q averages over P. KL of Q from P averages over Q. They answer different questions and give different numbers. Fitting with the forward direction covers all the mass. With the reverse direction, it seeks a single mode. Always report the direction and choose a symmetric measure deliberately when you need one.

Cross-entropy · The log-loss behind every classifier

Cross-entropy is the average surprise under the wrong map and equals entropy plus KL divergence, so a classifier's log-loss is a divergence in disguise. In an EEG decoder, chance sits at two bits for four balanced classes, and only held-out cross-entropy gives a lower bound on mutual information, because training loss can be memorized.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cartographer's cabinet, in a map that most of you have already drawn without knowing it. Every time a classifier is trained with a log-loss or cross-entropy loss, it is minimizing a Kullback-Leibler divergence. Today we see exactly how and what the numbers of a training curve mean in bits.

Start with intuition. Last time, a traveller following the true distribution P used a map Q. The cost per step in bits was the cost of the right map plus the penalty for the wrong one. The total the full cost of navigating with Q has a name of its own, the cross-entropy of P and Q.

Here is the calculation in one picture. The cross-entropy is the sum over outcomes of P times log of 1 over Q. It is the average surprise of the outcomes measured with the wrong map. And it splits exactly into two parts. The entropy of P, which no map can avoid, plus the KL divergence of P from Q, the penalty for the wrong map. Cross-entropy equals entropy plus divergence.

Since the entropy of P does not depend on Q, minimizing the cross-entropy over Q is the same as minimizing the divergence. The best possible map is P itself where the divergence vanishes and the cross-entropy reaches its floor, the entropy.

Now to classifiers. Suppose we train a decoder to read from a short window of EEG which of four images a participant is looking at. For each trial the decoder outputs a distribution over the four classes, its map. The truth for that trial is a single class with probability 1.

The loss for that trial is the surprise of the true class under the decoder's map, log of 1 over the probability the decoder gave to the right answer. If the decoder said 70% for the correct image, the loss is log base 2 of 1 over 0.7, about 0.51 bits. If it said 10%, the loss is about 3.32 bits. If it said 100%, the loss is 0.

Average over trials, and you get the cross-entropy between the true labels and the decoder's predictions. For a single trial with a certain label, the entropy part is zero, so the loss is entirely divergence, the penalty for not having put all the probability on the truth.

What does chance look like? A decoder that knows nothing and spreads its probability evenly over four classes always gives a quarter to the truth. Its loss is log base 2 of 4, 2 bits per trial. That is the ceiling to beat. Any decoder whose held-out loss sits at 2 bits has learned nothing usable about the images.

So a training curve in bits reads naturally. Start near 2 bits. Every bit shaved off is a bit of uncertainty about the image that the decoder has learned to remove, using the EEG.

There is a deeper link with information. If the cross-entropy is measured on new, held-out trials, then the entropy of the labels minus that cross-entropy is a lower bound on the mutual information between the labels and the EEG features the decoder uses. With four balanced classes, a held-out loss of 1.5 bits guarantees at least half a bit of information. This bound is due to David Barber and Felix Agakov in 2003, and we will use it again with variational estimators in Chapter 5. A decoder bounds information from below, it does not measure it exactly. Chapter 12 returns to that distinction.

What is cross-entropy sensitive to?

First, the base of the logarithm. Deep learning libraries use the natural logarithm, so their losses are in nats. Divide by the natural log of 2, about 0.693 to get bits. A chance-level loss of 1.386 in nats is 2 bits for 4 classes.

Second, the class balance. Chance level is the entropy of the labels, not always log of the number of classes. With unbalanced classes, say 70% of one class, a decoder can lower its loss just by learning the proportions, without looking at the EEG at all. Always compare to the entropy of the actual label distribution.

Third, confident mistakes. A decoder that gives nearly zero probability to the true class pays an enormous loss on that trial, because the surprise of a near-impossible event is huge. One confident error can dominate the average. Implementations clip probabilities away from zero for exactly this reason, which means the reported loss also depends on that clipping.

Here is the classic pitfall. A training loss is reported as evidence of how much information the brain's signal carries. But training loss is measured on the same trials the decoder learned from, and a flexible decoder can memorize them. Its training loss can fall far below the entropy of the labels while its loss on new trials stays at chance. Only held-out cross-entropy from proper cross-validation supports any claim about information. And even then it is a lower bound, limited by the decoder's architecture and by the amount of training data.

Here is a fictional training log to practice on. Epoch 1, training loss 1.98 bits, held-out loss 1.99. Epoch 50, training loss 0.4 bits, held-out loss 1.7. What can we conclude? The training curve says nothing about the brain. The decoder has partly memorized its training trials. The held-out loss says that, with this decoder and this much data, the EEG features carry at least about 0.3 bits about the image out of two. That is a modest but honest statement. The large gap between the two curves is a warning about overfitting, not a discovery.

The same logic applies far beyond classifiers. Encoding models that predict neural responses or models of spike trains are often compared by their log-likelihood on held-out data. Expressed in bits per trial or bits per spike, that log-likelihood is a cross-entropy, and differences between models are differences in average surprise.

A useful translation to finish. The quantity 2 to the power of the cross entropy in bits is called the perplexity. It reads as an effective number of equally likely options the decoder is still hesitating between. Two bits of loss means a perplexity of four. The decoder is as confused as a fair four-sided die. One bit means two options left.

The companion notebook trains a simple classifier on simulated features, plots training and held-out cross-entropy in bits, compares them with chance, and computes the lower bound on mutual information.

To recap, cross-entropy is the average surprise under the wrong map, the sum of p times log 1 over q. It equals entropy plus KL divergence, so minimizing it minimizes the divergence. A classifier's log-loss is a cross-entropy. Chance is the entropy of the labels, two bits for four balanced classes. Held-out cross-entropy gives a lower bound on mutual information. Watch the log base, the class balance, confident errors, and the difference between training and held out loss.

Jensen–Shannon and the f-divergence family

The Jensen-Shannon divergence compares two distributions to their mixture, which makes it symmetric, always finite and capped at one bit. It equals the mutual information between a draw and the hidden fair coin that chose its source, and its square root is a true metric. KL and Jensen-Shannon belong to the f-divergence family, so the member used must be named.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cartographer's cabinet, to meet a gentler cousin of the KL divergence. It is symmetric, it never explodes, and it hides a beautiful connection to mutual information. Then we step back and see that the KL divergence belongs to a whole family, the f-divergences.

Start with intuition. The KL divergence compares a territory with a map, and it can be infinite when the map rules out something that happens. What if, instead of judging one distribution by the other, we compared both to a compromise between them?

That is the Jensen-Shannon divergence, introduced in this form by Jianhua Lin in 1991. Take the two distributions P and Q. Build their average the mixture M halfway between them. Compute the KL divergence of P from M and of Q from M. Average the two. That is the Jensen-Shannon divergence.

Here is the calculation in one picture with our two coins from last time, 50-50 and 90-10. The mixture is 70-30. The divergence of each coin from the mixture is small, and their average is about 0.147 bits. Swap the two coins, and you get exactly the same number. The construction is symmetric by design, and it never explodes. The mixture puts probability wherever either distribution does, so neither P nor Q can ever fall where the mixture is zero. The KL divergences inside the formula are always finite.

In fact, the Jensen-Shannon divergence is bounded. Measured in bits, it lies between zero and one. Zero when the two distributions are identical. One bit exactly when they do not overlap at all. When every outcome possible under P is impossible under Q and the reverse. In that situation, the KL divergence would be infinite in both directions. Jensen-Shannon simply says, one bit.

Why one bit? Here is the beautiful connection. Imagine a game. Flip a fair coin. On heads draw an outcome from P, on tails draw it from Q. Show me only the outcome and ask me to guess which side the coin landed on. The mutual information between the outcome and the hidden coin is exactly the Jensen-Shannon divergence between P and Q.

If P and Q are identical, the outcome tells me nothing about the coin, zero bits. If they never overlap, the outcome tells me the coin with certainty, one full bit, the entropy of a fair coin. Everything in between measures how well the two distributions can be told apart from a single draw. That is why the Jensen-Shannon divergence leads straight into chapter 4.

One more property makes it useful in practice. Its square root is a true metric. It is symmetric and it obeys the triangle inequality, a result shown by Dominik Endres and Johannes Schindelin and independently by Ferdinand Österreicher and Igor Vajda in 2003. So when you need a genuine distance between distributions for clustering or for maps of conditions, the square root of the Jensen-Shannon divergence is a safe choice.

Back in the lab, with the illustrative alpha distributions of the last episode, at rest and during a task, the two KL divergences were about 1.19 and 1.07 bits, depending on the direction. The Jensen-Shannon divergence is about 0.25 bits, the same in both directions, and its square root about 0.5.

Now step back. KL, reverse KL, and Jensen-Shannon are all members of one family, defined independently by Imre Csiszár and by Syed Ali and Samuel Silvey in the 1960s. Pick a convex function f with f of 1 equal to 0. Average f of the ratio p over q, weighted by q. That is the f-divergence of p from q.

Different choices of f give the famous members. The KL divergence. The reverse KL. The total variation distance, half the sum of absolute differences, 0.4 for our two coins. The chi-squared divergence, familiar from goodness-of-fit tests. The squared Hellinger distance. And Jensen-Shannon. Why group them? Because they share important properties. Every f-divergence is zero when the distributions are equal and positive otherwise. And every f-divergence obeys a data processing inequality. Passing both distributions through the same noisy channel, the same filter, or the same coarse binning can only make them harder to tell apart, never easier. We will meet the version of that inequality for mutual information in episode 4.5.

The family also matters for estimation. In chapter 5, variational estimators of mutual information are built on dual representations of these divergences, using neural networks as critics. The same mathematics drives generative adversarial networks in machine learning, whose original training objective is linked to the Jensen-Shannon divergence.

What are these quantities sensitive to? Each member weights differences differently. Total variation looks at the largest gap in probability. Chi-squared punishes small q heavily. KL is in between and explodes at zeros. Jensen-Shannon saturates at one bit, so it cannot tell apart two pairs of distributions that are both completely disjoint. Choose the member that matches your question, and report which one you used.

Here is the classic pitfall. A paper reports a divergence between brain states, calls it the divergence, and a reader assumes it is KL when it was Jensen-Shannon or the reverse. Numbers from different members are not comparable. 0.25 bits of Jensen-Shannon and 1.19 bits of KL can describe the very same pair of distributions. Always name the divergence, its direction if it has one, and its unit.

The companion notebook computes Jensen-Shannon for the coins and the alpha example, checks the bound of 1 bit for disjoint distributions, verifies that it equals the mutual information of the coin game by simulation, and computes several f-divergences side by side.

To recap, the Jensen-Shannon divergence averages the KL divergences of P and Q from their mixture. It is symmetric, always finite, and between 0 and 1 bit. It equals the mutual information between a draw and a hidden fair coin that chose P or Q. Its square root is a metric. KL, reverse KL, total variation, chi-squared, Hellinger, and Jensen-Shannon are all f-divergences, sharing positivity and the data processing inequality. Name the member you use.

Surprise in the brain · What is Shannon and what is not

Three quantities hide behind the word surprise: Shannon surprise measures how improbable an event is, Bayesian surprise measures how far beliefs move, and free energy adds a KL penalty for approximate inference to Shannon surprise. EEG studies test them as trial-by-trial regressors, and results depend on the learning model assumed. The episode takes no side in the debates.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Part 1

Last episode in the cartographer's cabinet and the most delicate one. The word surprise appears all over cognitive neuroscience. Surprise signals, prediction errors, the brain as a surprise-minimizing machine. But at least three different quantities hide behind that one word. Today we lay them side by side, say what each computes and what each assumes, and take no side in the debates around them.

Start with intuition. You are walking home along your usual street and a red car passes. Two different things can happen inside you. First, the event can be unlikely under what you expected. Red cars are rare on this street. That is surprise as improbability. Second, the event can change what you believe. Perhaps a new neighbour has moved in and you update your picture of the street. That is surprise as belief change. These are not the same. A rare event can leave your beliefs untouched if you are very sure of them. And a modest event can shift your beliefs a lot if you are unsure.

Some authors call the first kind puzzlement and the second enlightenment. The terms come from work by Mohammadjavad Faraji and colleagues, whom we will meet again in a moment. Each has a precise formula.

The first is Shannon's surprise, the quantity of chapter 2. Under a model that predicts the next observation, the surprise of what actually happened is minus the log of its predicted probability. It depends only on how probable the observation was before it arrived.

The second is Bayesian surprise, defined by Laurent Itti and Pierre Baldi in 2005. An observer holds beliefs about the world, a probability distribution over hypotheses, the prior. An observation arrives, and Bayes' rule turns the prior into a posterior. Bayesian surprise is the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the posterior from the prior, how far the beliefs moved. Baldi and Itti even proposed a unit for it, the wow, by analogy with the bit.

Here is the calculation in one picture. An oddball sequence, an observer learns the probability of a deviant tone, and currently believes it is about 20%. A deviant arrives.

The Shannon surprise is minus log base 2 of 0.2, about 2.32 bits. Now the Bayesian surprise. It depends on how firmly the observer holds its belief. Suppose the belief rests on the equivalent of only 10 past tones. Then one deviant shifts it noticeably, and the Bayesian surprise is about 0.26 bits. Suppose instead it rests on 100 past tones. One more deviant barely moves anything, and the Bayesian surprise drops to about 0.03 bits.

Same tone, same predicted probability, same Shannon surprise. Bayesian surprises that differ by a factor of almost 10. The two quantities answer different questions. Baldi and Itti also showed that, during sequential learning, Bayesian surprise tends to decrease roughly as one over the number of observations, while Shannon surprise does not have to. A rare event stays improbable even after a lifetime of experience.

The third quantity is variational free energy, central to the free energy principle of Karl Friston. Here the observer has a generative model, a model of how hidden causes produce observations. Computing the exact posterior is usually intractable, so the observer uses an approximation, Q. Free energy can be written as the Shannon surprise of the observation under the generative model, plus the KL divergence between the approximate posterior Q and the true posterior.

Since a KL divergence is never negative, free energy is always greater than or equal to the Shannon surprise. It is an upper bound on it. When the approximate posterior is exact, the bound is tight and free energy equals Shannon surprise. Minimizing free energy therefore does two things at once. It improves the approximate posterior and it pushes down a bound on surprise.

A small numerical illustration helps. Suppose the Shannon surprise of an observation under the generative model is 3 bits. If the observer's approximate posterior is exactly right, its free energy is 3 bits too. If the approximate posterior is off by half a bit of KL divergence from the true one, its free energy is 3.5 bits. The extra half bit is the price of approximate inference. Learning to infer better lowers free energy without changing the world at all.

Here is the table to keep. Shannon surprise, computed from the predictive probability of one observation, requires a predictive model, measures improbability. Bayesian surprise, computed from the change between prior and posterior beliefs, requires a model of beliefs and a way to update them, measures belief change. Free energy, computed from a generative model and an approximate posterior, requires both, bounds Shannon surprise from above and is also used as a principle of brain function.

Part 2

What does this mean for experiments? Each quantity computed trial by trial gives a different regressor for the same data. That makes them testable against each other.

Several EEG studies have done exactly that. Dirk Ostwald and colleagues, in 2012, used a somatosensory roving paradigm and single-trial modelling, and reported that a response around 140 ms in secondary somatosensory cortex was better described by Bayesian surprise than by simple stimulus change. Later work from the same group, by Sam Gijsen, Miro Grundei, and colleagues in 2021, compared several surprise functions in 40 participants and reported an earlier signature resembling a confidence-corrected surprise and a later one resembling Bayesian surprise. Other studies of the P300 favoured predictive Shannon-type surprise. The picture depends on the modality, the time window, the paradigm, and crucially on the learning model assumed. That confidence-corrected surprise belongs to a further family, proposed by Faradji and colleagues in 2018, which takes into account how confident the observer was, not only how improbable the event was. The menu of surprises keeps growing, which is one more reason to say precisely which one you computed.

That last point deserves emphasis. A Bayesian surprise is only defined once you have chosen in how the observer represents the world and how it learns. Which hypotheses, which prior, how fast old evidence is forgotten. Seemingly small differences in these choices can produce very different surprise regressors, and therefore different conclusions, as Ostwald's group has itself pointed out. The model is part of the claim.

What is all this sensitive to? To the learning model first, memory length, prior, hypothesis space. To the observation that counts as an event. And to confounds that every surprise regressor shares with simpler explanations, such as adaptation, the time since the last deviant, or the physical difference between stimuli. A good design, such as a roving paradigm, makes the deviant and the standard physically identical on average, so that only history distinguishes them.

The mismatch negativity offers a good example of why this matters. For years, two accounts have been debated—that it reflects a prediction error, a surprise signal in some sense, or that it reflects adaptation of neurons responding to the repeated standard, which simply respond less. Marta Garrido and colleagues reviewed this debate in 2009 and argued that both mechanisms may contribute. Whichever way the evidence points, it is the comparison between explicit models that carries the weight, not the vocabulary.

Here is the classic pitfall. A study reports a prediction error or a surprise response and moves freely between the three meanings. It computes Shannon surprise, interprets the effect as belief updating, and concludes in favour of the free energy principle. Each step changes the quantity. Shannon surprise is not Bayesian surprise, neither is free energy, and finding that a brain signal tracks one of them does not, on its own, confirm a theory of brain function. Say which quantity you computed under which model and what it was compared against.

A word on the debates. The free energy principle, predictive coding, and the Bayesian brain hypothesis are active areas of research, with strong supporters and serious critics. Some critics argue that the free energy principle is so general that it cannot be falsified. Supporters answer that it is a framework from which specific and testable process models are derived. This series takes no side. What it offers is the vocabulary to tell the quantities apart so that the debates can be about the brain and not about the meaning of a word.

To recap, Shannon surprise measures improbability minus log of the predicted probability. Bayesian surprise measures belief change, the KL divergence of the posterior from the prior and it shrinks as beliefs firm up. Free energy adds a KL penalty for an approximate posterior to Shannon surprise and so bounds it from above. Each needs a model and each gives different trial-by-trial predictions.
