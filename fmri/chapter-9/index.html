How many people do you need?

Power depends on effect size, noise and sample size. Task activations are often cliffs that few people can detect, while brain-behavior correlations across people are meadows: a true correlation of 0.1 needs close to 800 people. Small studies miss real effects and inflate those they find, so realistic effect sizes, power analysis, pooling or within-person designs help.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the last chapter. We are at the foot of the mountain and the question for this whole chapter is simple. Will your result hold up? First stop how many people do you need?

Picture two hikers with noisy altimeters. The first stands at the foot of a cliff. Three readings at the bottom, three at the top and the difference is obvious. The second walks up a gentle meadow. Each reading jumps up and down by a few meters, much more than the slope itself. With three readings, she might conclude the meadow goes downhill. With thousands, the gentle slope finally shows.

That is statistical power, the chance that a study detects an effect that is really there. It depends on three things. The size of the effect, the amount of noise, and the number of measurements. A cliff needs few readings. A meadow needs many.

Let us draw a power curve. Suppose the true correlation between a brain measure and a trait is 0.5, a cliff. About 30 people give you an 80% chance of detecting it. Now suppose the true correlation is 0.1, a meadow. You need close to 800 people for the same 80%.

So which kind of effect does fMRI usually chase? It depends on the question. A robust task activation, like the visual cortex responding to a flashing checkerboard, is a cliff. It shows up in almost everyone, and a modest group is enough.

But many studies ask a different question, do people who differ in a trait like anxiety or intelligence also differ in their brains? These are called brain-wide association studies. In 2022, Marek and colleagues pooled three of the largest datasets, about 50,000 people in total, to measure how big these associations really are. The answer, very small. The strongest reproducible correlations were only around 0.1 or a bit more, and most were far smaller. And the median published neuroimaging study had about 25 participants.

What happens when you hunt a meadow with 25 people? Two things. Most real effects are missed, and the few that do cross the significance threshold are exactly the ones where the noise happened to push the estimate up. So published effects are inflated. This is sometimes called the winner's curse. In the Marek analysis, effect sizes shrank as samples grew, and replication rates improved only when samples reached the thousands.

The authors also found where effects were more robust, functional MRI rather than structural, cognitive tests rather than mental health questionnaires, and multivariate methods rather than single regions. Still, for differences between people, thousands is the order of magnitude.

So what can a single lab do? First decide which question you are asking. Group activation or individual differences. Then estimate a realistic effect size, ideally from large studies, not from a small pilot, because a pilot inflates effects too. Run a power analysis before you scan. And if the numbers are out of reach, change the design. Pool data with other labs, use open datasets, or switch to a within-person design, measuring each person many times, as in the precision fMRI episode. Within-person effects are often cliffs, where between-person effects are meadows.

So to recap, power depends on effect size, noise, and sample size. Task activations are often cliffs, and brain behavior correlations across people are meadows. Small studies miss real effects and inflate the ones they find. Plan your sample from realistic effects or change the design.

PLATE 9.24:01

Reliability · Same person, same scan, same answer?

A task can give a reproducible group map yet unstable rankings of individuals, like a race whose steep section is always slow but whose finishing order changes. Reliability, measured by the intraclass correlation, averaged about 0.4 in a meta-analysis of task fMRI. Low reliability shrinks correlations and inflates sample needs, so more data per person helps.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked how many people a study needs. Today a quieter question. If you scan the same person twice, do you get the same answer? This is reliability.

Picture a mountain trail race run on two Saturdays in a row by the same 20 runners. Both times everyone slows down on the steep section. That part is perfectly reproducible. But look at the rankings. The runner who came second the first week comes 11th the second week. The leader drops to 8th. The race tells you reliably where the trail is steep, but it tells you poorly who the fastest runner is.

fMRI tasks often behave exactly like this race. The group effect, a region that activates on average, can be very reproducible. But the ranking of individuals who activates more and who activates less can change a lot from one session to the next.

We measure this with the Intraclass Correlation, or ICC. It asks how much of the variation in a measure comes from stable differences between people, rather than from session to session noise. An ICC of 1 means perfectly stable rankings. 0 means the ranking is pure noise. A common rule of thumb calls values below 0.4 poor and above 0.75 excellent.

In 2020, Elliott and colleagues reviewed the test-retest literature. A meta-analysis of 90 experiments with about 1,000 participants in total found a mean ICC of about 0.4. Poor by the usual standard. They then looked at 11 common tasks in two datasets, the Human Connectome Project and the Dunedin study. Reliabilities in the expected regions ranged from almost 0 to just under 0.5.

Why does this matter so much? Because reliability caps every correlation you can find. If your brain measure is noisy from day to day, its correlation with anything else, like a symptom or a personality score, is pulled towards zero. With a reliability of 0.4, a true correlation of 0.3 can shrink to below 0.2. And you would need far more people to detect it, which brings us straight back to the previous episode.

There is a paradox here. The tasks that work best for group maps are often the worst for individual differences. A task that everyone does in the same way produces a strong, reliable average, precisely because people differ so little. Little difference between people means little stable variance to rank them by.

So is task fMRI useless for individuals? No, but it needs care. Several things help. Collect more data per person. Longer runs and repeated sessions raise reliability, as the precision fMRI episode showed. Use measures that pool information like multivariate patterns or connectivity, which tend to be more reliable than the average of a single region. Design tasks that spread people out rather than tasks everyone finds easy. And always check the reliability of your measure before building a biomarker on it.

There is one more lesson. A good task for mapping is not automatically a good test for individuals. Elliott and colleagues make the point that these measures were designed to find where things happen in the average brain, not to rank people.

So to recap, reliability asks whether the same person gives the same answer twice. Task fMRI often gives reproducible group maps but unstable individual rankings, with a mean ICC around 0.4. Low reliability shrinks correlations and inflates sample needs. More data per person, pooled measures and better design tasks can help.

Next time we follow 70 teams down 70 different paths with the very same data. The Garden of Forking Paths

PLATE 9.33:44

The garden of forking paths

Every analysis is a path through many forks: smoothing, motion regressors, software, threshold. When 70 teams analyzed the same dataset, no two used the same workflow and conclusions differed for several hypotheses, though combined maps showed consensus. Pre-registration fixes the path in advance, and multiverse analysis shows all paths at once.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we scanned the same person twice. Today we analyzed the same data twice. Or rather 70 times. Welcome to the garden of forking paths.

Picture a group of hikers leaving the same mountain hut at dawn, with the same map. At the first fork some go left and some go right. At the next fork they split again. After a dozen forks nobody is on the same trail, and they reach different summits. Each hiker made reasonable choices. But the destination depends on the path.

An fMRI analysis is full of forks. How much do you smooth? Which motion regressors do you include? Do you remove noisy volumes? Which model of the blood response? Which software? Which threshold and which correction? Each choice is defensible on its own. Together, they multiply into thousands of possible pipelines.

The statisticians Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken gave this problem its name. The danger is not dishonesty. It is that when the data are already in view, we tend to take the forks that lead to a clean, significant result, often without noticing.

In 2020, the NARPS project put this to the test. Botvinik-Nezer and colleagues gave the very same fMRI dataset, from more than 100 participants accepting or rejecting gambles to 70 independent teams. Every team tested the same nine hypotheses chosen in advance.

No two teams chose identical workflows, and the answers diverged. For several hypotheses, some teams reported a significant effect while others did not, even when their underlying statistical maps were quite similar. Part of the variation was linked to specific choices, such as how much the data were smoothed and which software was used.

There was a second surprise. Before the results came out, researchers bet on the outcomes in prediction markets. They overestimated how often teams would find significant effects, even those who knew the dataset.

In a hopeful finding, when the team's maps were combined in a meta-analysis, a clear consensus emerged on which regions were activated. The crowd, taken together, was more reliable than any single path.

So what can you do about the forking paths? First, decide before you look. A pre-registration writes down your hypotheses and your pipeline before you see the data, so the forks are chosen blind. Second, when choices are genuinely open, take them all. A multiverse analysis runs every reasonable pipeline and reports the whole spread of results. If the effect survives across most paths, it is robust. If it appears only on a few say so. Third, share your code and your unthresholded maps so others can retrace your steps. And fourth, when possible, aggregate across analyses rather than trusting a single one.

Standard pipelines also help. Tools like fMRIPrep reduce the number of arbitrary choices in pre-processing, and they record exactly what was done. That does not remove all forks, but it shortens the list.

So to recap, every analysis is a path through many forks. With the same data, 70 teams reached different conclusions for several hypotheses, and no two used the same workflow. Pre-registration fixes the path in advance. A multiverse analysis shows all the paths at once. And sharing code and maps lets others walk the same trail.

Next time, we look at one particularly sneaky fork, choosing your voxels with the very data you then test. Circularity and double dipping.

PLATE 9.43:49

Circularity and double dipping

Selecting voxels with the very result later tested is circular, like a gold panner keeping only shiny pebbles and declaring the stream full of gold. Even pure noise then yields correlations above 0.6, collapsing to about zero when selection and testing use separate halves. Localizers, separate runs, or selection inside cross-validation keep estimates honest.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we saw how many forks an analysis can take. Today, one fork deserves its own warning sign, choosing your data with the very result you are about to test. This is circularity, also called double dipping.

Picture a gold panner in a mountain stream. She swirls her pan and keeps only the pebbles that sparkle. Then she looks at her pile and announces, everything I found is shiny, this stream is full of gold. Of course it is shiny. She kept the shiny ones. Some sparkled because of gold, but many only caught the sunlight at the right moment. To know, she must send her pebbles to an assayer, who tests them with a different method on a fresh sample.

In fMRI, the pan is the selection step. Suppose you correlate every voxel with a personality score, keep the voxels that correlate best, and then report the correlation in those voxels. You pick them because they correlated strongly in this data. Part of that strength is real, and part is noise that happened to line up. Reporting it again in the same data counts the lucky noise as signal.

How big can the illusion get? Let us try it on pure noise. 20 people, 1,000 voxels, a random personality score, and no true relation at all. Select the voxels whose correlation passes a strict threshold, then average their correlation. The answer comes out above 0.6. Impressive and completely empty.

Now do it the honest way. Split the people into two halves. Choose the voxels in the first half and measure the correlation in the second half. The impressive number collapses to about zero.

This is not a rare mistake. In 2009, Kriegeskorte and colleagues examined all 134 fMRI papers published the previous year in five leading journals. 42% contained at least one non-independent selective analysis, and another 14% might have. The same year, Vul and colleagues pointed out that many published brain behavior correlations were higher than measurement reliability should allow. If brain measures have a reliability around 0.7 and questionnaires around 0.8, the largest correlation you should expect is about 0.74. Several reported values exceeded it.

Circularity has many disguises. Defining a region of interest from a contrast, then testing the same contrast there. Plotting the time course of voxels selected because they responded, and showing that they respond. Selecting features for a decoder using all the data, then cross-validating only the classifier. That last one is the leak from episode 7.2.

The rule is simple. Selection and testing must use independent data. A separate localizer run defines the region. Different runs or different people are used for selection and for testing. Or the whole selection step goes inside the cross-validation loop. When the selection is truly independent, the estimate you report is honest. And when you must use the same data, say so and treat the numbers as descriptive, not as evidence.

So to recap, selecting data with your result, then testing that result in the same data, is circular. Even pure noise can then look like a strong effect. 42% of high-profile fMRI papers in one survey had this problem in at least one analysis. Keep selection and testing independent, a localizer, separate runs, or selection inside cross-validation.

Next time we look at another trap of interpretation. The insula lights up so people felt disgust. Reverse Inference.

PLATE 9.54:01

Reverse inference

Reverse inference infers a mental state from an active region, like assuming a marmot's whistle means an eagle. Regions such as the insula respond to many processes, so one activation is weak evidence for any single one, and Bayes' rule with large databases shows how weak. It is a hypothesis, stronger with selective regions and decoders validated on independent data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we saw how selection can fool a test. Today the trap is in the interpretation. A region lights up so people must have felt something. This is reverse inference.

Picture a marmot on a mountain slope. Marmots whistle when danger comes. A hiker hears a sharp whistle and says, an eagle must be overhead. But marmots also whistle at foxes, at dogs, and at hikers like her. The whistle is real. What caused it is a different question. To guess well, she needs to know two things, how many different threats trigger a whistle and how often each of them passes by.

In fMRI, the forward direction is safe. You manipulate a task, say looking at disgusting pictures versus neutral ones, and you observe which regions respond. Task to brain. Reverse inference goes the other way. You see a region activate and you infer a mental state. Brain to mind. The insula is active, so the participants felt disgust.

The problem is selectivity. The anterior insula responds during disgust, but also during pain, effort, surprise, interoception, uncertainty, and many tasks that demand attention. In large databases, it is among the most frequently activated regions of all. A region that responds to almost everything tells you very little about which thing happened.

Russell Poldrack made this precise in 2006 with Bayes' rule. What we want is the probability of a mental process, given the activation. That depends on how often the region activates when the process is engaged, how often it activates when the process is not engaged, and how likely the process was to begin with. He tested a favorable case, Broca's area and language, using the BrainMap database. Starting from even odds, seeing activation in Broca's area raised the probability of language to about 0.69, a Bayes factor of about 2.3, which counts as weak evidence. And that was a best case.

Large meta-analytic databases now let us estimate these numbers for every region. Neurosynth, introduced by Yarkoni and colleagues in 2011, automatically extracts activation coordinates from thousands of published articles and links them to the words used in each paper. It can show the forward map where a term tends to activate and the reverse map how specific a region is to that term compared with all the others.

Let us take apart a typical headline in three steps. A newspaper once claimed that people literally love their iPhones because the insula responded when they saw or heard them. Step one was the region measured cleanly, with a proper contrast and correction? Step 2. How selective is that region? The insula appears in a large share of all studies, for many different reasons. Step 3. Is there a direct test of the mental state, such as a behavioral measure or a pattern classifier validated on independent data? Without those, the headline is a guess. Dozens of neuroscientists signed a letter saying exactly that.

Reverse inference is not forbidden. It is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. It becomes stronger when the region is selective, when a whole pattern is used rather than a single spot, when the decoder is trained and tested on independent data, as in chapter 7, and when the inference is checked against behavior.

So to recap, forward inference goes from task to brain, reverse inference from brain to mind. Most regions respond to many processes, so one activation is weak evidence for any single one. Bayes' rule and large databases tell you how weak. Treat reverse inferences as hypotheses to test, not as findings.

Next time, the final episode of the series, report your work so that others can redo it and get the same map.

PLATE 9.63:46

Report it so others can redo it

A report should work like a climbing topo: precise enough for strangers to repeat the climb. This means following reporting checklists, pre-registering hypotheses and plans, and sharing data in BIDS on OpenNeuro, unthresholded maps on NeuroVault, and code in a container. Reporting everything, including null results, allows computational reproducibility, where another lab obtains the same map, and replication, where new data show the same effect.

TRANSCRIPTEN

This is the last stop on the mountain and the last episode of the series. We have seen how results can wobble. Small samples, unreliable measures, forking paths, circular analyses, hopeful interpretations. Today, the remedy that ties them together. Report your work so that others can redo it.

Picture a climbing team that opens a new route to a summit. When they come down, they write a route description called a topo. Where the route starts, how many pitches, how hard each one is, which gear they used, where the rope can be anchored. A good topo lets a second team who has never met the first repeat the climb and reach the same summit. A vague topo like turn left at the big rock sends everyone somewhere else.

For fMRI, the topo is your methods section and much more. In 2014, the Organization for Human Brain Mapping created a committee called COBIDAS for best practices in data analysis and sharing. Its report, summarized in 2017, gives checklists across seven areas— experimental design, acquisition, pre-processing, statistical modeling, results, data sharing, and reproducibility. The idea is simple. Another researcher should be able to redo your study from what you wrote.

Before the climb, there is pre-registration. You write down your hypotheses, your sample size, and your analysis plan, and you time stamp them before collecting or looking at the data. This fixes the path, as we saw with the forking trails. Some journals go further with registered reports, where the study is reviewed and accepted before the results exist.

During and after the climb, share the gear. First, the data. OpenNeuro is a free archive for neuroimaging data in the BIDS format. Every dataset must pass the BIDS validator before upload. By 2021, it already held more than 600 datasets from more than 20,000 participants, and it keeps growing.

Second, the maps. A thresholded figure shows only the peaks that crossed a line. NeuroVault stores the full, unthresholded statistical maps so that others can see the whole landscape, combine studies in meta-analyses, and test their own regions.

Third, the code in the software environment. Share your analysis scripts and freeze the exact versions of the tools inside a container, such as Docker or Apptainer. Remember Chapter 4, the same pipeline with different versions can give different results. A container is the rope that keeps the second team on the same route.

And report what you did, not only what worked. All conditions, all analyses, the exclusions and their reasons, the effect sizes with confidence intervals, and the null results.

Now the moment this whole series has been climbing towards. Another lab downloads your data from OpenNeuro, runs your container, follows your pre-registered plan, and obtains the same map. Not a similar story. The same map. That is computational reproducibility. And when a new team collects new data, follows your topo, and finds the same effect, that is replication, the true second ascent.

So to recap, a report is a topo, precise enough for strangers to repeat the climb. Use the COBIDAS checklists. Pre-register your plan. Share data in BIDS on OpenNeuro, unthresholded maps on NeuroVault, and code in a container. Report everything, including what did not work.
