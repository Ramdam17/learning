Bin by bin

Two shelves of three jars hold zero point five, zero point three and zero point two, and the reverse. Half the sum of the gaps gives total variation, 0.3: the share of sweets to move, the largest gap any event creates, and a guessing limit of sixty-five percent. Welcome to the confectioner's shop. Every shelf in here holds jars, and every jar holds sweets.

Until now our objects were lists of numbers, sets, sequences and signals. This chapter changes the object. It is no longer a measurement. It is the way measurements spread out. A histogram. Whenever a logarithm shows up in this chapter, we will count in bits, and the unit will be written on screen.

A histogram is a row of bars. Each bar is a bin, and a bin is a jar. The heights are never negative, and they add up to exactly one. Think of one full measuring cup of sweets, poured into the jars.

Here are two shelves, with three jars each. The first shelf holds zero point five, zero point three and zero point two. The second holds zero point two, zero point three and zero point five.

How far apart are these two shelves?

Lay the second shelf over the first. Where the first sticks out, there are sweets that the second does not have. In the first jar, that is zero point three. The second jar matches. In the third jar it is the second shelf that sticks out, again by zero point three.

Now add up the gaps, jar by jar. Zero point three, zero, zero point three. That is zero point six. This is the city-block distance of episode two point two. Cut it in half and you get zero point three. This is the total variation distance.

Why half? Because every sweet that sticks out in one place is a sweet that is missing in another. The two excesses are the same amount. Counting both would count everything twice.

And this one number can be read in three ways.

First reading. It is the share of the sweets that must change jar. Take zero point three of the cup, and put it where it is missing. Then the two shelves are identical. There is one condition. This is true when carrying a sweet costs the same, wherever it goes to. Remember that condition, because the next chapter breaks it.

Second reading. Pick any event you like. For example, a sweet lands in the first or the second jar. Each shelf gives that event a probability. Third reading. Someone hides one shelf, chosen by a coin flip, and draws a single sweet. You must say which shelf it came from. Divergence has a direction

The Kullback-Leibler divergence is the extra bits paid for describing one distribution with the code of another. A fair coin against a coin landing heads nine times in ten costs 0.74 bits one way and 0.53 back. It breaks the triangle rule in about one random triple in four, and is infinite when a reference jar is empty. Always say which distribution is the reference.

PLATE 5.58:14

One family

Total variation, Hellinger, Kullback-Leibler, chi-square and Jensen-Shannon are one recipe: take the ratio of two histogram heights in each bin, pass it through a convex die, and average. Renaming bins changes nothing, and merging bins can only lower the divergence. Up close the members agree, far away they split, and cutting finer raises the number.

Half the chi-square: zero point zero zero zero two zero. Four times the squared Hellinger distance: zero point zero zero zero two zero. Kullback-Leibler: zero point zero zero zero two nine bits. Four times Jensen-Shannon: zero point zero zero zero two nine bits.

Up close, the dies agree. The two numbers differ by one point four four, which is one over the natural logarithm of two.

Now look far away. Two peaks that do not overlap. Total variation: one. Hellinger: one. Jensen-Shannon: one bit. Kullback-Leibler and chi-square: infinite.

Far away, the dies disagree about what to do with the bins where the ratio is huge. And none of them can tell you how far apart the two peaks are.

One knob walks through these behaviors. It is the order of the Rényi divergence, from nineteen sixty-one. It is the logarithm of an average, with the ratio raised to a power.

At order one half, it is twice the Bhattacharyya distance: zero point two zero bits. Close to order one, it is Kullback-Leibler: zero point four zero bits. At order two, it is the logarithm of one plus the chi-square: zero point seven zero bits.

The higher the order, the more the bins with a large ratio weigh. At the very top, only the largest ratio counts, here two point five, which is one point three two bits.

One inequality ties the two ends of the family together. It is Pinsker's. The total variation is never larger than the square root of zero point three five times the Kullback-Leibler divergence in bits. Here, zero point three zero is below zero point three seven. We checked twenty thousand random pairs. No exception.

And now the trap. It comes straight from merging.

Take the same two distributions, two bumps. Cut them in five bins. Kullback-Leibler says zero point two four bits. Cut them in twenty bins. It says zero point two six bits. In two hundred bins, zero point two six three.

The same two distributions, the same divergence, and three different numbers. Cutting finer can only raise it.

Two studies that do not cut the same way cannot be compared. Always say how many bins.

Here is the signature of the family. These divergences do not know what the bins are. That is why they work on words, on cell types, on sleep stages.

And that is why none of them can say that eleven hertz is closer to ten than thirty is. The next chapter lifts that limit.

To remember. Ratio, die, average: one recipe, many dies. Renaming bins changes nothing. Merging bins can only lower the divergence. Up close the members agree, far away they split, and the number of bins is part of the answer.

PLATE 5.66:42

Histograms lie a little

Two scoops of a hundred sweets from one barrel are never at distance zero: total variation gives 0.11 at five bins, 0.24 at twenty and 0.52 at a hundred. This floor rises with the bins and falls slowly with the points. Measure it by shuffling labels, fix bins by a rule, or use nearest neighbours, and always report the floor.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the confectioner's shop. Every distance card in this chapter has a line that says what the distance costs. So far we wrote the same thing every time: one pass over the bins.

That is the price of computing the distance. It is not the real price. The real price is knowing the histograms.

Because you never get the histogram. You get a scoop. Picture one big barrel of sweets, cut into flavours that are all equally likely. You dip in, take a hundred sweets, and count the flavours. That count is your histogram. It is an estimate of the barrel, and an estimate wobbles.

Take two scoops from the same barrel. They describe the very same thing, so the true distance between them is zero. Let us measure it, with the total variation distance, on simulated scoops of a hundred sweets. With five bins, the distance is zero point one one. With twenty bins, zero point two four. With a hundred bins, zero point five two. Each of these is an average over two thousand pairs of scoops.

Now take a thousand sweets in each scoop. Zero point zero four, zero point zero eight, zero point one eight. The distance between two identical things is not zero. It climbs with the number of bins, and it falls slowly with the number of points. Ten times more sweets divides it by about three.

Why? In each bin, the count wobbles around its true value. The gap in that bin is the wobble of one scoop minus the wobble of the other. And a distance takes the size of a gap, so the pluses and the minuses never cancel. Add up the sizes of all the little gaps, and what is left is a floor. More bins means more gaps to add. More sweets means smaller wobbles, but only as fast as the square root of their number.

For the Jensen-Shannon divergence, this floor can be predicted. In bits, it is about the number of bins minus one, divided by two point seven seven times the number of points. Two point seven seven is four times the natural logarithm of two. The four comes from having two scoops, and the logarithm turns nats into bits. With twenty bins and a hundred points, the formula says zero point zero six nine bit. The simulation says zero point zero seven four bit. The formula is good while the bins are few compared with the points. With a hundred bins and a hundred points, it says zero point three six bit, and the simulation says zero point four one.

For the Kullback-Leibler divergence, it is worse. It divides by the height of a bin in the second scoop. If that bin is empty while the first scoop has sweets in it, the divergence is infinite. With twenty bins and a hundred points, this happens in twelve percent of the draws. With a hundred bins, it happens in every draw. Two scoops from the same barrel, and the divergence says: infinitely different.

So what can we do? There are three answers.

First answer: measure the floor. Pool the two scoops. Shuffle the labels, so that each sweet is dealt to one scoop or the other at random, and measure the distance. Do it two thousand times. This is the shuffle of the chapter What do we shuffle, in the Statistics series. Every shuffled pair comes from one barrel by construction, so its distance shows what zero looks like, at this number of bins and this number of points.

Here is a real difference. Two barrels that really are zero point one zero apart, twenty bins, a hundred sweets per scoop. We measure zero point two two. It looks large. But the floor is zero point two four. The raw number is below its own zero. With a thousand sweets per scoop, we measure zero point zero nine six, against a floor of zero point zero seven eight. Now there is something above the floor, but only just: nine shuffles in a hundred reach it. Report the distance together with its floor.

Second answer: fix the bins by a rule, before you see the result. Scott, in nineteen seventy-nine, and Freedman and Diaconis, in nineteen eighty-one, gave rules that set the width of the bins from the number of points and the spread of the data. On bell-shaped samples, Scott's rule gives about seven bins for a hundred points, and nineteen for a thousand. Freedman and Diaconis give about nine, and twenty-five. These rules were made to estimate one density well, not to compare two. But they have one virtue here. The number of bins is decided by the rule, and not by whichever picture looks best. Use the same bins for both scoops.

Third answer: no bins at all. Wang, Kulkarni and Verdú, in two thousand nine, estimate the divergence from distances between points. For each point of the first scoop, find its nearest neighbour in the same scoop, and its nearest neighbour in the other. If the other scoop is much farther, the first one is crowded where the second is not. Average the logarithm of these ratios, and you estimate the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

We tried it on two bell curves, one unit apart, where the true value is zero point seven two one bit. With a hundred points per sample, the estimate averages zero point six seven bit, with a spread of zero point three five. With a thousand points, zero point seven one, with a spread of zero point one two. The histogram version, with twenty bins, was infinite in ninety-eight percent of the draws at a hundred points, because some bins in the tails were empty.

Now the trap. Comparing distances that were computed with different numbers of points. Take three groups that all behave exactly like the same barrel. Compare each one with the same large reference, in ten bins. The group of twenty trials sits at zero point two six. The group of a hundred, at zero point one two. The group of a thousand, at zero point zero four. The group with the fewest trials looks the most different, and it is not different at all.

So the card gets a fuller cost line. A pass over the bins, plus all the sweets needed to know them.

A distance between distributions is a distance between two estimates. Its zero is not at zero. It sits where two samples of the same thing fall, and that place depends on the number of points and on the number of bins. A bin by bin distance is always reported with its floor.

To remember. Two scoops from one barrel are never at distance zero. The floor rises with the number of bins, and falls slowly with the number of points. Measure it by shuffling, fix the bins by a rule, or leave the bins out.

PLATE 5.77:35

No bins at all

Sorting data and stepping up at each point draws a staircase, and the biggest vertical gap between two staircases is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance, which needs no bins. A logarithm or cube applied to both samples leaves it at 0.45. But it hears only the biggest gap: ten hertz against eleven and against thirty both read one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the confectioner's shop. Today we take the jars away.

Every distance so far in this chapter needed bins. And bins are a choice. How many? How wide? Where do they start? Change them, and the distance changes. So here is the question. Can we compare two distributions with no bins at all?

We can. Take your data and sort them, smallest first. Now walk along the axis, from left to right. Every time you pass a data point, take one step up. All the steps have the same height: one divided by the number of points. At the far left you stand at zero. At the far right you stand at one.

You have drawn a staircase. It is called the empirical cumulative curve. At any position, its height is the share of the data that lies at or below that position.

No jar appears anywhere. There is no width to choose. Each data point is its own step.

Here is a tide line, sweeping over the axis. Fifteen blue pebbles lie on it, from one sample, and fifteen pink pebbles, from another. Whenever the tide passes a pebble, the pebble drops into its tube, and the level in that tube rises by one fifteenth. Two tubes. Two levels. Two staircases.

Now ask, at every position, how far apart are the two levels? Watch the gap grow, and shrink, as the line moves. Keep the largest gap you ever see. That is the Kolmogorov and Smirnov distance. It is the biggest vertical gap between two staircases.

Kolmogorov proposed it in nineteen thirty three, to compare a sample with a known law. Smirnov extended it to two samples in nineteen thirty nine. For our thirty pebbles, the biggest gap is zero point four.

The shelves of episode five point one fit in too. Read their three jars in order, and pour them into a staircase of three steps. The first shelf climbs to zero point five, zero point eight, one. The second climbs to zero point two, zero point five, one. The biggest gap is zero point three, the same as the total variation. That is a coincidence of this example. But look at what we just did. We read the jars in order. In a staircase, jar two stands between jar one and jar three.

Now the distance card. The object is a sample, or a distribution on a line. The distance is the largest vertical gap between the two cumulative curves. And the four rules? Never negative: yes. Zero only for the same curve: yes. Same in both directions: yes. And no shortcut: yes, because a gap at any position is never larger than the sum of two gaps through a third curve. The four lights are on.

What does it ignore? Here comes the surprise. It ignores any stretching or squeezing of the axis, as long as the order of the points is kept.

Take two samples of forty positive values. Their distance is zero point four five. Now apply the logarithm to both samples. Zero point four five. The exponential. Zero point four five. The cube. Zero point four five.

The staircases are bent sideways, but every step keeps its place in the order, and the gap is only measured upward. Only the ranks matter, as in episode three point four. Bins do not behave like this. On twelve equal bins, the total variation of the same pair reads zero point four five, then zero point five, zero point three five and zero point four.

Careful, though. This is one deformation applied to both samples. A change made to only one of them is another story, and the row of changes shows it.

Here is the row, on the signal of episode five point one, with its four thousand samples. Shifted: the distance sees it, zero point two six. Amplified: zero point one three. Delayed: zero. Shuffled: zero. Noisy: zero point zero three.

The two zeros are the same as before. A delay and a shuffle only reorder the same samples, and a cumulative curve only counts them. No bins were needed to forget time.

Now the best part. Draw two samples of one hundred points from the very same distribution. Their distance is not zero. Where does it land? We repeated the draw four thousand times. In ninety five draws out of a hundred, it stays under zero point one nine. With a thousand points, under zero point zero six.

We did it for the normal law, the exponential, the uniform and the Cauchy law. The same floor, every time. Because only the ranks matter, the floor does not depend on the distribution of your data. It is known in advance, with a formula: one point three five eight times the square root of two divided by the number of points. For histogram distances, we had to measure that floor ourselves.

What does it cost? A sort, and then one pass over the sorted values.

Now the traps. There are four.

First, it only hears the biggest gap, and the biggest gap almost always sits in the middle. Take a normal law, and a Student law with three degrees of freedom, scaled to the same spread. Beyond three standard deviations, the second one has about five times as many values. Yet the largest gap between the curves is zero point zero nine. With three hundred points in each sample, that is under the floor. You would not see it. A variant by Anderson and Darling, in nineteen fifty two, gives more weight to the extremes.

Second, a fine ripple escapes it. Take a density with fifty ripples. Against a flat density, the total variation is zero point three two. But the two cumulative curves stay almost on top of each other: the largest gap is zero point zero zero six. Each ripple adds mass on one side and removes the same mass on the other, so the running total hardly moves.

Third, it needs an order. On a line there is one way to accumulate, from left to right. In two dimensions there are several. Accumulate from the lower left corner, or from the upper left, or from the right, and the same two clouds of thirty points give gaps from zero point two zero to zero point four three. Peacock proposed, in nineteen eighty three, to take the largest over all the orderings.

Fourth, it measures only the height of the gap. A thin peak at ten hertz against a thin peak at eleven hertz: one. Against a thin peak at thirty hertz: one. The same saturation as before. But look at the area between the two staircases. It is one hertz in the first case. It is twenty hertz in the second.

Cumulating did something important. In a histogram, jar seven knew nothing about jar eight. In a staircase, every step stands on all the steps before it. The bins finally know their neighbours.

The height of the gap says how much mass is misplaced. The area says how far it must travel. That area is what the next chapter measures.

To remember. Sort the data, and climb a staircase. The distance is the biggest gap between two staircases. It needs no bins, it ignores any bending of the axis that keeps the order, and its floor depends only on the number of points. It hears only the biggest gap, it misses fine ripples, and in two dimensions it needs an order. Its height says how much. Its area will say how far.

Three ideas, each an afternoon of work. None of them is a result.

One question separates the two columns. Do your bins have neighbours?

If your bins are names, like sleep stages, cell types or words, this chapter is enough. If your bins are positions, like frequencies, amplitudes or instants, then bin by bin throws away information that you have.

So here is the idea to keep. Comparing bin by bin is a choice of invariance, like any other. It is right for categories. For anything that sits on an axis, you must pay for the move.
