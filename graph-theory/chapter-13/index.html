A number on every node

A farmer's ten orchard thermometers, one of them silent, become a graph once each post is linked to its nearest posts. A number on every node is a signal on a graph, smooth when neighbours agree and rough when they disagree. The same ten readings have roughness 78.6 under one neighbour rule and 6.1 under another, so the graph decides.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. What does it mean for numbers to vary smoothly across a graph?

Let us find out in an orchard.

A farmer keeps ten thermometers on posts between the rows of apple trees. Tonight there will be frost, and the farmer needs every reading.

But one thermometer has stopped answering. Its value can be guessed from its neighbours. Which neighbours?

The posts do not stand on a grid. There is no row and column to count along.

By the fence hangs a board with a map of the posts. Ten pins, and one question mark.

Keep the pins. Now link each post to its two nearest posts. That makes thirteen links.

Dots and lines. A graph, and out steps PEEK.

Now give every dot a number, the temperature that its thermometer reads. Every dot, except one.

A number on every node. This is a signal on a graph.

An overview by Shuman and colleagues, in two thousand and thirteen, is titled the emerging field of signal processing on graphs. This chapter follows that idea.

The same idea fits very different graphs.

On a map of stations, the signal is a temperature.

On a sketch of the brain, the nodes are regions, and the links are bundles of fibres between them. Its signal is how active the region is.

Among friends, the nodes are people. The signal there is each person's opinion, from minus one to plus one.

The brain and the friends are made up for this episode.

The graph says who is close to whom. The signal says what each node carries.

So what makes a signal smooth?

Take the orchard again, with a number on each post. These numbers rise gently from west to east.

Neighbours carry close values. We call this signal smooth.

Now deal the same ten numbers out again, in another order.

The numbers have not changed. But neighbours now carry values far apart, and the signal is rough.

Smooth means neighbours agree. Rough means neighbours disagree.

Chapter eleven gave us a number for this. Look at each link, and at the gap between its two ends. Square the gap, and add up over all the links.

That sum is the roughness. In chapter eleven it was written x transpose L x, with L the Laplacian.

A small roughness means a smooth signal.

Here, the gentle ramp has a roughness of fourteen point six. The same numbers, dealt in another order, give forty-eight.

Two thousand random deals of these numbers average forty-two point five. A shuffled signal is typically this rough.

One word of care. Compare two signals only on the same graph. The roughness grows with the size of the numbers and with the number of links.

Now for the surprise.

Back in the orchard, here are the readings of a cold night. They are simulated. The ground is a hill, and the air cools by six point five degrees per kilometre of height, as in the standard atmosphere, with a little noise.

Keep these ten numbers. Change only the rule that says who is a neighbour.

Rule one is the one we used. Each post is linked to its nearest posts on the map.

But temperature follows height, not the map. On a hillside, two posts close on the map can stand at very different heights.

Under rule one, the roughness is seventy-eight point six. That is as rough as a shuffle. Random deals of these numbers average seventy-six point four.

Rule two links posts that stand at about the same height. We keep the same number of links, thirteen. Some of them join posts far apart on the map.

Same ten numbers. The roughness is now six point one.

Nearly thirteen times smoother.

So is the signal smooth, or rough? Alone, it is neither. The graph decides, by saying what neighbour means.

Neither rule is wrong. Which one to use depends on the question we ask.

Remember the broken thermometer? Which neighbours should we trust? The answer depends on the rule. We will come back to it.

Choosing a rule for neighbour is choosing a distance between nodes. The Geometry of Data series studies that choice.

To sum up. A signal on a graph is a number on every node. It is smooth when neighbours agree, and rough when they disagree. The sum of squared gaps measures it. And the same numbers can be smooth on one graph and rough on another, because the graph says who the neighbours are.

Fourier on a graph

The Laplacian's eigenvectors act as the waves of a graph. On a ring of twelve posts they are the discrete Fourier waves, and the eigenvalue is the frequency. On the orchard graph, a gentle ramp keeps 97 percent of its energy in the three slowest harmonics, while the same numbers dealt in another order keep 19 percent. Smooth means mostly low frequencies.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. On a line of time, a signal splits into slow waves and fast waves. Can a signal on a graph be split the same way?

It can. And the tool is one we already own: the Laplacian of chapter eleven.

Back in the orchard, the farmer still has ten thermometers and one broken. We will come back to them. First, a place where the answer is known.

Twelve posts stand in a circle around a pond. Each post is linked to the two posts beside it. This ring is made up for the episode.

On a circle, the waves are familiar. One wave goes once around. Another goes three times around. The fastest flips sign at every single post.

Here are four of them, drawn as plates. Pink where the wave is positive, blue where it is negative.

Now the key fact. Each of these waves is an eigenvector of the Laplacian of the ring.

The wave that goes once around has eigenvalue zero point two seven. Three times around gives eigenvalue two. The one that flips at every post has eigenvalue four.

The eigenvalue is the frequency. It grows as the wave gets faster.

The flat plate has eigenvalue zero. It never changes, so it has no roughness.

We checked one more thing. Scale a wave to length one, and its roughness from chapter eleven equals its eigenvalue.

So on a ring, the eigenvectors of the Laplacian are exactly the waves of the classical discrete Fourier transform.

One detail. Waves of the same speed come in pairs, a cosine and a sine, and each pair shares one eigenvalue.

A ring is special. A map of roads or a circle of friends has no waves to count around.

But every graph has a Laplacian, and every Laplacian has eigenvectors. We take them as the waves of the graph, and call them harmonics.

Take the ten posts of the orchard, with the same thirteen links as last time. Sort the harmonics by eigenvalue.

The first is flat, with eigenvalue zero. Every post gets the same value.

The next has a small eigenvalue, zero point three four. No link joins a pink post to a blue one.

The last has an eigenvalue of five point eight. Eleven of the thirteen links join pink to blue.

Small eigenvalue, slow change from post to post. Large eigenvalue, signs that flip between neighbours. Shuman and colleagues call these the low and the high frequencies of a graph.

Two harmonics share the value three, so any mix of that pair would do as well. Like the cosine and the sine.

Now take a signal. Last time's gentle ramp, ten numbers rising from west to east.

First we remove the average. That is the flat plate, and it does not change the roughness.

To measure how much of one harmonic the signal holds, multiply the two post by post, and add up. That number is a coefficient.

The list of coefficients is the Fourier transform of the signal on the graph.

Add the harmonics back, each scaled by its coefficient, and the signal returns exactly. Nothing is lost.

For the ramp, almost all the energy sits in the slow harmonics. Ninety-seven percent is in the three slowest.

Now deal the same ten numbers in another order. Same numbers, same total energy.

But the energy moves to the fast harmonics. Only nineteen percent stays in the three slowest.

Keep the average and the three slowest harmonics, and drop the rest. The ramp is rebuilt with an error of seventeen percent of its size. For the dealt signal, the error is ninety percent.

Here is the link with last time. Multiply each eigenvalue by the energy at that frequency, and add up. The result is exactly the roughness.

Divide by the total energy, and you get the average frequency of the signal. It is zero point nine nine for the ramp, and three point two six for the dealt signal.

So smooth means mostly low frequencies. Rough means mostly high ones.

Do harmonics have shapes? Look at roads.

This is the road network of Minnesota. Two thousand six hundred forty two junctions, and three thousand three hundred four road links. It comes from David Gleich's MatlabBGL library, and Shuman and colleagues use this same graph in their overview.

Next comes the slowest harmonic after the flat one. It cuts the map into two regions, north against south.

The second also cuts the map in two, along another line.

The third and the fourth cut it into three patches each.

Slow harmonics are broad regions of the map. The frequencies of a graph are patterns in space.

Now a fast one, number two thousand. Seventy percent of the road links join pink to blue. The map looks like static.

One word on the other school. Sandryhaila and Moura, in two thousand thirteen, build the same idea from the adjacency matrix instead of the Laplacian. We stay with the Laplacian.

To sum up. On a ring, the eigenvectors of the Laplacian are the waves of the Fourier transform. On any graph, they are the harmonics. A signal splits into one coefficient per harmonic, and its roughness is the eigenvalues weighted by those energies. And the harmonics have shapes, patches of the map.

PLATE 13.35:32

Filtering

A graph filter is one gain per harmonic, but computing it with eigenvectors is costly. Choosing the gain as a polynomial of the eigenvalue turns the filter into a polynomial of the Laplacian, which needs no eigenvectors. A degree K filter reaches K steps, and each step is one round of messages between neighbours. On the orchard, both routes agree to fifteen decimals.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. A filter keeps the slow harmonics and turns down the fast ones. How do we build one on a graph?

The answer has two halves. First, what a filter is. Then, how to compute one without the harmonics at all.

Back in the orchard, the farmer still has ten thermometers and one is broken. That waits. First, the filter.

Picture a mixing desk. There is one slider for each harmonic of the orchard, sorted from slow to fast.

Each slider sets a gain. One lets the harmonic through. Zero mutes it.

A low-pass filter pulls the sliders down as the eigenvalue grows. Slow harmonics pass, and fast ones fade.

The gain depends only on the eigenvalue. So a filter is a function g of lambda.

Here we take g of lambda equal to e to the minus lambda. It is one at zero, and it falls smoothly. At the second harmonic the gain is zero point seven one. At the last, zero point zero zero three.

Now put a signal through it. Take the dealt signal of last time, the rough one.

Step one. Transform it, with one coefficient per harmonic.

Step two. Multiply each coefficient by the gain of its harmonic.

Step three. Add the harmonics back, scaled by the new coefficients.

The roughness falls from forty-eight to zero point four. Only four percent of the energy survives.

The gentle ramp passes much better. Its roughness goes from fourteen point six to two point one.

We never changed the graph. We only moved the sliders.

But this recipe has a cost. It needs every eigenvector.

The road map of Minnesota has two thousand six hundred forty two junctions. Its eigenvectors make a table of almost seven million numbers.

For a network of a million people, the table would hold a million million numbers.

We want the same filter, without the table.

The way out is to choose the gain curve with care. Let it be a polynomial in lambda. A constant, plus a multiple of lambda, plus a multiple of lambda squared, up to degree K.

A polynomial of low degree bends only a few times. It cannot follow every wish. But a smooth gain like ours is easy to follow.

Here are degrees one, two, three and four. Over the ten harmonics, the largest gap with the true gain is zero point four nine for degree one. Then zero point one seven, zero point zero five, and zero point zero one.

Why does a polynomial need no eigenvectors? Because of one line.

The Laplacian times a harmonic gives lambda times that harmonic. Twice over, you get lambda squared times the harmonic.

So a polynomial of the Laplacian multiplies each harmonic by the same polynomial of its eigenvalue. That is exactly a filter, and its gains are the polynomial's values.

We checked it on the orchard. Both routes agree to fifteen decimals.

And the Laplacian is cheap to apply. To multiply it by a signal, each junction needs only the values of its direct neighbours.

Apply it twice, and each junction has heard from the neighbours of its neighbours. Degree K reaches K steps, and no farther.

Test it on the road map. One spike, at a single junction near the middle.

Filter with degree one. Four junctions respond: the spike and its three neighbours.

Degree two reaches nine junctions. Degree four reaches twenty-four. Degree eight reaches ninety-nine.

Everything farther away stays at exactly zero. The filter is local.

So a local filter is a message-passing scheme. Watch it in the orchard.

Use the dealt signal again. Each post holds one number.

In one round, every post sends its number to its neighbours. Each post then subtracts a small share of the gap between its own number and each neighbour's number. The share is one over five point eight, the largest eigenvalue.

Do it three times, and keep a copy after each round.

Then mix the four copies with the polynomial's weights. The original, and the copies after one, two and three rounds.

The result is the degree three filter. It matches the eigenvector route to fifteen decimals. Three rounds of messages, and no eigenvector.

This is not only a trick. In two thousand eleven, Hammond, Vandergheynst and Gribonval fitted such polynomials with the method of Chebyshev, to build wavelets on graphs. They proved the locality: beyond K steps, the powers of the Laplacian are zero.

Then in two thousand sixteen, Defferrard and colleagues made the coefficients of the polynomial learnable, to build a neural network on graphs.

And in two thousand seventeen, Kipf and Welling simplified it to degree one. One round of messages per layer.

Chapter seventeen starts from there.

To sum up. A filter is one gain per harmonic. Choose the gains as a polynomial of the eigenvalue, and the filter becomes a polynomial of the Laplacian. It needs no eigenvectors. It reaches K steps. And each step is one round of messages between neighbours.

Smoothing and filling gaps

Smoothing noisy readings and filling a silent thermometer both bet that the signal is smooth on the graph. With neighbours chosen by map distance, the filter made the error worse, 1.6 degrees. Linking posts of equal height brought it to 0.8. A silent post takes the average of its neighbours. When the bet fails, change the graph, not the filter.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. A filter can calm a noisy signal. But what do we do with a thermometer that says nothing at all?

Both jobs rest on one idea. We bet that the true signal is smooth on the graph.

This episode tests that bet, in the orchard, with the two neighbour rules of episode thirteen one.

Start with noise. It is a cold night, and each of the ten thermometers reads a little wrong. We add a random error of one degree to every reading. The data are simulated, with seed two.

The typical error against the true night is one point one degrees.

Now run the filter of last time, the gain e to the minus lambda. Use rule one, which links the nearest posts on the map.

Look at the result. Every post sits between twelve point four and thirteen. The cold has vanished. The error has grown to one point six degrees. The filter made things worse.

Why? A filter assumes that neighbours agree. Here they do not. A cold post on the ridge is a neighbour of a warm post in the valley. The filter takes that real difference for noise, and wipes it out.

So keep the same filter, and change only the links. Rule two links posts that stand at the same height.

Same noisy readings, same gain. This time the cold stays cold. Post C comes back at ten point seven, and F at eleven point two. Here the error falls to zero point eight.

Was that luck? We drew two thousand different noises. With rule two, the filter beat doing nothing ninety-nine times in a hundred. Rule one, less than once in a hundred.

The average error is zero point nine seven for the raw readings, one point five one on rule one, and zero point five four on rule two.

Smoothing is a bet. We assume that the signal is smooth on the graph we chose. If the graph is wrong, the filter erases the truth.

Now the silent thermometer. Post C says nothing. Nine readings are known.

We look for the smoothest signal that keeps the nine known values. Smoothest means the smallest roughness, the sum of squared gaps along the links, from episode eleven one.

Only one number is free, the value of C. The roughness is smallest when C equals the average of its neighbours. We checked it by trying every value.

On rule one, C has four neighbours. E, G, H and I read twelve point seven, fourteen point six, twelve point two and eleven. Their average is twelve point six. The true value was ten point one. That misses by two and a half degrees.

On rule two, C has one neighbour, F, which reads ten point six. The estimate is ten point six. The miss is half a degree.

Why such a difference? C stands on the ridge, at seven hundred twenty-five metres. Its neighbours on the map are lower, between fifty-eight and five hundred forty-four metres. The mountain hides the cold. F stands at seven hundred nine metres.

Is C a special case? Hide each post in turn, and fill it from the other nine. Rule two wins on all ten posts. Typically, rule one errs by one point eight degrees, and rule two by zero point four.

If several posts are silent, nothing changes. Each one is still the average of its neighbours, and we solve for all of them at once. Zhu and colleagues used this idea in two thousand three, to fill in missing labels on a graph. Narang, Gadde and Ortega, in two thousand thirteen, treated missing values on a graph as a signal to reconstruct.

The same idea fills a broken electrode. The usual tool since Perrin and colleagues, in nineteen eighty-nine, is a spline over the scalp. It bets on smoothness too. A graph lets us choose who the neighbours are.

One warning. We chose rule two because we knew that cold follows height. We know it because we built the simulation. On a real night, you have to justify the bet. Then test it, by hiding a known reading, as we did.

To sum up. Smoothing and filling gaps both bet that the signal is smooth on the graph. A silent post takes the average of its neighbours. And when the bet fails, change the graph, not the filter.

Last time, we ended on a question. The graph is the wiring of the brain. What does activity look like on it?

Start with the wiring. It comes from Griffa and colleagues, in two thousand nineteen. They scanned seventy healthy adults, and followed the fibres of the white matter with diffusion imaging.

The graph has eighty-three regions. Sixty-eight sit on the cortex, fourteen lie deeper down, and one is the brainstem. A link means that fibres join two regions. There are one thousand sixty-seven links, about a third of all pairs. The weight of a link is its fibre density.

Now we need a signal. We have no recording of activity over time. We have something else, from the same seventy adults and the same eighty-three regions: a functional matrix. For each pair of regions, it says how much their activity rises and falls together at rest.

Its strongest patterns are maps. Each one is a number per region. Pink is above the average, blue is below. That is a signal on the graph. We took the six strongest patterns.

Be clear about what these are. We computed them from the group matrix. They are not maps from a paper, and they are not activity over time.

Now ask the question of episode thirteen point one. Is a pattern smooth on the wiring? Roughness adds up the squared gaps between linked regions, weighted by fibre density. Each region's value is first divided by the square root of its total wiring, as Preti and Van De Ville did. This stops a few weakly wired regions from dominating. We give every pattern the same size, so the numbers can be compared.

Pattern four has a roughness of zero point five four. Shuffle the same numbers among the regions two thousand times, and the average roughness is one point zero one. Pattern four is about half as rough as chance. Not one of the two thousand shuffles is as smooth.

Patterns two and three also beat every shuffle. So do patterns one, five and six. The weakest is pattern five, at seven tenths of chance.

Now take the harmonics, as in episode thirteen point two. They come from the same normalized wiring. The harmonics of this graph are maps too. The first one just follows how much wiring each region has. The ones after it vary slowly across the brain. The second one mostly separates the left hemisphere from the right.

Count the eight smoothest harmonics after the first, a tenth of the spectrum. A map pointing at random would put about ten percent of its energy there. Our six patterns put between thirty-one and fifty-five percent.

Pattern three puts twenty-nine percent of its energy on a single harmonic, the fourth. The match between the two maps is a correlation of zero point five. So the wiring does not explain the whole pattern. But it keeps a large part of it.

Others have done this at a finer scale. Atasoy, Donnelly and Pearson, in two thousand sixteen, used ten people from the Human Connectome Project. Their graph had twenty thousand points on the cortex, joined to their neighbours and by long fibres. They called its eigenvectors connectome harmonics. They compared them with seven resting-state networks. The default mode network matched one harmonic, the ninth, give or take two. This is reported by them, not recomputed here.

Huang and colleagues, in two thousand sixteen, cut the activity of thirty-eight volunteers into slow and fast graph frequencies. The volunteers were learning a finger sequence. Their graph came from functional links, not from fibres. Smooth patterns went with better learning while the task was new. Spiky patterns went with better learning once it was familiar. Huang and colleagues reviewed the field in two thousand eighteen.

Now the best result. Preti and Van De Ville, in two thousand nineteen, used fifty-six volunteers from the Human Connectome Project, and three hundred sixty cortical regions. They split brain activity in two. One part is made of the smooth harmonics. It follows the fibres. The other is made of the rough harmonics. It does not.

The cut sits where the two halves hold equal energy, at harmonic twenty-one. For each region, the ratio of the two parts is the structural decoupling index. It tells how far that region's activity breaks away from the wiring.

Sensory regions follow the fibres. Visual, auditory and somatomotor activity is strongly coupled to the structure. Orbitofrontal, temporal and parietal regions break away.

Order the regions by this index, and a gradient appears. It runs from the senses to abstract thought. It resembles a gradient drawn from functional connectivity by Margulies and colleagues. A simple split into graph frequencies draws a map of the brain's hierarchy. Again, this is reported by the authors, not computed by us.

One question is left. Our chance was a shuffle. It keeps the numbers and scrambles the regions. But close regions look alike anyway. A shuffle destroys that too. So what should chance keep fixed?

To sum up. Brain activity on the connectome is a signal on a graph. All six of our patterns are smoother on the fibres than chance. Smooth harmonics hold a large share of them. And in the best result, the share that breaks away from the wiring separates the senses from association.

Testing on a graph

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. Our chance was a shuffle. It keeps the numbers and scrambles the regions.

But close regions look alike anyway. A shuffle throws that away too. So what should chance keep fixed?

Here is one answer, from Pirondini, Vybornova, Coscia and Van De Ville, in two thousand sixteen. Keep the smoothness of the signal. Change everything else.

The recipe uses the harmonics of episode thirteen point two. Write the signal as a sum of harmonics. Each harmonic has a coefficient. Its size says how much of the signal lives at that frequency.

Now toss a coin for each harmonic. Heads, the coefficient keeps its sign. Tails, its sign flips. Then add the harmonics back up.

The result is a new map, and its values are different. But every harmonic has exactly the same size as before. So the spectrum is the same. The authors call such maps surrogates.

Take pattern one from last time. Its first ten harmonics carry forty-five percent of its energy. Toss a coin for these ten, and for the other seventy-three. The new map looks nothing like the old one. The correlation between their values is minus zero point zero six.

Yet the roughness is the same. Roughness adds up the squared gaps between linked regions, each value divided by the square root of its region's wiring. For pattern one it is zero point six eight. For the surrogate it is identical, up to rounding error.

That is no accident. Roughness is the energy of each harmonic, its size squared, times its frequency, added up. Signs never enter it. So surrogates cannot test smoothness. They keep it by construction.

And this is the point of the episode. What chance keeps fixed defines the question you can ask. A shuffle keeps the values, so it asks whether a map is smoother than a scramble. A surrogate keeps the smoothness, so it must ask something else.

Here is something else. Does a pattern line up with another map? Take the position of each region along one direction of the head, from front to back. That is a map too. It is smooth on the wiring: its roughness is zero point four eight, against about one for a shuffle.

Pattern one lines up with it. The correlation is zero point four two. Under a shuffle, the chance correlation averages zero point zero nine. Out of ten thousand shuffles, none reaches zero point four two. The scramble says: significant.

Now draw ten thousand surrogates instead. Each is as smooth as pattern one. Two smooth maps match by accident far more often. The chance correlation averages zero point two three. And nineteen percent of the surrogates reach zero point four two or more. The same match is no longer surprising.

We did this for all six patterns and three directions: left to right, front to back, and down to up. That makes eighteen tests. At five percent, the shuffle finds seven significant. The surrogates find four. Three verdicts fall, and none goes the other way. We did not correct for running eighteen tests.

The shuffle had set the bar too low. For front to back, the bar a correlation must pass rises, on average, from zero point two two to zero point four.

The surrogate does not say the match is unreal. It says the evidence is weaker than the shuffle suggested. Its question is also narrower: given this much smoothness, is anything else going on?

Chapter ten asked the same thing about networks. A reference network could keep the degrees, or the weights and the space. Here the surrogate keeps the spectrum. Each choice defines the hypothesis.

Dong and colleagues, in two thousand sixteen and again in two thousand nineteen, work in the other direction. They start from signals, and learn the graph that makes them smooth.

The Statistics series asks the same question for signals in time.

To sum up. Keeping the values tests smoothness. Keeping the smoothness, with a spectral surrogate, tests whether a pattern matches something beyond it. The thing held fixed is the hypothesis. Here, it turned seven significant results into four.
