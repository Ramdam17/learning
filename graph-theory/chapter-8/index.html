PLATE 8.14:32

Dense inside, sparse between

Zachary's karate club of thirty-four members split in two, and the question is whether the links alone predict who joined which side. Sixty-seven of the seventy-eight links stay inside a camp. Removing the most strained links breaks the club near the real line, yet cliques and other methods give different groups. A community always depends on what a method means by more.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Nineteen seventy. A karate club at an American university. It has an instructor, whom the study calls Mister Hi. And it has an administrator, John A. Both names are pseudonyms.

Then the two of them fall out. Every member is pulled in. Some side with the instructor. Others side with the administrator. In the end, the club splits in two.

A researcher, Wayne Zachary, was watching. For three years, from nineteen seventy to nineteen seventy-two, he noted who spent time together outside the club. He published the result in nineteen seventy-seven. In the version everyone uses, there are thirty-four members and seventy-eight links.

Here is the question. From the links alone, could you have predicted who joins which side?

A dojo does not look like a graph. But put a point on each member. Draw a line between two who spent time together. This is the hidden graph.

Now colour each member by the side they really joined. Seventeen followed Mister Hi. Seventeen sided with the administrator.

Look at the links. Sixty-seven stay inside a camp. Only eleven cross between camps. Inside a camp, about one pair in four is linked. Between the camps, about one pair in twenty-six.

Dense inside, sparse between. That is the idea of a community. A group of nodes more linked to each other than to the rest.

We met this picture in chapter one, as a table. First, the club in the order of its member numbers. Now sort the members by camp. Two dense blocks sit on the diagonal. Eighty-six percent of the links fall inside a block.

But nobody gave us the camps in advance. Can the links find them?

In two thousand two, Girvan and Newman proposed a way. Picture a patchwork of thirty-four patches, stitched together along the links. Every pair of members sends a message by the shortest routes. A stitch is strained by the routes that cross it.

Chapter six counted this at a person. Here we count it at a link. A link that joins two camps is the way through for many pairs, so it carries a lot.

Take a seam ripper to the most strained stitch. The first to go joins member one and member thirty-two. It carries about seventy-one of the five hundred sixty-one pairs.

Then count again. The strains change. Rip the most strained stitch again. And again. Stop when the patchwork falls in two.

It takes eleven removals. The two pieces have fifteen members and nineteen. Now compare them with the real camps. Thirty-two members land on the right side. Two do not, members three and nine.

Five of the eleven removed links joined different camps. Six did not. The method still broke the club close to the real line. Zachary's own prediction, a minimum cut like the one in chapter three, missed only member nine.

So have we found the communities? Not so fast. We asked for groups more linked inside than outside. More than what?

Here is one answer. Every member must have more links inside the group than outside. Radicchi and colleagues made that a definition in two thousand four.

Test it on the real camps. Member nine has two links inside and three outside. Members ten and thirty-one tie. By this strict test, the camps people chose are not quite communities.

A second answer is stricter still. Everyone in the group is linked to everyone. That is a clique. The largest clique here has five members. The camps have seventeen.

A third answer is the one we just used. Rip out the busiest links, and see what falls apart. It gives fifteen and nineteen.

A fourth answer scores a split against what chance would give, then looks for the best score. On this club, one such method finds three groups, of seventeen, nine and eight.

Later episodes add more answers. Models where each group has its own linking rate. Walkers who get stuck inside groups.

Same club. Same links. Two groups, three groups, or a clique of five, depending on what you mean by more. There is no single definition of a community. Each method has its own more than what.

To sum up. A community is a group more linked inside than outside. Sort a table by group, and blocks appear. Remove the busiest links, and the network breaks along its seams. But always ask the method what it means by more.

Next time, one question. How do we give a split a score? Modularity.

PLATE 8.25:35

Modularity

Modularity scores a split as the share of links inside groups minus the share expected when links are drawn at random with degrees kept. The karate club's real camps score 0.358. Louvain climbs toward high scores and Leiden keeps groups connected. Random networks also score high, between 0.30 and 0.41, so a modularity value must be compared with random networks.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, one club gave us several answers. Two camps. Three groups, of seventeen, nine and eight. Even a clique of five.

Here is a sharper question. Take two splits of the same club. Which one is better? And by how much?

We need a score. A number that says how well a split keeps friends together.

First try. Count the links that stay inside a group. For the real camps, that is sixty-seven of seventy-eight. Eighty-six percent.

But now put all thirty-four members in one group. Every link is inside. A hundred percent. That cannot be a good split.

So counting is not enough. We must ask how many links would stay inside by chance.

Here is a tombola. Cut every link in two. Each member keeps the halves that were theirs. There are one hundred fifty-six halves in all, because two times seventy-eight is one hundred fifty-six.

Pour them into the drum. Shake. Draw two halves and glue them into a new link. Draw again, until the drum is empty.

Every member keeps the same degree. A popular member has many halves in the drum, so it is drawn often. That is what chance means here. Links drawn at random, degrees kept.

Now count the new links that fall inside the two camps. In this draw, forty-one. Over two thousand draws, about thirty-nine on average. About half.

We can get this without drawing. Mister Hi's camp holds eighty-one of the one hundred fifty-six halves. Its chance share of links is eighty-one over one hundred fifty-six, squared. Twenty-seven percent. The other camp, twenty-three percent. Together, fifty percent.

The score is the gap. Eighty-six percent inside in the real club. Fifty percent in the tombola. The difference is zero point three six.

That is modularity, from Newman and Girvan, two thousand four. The share of links inside the groups, minus the share you would expect at random, keeping the degrees.

Everyone in one group scores zero. A hundred percent inside, a hundred percent by chance. Everyone alone scores below zero.

Now compare splits. The real camps score zero point three five eight. The Girvan and Newman split of last time, zero point three six zero. Three groups of seventeen, nine and eight, zero point three eight.

The camps are not the top score. The score does not ask who joined which side. It only asks how the links are arranged. A good split keeps far more links inside than chance would.

Can we search for the best score? There are far too many splits to try them all. So we climb.

That is the Louvain method, from Blondel and colleagues, two thousand eight. Start with every member alone. Visit a member. Try moving it into a neighbour's group. If the score goes up, keep the move. Go round again, until no move helps.

Then the second idea. Each group becomes one big node, and the links between groups are bundled. Start again, now merging groups.

On the club, the first round ends with seven groups, scoring zero point three seven. The second round merges them into four, of twelve, eleven, six and five members. Zero point four two.

The visits come in random order, so we fix a seed. Another order can give another answer.

Louvain has a flaw. When a member moves out, the group it leaves can fall in pieces, and nothing glues them back. Traag, Waltman and van Eck showed this in two thousand nineteen. Some groups end up badly connected inside. Some are even disconnected. In their tests, up to a quarter were badly connected, and up to sixteen percent were disconnected.

Picture a group of seven. One member links two triangles. That member leaves. Two triangles remain under one name, with no link between them.

The Leiden method adds a step that splits each group into well-connected parts. It guarantees connected groups.

Now, a warning. Louvain scores zero point four two on the club. Is that a lot?

Test it on networks with no communities at all. Draw a random network with thirty-four nodes and seventy-eight links, the same size as the club. Links placed anywhere, by chance. Run Louvain ten times, keep the best.

Here are twelve of them. Each one gets groups. Each one gets a score, between zero point three four and zero point four one.

Now five hundred. The scores pile up around zero point three six. The lowest is zero point three zero. The highest, zero point four one.

Chance has communities. Guimerà, Sales-Pardo and Amaral showed in two thousand four that random networks have modularity.

Remember the real camps, zero point three five eight. The best split of an average random network scores as high.

The club's zero point four two sits above all five hundred. So here, the comparison supports real structure. But not by a mile.

A modularity value proves nothing alone. Compare it with comparable random networks. Chapter ten builds those references with care.

To sum up. Modularity is the share of links inside groups, minus the share expected by chance, degrees kept. Louvain climbs towards a high score by merging groups. Leiden keeps the groups connected. And always compare the score with random networks.

Next time, one question. Can a score miss a group that is plainly there?

PLATE 8.34:59

Too coarse to see

In a ring of thirty five-member cliques, modularity prefers merging neighbouring cliques in pairs, although thirty groups are plainly there. This is the resolution limit. A gamma knob changes the grain, and the karate club yields two, three, four, five, seven or twelve groups depending on it. Many different partitions also score almost as well as the best.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. Can a score miss a group that is plainly there? It can. Here is one, and nothing about it is hidden.

Picture a necklace. Thirty small groups, set in a ring. Each group has five members, and every member knows the other four. A single link joins it to the group on its left, and another to the group on its right.

Nobody would argue about this one. There are thirty groups, and you can count them.

Now score two splits with modularity. In the first, each tight group is a group. In the second, neighbours are merged in pairs.

Begin with a short necklace, ten groups. One group per clique scores zero point eight zero nine. Pairs score zero point seven five five. The natural split wins, as it should.

Now the long necklace, thirty groups. Here, one group per clique scores zero point eight seven six. Merged in pairs, they reach zero point eight eight eight. Pairs win.

Same groups, longer necklace, and the score changes its mind.

Where does it flip? Compute both scores for every even length. Up to twenty groups, the natural split wins. At twenty-two, a tie. From twenty-four on, pairs win.

Why? Merging two groups pays when the real links between them beat the links chance would put there. Two neighbours share one real link.

Chance is worked out on the whole network. It expects the degree total of one group, times the degree total of the other, divided by twice the number of links. For a clique here, the degree total is twenty-two.

In the necklace of ten, chance puts two point two links between neighbours. One real link is less than chance. Make it thirty, and chance puts only zero point seven three. Now one real link beats chance. At twenty-two, chance puts exactly one.

Fortunato and Barthélemy described this in two thousand seven. Their example is the same necklace, thirty cliques of five, with the same scores. Their rule says the natural split wins when five times four, plus two, is larger than the number of cliques. That is twenty-two, as we found. We also checked it for cliques of three to seven members.

The score cannot see groups below a size set by the whole network, not by the group. They called it the resolution limit.

Does a real optimiser fall for it? Run Louvain fifty times, with fixed seeds. Not one run returns the thirty groups. Each run returns between fifteen and eighteen, always made of whole cliques. The best scores zero point eight eight eight, the same as pairs.

There is a knob. Reichardt and Bornholdt added it in two thousand six, and called it gamma. It sets the weight of the chance term. Gamma of one is plain modularity. A larger gamma counts chance more heavily, and asks for smaller groups.

Turn it on the necklace. At zero point two five, we get nine groups. Gamma one gives sixteen. Raise it to one and a half, and the thirty cliques appear. The flip is at gamma one point three six.

Now the club. A gamma of zero point two gives one single group. Zero point three, two groups. Zero point eight, three. One, four. One and a half, five. Two, seven. Three, twelve.

Each of these is the best partition Louvain found for its own setting, among ten seeded runs. Whether one is defensible depends on the grain you wanted. The data alone does not choose gamma.

So how many communities does the club have? Two, three, four, five, seven, twelve. There is no single answer.

Even at one setting, the best partition is not alone. Good, de Montjoye and Clauset showed in two thousand ten that modularity has many near-equivalent solutions. They can disagree about the largest groups.

See it here. On the club, one thousand seeded Louvain runs gave nineteen different partitions, scoring from zero point three eight five to zero point four two. Three lie within one percent of the best. All three have four groups, and they are not identical. For the club, that set is small.

On the necklace, it is huge. Take every partition made of single cliques and neighbouring pairs. There are one million, eight hundred sixty thousand, four hundred ninety-eight of them. Each merged pair adds the same small gain. All of them score between zero point eight seven six and zero point eight eight eight. About eight hundred fifty-two thousand lie within half a hundredth of the best.

To sum up. Modularity measures chance on the whole network, so in a long necklace it merges groups that belong apart. Gamma sets the grain, and each grain gives its own partition. And many partitions score almost as well as the best.

PLATE 8.45:25

Blocks that generate the graph

A stochastic block model gives each node a colour and a table of link chances, and fitting it backwards recovers hidden blocks: dense groups, a core with a periphery, or two kinds of nodes. On the karate club the plain model sorts members by popularity instead of camps. Giving each node its own popularity, the degree-corrected model leaves two members on the wrong side.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. What if, instead of hunting for dense groups, we asked which wiring rule could have drawn this graph?

Here is such a rule. Give every node a colour. That colour is its block. Then write a small table. For each pair of colours, it gives the chance that two nodes with those colours are linked.

Take sixteen nodes, in two blocks of eight. Inside a block, the chance is seventy-five percent. Between the blocks, it is ten percent.

Now draw the graph. Go through all one hundred and twenty pairs. For each pair, look up the two colours, and roll a die. The die says link, or no link. This seeded run ended with forty-four links.

That is the stochastic block model. Holland, Laskey and Leinhardt published it in nineteen eighty-three. One rule, and every run of the dice gives a different graph. This is one of them.

Now run it backwards. You are given only the graph. No colours, and the nodes in any order. Which colours, and which table, make this graph most likely?

Two things make this easy to attack. For any colouring you try, the best table is simply what you observe: the fraction of linked pairs, inside each block and between each two blocks. So only the colouring is left to search. And each colouring gets one score, the likelihood of the graph under its own best table.

The search is plain. Start from a random colouring. Move one node at a time to the colour that raises the score most. Stop when no move helps. Do this from three hundred random starts, and keep the best.

Here is the graph as a grid, with rows in scrambled order. No pattern. Now sort the rows and columns by the colours we found. Two dense squares appear. All sixteen nodes land in their planted block. The table it reads back says eighty-two percent and sixty-four percent inside, and five percent between. A random colouring scores about minus ninety-three. The best colouring scores minus fifty-four.

So far, the blocks were communities. They did not have to be.

Keep the idea, change the table. Here are three simulated graphs, thirty-six nodes each. In the first, links stay inside blocks. In the second, twelve core nodes link densely to each other, and to the periphery, while the twenty-four periphery nodes rarely link among themselves. In the third, links run almost only between the two blocks, as in a graph with two kinds of nodes.

We run the same fit, with no change. It finds every node's block, in all three. Sort the grids, and look. Squares on the diagonal. An L shape, with a dense corner. And two blocks off the diagonal.

These are the patterns of the grid from episode one point five. A block is not a dense group. A block is a set of nodes that wire the same way to everyone.

A score that rewards dense groups disagrees about the last two. Modularity of the planted split is zero point four four in the first graph, minus zero point one one in the second, and minus zero point four three in the third. Fewer links inside than chance.

Now a catch, and it is a real one. Take Zachary's karate club, and ask the plain model for two blocks.

It does not find the camps. It puts five members in one block: members one, two, three, thirty-three and thirty-four. Each has nine links or more. The other twenty-nine have six at most. The real camps score minus two hundred sixty-seven point six. This split scores minus two hundred thirty-three point five. Higher is better, so the fit prefers popularity.

Karrer and Newman saw the same thing in two thousand eleven. The plain model splits the club into a group of high-degree nodes and a group of low-degree ones.

Why? In the plain model, a pair of blocks has one chance, whoever is in them. But two popular nodes are more likely to be linked just because each has many links. The cheapest way to copy that is to put the popular nodes together.

The fix is to give each node its own popularity number. The chance of a link now grows with both popularities, and the table only has to explain what popularity does not. This is the degree-corrected model.

Fit it to the club. Seventeen members on each side. Two are on the wrong side, members nine and ten. Karrer and Newman report one. Putting member ten back gives a split that scores almost the same.

They also tried it on political blogs. That network has twelve hundred and twenty-two blogs, the largest connected piece, with links read as undirected. Compared with the liberal and conservative labels, the corrected model reaches a normalized mutual information of zero point seven two. The plain model gets zero point zero zero zero one. We did not rerun this one. These are their numbers.

One limit. We told the model how many blocks to find, two. Choosing that number is a separate problem.

To sum up. A block model is a wiring rule: a colour for each node, and a table of chances. Fit it, and the blocks need not be communities. Without the degree correction, it may sort nodes by popularity alone.

PLATE 8.56:16

Is there a true partition?

Normalized mutual information compares found groups with a label. On the karate club the same two methods swap verdicts when the label changes from camp to popularity. On the college football network the match with conferences is 0.88, and correcting the recorded conferences raises it to 0.93. Labels are not ground truth, so a module always comes with the method that found it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we ended on a question. When the groups we find are compared with a label we already know, do they match?

A label is a name attached to each node, written down by someone, for some reason. The camp a karate student joined. The conference of a football team. The department of a researcher. The functional system of a brain region.

To compare groups with a label, use one number. It is called normalized mutual information. It is one when the two groupings are the same. It is close to zero when knowing one tells you almost nothing about the other.

Start with Zachary's karate club, and its label: the camp each member joined. Put that label on a sheet, and lay the sheet over the club.

Now lay over it the groups of the degree-corrected block model from last time. The score is zero point six eight. Two members sit on the wrong side, members nine and ten.

The method of Girvan and Newman, from the start of the chapter, scores zero point seven three. Its two members are three and nine. Louvain finds four groups, and scores zero point six zero. The plain block model scores almost nothing, zero point zero one. It had found the hubs, not the camps.

Member nine is wrong for two methods. Peel, Larremore and Clauset tell why. During the dispute, member nine supported the president. But he was two weeks from his black belt exam, and he joined the instructor's faction. The label records what he did. The links may say something else.

Now the move that matters most. Swap the label. Instead of the camp, use a label that follows popularity: the five members with nine links or more. This label is ours, invented for the demonstration.

The plain model, which failed against the camps, now scores one. The degree-corrected model, which scored zero point six eight, now scores almost nothing. Same network. Same two methods. Opposite verdicts.

That is the point of their paper. For the club, they show that the block model has two good answers. One lies near the factions. The other splits leaders from followers. The label decides which one looks like a success.

A bigger example. The American college football network has one hundred fifteen teams and six hundred thirteen games, played in the fall of two thousand. The label is each team's conference.

Louvain finds ten groups in the run shown. Over a hundred seeds it finds eight to ten, and the match with the conferences runs from zero point eight one to zero point eight nine. Shuffle the conferences, and the match falls to about zero point two one. In the run shown the score is zero point eight eight, and fifteen teams sit outside the group of their conference.

Look at those fifteen. Only seventeen percent of their games are inside the conference they carry. For all teams, it is sixty-four percent.

The reason is a footnote of the paper, about this very dataset. The conferences in the file were recorded in a different season from the games. Evans checked in twenty ten, and published a fix. Twenty-two teams change conference.

Put the corrected label on the sheet. The match rises to zero point nine three. Fourteen teams still disagree. Eight are independents, teams with no conference at all. The other six form the Big West, which Louvain merged with the eight teams of the Mountain West.

So there are three kinds of disagreement. A wrong label. A label that is not a group. And a method that may be too coarse. The match alone cannot tell them apart.

This is the argument of the paper. Metadata are not ground truth. When a method fails to recover them, there are four possible reasons. The label may be irrelevant to the structure. The label and the groups may capture different structure. The network may have no structure to find. Or the method may be poor.

They add two results. One network can come from several different processes, each with its own true groups, so the true groups of a single network are not unique. And averaged over all possible problems, no method does better than another. This is a no free lunch theorem. It does not make community detection useless, because a method can still win on a narrower family of problems.

They also propose a test of whether a label is related to the structure at all. In a law firm's friendship network, the office label is strongly tied to the links, with a p-value below one in a million. The law school label is not significant, with a p-value of zero point one three. They warn that a p-value measures significance, not strength.

The same warning applies to the brain. A region has a label, its functional system. A method gives modules. The review of Sporns and Betzel surveys the many methods now used to find modules in brain networks, with the advantages and shortcomings of each. So a module comes with the method that found it.

See a choice at work on a real neural network. Not a human one: the wiring of the worm C. elegans. Keep every connection of at least one synapse, and Louvain finds six groups. At least two synapses, seven. At least three, nine. At least five, thirteen.

On the neurons that stay in the largest piece throughout, the partition at five synapses matches the first one with a score of only zero point four three. A worm is not a brain, and this is one method with one seed. But in this chapter we have now seen each choice move the groups: the method on the club, the resolution in episode eight point three, the threshold on the worm.

So, is there a true partition? Not one to recover. There are partitions, and each answers a question. Where the links are dense. Which wiring a block model reads. What a label records.

To close the chapter: a community is a group found by a method, for a reason. One more label is one more answer, never the answer. Communities that change in time wait in chapter fifteen.
