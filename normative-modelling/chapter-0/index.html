You are not the average

Pencil marks on a door frame become dots on a growth chart, and each mark gains meaning once a crowd of other children stands behind it. A growth chart places a child inside a population. The same move applied to brain measures gives a norm, a deviation for one person, and an uncertainty that always travels with it.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Look at this door frame. It is covered in pencil marks, one above the other. Each mark has a name and an age. Lea, four years old. Lea, five years old. Lea, six years old.

A pencil mark on wood says only one thing. How tall she was, on that day.

Now the marks lift off the wood. They fly out, and settle on a curve. It is a growth chart. Age along the bottom, height up the side.

And something changes. Behind each mark, a crowd appears. Thousands of other children, each one a grey dot.

Suddenly the mark says much more. It no longer says tall, or short. It says: taller than this many children of her age. A growth chart does not describe a child. It places a child inside a population.

This series is about doing exactly that for the brain.

The chart changes. Age still runs along the bottom. But the side now shows a measure of the brain, such as the thickness of the cortex. And every dot is a small brain, seen from the side.

A pink line runs through the middle. That is the median. Blue lines mark the edges, where almost everyone falls. Beside the chart stands a tall, smiling ruler. Her name is Centi. She is the norm. She is the reference model, and she knows what to expect at every age.

The idea is not new. In 2016, Marquand and colleagues proposed it for psychiatry, and compared it to growth charts. Six years later, Bethlehem and colleagues built brain charts for the whole lifespan. They pooled nearly one hundred and twenty-four thousand scans, from more than one hundred thousand people, from before birth up to a hundred years of age.

It works for any measure you can follow across ages. An MRI measure, a band of the EEG, a signal from fNIRS.

Now comes the question that drives the whole series.

Picture a typical paper that compares patients with healthy controls. Its headline reads: patients have a thinner cortex, on average. That sentence is about two groups. It tells you nothing about the person sitting in front of you.

A normative model asks something else. It says: this person is below the first centile for their age. Here, on the chart. And here is how uncertain we are.

Watch. A round pin drops onto the board. Its name is Pin. Pin is one individual, not a group. It lands low in the crowd, below the lower blue line. Under it, a small label tells you where this crowd comes from. Simulated. In this picture, the measure is a fictional index in arbitrary units, for four hundred and fifty simulated people, aged from eight to eighty.

Around Pin, a thin blue mist appears. That is our uncertainty. A deviation is never just a number. It comes with a doubt.

That gives us the loop of the series. First, a norm, built from a reference population. Then, a deviation, for one person. And then, the hard part: what we do with it.

We will walk this loop one step at a time, and learn where a norm can mislead us.

So, to sum up. A group average tells you about a group. A norm lets you place one person, with their uncertainty. You are not the average.
