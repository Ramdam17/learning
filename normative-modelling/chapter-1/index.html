PLATE 1.14:49

The myth of the average patient

A shoe shop stocked only with the average size fits nobody, and the same holds for patients. In an air force survey of 4,063 men, none was average on all ten measurements. Three simulated patient groups can share one mean difference yet look completely different, so only individual positions reveal who the average patient is.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the teaser, we saw that you are not the average. Today we ask a harder question. What happens to a patient when we forget it?

Welcome to a shoe shop. Picture the shelf. Every pair on it is the average size of the whole town. Not too big, not too small. The one size that is, on average, right.

Customers come in. One has long, narrow feet. One has short, wide feet. A child needs something tiny. Nobody can wear the average shoe. They all leave barefoot.

The shop did nothing wrong in its arithmetic. The average size is real. It is a number we can compute. But no customer is standing on it.

This is an old trap. In the early nineteen fifties, the United States Air Force had a survey of its flying personnel. Four thousand and sixty-three men, each measured in over a hundred ways. A lieutenant named Gilbert Daniels, at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio, asked a simple question. How many of these men are average?

He picked ten body measurements. Height. Chest. Sleeve length. Waist. Thigh. And five more. For each one, he called a man average if he fell in the middle thirty percent of the group. A generous definition.

Then he counted. On height alone, one man in four was average. Add the chest, the sleeve, and the rest, one measurement at a time, and the crowd melts. After three measurements, fewer than four men in a hundred are left. After nine, only two men.

And after all ten? Not one. Zero men out of four thousand and sixty-three were average on every measurement. Daniels wrote it up in December nineteen fifty-two. The average man, he warned, is a pitfall for anyone designing for people.

Keep that picture. We are going to move it from bodies to brains.

Now we turn to a research study. Two clouds of points. Each dot is one person, with one brain measure. The grey cloud is the control group. The pink cloud is the patient group. Both clouds are simulated. Every number here is made up for the lesson, and the screen says so.

Each cloud has its mean, a bright marker in the middle. The two markers do not sit in the same place. The patients are lower, on average.

Now run the test. With enough people, even a small gap in the means comes out significant. The paper says: patients differ from controls. Then the paper describes the typical patient, the one at the pink marker.

Here is the catch. Look at the two clouds again. They overlap. A lot. Many patients sit right inside the controls. The significant gap tells us about the groups. It does not tell us that every patient is shifted.

So the question for today is this. Who is standing on the pink marker?

Time for the key moment. Let us build three different groups of simulated patients. Three stories. We will build each one so that its mean lands in exactly the same place.

In the first story, every patient is a little lower than the controls. A small shift for everyone. The mean is the shift. In this story, the average patient really exists. Many patients sit close to the marker.

In the second story, most patients look just like the controls. But a few are very far below. Those few are enough to drag the mean down. Now the marker sits in a place where almost no patient is. Most are above it. A handful are far below.

In the third story, there are two subgroups, pulled in opposite directions. One is stronger, and it wins the tug of war. The mean lands in the gap between the two groups. And almost nobody lives in the gap. The average patient is nobody.

Three very different realities. One and the same difference between group means. The test cannot tell them apart. The mean cannot tell them apart. Only the individual points can.

Remember the shoe shop. Three towns can have the same average shoe size and completely different feet. If you sell only the average shoe, you will fail all three towns in different ways.

A group difference is a fact about the group. It can be true, significant, and published, while hardly any patient looks like the patient the paper describes. In the Information series, you meet the idea that every summary throws information away. The mean is the roughest summary of all.

So, to sum up. A difference in means can be significant even when almost no patient resembles the average patient. To help the person in front of us, we need to see where that person sits, not only where the group sits.

Next time, we take one diagnosis. One label. And we look inside many brains that share it.

PLATE 1.25:11

One label, many brains

One diagnostic label, like one hiking-boot order, covers very different people. Depression criteria allow hundreds of symptom combinations, and two patients may share no symptom at all. Normative deviation maps across six conditions show that any single brain region deviates in under seven percent of people with the same diagnosis, though deviations often fall within shared circuits.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we met the average patient, and found that almost nobody looks like them. A difference between two group means can hide very different people. Today we ask a harder question. What if the people share a diagnosis, and still share almost nothing else?

Back in the shoe shop. A line of customers waits at the counter. Every one of them says the same thing. "I would like a hiking boot, please."

The same words. The same label. And yet the first customer leaves with a heavy leather boot. The second takes a light trail shoe. The third wants a high ankle, and the fourth, a wide toe box. The label told the seller what kind of walk it was. It did not tell the seller what to put on the foot.

A diagnosis works a bit like that label. It names a kind of trouble. It does not say what the trouble looks like in each person. Let us look at the diagnostic criteria, and count.

Take major depression, as the fifth edition of the DSM defines it. The manual lists nine symptoms. A person qualifies with at least five of them, and one of the five must be low mood, or loss of interest.

Some of those symptoms come in two opposite forms. Sleep can go down, or it can go up. Appetite and weight can fall, or rise. A person can be slowed down, or restless.

So picture two people. The first feels low, sleeps too little, loses weight, and feels agitated. The second has lost interest in everything, sleeps too much, gains weight, and feels slowed down. Each adds one more symptom, and those differ too. Both receive the same diagnosis. And they do not share a single symptom. Fried and Nesse state it plainly. Two people who qualify may not have one symptom in common.

This is not a trick of one clever pair. The same authors counted. Using only the nine criteria, there are two hundred and twenty-seven different ways to qualify for the diagnosis. Then they looked at real people. In a study of three thousand seven hundred and three outpatients, they used a finer list of twelve symptoms, with the opposite forms counted apart, and without the rule of five. That list allows four thousand and ninety-six possible profiles. They found one thousand and thirty different profiles. About half of those profiles belonged to exactly one person.

One label. Many people.

Now, from symptoms to brains. If the symptoms differ so much, do the brains at least agree?

Here are the brain maps of several simulated patients. They are simulated, not real people, and the screen says so. All of them carry the same diagnosis. Each map shows where that person's grey matter volume sits above or below what we would expect for their age. Pink is more than expected. Blue is less.

Look closely. A pink patch at the front for one person. A blue patch at the back for another. A third map, almost quiet. Lay the maps over each other, and the patches barely touch. The group average would paint a faint, smooth shadow. No single person looks like that shadow.

Is this just our drawing? No. This is the key moment of the episode.

In twenty twenty-three, Segal and colleagues published a study in Nature Neuroscience. They gathered one thousand two hundred and ninety-four people with one of six conditions: attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism, bipolar disorder, depression, obsessive compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia. They also gathered one thousand four hundred and sixty-five matched controls.

For each person, they used a normative model. The model says what grey matter volume to expect in each region, for someone of that age. Then it measures how far each person departs from that expectation.

The result is striking. Take any one region of the brain. Among people sharing the same diagnosis, that region was deviant in fewer than seven percent of them. Fewer than seven people in a hundred, with the same label, off in the same place.

But the story does not end in noise. The scattered deviations did not land just anywhere. They fell within shared functional circuits and networks, in up to fifty-six percent of cases. Two people may be affected in different places, yet in places that belong to the same circuit.

That changes how we read heterogeneity. It is not measurement noise to be averaged away. It is the rule. Different locations, perhaps common circuits. And you can only see that by placing each person on their own, against a reference population.

A word of care. These numbers belong to this study, these six conditions, and this measure. They do not say that every disorder, or every measure, behaves the same way.

So, to sum up. One diagnostic label covers many people, in symptoms and in brains, and the group average erases them. Heterogeneity is not noise. It is the signal we need a better tool to read.

Next time, we put the tool in the seller's hand. From comparing groups, to placing a person.

PLATE 1.35:26

From comparing groups to placing a person

The case-control design compares two shelves, patients and controls, and assumes each is a well-defined group. A normative model instead measures every person against a whole reference population. Four cohort scenarios (one cluster, subgroups, a continuum, a mixture) look identical in a group mean but differ when each person is placed individually.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we met two people with the same diagnosis and almost nothing in common. One label, many brains. So here is the question for today. If patients differ this much from each other, what are we really doing when we compare them to a group of controls?

Back in the shoe shop. The salesman has two shelves. One says patients. One says controls. Every customer who walks in is sent to one shelf or the other, and the whole job is to compare the two shelves.

That is the case-control design. It is a good design. It asks one clear question. Do these two groups differ, on average? Often the answer is yes, and that answer is real.

But look at what the design has quietly assumed. It assumes that the patients form one group, and the controls form another, and that each group is a well-defined thing. A box with a label on it.

Now the salesman has a better idea. He puts the two shelves away, and takes out a tape measure.

He measures every customer, not against the other shelf, but against the whole clientele. This customer's foot is longer than most feet of her age. That customer's foot is near the middle. Nobody is sent to a shelf. Each person is simply placed.

That is the normative approach. We build a reference from many people. We learn how a measure varies with age, and with whatever else matters. Then for any new person, we ask where they fall, and how sure we are.

Let us see why this matters. Picture a clinical cohort, and think of four ways it could be built. These four pictures follow the ones drawn by Andre Marquand and colleagues, in twenty sixteen. Every dot here is illustrative, not a real measurement.

Scenario one. A well-defined group. The patients really do form one cluster, shifted away from the controls. All of them are a bit different, in the same direction. Here the case-control design works well. The group average is a fair summary of every patient.

Scenario two. Subgroups. The patients split into several clusters, each with its own kind of difference. One group is shifted up. Another is shifted down. Each cluster is a distinct pathology, and the label has glued them together.

Scenario three. A continuum. There is no gap between patients and controls at all. The disease-related variation is nested inside the healthy variation. Patients simply tend to sit a little further along the same axis, and the boundary between the two shelves is drawn by a diagnostic habit, not by nature.

Scenario four. A mixture. The clinical group is diffuse. Some people were misdiagnosed. Some have two or three conditions at once. Some are the sum of different pathologies. The label says one thing, the brains say many.

Now ask what the group average says in each scenario.

In the first, it describes everybody. In the second, it can land in a place where nobody is. Two clusters, one up and one down, can cancel out and leave an average that looks like a control. In the third, it describes a tilt of the whole crowd, not a difference between kinds of people. And in the fourth, it describes a typical patient who does not exist.

The case-control design cannot tell these four apart. It hands you the same kind of answer in every case. A difference of means.

The normative approach starts somewhere else. It does not cut the cohort into groups. It models the variation across the whole reference population, and then it puts each person inside that picture. A patient is not a member of a shelf. A patient is a point, with a position, and an uncertainty around it.

And that works in all four scenarios. If the patients really form one cluster, the positions will show it. If there are subgroups, the positions will gather in several places. If it is a continuum, they will spread along a line. The data decide what the cohort looks like. We do not decide it in advance.

Marquand and colleagues put the central point plainly. The case-control design introduces an artificial symmetry, where both cases and controls are assumed to be well-defined groups. In biology, they are often highly heterogeneous.

Here is the part that is easy to forget. The two approaches answer different questions.

Case-control asks, do these groups differ? Normative asks, where does this person sit, compared with a reference? If you want to know whether a treatment or a risk factor shifts a group, the first question is the right one. If you want to know about one person, or about how much people differ from each other, you need the second.

Neither replaces the other. One is a comparison of shelves. The other is a tape measure. A good shop keeps both, and knows which to pick up.

There is also a catch, and we will not forget it. The tape measure is only as good as the clientele it was built from. A person can only be placed among the people who were measured. That thought comes back again and again in this series.

So, to sum up. Case-control compares two groups. A normative model places a person inside a whole population, without cutting the cohort in pieces.

PLATE 1.45:23

A short history of norms

The idea of placing a person against a reference by age is almost two centuries old, from Quetelet's average man and Galton's percentiles to children's growth charts, intelligence scales and EEG norms. Brain normative models and lifespan brain charts keep that idea. What changes is the reference size, the modelling of the whole distribution, and honest uncertainty for each person.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the shoe seller put away the two shelves, patients and controls, and took out a tape measure. Every customer is measured against the whole clientele. Today we ask where that tape measure came from. Is it new?

The shop keeps a ledger, with one page per era. Let us turn the pages.

First page. Eighteen thirty-five. A Belgian astronomer and statistician, Adolphe Quetelet, publishes a book on man and the development of his faculties. He notices that human measurements cluster around a central value. He calls it the average man. A norm for a whole population.

Quetelet also leaves us a ratio, weight divided by height squared. It was renamed body mass index in nineteen seventy-two. Even then, a norm can be a single number.

Second page. Eighteen eighty-four. In London, Francis Galton opens an anthropometric laboratory at the International Health Exhibition. Visitors pay threepence to be measured. By the end, nine thousand three hundred and thirty-seven people have been measured, seventeen ways each.

In eighteen eighty-five, Galton publishes tables of heights, weights and grip strength. In them he defines a new word, percentile. The value that a given share of people fall below. The norm is no longer one centre. It is a ladder of ranks.

Third page. Children. In eighteen seventy-seven, Bowditch publishes the heights and weights of Boston schoolchildren, for the state board of health. Boys and girls are tabulated separately. So are children of American parents and children of Irish parents. A first hint that a norm belongs to a population. In eighteen ninety-one, he reworks the same data with Galton's percentile grades, from the fifth to the ninety-fifth.

Nineteen seventy-seven, again. American national charts, drawn from surveys of children, become the clinical growth charts of the United States. Then, in two thousand and six, the World Health Organization publishes growth standards. They come from eight thousand four hundred and forty healthy, breastfed children, in six countries. Brazil, Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States.

Notice the difference. The older charts describe how certain children grew, in a certain place and time. The WHO standards describe how children grow in good conditions. Two kinds of norm. We come back to them in chapter two.

Fourth page. The brain, before imaging. In nineteen thirty-nine, David Wechsler publishes an intelligence scale. Its score is a deviation from people of the same age. A norm, again, for each age.

In nineteen seventy-three, Matousek and Petersén compute an EEG age from the frequency spectrum of a child, and compare it with the real age. Four years later, in nineteen seventy-seven, Roy John gives this approach a name, neurometrics. Compare a person's EEG with a database of normal records, and measure how far it deviates. As in the EEG series, the spectrum changes with age, so the norm has to change with age too.

Fifth page. The last one, for now. In twenty sixteen, Marquand, Rezek, Buitelaar and Beckmann publish normative models for brain imaging. They say it themselves. This is like using growth charts to map child development. A model predicts a brain measure from covariates such as age, and gives a confidence for each prediction. Their example is brain activity in four hundred and ninety-one healthy people from the Human Connectome Project.

Then, in twenty twenty-two, a team led by Bethlehem and Seidlitz publishes brain charts for the human lifespan, in Nature. One hundred and twenty-three thousand, nine hundred and eighty-four scans. One hundred and one thousand, four hundred and fifty-seven people. More than a hundred studies. Ages from before birth to one hundred years. They fit the charts with a family of models called GAMLSS, and report each brain measure as a centile, the same word as on the pediatric chart.

Now the key moment. Look at the whole ledger, from the first page to the last.

The idea is old. Place a person against a reference, by age. It is already there in Quetelet, Galton, Bowditch and Wechsler.

Each page used the data of its day. Quetelet used the people he could count. Bowditch used schoolchildren of one city. The question of who is in the reference never went away, and it will come back all through this series.

So what is new? Three things. The reference populations are far larger, tens of thousands of people instead of thousands. The models describe the whole distribution at each age, its centre, its spread and its shape, and not only the average. And the uncertainty is reported for every person, instead of being swept under a single line.

So, to sum up. Norms are almost two centuries old, and the idea of placing a person against a reference has not changed. What changed is the size of the reference, the shape of the distribution, and the honesty about uncertainty.
