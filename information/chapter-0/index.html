Bits in the brain

Two signals can have zero correlation and still tell a lot about each other, which is what mutual information captures, in bits. A bit is one fair yes-or-no question answered. The series applies the same measures to EEG, heart, pupil and behavior, and warns that information is easy to compute and easy to get wrong without proper estimators and statistics.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Two signals dance on the screen. When one moves out to the left, the other rises. When it moves out to the right, the other rises too. Ask the usual question, the correlation and the answer is 0. Nothing to see.

Ask a different question. If I know the first signal, how much do I learn about the second? Now the answer is large. That question has a name, mutual information, and it is measured in a unit that fits every signal you will ever record, the bit.

This series is about that unit and about the family of measures built on it.

What is a bit? It is one fair yes or no question answered. Learn which side a coin landed on and you have gained one bit. Learn which of eight equally likely boxes holds the prize and you have gained three bits. Every measure in this series, however sophisticated, ends up counting questions like these.

Neuroscience has used these ideas for decades. To ask how many bits a neuron carries about a stimulus, which brain regions help predict each other and how integrated a network is. They are powerful because they assume so little about the signals. And for exactly the same reason, they demand care.

Information theory does not care what your signal is made of. A brain rhythm, a heartbeat, the size of a pupil, a button press, a word, and a sentence. The same question can be asked of any of them, what does this tell me about that?

So in this series you will see the same tools applied to an EEG, to a heart, to a pupil, and to behavior recorded together in the same people. And at the end, you will see them applied between two brains in conversation.

Along the way, we will build the whole family. Surprise and entropy. Divergences. Mutual information. Measures with a direction like transfer entropy. Measures that go beyond pairs, like synergy and the O information. Measures of complexity and integration.

But there is a warning that runs through every episode. Information is the easiest measure to compute, and one of the easiest to get wrong. With too little data, it finds structure in pure noise. With the wrong settings, it can produce almost any number you like.

That is why a whole part of this series is about estimators, null distributions, and statistics. We will not just define the measures. We will learn when to trust them.

Each episode holds a single idea. Some last a few minutes, some much longer because some ideas need room. Two levels of reading. A core track to follow in order. And deep dives to watch when you need them. Every measure comes with a Python notebook, so you can rebuild every number on screen. And every episode ends with a short recap and a pointer to where the idea goes next.

Welcome to the information series. We are glad you are here. Ready? Let's start with the grammar of it all, probability.
