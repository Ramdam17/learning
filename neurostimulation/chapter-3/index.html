PLATE 3.16:30

Inside the machine

A TMS machine stores energy in a capacitor and releases it through a coil in under a millisecond, and the changing magnetic field induces an electric field in the head without current through the skin. It acts mostly on axon terminals in the superficial cortex, shaped by waveform, current direction and pulse width. Percent of maximum output means nothing outside its own machine and coil.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Every TMS pulse starts in a box on a trolley, at the end of a thick cable. The box has one job. It stores energy slowly, then releases it all at once, through a coil. Today we open the box. We follow one pulse from the capacitor to the cortex, and we meet the numbers that describe it. Then we meet a trap that makes those numbers hard to compare.

In the forge, a drop hammer works in two beats. A winch lifts the weight, slowly. Then a pin is pulled, and all the stored energy lands in an instant. A TMS machine works the same way, with electricity instead of a weight. This is a picture, not a circuit.

Here is the real circuit, in three parts. First, a capacitor. It stores charge, and between pulses a charger fills it back up. Second, a switch. When it closes, the capacitor empties into the coil in a fraction of a millisecond. Third, the coil itself, a wire wound many times. According to an international clinical guide, a stimulator works with voltages from about four hundred volts to three kilovolts, and currents from four to twenty thousand amperes. The exact values depend on the device.

That current makes a magnetic field. At its peak, the field reaches one to two and a half tesla, and it lasts less than a millisecond. For comparison, a three tesla MRI scanner holds a field of that size, steadily, for the whole scan. The TMS pulse is of the same order, but it is gone in under a millisecond. Size is not what matters here. Speed is.

In 1831, Michael Faraday found that a magnetic field drives a current only while it changes. A steady field does nothing. A changing field pushes charge around in loops, and the faster the change, the stronger the push. This is electromagnetic induction. In the head, the loops follow the winding, and they run opposite to the current in the coil. The magnetic field itself crosses the scalp and skull with minimal attenuation. So no current has to enter through the skin. The tissue only feels the induced electric field. At motor threshold, as we saw in episode 2.2, that is roughly fifty to a hundred volts per metre in the cortex.

Now the shape of the pulse. The capacitor and the coil together ring like a bell. A biphasic stimulator lets them ring for one full cycle, so the pulse looks like a cosine. One measurement study tested seven coils on three stimulators. Their pulses lasted from one hundred seventy-six to three hundred fifty-three microseconds. A monophasic stimulator damps the ringing, so that a strong first phase is followed by only a small return current.

Why keep both? Because they do not act the same way. In twelve volunteers, biphasic pulses gave the lowest motor threshold, and a strongly asymmetric monophasic pulse gave the highest. And the phases do not count equally. In a biphasic pulse, the second phase is the most effective one on the motor cortex. In a monophasic pulse, it is the first.

Direction counts too. In eight volunteers, with two different stimulators, a monophasic pulse worked better when the current flowed through the motor cortex from back to front than from front to back. Recordings from the spinal cord of conscious patients show why this is not a detail. A weak back-to-front pulse sends down a single early volley, called I1. Front-to-back pulses favour later ones. Why direction matters so much is the subject of episode 2.3.

So what does this field stimulate? Mostly not the cell bodies. A consensus paper by Siebner and colleagues, in 2022, describes modelling work that points to axons, and in particular to axon terminals in the crown and lip of the cortical folds, where the induced field is strongest. In one simulation with realistic neurons, layer five pyramidal cells had the lowest thresholds. Layer two-three pyramidal cells and inhibitory basket cells were also activated at most intensities. From there, excitation travels along the stimulated axons, and on to the neurons they connect to.

The details are still debated. Which cells fire in a living brain, and in which order? How much of the response comes from the circuit, rather than from the first cells hit? And the pulse is not clean. It also stimulates nerves in the scalp. The coil also clicks. At full output, seven coils measured between ninety-eight and one hundred twenty-five decibels at twenty-five centimetres, and close to the coil it can approach one hundred forty. The authors recommend hearing protection. We will meet both effects again, in episodes 3.7 and 9.1.

Now the main parameter, intensity. On the machine, it is a percentage of the maximum stimulator output. That percentage is the capacitor voltage, as a fraction of its maximum. It is not energy. And the induced field rises almost linearly with it.

Here is the classic trap. In that same seven-coil study, at one hundred percent output, the peak field measured fifteen millimetres below the coil, in a model head, ranged from one hundred forty to two hundred sixty volts per metre. That is a factor of nearly two, for the same number on the dial. Pulse width and damping also differ between devices and coil models, so identical settings can give quite different waveforms. So fifty percent means nothing outside its own machine and coil. Two papers that both say fifty percent have not said the same thing.

The usual remedy is to relate the intensity to the person's own motor threshold, and to report the device, the coil, the waveform, and the current direction. Motor threshold is the subject of episode 3.3, and it has limits of its own.

To sum up. A capacitor stores, a switch releases, and a coil turns the current into a magnetic field that changes in under a millisecond. Induction turns that change into an electric field in the head, with no current through the skin. It acts mostly on axons in the superficial cortex. Waveform, current direction and pulse width all shape the effect. And a percentage of maximum output means nothing outside its machine and coil.

Next: the coils themselves. The round coil, the figure-of-eight, and the H.

PLATE 3.25:33

Coils: figure-8, round, H

A TMS coil acts like a stamp pressed into jelly: the winding shape decides where the induced field lands. The round coil leaves a doughnut with zero at its centre, the figure-of-eight adds two circles into one spot, and the H-coil reaches deeper but spreads much wider. No coil escapes the depth-focality trade-off, and the surface always receives more field than the target.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In the forge, a hammer blow is only as good as the shape of its head. A flat head, a pointed head and a wide head leave very different marks in the same piece of metal. TMS coils are the same. The shape of the winding decides where the field lands, how wide it spreads, and how deep it reaches. Today: the round coil, the figure-of-eight, and the H.

First, what every coil does. A brief, intense current runs through the winding. It makes a magnetic field that changes in less than a millisecond. That changing field induces an electric field in the head, in loops that follow the shape of the winding. So the winding is a stamp, and the head is the jelly. To keep the picture simple, we press three stamps into a block of jelly. The print on the surface shows where the field is strong, and the depth of the dent shows how far it reaches. This is a picture, not a model.

Start with the round coil. It is the oldest design. In 1985, Barker and colleagues used a round coil to stimulate the human motor cortex. Inside the head, the induced currents flow in circles. Right under the centre of the coil, the field is zero. It grows toward the winding and peaks in a ring near its edge. So a round coil does not leave a spot. It leaves a doughnut.

Now the figure-of-eight. Take two round windings, side by side, with currents running in opposite directions. Each one drives a circle of current in the tissue. At the crossing, the two circles run the same way, so they add. This design was proposed by Ueno and colleagues in 1988. In their simulation, the field at the target was about three times higher than elsewhere. The doughnut becomes a single spot, right under the crossing. That is why the figure-of-eight is the workhorse for finding the motor hotspot, which is the next episode.

How focal is that spot? A 2013 simulation compared fifty coil designs in the same simple, spherical head model. Think of the area of cortex where the field stays above half of its peak. For figure-of-eight coils, that area got as small as five square centimetres, depending on the winding size. For round coils, the smallest was about thirty-four.

How deep does it go? In the same simulation, the field falls to half of its value at the cortex within about one to three and a half centimetres, for round and figure-of-eight coils alike. A few centimetres, no more. Smaller windings are sharper, but they fade faster with depth.

Here is the compromise from episode 2.4, now coil by coil. Across all fifty designs, not one escaped it. A coil that reaches deeper cannot be as focal as a shallow one. And whatever the coil, the field is always stronger at the surface than deeper inside. In a sphere, this can be proved.

The H-coil is built to push against the depth limit. Roth, Zangen and Hallett described it in 2002. Its windings are spread over a helmet-shaped frame, so that the fields add up across a wide region and fade more slowly with distance. In 2005, Zangen and colleagues tested it in six volunteers. The motor cortex could still be activated at a distance of five and a half centimetres from the H-coil, against two centimetres for a figure-of-eight coil. In 2007, a saline head model, with the output set at one hundred twenty percent of motor threshold, showed one H-coil version above threshold up to four to five centimetres deep in frontal regions. The figure-of-eight reached about one and a half.

But there is a price. In the fifty-coil simulation, the older H-coil designs gained between twenty-two and seventy-two percent in half-value depth over a seventy-millimetre figure-of-eight. Their spread grew by roughly five to nine times. Two depths are being measured here. One is how far the field still crosses the firing threshold when you turn it up. The other is how fast it fades. The H-coil wins the first, and wins the second only a little.

A third design sits in between. The double cone coil has two large round windings, tilted toward each other like an open book. In the simulation, its half-value depth was about two and a half to three centimetres, with a spread comparable to the modelled H-coils or smaller. A follow-up simulation in 2014 found it a good balance among standard commercial coils. H-coils are also built into some commercial treatment systems. Their regulatory status is for chapter twelve.

Now the classic trap. The word deep suggests a spot in the middle of the brain. It is not. A deep coil stimulates a bigger volume, a little deeper, and the cortex under the coil still gets more field than the target. That 2014 work also estimated that a target four centimetres deep or more already pushes the surface field above the limits of current safety guidelines. At about six centimetres, it judged stimulation almost certainly unsafe. So deep, in TMS, means a few centimetres deeper, and much wider.

To sum up. A coil is a stamp. The round coil leaves a doughnut, with zero at its centre. The figure-of-eight leaves a spot. The H-coil reaches deeper, and spreads wider. No coil escapes the trade-off between depth and focality, and the surface always receives more than the target.

Next: single pulses, the motor threshold, and the motor evoked potential.

PLATE 3.36:58

Single pulse, motor threshold and the MEP

A single pulse over the hand area of the motor cortex makes a muscle answer, the motor evoked potential, the last bell in a row of dominoes from cortex to hand. The resting motor threshold is the lowest intensity giving fifty microvolts in half of the pulses, a noisy probability. It is the dosing ruler across the brain, assuming other targets get the same field.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A forge hammer comes with a gauge. You know how hard you struck, because you can see the dent. A TMS coil has no such luck. Over most of the brain, a pulse leaves nothing you can see or feel. Over one small patch, it does. Today: the single pulse, the motor evoked potential, and the motor threshold, the ruler that doses almost all of TMS.

Here is the picture. A row of dominoes, standing from the head to the hand. The first domino is the motor cortex, in the strip of cortex that controls the hand. The next ones are the corticospinal pathway down the spinal cord, then a motor neuron, then a nerve, and then a muscle. The last domino rings a small bell. This is a picture, not a model.

Now the real thing. A single pulse over the hand area of the motor cortex sets activity going down that chain. Two small stick-on electrodes over a hand muscle, for example at the base of the thumb or beside the index finger, record its electrical response. That recording is the electromyogram, and the response to the pulse is the motor evoked potential, the MEP. For a hand muscle, it arrives roughly twenty to twenty-five milliseconds after the pulse. Notice what it is. The MEP is the bell at the end of the row. It is a sum over the whole chain, not a clean reading of the cortex.

Before measuring anything, find the right spot. This is the hotspot search. The coil is held over the motor cortex, turned about forty-five degrees, so that the current in the brain runs from back to front, roughly across the central sulcus. That is the orientation story of episode 2.3. Then the coil is moved in small steps, with the same pulse each time. The hotspot is the position that gives the largest, most consistent responses in the target muscle.

Now the threshold. The classic definition comes from the international federation of clinical neurophysiology. The muscle must be fully relaxed, and the electromyogram is used to check it. The resting motor threshold is the lowest intensity that gives a response above fifty microvolts, peak to peak, in at least five out of ten pulses. In the usual procedure, the intensity goes down one percent at a time. The threshold is the last level that still passed. Intensity is a percentage of the maximum output of the machine, which is why episode 3.1 warned you about comparing those numbers across machines.

Why five out of ten, and not the first response? Because the same pulse, in the same place, does not give the same answer twice. Already in 1993, Kiers and colleagues showed that the size of the response varies from pulse to pulse, and that this variability is larger at low intensity. Back to the dominoes. Near threshold, the row falls on some tries and not on others. So the threshold is not a switch. It is the point where the odds are about fifty-fifty.

That has a cost. The same guideline notes that five out of ten is a weak test. It reports that ten out of twenty pulses are needed for a reproducible estimate. Adaptive methods, introduced by Awiszus in 2003, do better. They fit an S-shaped curve of response probability as the pulses come in. In a 2017 comparison, one such method needed about fifteen fewer pulses than the classic one to find the resting threshold. And the guideline also notes that judging the threshold by watching the thumb twitch gives values about ten percent higher, on average, than the recording. The bell hears what the eye misses.

There is a second version. In the active motor threshold, the participant holds a slight contraction, about twenty percent of maximum strength, and the response criterion is one hundred microvolts.

What does the resting threshold tell you? A 1996 study by Ziemann and colleagues gave volunteers antiepileptic drugs. Drugs that block sodium and calcium channels raised the threshold. Drugs that boost the inhibitory transmitter GABA did not. The authors concluded that it reflects how excitable the membranes along the pathway are. So the threshold is the strength needed to tip the first dominoes, not a measure of how well the brain works.

Above threshold, the response grows with intensity along an S-shaped curve, and flattens out at a plateau. That is the recruitment curve described by Devanne and colleagues in 1997. The threshold is only the bottom of it.

Now the strong point of this episode. The motor threshold is the ruler used to dose almost everything else. The usual rule says to set the intensity as a percentage of it, wherever the coil is. A well-known 2007 multisite trial in depression, for example, stimulated the left prefrontal cortex at one hundred twenty percent of motor threshold. It is convenient, because the motor cortex is the only place with a bell. But it carries a hidden assumption. It assumes that one percentage gives about the same field at the target as at the hand area. And the heads do not agree.

The coil sits on the scalp, and the field fades with distance. Stokes and colleagues placed spacers under the coil, over the motor cortex. In 2007, they reported that each extra millimetre of coil-to-cortex distance needed about two point eight percent more of the machine's maximum output to keep the same effect. In a small series of twenty MRI scans, the prefrontal target was one to four and a half millimetres farther from the scalp than the motor cortex, in eleven of them. With that rate, that is roughly three to thirteen points of machine output. This is our own back-of-the-envelope arithmetic, not a measurement. And it still assumes that both regions respond equally to the same field. In 2024, Numssen and colleagues made the case for dosing by the electric field that actually reaches the target, instead of by threshold. Episode 7.3 comes back to this.

The classic trap is to treat the threshold as a fixed property of a person. It is one estimate, for one muscle, one machine and coil, one state of rest, on one day. The drugs a person takes change it. A slight contraction changes it. A different coil changes it. Quote it with its conditions, or the number means little.

To sum up. A single pulse on the hand area makes a muscle answer, and that answer is the motor evoked potential. The resting threshold is the lowest intensity that gives fifty microvolts in half of the pulses. It is a probability, estimated with noise. It is used as a dosing ruler everywhere in the brain, and that is convenient, and debatable.

Next: paired pulses, and how two hammer blows reveal the circuits between them.

PLATE 3.47:06

Paired pulses: probing inhibition and facilitation

Two pulses a few milliseconds apart turn the coil into a probe. A faint conditioning pulse shrinks the answer to a test pulse at one to six milliseconds, short-interval inhibition, and enlarges it at ten to fifteen, facilitation; two strong pulses give long-interval inhibition. Drug studies suggest GABA-A, a mixed picture and GABA-B, but each value depends on intensities and state.

TRANSCRIPTEN

One pulse gives one answer. Two pulses, a few milliseconds apart, give an answer that depends on the gap. That small change turns the coil into a probe. Today: paired pulses, what they seem to measure, and how far each attribution can be trusted.

A reminder from episode 3.3. A single pulse over the hand area makes a muscle answer, and that answer is the motor evoked potential. Everything today is built from that one response.

Here is the picture. A sealed booth, with a speaking horn on its side. We never open the booth. We knock on the door, and a voice answers through the horn. How loud the answer is, that is our motor evoked potential. The booth stands for the circuits of the motor cortex. This is a picture, not a model.

Now the trick. Before the standard knock, we give a faint knock, too faint to get any answer by itself. In the real protocol, the faint knock is the conditioning pulse. It is set below the motor threshold, and it produces no response. The standard knock is the test pulse, set to give a clear response. We measure the answer to the test pulse alone. Then we measure it again after the faint knock. The measure is the difference between the two answers, usually written as a ratio.

Why believe the booth is the cortex, and not the spinal cord? Two lines of evidence. In 1993, Kujirai and colleagues showed that the conditioning pulse left a spinal reflex unchanged. And in 1998, Di Lazzaro and colleagues recorded from the spinal cord of two awake people. The faint knock produced no descending activity of its own, yet it reduced the descending volleys of the test pulse. The change happens upstream, in the cortex.

Now the gap. Kujirai and colleagues, ten healthy volunteers, in the Journal of Physiology. When the faint knock came one to six milliseconds before the test pulse, the answer shrank. That is short-interval intracortical inhibition, SICI. At ten and at fifteen milliseconds, the same pair gave a larger answer. That is intracortical facilitation, ICF.

The long version uses two strong knocks. Both are above threshold, so each one makes its own answer. In 1992, Valls-Solé and colleagues found that, with strong pulses, the second answer was reduced at gaps of about sixty to a hundred and fifty milliseconds. Later work gives fifty to two hundred, depending on the study. That is long-interval intracortical inhibition, LICI.

So what is inside the booth? Here we must be careful, because we never see it. The attributions come from drug studies in healthy volunteers. A drug acts on the whole brain, so a drug study can only say: the measure changed when we changed this system. The results are consistent with a mechanism. They do not prove it.

SICI and GABA-A. Lorazepam is a drug that boosts GABA-A receptors. In 1996, Ziemann and colleagues gave it to volunteers, and inhibition tended to increase. In 2000, Di Lazzaro and colleagues found that lorazepam increased the inhibition, most at four and five milliseconds, and reduced the late descending waves, in one person with a recording electrode on the spinal cord. Several studies agree. This is the best-supported attribution of the three. It is still indirect.

LICI and GABA-B. In 2006, McDonnell, Orekhov and Ziemann gave nine volunteers baclofen, a drug that activates GABA-B receptors. It increased LICI. It also reduced SICI. The same drug moved both measures, in opposite directions. So the two dials are not independent, and nine volunteers is a small group.

ICF is the weakest case. Dextromethorphan blocks NMDA receptors, a kind of glutamate receptor. In 1998, Ziemann and colleagues gave it to eight volunteers, and ICF went down. That suggests glutamate. But in 1996, lorazepam, a GABA drug, almost completely suppressed ICF. So ICF is not a clean glutamate meter. We can say it is consistent with a mix.

One neighbour, in a sentence. A small electrical shock to a nerve of the hand, shortly before the pulse, also reduces the answer. It is called short-latency afferent inhibition. Tokimura and colleagues described it in 2000, and Di Lazzaro and colleagues showed in 2000 that scopolamine, a drug that blocks acetylcholine receptors, reduced it, while SICI and ICF stayed unchanged. That points to acetylcholine.

Now the most important part. These numbers depend on the dials. Kujirai and colleagues found the strongest inhibition when the faint knock was between seventy and ninety percent of the resting motor threshold. At threshold or above, inhibition became weaker, or turned into facilitation. They also found that small test responses were inhibited more than large ones. So the same circuit gives a different number if you change the conditioning intensity, or the size of the test response. Whether the percentage refers to the resting or the active threshold matters too.

A SICI value is therefore never the inhibition of a person. It is the inhibition under one conditioning intensity, one test size, one interval and one state of the muscle. Say all four, or the number cannot be compared.

How repeatable is it? In sixty-two older adults and stroke patients, tested on two days, Schambra and colleagues found intraclass correlations for SICI from about point three to point nine, depending on the group and the intensity. For ICF, they went from zero to about point seven. In eighteen healthy adults, Nielsen and colleagues found that SICI was moderately to well reproducible between days. But most of the variation lay between people, and repeatability within one person was poor. So these measures separate groups better than they detect a change in one person. The variability of the response itself is the subject of episode 10.2.

Here is the classic trap. Reading SICI as a GABA meter. It is a ratio of two answers, measured at one setting. A drug, a state, or a different intensity can move it, and the number alone does not say which.

To sum up. A faint knock before a test pulse changes the answer. At one to six milliseconds the answer shrinks, SICI. At ten to fifteen it grows, ICF. With two strong pulses, about sixty to a hundred and fifty milliseconds apart, it shrinks again, LICI. Drug studies suggest GABA-A for SICI, GABA-B for LICI, and a mixed picture for ICF. Every value depends on its intensities, and repeats modestly well in one person.

Next: repeated pulses. Episode 3.5, rTMS and theta burst.

PLATE 3.57:58

rTMS and theta burst

Repetitive TMS plays pulses as rhythms: slow or fast trains, and theta bursts in continuous or intermittent patterns, leaving traces of minutes to an hour that are probably synaptic. On average continuous theta burst suppresses and intermittent theta burst facilitates the motor response, yet about one person in three does not follow the rule. Dose and state can even flip the sign.

TRANSCRIPTEN

One pulse is one hammer blow. But in the forge, nobody shapes metal with a single blow. The smith strikes again and again, and the rhythm matters as much as the force. Repetitive TMS works the same way. Today we read the rhythms of the forge, from slow and steady to a fast triplet. And we ask what each one leaves behind.

Picture each protocol as a score. Every pulse is a note. The tempo is the frequency. A group of notes is a train, and the silence between trains is a rest. A score is only paper, and the same score, played in two different heads, does not always sound the same. This is a picture, not a model.

Start with the two classic tempos. By convention, one pulse per second or slower is called low frequency. Five pulses per second or faster is called high frequency. In 1997, Chen and colleagues gave healthy volunteers eight hundred ten pulses at point nine hertz, over fifteen minutes. The motor evoked potential, the MEP, fell by about twenty percent on average. It stayed lower for at least fifteen minutes after the end. A much slower beat, one pulse every ten seconds for an hour, changed nothing. And the effect outlasts the stimulation.

High frequency is played in trains, with rests between them. A standard schedule for depression, listed in a United States regulatory summary, is ten pulses per second. Four seconds on, twenty-six seconds off, seventy-five trains. That is three thousand pulses, and about thirty-seven minutes, at one hundred twenty percent of the motor threshold.

Then, in 2005, Huang, Rothwell and colleagues described the theta burst. A burst is three pulses, fifty per second, twenty milliseconds apart. Bursts repeat five times per second, in the range of the EEG theta rhythm. Continuous theta burst, cTBS, plays them without a break for forty seconds. Intermittent theta burst, iTBS, plays two seconds of bursts, then eight seconds of silence, twenty times. Both deliver six hundred pulses. The intermittent version takes about three minutes. In the original study, the intensity was eighty percent of the active motor threshold, and the continuous pattern lowered the motor response, while the intermittent one raised it. The effects lasted from about fifteen minutes to nearly an hour, depending on the protocol. Earlier rTMS effects, the authors noted, were often weak, variable, and rarely lasted longer than thirty minutes.

What does all this act on? As in episode 3.1, axons under the coil. But repeated stimulation does not stay under the coil. In 1997, Paus and colleagues delivered TMS trains over the left frontal eye field, during positron emission tomography. Blood flow rose with the number of trains at the site, and also in distant parietal and occipital regions. The pattern matched the known connections of that area in the monkey. So the target is the cortex under the coil, and the regions it talks to.

Why would a trace remain? The leading idea is plasticity of the LTP and LTD kind, at synapses. In six volunteers, memantine, which blocks the NMDA receptor, abolished both the drop after continuous theta burst and the rise after the intermittent one. So the effect is probably synaptic. But the word is like. We measure a motor response, not a synapse.

Now the dials. Frequency or pattern. Number of pulses. Intensity, set relative to the person's own motor threshold, from episode 3.3. Interval between trains. Number of sessions. And the state of the participant. Note that the original theta burst work used eighty percent of the active threshold, while clinical schedules use one hundred twenty percent of the resting one. A percentage means nothing without its reference.

More is not always more. In fourteen volunteers, Gamboa and colleagues doubled the length of the intermittent pattern, and facilitation turned into suppression. Doubling the continuous pattern did the opposite. And the participant counts. In ten volunteers, Iezzi and colleagues had people make brief finger movements before theta burst, and the direction of both aftereffects reversed.

So what holds at group level? In 2020, Corp and colleagues pooled four hundred thirty healthy volunteers, from twenty-two studies. On average, the intermittent pattern facilitated the motor response, and the continuous one suppressed it. The facilitation was detectable up to thirty minutes. The suppression, mostly in the first ten. But person by person, sixty-eight percent showed facilitation after the intermittent pattern, a hundred eighty of two hundred sixty-five. And sixty-five percent showed suppression after the continuous one. About one person in three did not follow the rule. The authors found no sign of two separate groups, responders and non-responders. And part of that spread comes from how we measure, since the motor response itself fluctuates. Another study, in fifty-six volunteers, found that the response to theta burst tracked the latency of the motor response evoked by one particular current direction. That hints that which neurons each pulse recruits differs between people. Episode 9.5 comes back to this.

In depression, this has become a clinical tool. In the Three-D trial, in 2018, four hundred fourteen adults with treatment-resistant depression, at three Canadian hospitals, received either ten hertz stimulation or intermittent theta burst, over the left prefrontal cortex. A session lasted three minutes, against thirty-seven and a half. The two groups improved by almost the same amount, within a margin of two and a quarter points on a seventeen-item scale. Here are the status stamps, as of November 2026. In the United States, the FDA cleared a theta burst system on August fourteenth, 2018, for adults with major depression who have not improved on antidepressant medication in the current episode. In May 2026, it cleared accelerated schedules, with several sessions a day, for one system, with the same indication. In Canada, Health Canada approved rTMS for depression in 2002. The public licence list names the devices, but not their indications. In Europe, the first CE mark for depression dates from 2011, for adults who did not improve on two prior antidepressants. That is regulatory status. The expert guideline of 2020, level A for high frequency stimulation of the left prefrontal cortex, is evidence, not status. Chapter twelve takes this up.

Here is the classic trap. High frequency excites, low frequency inhibits. It is a summary of group averages, in the motor cortex, read from the motor evoked potential. Even there, it bends. In twenty volunteers, Maeda and colleagues found that one hertz lowered the motor response, and twenty hertz raised it. But ten hertz, the tempo of the standard depression protocol, produced no significant change. For one person, or another region, the rule is a tendency. It is not a law.

To sum up. Repetitive TMS writes rhythms. Slow and fast trains, or theta bursts, in continuous or intermittent patterns. They leave a trace of minutes to about an hour, probably synaptic, and reaching connected regions. On average, the patterns do what they were designed to do. In any one person, they vary a lot, sometimes in the opposite direction. And dose, duration and state change the sign.

Next: where do you put the coil? From rules of thumb to neuronavigation.

PLATE 3.68:20

Targeting: from rules of thumb to neuronavigation

A scalp rule puts the coil where a note says, not where the brain needs it, like a tailor's chalk mark: the five-centimetre rule landed on different cortex in different heads. The ten-twenty system scales with the head, neuronavigation uses the person's own scan, and connectivity picks a target by its network. A target is a place, an angle and a method.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Where, exactly, do you put the coil? Chapter three has covered the machine, the coil, the motor threshold, paired pulses and trains. This episode is about aim.

A figure-eight coil is a few centimetres wide, and the cortex under it is hidden behind skin, bone and fluid. So the target has to be found indirectly. There are four ways, from a tape measure to a camera. And one image will follow us: a tailor's chalk mark.

Picture a note that says: mark the pocket five centimetres in front of the shoulder seam. The tailor measures, chalks, and the mark lands exactly where the note says. But the note says nothing about the body under the cloth. A rule on the surface is not a place in the body.

The oldest rule for the prefrontal cortex goes like this. First, find the motor hotspot, the spot where a pulse makes the hand twitch, as in episode three point three. Then slide the coil five centimetres forward, along a line on the scalp. That spot is taken as the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the DLPFC. The first depression trials of the mid nineties, George in nineteen ninety-five and Pascual-Leone in nineteen ninety-six, are the papers later work cites for it. It is quick, it needs no scan, and it is easy to teach.

In two thousand and one, Herwig and colleagues checked it. They applied the five-centimetre rule in twenty-two people. Then they used neuronavigation on each person's own MRI to see what lay under the coil. In seven of the twenty-two, the coil sat over Brodmann area nine of the DLPFC. In the other fifteen, the centre of the coil was more dorsal, for example above the premotor cortex. Same rule, same five centimetres, a different cortex underneath. The study is small, and it measured where the coil landed, not how patients did. But it shows the point. A distance on the scalp is not a place in the brain, because heads differ in size and in folding.

The second way comes from the EEG. The ten twenty system places its positions at ten and twenty percent of the head's own distances, nasion to inion and ear to ear. So it scales with the head. F3, over the left frontal lobe, is often used as the DLPFC target. In two thousand and nine, Beam and colleagues described a way to find F3 with three tape measurements and a short calculation, without a full cap. It is a better rule than a fixed distance, because the head sets the scale. But it is still a rule on the surface. Okamoto and colleagues projected ten twenty positions onto the cortex in seventeen adults, using MRI. Across people, a given position still varied, with an average standard deviation of eight millimetres in standard brain space.

The third way uses the person's own MRI. Before the session, the structural scan is loaded. A camera tracks small markers fixed to the head and to the coil. The software shows, in real time, where the coil sits on this participant's brain, and the operator moves it onto the planned point, at the planned angle. How accurate is it? A simulation study by Nieminen and colleagues, in two thousand and twenty-two, estimated a total coil-to-head error between two point two and three point six millimetres, and about one degree. Most of it came from matching the head to the scan at the start, not from the camera. So: a few millimetres. That is the accuracy of the coil relative to the scan. It is not the accuracy of the field in the tissue.

Does the extra work pay off? Two examples. In two thousand and nine, Fitzgerald and colleagues randomised fifty-one people with treatment-resistant depression to ten hertz stimulation of the left DLPFC. Twenty-seven were targeted with the five-centimetre rule. Twenty-four were targeted at the junction of Brodmann areas forty-six and nine, found on their own MRI. At four weeks, the navigated group had a better depression score, with a p value of zero point zero two. One modest trial, which asked for confirmation in larger samples.

The second example is not about depression. Sack and colleagues compared four ways of positioning the coil over the right parietal cortex of healthy volunteers, in one number task. Individual fMRI guidance gave the largest effect, and the EEG position P4 gave the smallest. The number of participants needed to show an effect was five, nine, thirteen and forty-seven, from the most precise method to the least. One task, one site. But the cost of imprecision is paid in sample size.

The fourth way picks the target by connectivity, not by anatomy. In two thousand and twelve, Fox and colleagues took resting fMRI from ninety-eight healthy people. For each left DLPFC site used in earlier depression studies, they asked how it was connected to the subgenual cingulate, a deep region linked to depression. Sites that had worked better were more negatively correlated with it. They proposed a target in Brodmann area forty-six, and the pattern held in thirteen patients. This is a correlation across published sites. It is not proof that this connection causes the benefit.

Can a personal target be computed reliably? Cash and colleagues, in two thousand and twenty-one, found that in one thousand healthy adults the personalised target moved by a median of about two millimetres between scans on separate days. It stayed at a median of two point seven millimetres after a year. And targets differed between people up to nearly seven times more than within a person.

Then came a trial. In two thousand and twenty-two, Cole and colleagues ran a sham-controlled trial of accelerated theta burst, with a target chosen from each person's own connectivity. At the interim analysis, twenty-nine participants had been treated. Depression scores fell by fifty-two point five percent with active stimulation and eleven point one percent with sham, at four weeks. That trial tests the whole package: the dose, the schedule and the target together. It cannot say how much comes from the target alone. On the regulatory side, a system built on this method was cleared by the FDA in September two thousand and twenty-two, for adults with major depression who did not improve enough on antidepressants in the current episode. In Canada, no licence for this system was found in the active licence listing. In Europe, the manufacturer's website mentions no CE mark. As of November two thousand and twenty-six.

Position is only half of aim. Orientation counts as much. Episode two point three showed that a neuron responds to the part of the field along its axis. Over the motor cortex, Brasil-Neto and colleagues, and Mills and colleagues, both in nineteen ninety-two, found that the largest responses come when the induced current runs roughly across the central sulcus, from back to front. For the hand area, that means the coil handle pointing backward, about forty-five to fifty degrees off the midline. Rotate the coil, and the same spot gives smaller responses. Neuronavigation records the angle too.

Here is the classic trap. A methods section says: we targeted the DLPFC. But the coil was placed by a scalp rule. Report how the target was found, with which method, and at which angle. A target is a place, an angle and a method. How much field actually arrives at that place changes from head to head. That is for chapter seven, episodes seven point two and seven point three.

To sum up. A scalp rule is a note to a tailor: it puts the chalk where the note says, not where the body needs it. The five-centimetre rule landed on different cortex in different heads. The ten twenty system scales with the head, but still varies. Neuronavigation places the coil on your own scan, within a few millimetres. And connectivity picks a target by its network. Whatever the method, the angle counts too.

TMS-EEG and its artefacts

TMS-EEG listens to the brain milliseconds after the pulse, but a parade of artefacts crosses the view: the pulse voltage, a slow decay tail, scalp muscle twitches, the click's sound and the coil's touch. Sound and touch evoke brain responses of their own, so a flicker may come from ear and skin rather than cortex. Cleaning helps, but the result depends on the pipeline.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Until now, we judged a pulse by what a muscle did. TMS-EEG asks something bolder. It listens to the brain itself, in the milliseconds after the hammer falls. The idea is old. In 1997, Ilmoniemi and colleagues recorded EEG during TMS of the motor and visual cortex, and read the response as a sign of cortical reactivity and connectivity. But the hammer is loud, and the signal is small. Today, in two parts: the parade of artefacts, and what we do about each one.

Here is our picture. A tiny lantern sits on a far hill. It is the response of the cortex, a few microvolts. We want to read its flicker. But the pulse sends a parade down the street, right in front of our window. Each marcher blocks the view in its own way. This is a picture, not a model.

Part one. The parade of artefacts.

First comes the drummer, the pulse artefact. The magnetic pulse induces a voltage in the loops formed by the electrode leads. It can reach several volts, and it can saturate the amplifier. The first TMS-EEG systems held the recording for a few milliseconds, with a circuit called sample and hold. Modern amplifiers are built to survive the field, and record continuously. Sampled fast enough, the artefact lasts about as long as the pulse itself. But whatever it covers is lost for good, and that includes the very first response of the stimulated cortex.

Second comes the echo, the decay artefact. The pulse polarises the contact between gel and skin, and that charge leaks away slowly. The result is an exponential tail that can last for hundreds of milliseconds. It looks like a slow wave, and it is not brain.

Third comes the strongman, the muscle artefact. Over the side of the head, the pulse can excite the nerves that drive scalp, face and jaw muscles. The recording shows a large biphasic deflection, in the range of millivolts. That is up to a thousand times bigger than the neural response. It lasts roughly ten to thirty milliseconds, sometimes followed by a slow return. Targets like the language areas, or the lateral prefrontal cortex, are the usual suspects. It hides the earliest part of the response.

Fourth comes the trumpeter. A TMS pulse is loud. The forces on the coil windings produce a sharp click. That click is a sound, and a sound evokes its own response in the brain. It is largest at the vertex, between about one hundred and two hundred milliseconds. Part of the sound reaches the ear through the bone of the skull, even with the ears blocked.

Fifth comes the tickler. The coil vibrates against the scalp, and the pulse can excite nerves of the skin. Muscle twitches send their own signals back up. All of that arrives in the somatosensory system, and evokes responses that do not come from stimulating the cortex directly.

Two more guests cameo. The stimulator recharge can leave a spike, depending on the device. And the participant may blink.

Now the strong point. The trumpeter and the tickler do not only block the view. Each one carries a lantern of its own. Sound and touch light up the brain, with or without the magnet. So when a flicker appears, it may belong to the cortex under the coil, or to the ear and the skin. That is the debate behind this field.

Part two. What we do about each one.

Against the drummer, hardware. A TMS-compatible amplifier, with a high sampling rate and a cut-off chosen to avoid long filter ripples. Then software: the contaminated stretch is removed and filled in. A typical window starts one or two milliseconds before the pulse and ends up to five to ten milliseconds after it, with the gap filled by smooth interpolation.

Against the echo, low impedance. Consensus among experts is to keep electrode impedance at five kilo-ohms or less. A better contact makes the charge leak away faster.

Against the strongman, move the coil. A slightly different place or angle, a lower intensity or a smaller coil can all reduce the twitch. When it stays, it is removed afterwards.

Against the trumpeter, quiet and noise. Earplugs and ear defenders, plus masking noise. The best noise copies the spectrum of the click and is tuned to each participant. Russo and colleagues shared an open tool to build it. A thin layer of foam between coil and scalp reduces the sound that travels through bone, and perhaps the vibration too, although its effect is debated. A prototype coil with a damping casing has cut the click by eighteen to forty-one decibels.

Against the tickler, there is no agreed solution. Foam may help a little.

Then, cleaning. The classic tool is independent component analysis. In 2014, Rogasch and colleagues applied it in two rounds, first to take out the biggest artefacts, then the rest. In nine volunteers, they sorted out muscle, decay, blink, auditory and noise components. After cleaning, the familiar motor cortex peaks, at about fifteen, thirty, forty-five, sixty and one hundred milliseconds, were still recovered. Other methods exist, such as SSP-SIR and SOUND. But cleaning has a cost. In 2021, Bertazzoli and colleagues ran four published pipelines on the same data from sixteen people. Amplitudes and global field power differed considerably between pipelines. The cleaned signal depends on how it was cleaned.

The most direct control is a sensory control. A realistic sham copies the click, and adds weak electrical pulses on the scalp to mimic the feeling. Episode nine point one covers how shams leak. Here they leak too: the percepts of a magnet and of an electrode differ, and participants can tell. Another control stimulates the shoulder instead.

What do these controls show? Studies disagree, depending on the site and the method. In 2018, Gordon and colleagues stimulated the motor cortex of twelve volunteers. Real pulses at both ninety and one hundred ten percent of motor threshold gave different responses from a realistic sham, and they concluded the difference came mostly from direct cortical activation. In 2019, Conde and colleagues stimulated prefrontal and parietal sites, and found that real and realistic sham responses looked very similar, early and late. In 2019, Biabani and colleagues compared motor cortex stimulation with shoulder stimulation. The responses were correlated, especially after about sixty milliseconds. They concluded that evoked potentials reflect a mix of transcranial and peripheral responses.

So here is the lantern that remains. The TMS-evoked potential, the TEP, over the motor cortex has peaks at about fifteen, thirty, forty-five, sixty, one hundred and one hundred eighty milliseconds. The expert review of 2023, by Hernandez-Pavon and colleagues, says the later peaks, beyond about eighty milliseconds, may be contaminated by sensory responses. And the earliest, like the one at fifteen milliseconds, may be contaminated by muscle. The middle of the window is the least exposed, though even there, it has to be shown.

Here is the classic trap. Reading the whole response after the pulse as the brain answering the coil. A late peak is not a clean cortical measure unless you have shown, with a sensory control, that it survives without the sound and the touch. One more design helps. If every condition has the same click and the same touch, as in a before-and-after test, the sensory part is the same, and the difference between conditions is easier to attribute.

To sum up. TMS-EEG listens to the cortex right after the pulse. The recording is crossed by a parade: the pulse, the decay, the muscle, the sound and the touch. Hardware, a quiet set-up, noise, careful cleaning and sensory controls each clear part of the view. What remains is the TEP, and the later peaks are the ones to doubt first.

The full processing chain is for episode B point two. And episode ten point six will use these responses to build a measure of complexity.
