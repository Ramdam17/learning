Writing to the brain

Recording shows that brain activity and behaviour go together; stimulation changes the activity and tests whether behaviour follows, which is a causal test. Four tools sit on a map of depth against focus: a magnetic coil, scalp electrodes, focused ultrasound and an implanted lead. Three limits frame the series: the pen is wide, the dose is poorly known, and effects vary between people.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Most of what we know about the living human brain, we learned by listening. An EEG cap listens to its electrical rhythms. A scanner listens to its blood flow. Every one of these tools does the same thing. It records.

Picture a telephone switchboard. Until now, we have only listened in. So let's pull out the plug marked record, and push it into another socket. This one says stimulate. Now the line carries a signal into the brain, not out of it.

Why does that matter? Because recording can only show that two things go together. The brain is active when a person moves, remembers or feels. That is a correlation. It does not say that the activity causes the behaviour. A 2022 review by Siddiqi and colleagues puts it plainly. Imaging shows correlations. Lesions and stimulation can test causes. Stimulation lets you change the activity yourself, and see whether the behaviour changes with it. That is a causal test.

This series is about that second plug. Four tools carry most of the story. Meet COIL. It sends brief magnetic pulses through the skull. That is transcranial magnetic stimulation, T-M-S. Meet PAD. It passes a weak electric current between electrodes on the scalp. Meet SONO. It focuses ultrasound through the skull. And meet LEAD, a wire implanted inside the brain, the tool of deep brain stimulation.

We can place them on a map with two axes. Across, how deep a tool can reach. Up, how sharp its focus is. Scalp electrodes are broad and shallow. A coil reaches the surface of the cortex. In simulations, a figure-eight coil concentrates its field on as little as five square centimetres of cortex, against thirty-four for a round coil. But a study of fifty coil designs found a hard trade-off. The deeper a coil reaches, the wider its field spreads. Focused ultrasound comes closest to deep and sharp without surgery. Its focus is measured in millimetres, though the skull makes it hard to control. An implanted lead is both deep and sharp, with contacts about a millimetre and a half long on common leads. But it needs an operation.

Now, the three limits. This series will not hide them.

The first limit: the pen is wide. A cubic millimetre of human cortex holds tens of thousands of neurons. So even a focus one millimetre wide writes on thousands of them at once. We never write on a single cell.

The second limit: the dose is poorly known. The number on the machine is not the dose. The dose is the electric field that reaches the tissue, and it is rarely measured. With weak transcranial currents of two milliamps, recordings inside the human head found fields below one volt per metre. And between people, it changes. In one simulation study of twenty-four heads, for current stimulation over the motor cortex, the thickness of the fluid around the brain explained about half of the difference in the field.

The third limit: effects vary from one person to the next. In a study of fifty-six healthy volunteers, only about four in ten responded as expected to each of three stimulation protocols. Another study found the same wide spread with theta burst T-M-S, and linked it to which circuits each pulse recruits.

These limits shape everything. A wide pen means we cannot claim to have changed one circuit. An unknown dose means two labs can believe they did the same thing, and have not. Large variability means an average can hide people who responded, and people who did not. Later chapters build the designs that cope with this: sham, blinding, controls, and honest statistics.

So here is the promise. By the end, you will understand what a stimulation really does to neurons. You will be able to design a study that can fail. And you will be able to read a clinical announcement together with its level of evidence. Clinical status differs by technique, by indication and by country, so every clinical claim will carry its date.

The path has seventy short episodes. History, the physics shared by all techniques, each technique in turn, modelling the field, closed loop, study design, measurement, safety, clinical applications, and robustness. Thirty-two of them make a core path, marked with a star.

Writing to the brain is not magic. It is a wide pen, an ink whose dose we barely know, and a page that is different for every person. Let's learn to use it honestly.
