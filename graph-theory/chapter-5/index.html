States and moves

The wolf, goat and cabbage puzzle becomes a graph: ten allowed states as dots, crossings as links. A shortest path found by ripples gives seven crossings, with exactly two solutions. The Rubik's Cube is the same idea at scale, an implicit graph known only by a rule that lists neighbours, and every position lies within twenty moves of solved.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the ferryman's crossing. A river runs between two banks. On the left bank wait a ferryman, a wolf, a goat and a cabbage. All four must reach the right bank.

The boat is small. It carries the ferryman and one passenger, no more. And there is a catch. If the wolf is left alone with the goat, the wolf eats the goat. If the goat is left alone with the cabbage, the goat eats the cabbage.

The puzzle is very old. It appears in a medieval collection called the Propositiones ad acuendos juvenes, which means problems to sharpen the young. By tradition, the collection is credited to Alcuin of York, who died in the year eight hundred and four. The oldest known copy dates from the late ninth century.

Does this look like a graph? Nobody would say so. It is a riddle about animals and a boat. But look closer.

At any moment, the situation is a short list: who stands on which bank. Call that list a state. Now make every possible state a dot.

How many states are there? Each of the four travellers is on the left bank or the right bank. Two choices, four travellers: sixteen arrangements. But six of them are disasters, because somebody gets eaten. Cross those out. Ten states are allowed. That makes ten dots.

Now the moves. A crossing changes the situation. The ferryman goes over alone, or with one passenger. Each crossing takes one allowed state to another allowed state. So join them with a link. A crossing can always be undone by crossing back, so the links have no arrows. There are ten links.

Here is the hidden graph. A short path, then a loop, then a short path again.

Now read it. The first dot has everyone on the left. The last dot has everyone on the right. A solution is a path from the first dot to the last. Each link on the path is one crossing.

Which path is the shortest? We already know how to find out. In episode two point two, ripples spread from a stone, and each ring touched the next layer of dots. Do the same here, starting at the first dot.

One crossing reaches one state: the goat goes over. Two crossings reach the ferryman coming back alone. Three crossings reach two states, one on each side of the loop. Four crossings reach the next two. Five reach the dot where the loop closes. Six bring the ferryman back alone. Seven reach the goal.

So the shortest solution has seven crossings. And the ripples show something else. At the third ring, the loop splits in two, and both sides meet again at the fifth ring. So there are exactly two shortest solutions.

Take the goat across. Come back alone. Take the wolf, or take the cabbage. Bring the goat back. Take the other one. Return alone, and take the goat once more. The loop is only a choice of order.

That was a toy. Now a puzzle with a real size: the Rubik's Cube. Here a state is a position of the coloured squares. A move is a turn of one face: a quarter turn one way, a quarter turn the other way, or a half turn. We count a half turn as one move. Six faces, three turns each, so every position has eighteen neighbours.

How many positions are there? The exact count is on screen. It is about four point three times ten to the nineteenth. Each position is a dot. Could we draw them all? Even if we looked at a billion positions every second, visiting each one just once would take more than thirteen centuries.

So here is the strong moment. This graph is never drawn. Nobody has a picture of it, or a list of its dots. It exists only as a rule. Give the rule a position, and it hands back the eighteen neighbours. That is all a search needs. We make only the dots we need, as the search goes. A graph that lives only as a rule is called an implicit graph.

Let us try it, with ripples from the solved cube. We ran a program for the first five rings. Zero moves: one position. One move: eighteen. Two moves: two hundred and forty-three. Three moves: three thousand, two hundred and forty. Four moves: forty-three thousand, two hundred and thirty-nine. Five moves: five hundred and seventy-four thousand, nine hundred and eight.

After the first ring, each ring is about thirteen times bigger than the last, not eighteen, because two turns of the same face in a row make just one move. After five rings, we have built over six hundred thousand dots. We never drew any of them.

How far can a scrambled cube be from the solved one? In two thousand ten, Rokicki, Kociemba, Davidson and Dethridge proved that the answer is twenty. Every position can be solved in at most twenty moves. It took about thirty-five years of computer time, on idle machines donated by Google.

That number counts a half turn as one move. If only quarter turns are allowed, the answer is twenty-six, proved by Rokicki and Davidson in two thousand fourteen. In graph words, no dot is more than twenty links away from the solved one.

To sum up. A state is a complete description of a situation. A legal move is a link. Solutions are paths, and the ripples find the shortest one. And a graph can live as nothing but a rule that lists the neighbours of a state. Chapter twelve will let a walker wander over such a graph, one random move at a time.

What comes first

Before means arrow. Dressing, course prerequisites, recipes and spreadsheet formulas are directed graphs, and a topological sort respects every arrow. Kahn's algorithm repeatedly does a job with no padlock left. The morning routine has 91 valid orders. The longest chain is the critical path, twenty days for the shed, and a cycle makes the dependencies impossible.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the ferryman's crossing.

Before he can push off, the ferryman has three small jobs. Untie the rope. Load the goat. Push off.

He cannot push off before the rope is untied. Some jobs must come first. This episode is about that one word. And, as always, a graph is hiding in it.

Take a morning routine, invented for this video. There are seven things to put on. A shirt, a tie, trousers, a belt, socks, shoes and a jacket.

Some must come before others. Socks come before shoes. Trousers come before the belt. The shirt comes before the tie.

Make every item a dot. Draw an arrow from an item to each item that has to wait for it. An arrow means: before.

That gives seven dots and seven arrows. The arrows go one way only, so this is a directed graph, like the one-way streets of episode one point two.

What we want is an order. A list of all seven items, where every arrow points forward. That is called a topological sort.

Here is a way to find one. Put a padlock on a dot for every arrow that points into it. A padlock means: something has not happened yet.

The belt has two padlocks, because it waits for the shirt and for the trousers. The shirt has none.

Now repeat one rule. Find a dot with no padlock, and do it. Then snip one padlock from every dot it points to.

First, the shirt. The tie and the belt each lose a padlock.

Next, the trousers. The belt loses its second padlock, and the shoes lose one.

Then the socks. Now the shoes are free too.

After that, the tie, and the jacket loses a padlock.

Then the belt, and the jacket is free.

Shoes come next, and the jacket comes last.

Seven steps, and we are dressed. This is Kahn's algorithm. Arthur Kahn published it in nineteen sixty-two, under the title: Topological sorting of large networks, in Communications of the A C M.

The order is not unique. Several times, more than one dot was free, and we could choose. Altogether there are ninety-one valid orders. Every one is a correct way to get dressed.

Once you look for it, before is everywhere. A course catalogue, invented here, is a graph. Calculus comes before probability, and probability comes before statistics. A study plan is a topological sort.

A recipe is one too. You whisk the eggs before you mix the batter, and you mix before you fry. The pan must be hot before the frying starts.

A spreadsheet is a third. Each cell is a dot. If a formula in one cell uses another cell, there is an arrow between them. Edit the first cell, and only the cells downstream need to be recalculated, each one after the cells it uses. Spreadsheet programs use this idea to decide what to recalculate, and in which order.

Now a project, a garden shed, invented. Each task has a duration, in days. Planning takes two days, ordering wood takes five, digging takes three, and so on. The numbers are on the dots.

How fast can the shed be finished? Not faster than the longest chain of tasks that wait for each other.

Here is that chain: plan, dig, foundation, frame, roof, finish. Two plus three plus four plus six plus three plus two. Twenty days.

That chain is the critical path. Delay any task on it, and the whole shed is late. Win one day on the frame, and the shed is done in nineteen.

Tasks off the path have slack. The doors can be painted up to nine days late, and nothing changes. Ordering the wood has two days of slack.

Tools like this reached industry and the military in the late nineteen fifties. Walker at DuPont and Kelley at Remington Rand built the critical path method, and published it in nineteen fifty-nine. At almost the same time, the United States Navy built a similar tool, called PERT, for its Polaris missile programme. Kahn's algorithm was written for networks of this kind.

Now, what could go wrong? Suppose three jobs. A must come before B. B must come before C. And C must come before A.

Each job has one padlock. No dot is free. Nothing can ever start. This is called a deadlock.

Add one silly rule to the morning routine: the shoes must come before the socks. The socks already come before the shoes.

Run the loop again. It does five steps, then it stops. Two dots are left, the socks and the shoes, and both are locked.

The leftover dots hide a cycle. So the same loop that finds an order also detects a cycle, by getting stuck.

A set of dependencies is only possible if it has no cycle. A directed graph with no cycle is called a directed acyclic graph, or D A G. An order exists exactly when the graph is a D A G.

You will meet D A Gs again. In the Statistics series, episode nineteen point three, causal graphs are D A Gs. And in chapter sixteen, a hierarchy of concepts will be one too.

To sum up. An arrow means before. An order that respects every arrow is a topological sort, and snipping padlocks finds one. With durations, the longest chain sets the length of the whole project. And a cycle makes everything impossible, so dependencies must form a D A G.

Every bridge once

Königsberg's walk is impossible because a walk has only two ends, so at most two lands can touch an odd number of bridges, and four do. Hierholzer's splicing shows a circuit exists when every land is even. When intersections are odd, the Chinese postman repeats the cheapest streets, turning a 34 circuit into 42 in the example.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back. In the teaser, we made a promise. Königsberg has seven bridges, and no walk crosses each of them exactly once. The reason, we said, is that every land touches an odd number of bridges. Today, we prove it.

There is more. The same idea tells us when such a walk does exist. And it leads to a very practical job: the job of a postman.

Here is the city again. Four pieces of land, and seven bridges. The island touches five bridges. The other three lands touch three each.

Now turn each land into a hall, and each end of a bridge into a door. The number of doors in a hall is the number of bridges that land touches.

Follow a walker through one hall. Each time he comes in by a door, a blue flag goes up on that door. And each time he goes out by a door, a pink flag goes up.

He comes in here, and goes out there. Two doors, two flags. Every bridge is crossed once, so no door is ever used twice.

So a land that the walk merely passes through uses its doors in pairs. A second pass uses the other two doors. Four doors, two passes, and every door is used.

Now take a hall with three doors. One pass uses two of them, and one door is left. Suppose the walker comes in by that last door. He can never go out, because every other door is used. He is stuck, and the walk ends there.

So a hall with an odd number of doors can be where the walk ends. It can also be where the walk begins. At the start, the walker goes out without ever having come in, so one door has no partner. At the end, he comes in and never goes out, and again one door has no partner.

A walk has one start and one end. So at most two lands can be odd.

Königsberg has four. Four lands with a loose door, and only two places to put a loose door. No such walk exists. Euler gave this argument in his paper, presented in 1735 and printed in 1741. That is the proof, and the doors do the counting.

Now the other direction. Suppose every land is even, and the lands are all connected. Is there always a walk that crosses every bridge once, and comes back to the start? Yes. Euler stated it, but the first complete proof was published in 1873, by Carl Hierholzer, two years after his death.

His proof is a recipe. We run it on an invented town, with eight lands and ten bridges, all of them even.

Start anywhere, and walk. Never use a bridge twice, and keep going until you cannot.

Where can you get stuck? Not in the middle. Each time you enter a land that is not your start, you use one door, and an even number of doors leaves at least one spare. So you can only get stuck back where you started. Your walk is a closed loop.

Here it goes A, B, C, D, and back to A. Four bridges. Six are left.

If no bridge is left, you are done. Otherwise, find a land on your loop that still has unused bridges. Here, it is B.

Every land still has an even number of unused bridges, so the same argument applies. Start at B, walk until you are stuck, and you must come back to B. That is a second loop: B, E, F, and back to B.

Now splice. Like a film editor, cut the first loop at B, and tape the new loop in. A, B, E, F, B, C, D, A. One longer circuit.

One loop is still hanging at D. Same move: walk it, cut at D, and tape it in. Ten bridges, one unbroken circuit. A computer builds it in a time proportional to the number of bridges.

So here is the full rule. A connected graph has a circuit that crosses every bridge once exactly when every land is even. And with exactly two odd lands, there is a walk from one of them to the other: add one temporary bridge between them, take the circuit, and remove the bridge.

Now, a real job. A postman must walk every street of his district, and come back to the post office, walking as little as possible.

If every intersection is even, there is nothing to decide. An Euler circuit walks each street once, and no route can be shorter. But real districts have odd intersections: a T junction, a dead end. Then some streets must be walked twice. The question is which ones.

Here is an invented district: nine intersections, twelve streets. The thickness of a street is its length, in hundreds of metres. Four of the intersections are odd. The streets add up to thirty-four.

Remember the temporary bridge. Walking a street a second time is like adding a copy of it. We want every intersection even once the copies are in. Copy a whole route between two odd intersections, and both ends become even, while every intersection in between stays as it was.

So pair up the odd intersections. For each pair, walk the shortest route between them again. There are three ways to pair four intersections.

The first pairing costs ten extra. The second costs eight. The third costs fourteen.

The cheapest is the second. Copy those four streets, and every intersection is even. The postman walks the district in a circuit of forty-two: the thirty-four of the streets, plus eight.

This is a pairing problem, like the ones in chapter four: choose pairs so that the total cost is as small as possible. With four odd intersections, three pairings are easy to try. With forty odd intersections, there are about three point two times ten to the twenty-third. In 1973, Jack Edmonds and Ellis Johnson showed how to find the best pairing quickly, using a method for pairing called matching.

The problem has a name. In 1960, the Chinese mathematician Kwan Mei-Ko published it in the journal Acta Mathematica Sinica, in Chinese. An English translation followed in 1962. Because of him, it is called the Chinese postman problem. Today it is also called route inspection.

To sum up. In a hall with an odd number of doors, one door has no partner, and a walk has only two ends to give it. If every land is even, a circuit always exists, and splicing loops builds it. And when a district has odd intersections, pair them up with the least extra walking: that is the Chinese postman.

Every city once

Visiting every city once has no known counting rule, unlike crossing every bridge once. Hamilton's dodecahedron game has thirty such cycles. The travelling salesman asks for the shortest tour, which multiplies explosively, yet 85,900 cities were solved. In DNA assembly, using fragments as roads instead of cities turns a hard Hamilton problem into an easy Euler one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we walked over every bridge once, and one rule decided everything: count the odd lands. Today we change a single word. We visit every city once.

It sounds like the same question. It is not. On the way we meet a toy from the 1850s, a travelling salesman, and a laboratory that reads DNA.

In 1856, the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton invented a game. He presented it in 1857, and from 1859 a London toy maker sold it. It is called the Icosian game.

Its board is a dodecahedron, flattened onto paper. It has twenty corners, and thirty roads. Every corner touches three roads.

The goal is simple. Start at any corner, visit every corner exactly once, and come back to where you started. We call that a Hamiltonian cycle.

Compare it with last time. There, every road was used once, and the degrees told us the answer. Here, every corner is used once, and a cycle through twenty corners uses only twenty roads. Ten roads are never walked.

Let us search by hand, the way a program would. Start at corner seven. Always take the free road with the highest number, and never visit a corner twice.

Nineteen steps in, every corner has been visited. But the last corner is not next to the start. The way home is closed.

So we step back, and try another road. Another dead end. Back again. In all, the search takes thirty-one steps forward, meets three dead ends, and backs up twelve times, before the loop finally closes.

The cycle exists. In fact there are thirty of them, counting each loop once, whichever way you turn.

Now the surprise. For bridges, one count of the degrees settled every graph. For cities, no such count is known. No simple test, like all degrees even, tells you whether a cycle through every corner exists. The Hamilton circuit is on Karp's list of twenty-one hard problems, the list from episodes three point four and four point two.

Now give every road a length, and ask for the shortest tour. This is the travelling salesman problem.

Here are nine invented cities, placed at random with seed fifty-four. A tour visits each city once and returns. How many different tours are there? Fix the start, put the other eight in any order, and count a tour and its reverse as one. That makes twenty thousand one hundred and sixty.

A computer can try them all. The shortest tour has length three point three nine. The greedy tour, which always goes to the nearest city not yet visited, is twenty-eight point eight percent longer.

But the count explodes. With ten cities, there are a hundred and eighty-one thousand tours. With twenty cities, about six point one times ten to the sixteenth. With thirty cities, about four point four times ten to the thirtieth.

Yet people solve big ones. In 2005 and 2006, a team of seven researchers, using a program called Concorde, found the best possible tour through eighty-five thousand nine hundred cities. The tour is a hundred and forty-two million, three hundred and eighty-two thousand, six hundred and forty-one units long, and no tour is shorter. The program does not try every tour. It rules out whole families of tours at once.

Now the twist. A sequencing machine reads DNA in short overlapping pieces. Take a ten-letter string, A T G G C G T G C A, and cut it into pieces of three letters. That gives eight fragments. Real genomes are cut into far more pieces.

The job is to put the pieces back in order. So make each fragment a city. Draw an arrow from one fragment to another when the end of the first matches the start of the second. Ordering all the fragments is a path through every city once. This is a Hamilton problem.

On our toy there are eleven arrows, and two orders that work. For a real genome, this is hard in general. It was the way the first assemblers saw the problem.

In 2001, Pavel Pevzner, Haixu Tang and Michael Waterman turned the picture inside out. They built on work by Idury and Waterman in 1995. The cities are now the two-letter pieces: seven of them. And each fragment becomes a road, from its first two letters to its last two. This is called a de Bruijn graph.

Eight fragments, eight roads. We no longer want to visit every city. We want to use every road once. That is an Euler path.

Check the degrees, as we did last time. Only two cities are unbalanced. A T has one road out more than roads in, and C A has one road in more than roads out. The path must start at the first and end at the second.

A computer finds it in a time proportional to the number of roads. Read the letters along the path, and out comes A T G G C G T G C A. The original string.

A second order also fits, because the piece T G can be followed in two ways. A repeat can make several orders possible. That fact belongs to the data, not to the method.

Same fragments, same string. The only change was what we call a city, and what we call a road. That choice turned a hard problem into an easy one. In episode three point four, two questions that looked alike had opposite difficulties. Here, it was one question, asked in two ways.

To sum up. Every bridge once has a counting rule. Every city once has none that we know, and sits on Karp's list. The salesman's tours multiply faster than any computer can count, and yet one with eighty-five thousand nine hundred cities has been solved. And choosing roads instead of cities can turn a Hamilton problem into an Euler problem.

Who beats whom

In a league where every pair plays once, arrows from winner to loser form a tournament. Cycles are common, so a clean ranking can fail, as when three teams each beat one other. Yet a chain where each team beat the next always exists, built by insertion, and there is always a king reaching every other in two steps, as in hen pecking orders.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we looked for a path through every city once. Today the cities are teams, and every road has a direction.

Picture a small league. Six teams. Every team plays every other team once, and nobody draws. That makes fifteen matches.

Draw each match as an arrow, from the winner to the loser. A graph with exactly one arrow between every pair is called a tournament. Here are our six teams in a row, with their fifteen results. Arrows that point right run above the line. Those that point left run below it.

The question is simple. Can we rank the teams? A ranking would put one team first, then a second that it beat, then a third that the second beat, and so on down the line.

Count the wins. Teams B, C and D all have four. The scoreboard cannot choose between them. And look at what these three did to each other. B beat C. C beat D. And D beat B. Nobody is first. Each one has won once and lost once. This is a cycle.

Cycles are not rare. With three teams there are eight possible leagues, and two of them are cycles. With five teams there are one thousand and twenty-four. Only a hundred and twenty are fully consistent, with no cycle at all.

The Marquis de Condorcet met the same circle in 1785, with votes. Three voters rank three candidates. One votes A, B, C. One votes B, C, A. One votes C, A, B. Two voters out of three prefer A to B. Two prefer B to C. And two prefer C to A. The majority chases its own tail.

So a clean ranking by wins can fail. And yet something weaker always works. In 1934, László Rédei proved that in every tournament, the teams can be lined up so that each team beat the next one.

That is a Hamilton path, from last time: every team once, following the arrows. There, finding one was hard. Here it always exists, and it is easy to build.

Keep a chain in which each team beat the next. A newcomer arrives. If it beat the first team of the chain, put it in front. If not, the first team beat it. Walk down the chain until you meet a team the newcomer beat, and slide in just before it. The team ahead beat the newcomer, and the newcomer beat the team behind. The chain stays unbroken. If the newcomer beat nobody, it goes at the end.

Watch it with our league. B beat A, so B goes in front. C lost to B but beat A, so it slides in between. D beat B, so it goes in front. E beat nobody, so it goes at the end. F lost to the first three, but beat A, so it goes in before A.

One chain: D, B, C, F, A, E. Each team beat the next. The method is the proof, and it works for every league. We checked all thirty-two thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight leagues of six teams.

One match goes against the line: C beat D. The cycle has to break somewhere. And the ranking is not unique. Our league has three, because B, C and D can each take the lead. When there is no cycle at all, there is exactly one.

Now, a flock of hens. In 1922, the zoologist Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe described the pecking order. Each pair of hens settles who pecks whom. It is a tournament again, with pecks instead of matches. He found that hens could mostly be put in order, and he also reported triangles, three hens pecking in a circle.

In 1980, Stephen Maurer studied such flocks in Mathematics Magazine. He called a hen a king if it reaches every other hen in at most two pecks. Either it pecked her, or it pecked a hen who pecked her. His first theorem says that every flock has a king.

Here is a flock of seven, invented. No hen pecks all six others, so there is no clean top. Now count the wins. Hen C has five. Hen E pecked C, so C does not beat everyone directly. But C pecked A, and A pecked E. Two pecks reach every hen in the yard.

Why is the hen with the most wins always a king? Suppose a hen u pecks the top hen v, and none of v's victims pecks u. Then u pecks v and all of v's victims. That is more wins than v. But v had the most. So that cannot happen.

This flock has three kings: A, C and E. A king can be pecked, and a king does not need a ranking. Even when no order is consistent, one hen dominates in two steps.

To sum up. A league is a tournament, one arrow for every pair. Cycles are common, so who beats whom can go in circles. A chain where each team beat the next always exists, and insertion builds it. And there is always a king, a hen that reaches every other in two pecks.

Last time, we ranked teams and crowned a king. Today we leave the sports hall and open an electrician's notebook.

Here is a small circuit, made up for the purpose. It has eight junctions, called H, A, B, C, D, E, F and G. It has nine wires. To keep the numbers whole, every wire carries a resistor of one ohm.

In 1845, Gustav Kirchhoff wrote down two laws for circuits like this one. The first is about junctions. The current flowing into a junction equals the current flowing out. Nothing is lost there. The second is about loops. Go once around any loop, and the voltages add up to zero.

Take the first law, and write it for junction H. Five wires meet there, and their currents must balance. Every junction has its own equation. Eight junctions, eight equations.

Now put the equations in a table. One row for every junction, and one column for every junction. In row H, we write how each junction takes part in the equation of H. H itself gets a five, the number of wires it has. Each neighbour gets a minus one. Every other junction gets a zero.

Look at row H. Six cells hold a number. Two cells hold a zero, F and G, because they are not wired to H.

Fill every row the same way. Now add up each row. Each one gives zero. If every junction sits at the same voltage, no current flows anywhere.

So here is the answer to last time's question. The wires are hiding in the cells that are not zero. Every minus one is a wire. Read the table, and you can redraw the circuit. It is the grid from episode one point five, with the degrees written on its diagonal.

Count the cells. Twenty-six out of sixty-four are not zero. Now take a bigger circuit, a mesh of ten by ten junctions. Its table has ten thousand cells. Only four hundred and sixty are not zero. That is four point six percent. A junction still touches only a few wires, so the bigger the circuit, the emptier its table.

Kirchhoff did more than write these equations. In 1847 he published a paper on how to solve them. It already contains an idea that, today, counts spanning trees. Historians still debate how clearly he saw the count.

Remember spanning trees from episode two point six. They are the wires that connect every junction without closing a loop. Here is the recipe. Choose one junction as ground, say G. Delete its row and its column from the table. Seven rows are left. Now compute the determinant of what is left. It is a single number that a computer gets from any square table of numbers. For our circuit, it is nine.

Nine what? Nine spanning trees. To check, we list them. Pick seven of the nine wires so that every junction is connected and no loop closes. There are thirty-six ways to pick seven wires. Exactly nine of them work. Nine and nine.

Choose another ground, and the answer is still nine. We tried all eight.

Why nine? The triangle H, A, B can be opened in three ways. The triangle H, C, D can be opened in three more. The tail E, F, G has no choice. Three times three is nine. Today this is called Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem.

The table has a name, the Laplacian, and chapter eleven studies it. And the series on electronics builds Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws in its chapter two.

Now the part that surprises people. How does a computer solve these equations? It works through the table one unknown at a time. This is called elimination. Take one junction, and use its equation to remove its unknown from all the others.

In 1961, Parter showed that elimination has a picture. Removing a junction means taking it out of the drawing, and wiring all of its neighbours to each other. Every new wire fills a cell that was empty.

Set the ground aside. Seven junctions and eight wires remain. Try the hub first. H has five neighbours, A, B, C, D and E. Remove it, and link them in pairs. That makes ten pairs. Two are already wired. So eight new wires appear. Sixteen empty cells turn into numbers.

Now try another order. Start with F. It has one neighbour, E, so removing it links nothing. Then E. Then A, B, C and D, each with neighbours that are already wired. Finish with H. Not a single new wire.

Same circuit. Same answer. Count the entries each step must update. The hub first needs fifty-six updates. The leaves first needs twelve.

We tried all five thousand and forty orders of the seven junctions. Eight hundred and sixteen add no wire at all. The worst adds twelve.

The best orders come from looking at the drawing, not at the numbers. A popular rule of thumb is to remove a junction with the fewest neighbours first. On this circuit, it adds nothing. That is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee.

To sum up. The equations of a circuit make a table, one row for every junction. The cells that are not zero are the wires. Delete the ground, and the determinant counts spanning trees. And solving the table is a game on the graph, where the order of removal decides the work.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Five chapters in, you have met a lot of graphs. Most of them began as something that did not look like a graph at all. A ferry crossing. A bridge walk. A school timetable.

Today we collect the clues. Each clue is a sentence that people say out loud, and each one hides a graph. Think of a field guide to animal tracks. You find a footprint, and you name the animal.

Here, the footprint is the phrase, and the animal is the graph. On the right of each row, you will find the episode that explains it. We build the guide row by row. There are fourteen.

Someone asks: who is connected to whom? Or: who knows whom?

That is the simplest sign of all. Dots, and lines between them: vertices and edges. Everything else stands on this.

Next, a sentence with a direction. In one direction only. Who follows whom?

The lines now carry arrows. This is a directed graph, and every node counts what comes in and what goes out.

Two kinds of things, and links between the two. People and events, or tasks and time slots.

No link ever joins two things of the same kind. That makes it a bipartite graph.

In how many steps, at the shortest? Hear that, and start counting steps.

You are looking at a shortest path. With lengths on the links, you add them up instead of counting, and the question stays the same.

Connect everything at the least cost. Cables, pipes, roads.

Here the graph is a spanning tree: the cheapest set of links that still keeps everything connected.

How much can get through? Where is the bottleneck?

Now you are looking at a flow, and a cut. The largest flow and the smallest cut have the same value.

What happens if this link goes down? Somebody is worried about a failure.

Look for a bridge, or a cut vertex: one link, or one node, whose loss splits the network apart.

These two cannot be together. Keep it conflict-free.

That calls for a colouring. Every item gets a colour, and two items in conflict never share one.

Form pairs. Assign each person to one slot.

The graph is a matching: a set of links, no two of which share a node.

From this situation, I can move to something else. Whoever says that is describing a graph of states.

Each situation is a node, and each allowed move is a link. The graph can be far too big to ever draw.

Whenever you hear before, or depends on, or prerequisite, there is an order to respect.

Behind it sits a directed graph with no cycle, and the order of the tasks is hidden inside.

Pass through everywhere, just once. That could mean every bridge, or every city.

Every bridge gives an Euler circuit, and it has a simple test. Every city gives a Hamilton circuit, and it is far harder.

Who beat whom? A league, a pecking order.

That league is a tournament: every pair has exactly one arrow, from the winner to the loser.

Last sign: a big square table, almost empty.

This one is a sparse adjacency matrix: a graph written as a table, with only a few cells filled.

Now read the phrases again, and listen to the words. A baker says: mix before you bake. A builder says: pipes before plaster. A programmer says: tests before release. A hospital says: scans before the consult.

Four trades that never talk to each other. Four different tasks. But draw each one, and you get the same graph: one task first, two that can happen in either order, and one last.

Each of them has exactly two valid orders, and the same graph explains both.

It is not only before. Compatible, connects, goes through: the same words come back, in trades that never compare notes. A word that keeps coming back is a clue.

So here is the guide: fourteen footprints, and fourteen hidden graphs. You can carry it with you.

Next time you hear one of these sentences, in a lab, a kitchen or a meeting, stop and ask: where is the graph?

Until now, we looked for the graph. From here on, we have it on the table, and we measure it.
