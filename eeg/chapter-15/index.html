The multiple-comparisons mountain

With 64 channels, 500 time points and 30 frequencies, testing each value at five percent produces tens of thousands of false positives from noise alone, like convicting innocent people in a courtroom. Bonferroni is far too strict for correlated EEG data, and false discovery rate answers a different question. Cluster and threshold-free methods exploit structure, and one predefined hypothesis avoids the problem.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to chapter fifteen: statistics that hold up. First, the multiple-comparisons mountain.

An E-E-G dataset offers a huge number of places to look. Take sixty-four channels, five hundred time points, and thirty frequencies. That is nine hundred and sixty thousand values. If you test each one at the usual five percent level, you expect forty-eight thousand significant results from pure noise alone.

Here is what that looks like. Simulate random data for two conditions, with no true difference anywhere. Run a t-test at every channel and time point, and colour every p-value below zero point zero five. The map lights up with spots everywhere. Some of them even form convincing blobs, because neighbouring points are correlated.

Picture a courtroom. If you put a thousand innocent people on trial, each with a five percent chance of being wrongly convicted, about fifty will be convicted. No crime was committed. The verdicts are a property of the procedure, not of the people.

This is the multiple-comparisons problem. The more tests you run, the more false positives you get. What we want to control is usually the family-wise error rate: the probability of at least one false positive anywhere in the whole map.

The classic fix is the Bonferroni correction: divide the threshold by the number of tests. With nine hundred and sixty thousand tests, the threshold becomes about five times ten to the minus eight. It does control false positives. But it assumes that every test is independent.

E-E-G data are anything but independent. Neighbouring time points are correlated, because of filtering and slow dynamics. Neighbouring channels are correlated, because of volume conduction. Neighbouring frequencies are correlated, because of spectral smoothing. The effective number of independent tests is far smaller than the number of values. Bonferroni treats them as independent, and becomes far too strict: it throws away real effects.

Another approach controls the false discovery rate, introduced by Yoav Benjamini and Yosef Hochberg in 1995. Instead of preventing any false positive, it limits the expected proportion of false positives among the significant results, for example to five percent. It is more powerful than Bonferroni, but it answers a different question, and it says nothing about which specific discoveries are false.

The most popular approaches in E-E-G exploit the structure of the data. Real effects tend to extend over neighbouring points in time, space and frequency. Noise tends to produce smaller, scattered spots. Cluster-based permutation tests and threshold-free cluster enhancement, coming in the next episodes, build on this idea, and use permutations to get valid error control without assuming independence.

There is also a simpler solution, often overlooked: test fewer things. If your hypothesis concerns the P3 at parietal electrodes between three hundred and six hundred milliseconds, define that window and region in advance, and run one test. A well-defined, preregistered question is the most powerful correction of all.

David Groppe, Thomas Urbach and Marta Kutas reviewed these methods for E-E-G and M-E-G in 2011.

In Python, M-N-E provides Bonferroni and false discovery rate corrections, and the permutation-based cluster tests we meet next.

To recap. E-E-G offers hundreds of thousands of tests, and at five percent each, noise alone produces thousands of false positives. Bonferroni controls them but is far too strict for correlated data. The false discovery rate controls the proportion of false discoveries. Cluster-based and threshold-free methods exploit the structure of E-E-G. And testing a single, predefined hypothesis avoids the problem altogether.

Next time: permutation tests, by hand.

PLATE 15.24:06

Permutation tests by hand

A permutation test builds the null distribution from the data itself: if conditions made no difference, labels are exchangeable. For paired designs, random sign flips are recomputed thousands of times, and the p-value is the proportion of shuffles at least as extreme as the observed effect. Recording the maximum statistic across all tests controls family-wise error; exchangeability is the assumption that must hold.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Permutation tests, by hand. Building the null distribution from your own data.

Most classic statistical tests rely on assumptions about the shape of the data, such as a normal distribution. Permutation tests replace those assumptions with a simple, intuitive idea.

Start with a question. You recorded twenty participants in two conditions, A and B, and you measured the P3 amplitude in each. On average, A is two microvolts larger than B. Could such a difference arise by chance, if the conditions made no difference at all?

If the null hypothesis is true, the labels "A" and "B" are arbitrary. For any participant, swapping their two values should not change anything meaningful. That is the key assumption, called exchangeability.

So let us swap. For each participant, flip a coin. Heads, keep the labels. Tails, swap A and B. Then recompute the average difference. Do this a thousand times, or ten thousand. Each time, you get a difference that could have occurred if the labels meant nothing. Together, these values form the null distribution.

Picture a courtroom where the defence asks: "If my client's fingerprints were randomly reassigned to other people in the room, how often would we see a match this strong?" If almost never, the match is meaningful.

Now place the observed difference on this distribution. The p-value is the proportion of permuted differences that are as large or larger than the observed one. If only one percent of the shuffles reach two microvolts, p is about zero point zero one.

For paired designs, like this one, the permutation is a sign flip: swapping A and B within a participant simply flips the sign of their difference. For independent groups, you shuffle which participants belong to which group. The permutation scheme must match the design: only exchange what the null hypothesis says is exchangeable.

A few practical points. With twenty participants and sign flips, there are two to the twentieth possible permutations, over a million. In practice, a random subset of a few thousand is enough. The smallest p-value you can report is about one divided by the number of permutations plus one, so use enough permutations for the precision you need.

Permutation tests make no assumption about normality or equal variances. They work with any statistic: a mean difference, a t-value, a correlation, a decoding accuracy, or a connectivity value. And they extend naturally to many tests at once. At each permutation, record the maximum statistic across all channels and time points. The distribution of this maximum controls the family-wise error across the whole map.

But they are not magic. Exchangeability must really hold. If trials are not exchangeable, for example because of slow drifts or block designs, shuffling them creates a wrong null distribution. Permuting individual trials within a participant, then treating trials as independent, ignores the fact that they come from the same person. And permutation tests answer the question they are asked: they test the null hypothesis of exchangeability, not every possible difference.

In Python, M-N-E provides permutation t-tests with the maximum statistic correction, and SciPy offers a general permutation test function.

To recap. Under the null hypothesis, condition labels are exchangeable. Shuffle them many times, recompute the statistic, and build a null distribution from your own data. The p-value is the proportion of shuffles at least as extreme as the observed value. Use sign flips for paired designs and group shuffles for independent groups. Using the maximum across tests controls the family-wise error. Exchangeability is the one assumption that must hold.

Next time: cluster-based permutation tests.

PLATE 15.34:13

Cluster-based permutation tests

Cluster-based permutation tests threshold a statistic map, group neighbouring supra-threshold points in time and space, score each cluster by its summed statistic, and compare the largest cluster to its permutation distribution. This controls family-wise error with high sensitivity to broad effects. A significant cluster only shows a difference exists somewhere; onset, offset and location are not tested.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Cluster-based permutation tests. Using the shape of real effects.

Real E-E-G effects are rarely a single point. A true difference between conditions usually extends over neighbouring time points, neighbouring electrodes, and sometimes neighbouring frequencies. Noise, on the other hand, tends to produce isolated, scattered points. Cluster-based permutation tests, described by Eric Maris and Robert Oostenveld in 2007, turn this observation into a test.

Here is how it works, in four steps.

Step one: compute a statistic at every point, for example a t-value at every channel and time point.

Step two: choose a cluster-forming threshold, for example the t-value corresponding to p less than zero point zero five. Keep only the points that exceed it. Then group supra-threshold points that are neighbours, in time and in space, into clusters. Neighbours in space are defined by an adjacency matrix: which electrodes are next to which.

Step three: give each cluster a score, usually the sum of the t-values inside it, called the cluster mass.

Step four: compare with permutations. Shuffle the condition labels, as in the last episode, and repeat steps one to three. At each permutation, record only the largest cluster mass anywhere. After a thousand permutations, you have the distribution of the largest cluster expected by chance. An observed cluster is significant if its mass exceeds, say, ninety-five percent of these maxima.

Picture a courtroom where single, isolated pieces of evidence are not enough. The jury looks for a connected chain of evidence. A long, coherent chain is unlikely to arise by accident. A few scattered coincidences are not.

Because the test only uses the maximum cluster at each permutation, it controls the family-wise error rate across the whole map, with a single test. And because clusters capture the extent of effects, it is far more sensitive than Bonferroni for the broad effects typical of E-E-G.

Now, what exactly does a significant cluster tell you? This is the part most often misread. Jona Sassenhagen and Dejan Draschkow explained it clearly in 2019.

The test controls the error of the statement: "there is a difference between conditions somewhere in the data." It does not control the error of statements about where or when exactly. The cluster's first time point is not a reliable estimate of the effect's onset. Its edges are not reliable boundaries. A point inside a significant cluster is not individually significant. So you should not write "the effect started at one hundred and twelve milliseconds" or "the effect was present at electrode P4" based on the cluster alone.

The choice of cluster-forming threshold also matters. A strict threshold favours strong, focal effects. A lenient one favours weak, widespread effects. Both are valid, as long as the threshold is chosen in advance. But changing it after looking at the data is a form of p-hacking.

Positive and negative clusters are usually tested separately, or with a two-tailed procedure. And the adjacency definition should match your montage.

In Python, M-N-E provides permutation cluster tests for one sample or paired data, for several groups, and for spatio-temporal data with a channel adjacency, along with tools to plot clusters on topographies.

To recap. Cluster-based permutation tests threshold the statistic map, group neighbouring supra-threshold points into clusters, score them by their summed statistic, and compare the largest cluster to its permutation distribution. They control the family-wise error with high sensitivity to extended effects. But a significant cluster only says that a difference exists somewhere: it does not establish the precise onset, offset or location of the effect. Choose the threshold in advance.

Next time: threshold-free cluster enhancement.

PLATE 15.43:50

TFCE

Threshold-free cluster enhancement removes the arbitrary cluster-forming threshold by slicing the statistic landscape at many heights and combining, for each point, its height with the extent of its island. Permutations of the maximum score control family-wise error. It performs well but costs computation, and point-wise values depend on neighbours, so precise onset or location claims stay unsafe.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Threshold-free cluster enhancement. Clusters, without choosing a threshold.

Last time, cluster-based permutation tests needed one arbitrary choice: the cluster-forming threshold. A strict threshold finds strong, focal effects. A lenient one finds weak, widespread effects. And the result can change depending on which you pick.

Threshold-free cluster enhancement, or T-F-C-E, removes this choice. Stephen Smith and Thomas Nichols introduced it for brain imaging in 2009, and it has since been adapted to E-E-G and M-E-G.

Here is the idea. Instead of one threshold, use all of them. Imagine the statistic map as a landscape, with hills where the effect is strong. Now slice the landscape horizontally at many heights, from low to high. At each height, each point that is above water belongs to some island: a cluster at that height. Record the size of that island.

The T-F-C-E score of a point combines, over all heights, how high the point is and how big its island is at each height. A point on a tall, broad hill gets a high score. A point on a narrow spike gets a moderate score. A point on a low but very wide plateau also gets a decent score. Every point receives its own enhanced value.

Picture a courtroom where, instead of fixing one standard of proof in advance, the jury weighs each piece of evidence at every level of strictness, and gives more weight to evidence that stays connected to other evidence as the standard rises.

Two exponents control the balance between height and extent. The original values, for three-dimensional images, gave more weight to height than to extent. For E-E-G data, other values have been proposed, and software defaults may differ, so report the parameters you use.

Then comes the familiar step: permutations. Shuffle the labels, recompute the T-F-C-E map, and record its maximum value. The distribution of these maxima gives a threshold that controls the family-wise error across the whole map. Each observed point is compared to it.

What do you gain? No arbitrary threshold. Good sensitivity to both focal and extended effects. Simulation studies, such as the work of Cyril Pernet and colleagues in 2015 on event-related potentials, found that T-F-C-E performs well compared with cluster-based tests.

What do you pay? Computation time: the enhancement must be computed at many heights, for every permutation. For large spatio-temporal maps, this can take a while.

And interpretation still needs care. T-F-C-E gives a value at each point, which tempts us to read the result point by point. But the enhanced value of a point depends on its neighbours. A significant point may owe its score partly to a strong effect next to it. So, as with clusters, be cautious when making precise claims about onset times or exact locations. Statements like "the effect was present in this period and region" are safer than "the effect started exactly here".

In Python, M-N-E's permutation cluster functions accept a threshold argument in the form of a dictionary with a start and a step, which switches them to T-F-C-E.

To recap. T-F-C-E replaces the single cluster-forming threshold with all thresholds at once. Each point's score integrates its height and the extent of its cluster across heights. Permutations of the maximum score control the family-wise error. It removes an arbitrary choice and performs well, at a higher computational cost, and its point-wise values still depend on neighbours.

Next time: mixed models, trials within participants.

PLATE 15.54:04

Mixed models · Trials within participants

Averaging trials per participant before testing discards information. Mixed models analyse single trials, like marbles in jars, with fixed effects for the condition and random intercepts and slopes for participants, and weight participants by their information, handle unequal trial counts, allow trial-level predictors and crossed random effects for items. The random structure follows the design, and simplifications are reported.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Mixed models: trials within participants.

The classic E-E-G analysis is two-stage. First, average all trials of each condition within each participant. Then run a t-test or an ANOVA across participants. It is simple and robust. But it throws away a lot of information.

Picture a courtroom collecting testimony. Each witness, our participant, gives many statements, our trials. The two-stage approach summarizes each witness by a single average statement. It ignores that some witnesses gave many consistent statements, and others gave a few contradictory ones.

Mixed models keep both levels at once. Imagine marbles in jars. Each marble is a trial, each jar is a participant. Marbles in the same jar are more alike than marbles from different jars. A mixed model describes both: the variability between trials inside a jar, and the variability between jars.

Here is the structure. The model predicts each single-trial value, for example the amplitude of the N400 on each trial, from fixed effects and random effects. Fixed effects are what you want to test, like the condition. Random effects describe how participants differ. A random intercept lets each participant have their own baseline amplitude. A random slope lets the effect of condition differ between participants.

Why does this matter?

First, unequal trial counts. After artifact rejection, one participant may keep eighty trials and another only thirty. The two-stage approach gives both averages equal weight, though one is much noisier. A mixed model weighs each participant according to how much information they provide.

Second, trial-level predictors. You can include variables that change from trial to trial: reaction time, word frequency, the position of the trial in the experiment, or a continuous stimulus property. Averaging would erase them.

Third, items. In language experiments, the same words are presented to everyone. Some words may produce larger responses for reasons unrelated to the manipulation. Crossed random effects, for participants and for items at the same time, let you generalize to new participants and to new words. Harald Baayen and colleagues argued for this in 2008.

There are choices to make. Which random effects to include? Dale Barr and colleagues argued in 2013 for including the random slopes justified by the design, the "maximal" structure, to avoid inflated false positives. But complex models can fail to converge, and then need to be simplified, in a principled and reported way.

Single-trial E-E-G is noisy, so mixed models are often applied to a predefined measure: a mean amplitude in a window and region, or a band power. They can also be fit at every time point and channel, but then the multiple-comparisons problem returns, and you need the corrections from earlier episodes.

Ian Volpert-Esmond, Elizabeth Page-Gould and Bruce Bartholow wrote a practical guide to multilevel models for E-R-P data in 2021.

In Python, statsmodels offers linear mixed models with random intercepts and slopes, and the bambi package fits Bayesian mixed models with a formula syntax, including crossed random effects.

To recap. Averaging trials per participant discards information. Mixed models analyse single trials while accounting for participants, with random intercepts and slopes. They handle unequal trial counts, include trial-level predictors, and can model items as crossed random effects. Choose the random structure by design, report simplifications, and correct for multiple comparisons if you fit many models.

Next time: bootstrap, confidence intervals and effect sizes.

PLATE 15.63:59

Bootstrap, confidence intervals and effect sizes

A p-value says nothing about how large or how precise an effect is, so effect sizes and confidence intervals should accompany any test. The bootstrap resamples participants with replacement many times to obtain an interval for almost any statistic. Resampling must use independent units, not trials, and a wide interval including zero means the data cannot decide.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Bootstrap, confidence intervals and effect sizes. Beyond "p less than zero point zero five".

A p-value answers a narrow question: how surprising would these data be if there were no effect? It does not say how large the effect is, or how precisely you have measured it. A tiny, unimportant effect can be highly significant with enough participants. A large effect can miss significance in a small study.

So, alongside any test, report two things: the size of the effect, and a confidence interval around it.

Effect sizes come in two flavours. Raw effect sizes are in the original units: "the P3 was two point one microvolts larger in condition A." These are often the most interpretable, especially when the units are familiar. Standardized effect sizes divide by a measure of variability, like Cohen's d. For paired designs, the version called d sub z divides the mean difference by the standard deviation of the differences. Standardized sizes help compare studies, but they depend on how noisy the measure is, not only on the brain.

A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for the effect. A ninety-five percent interval is built by a procedure that, over many repetitions of the study, captures the true value ninety-five percent of the time.

How do we get one without assuming a normal distribution? With the bootstrap, introduced by Bradley Efron in 1979.

Here is the idea. You have twenty participants. Draw twenty participants from your sample, with replacement: some will appear twice, some not at all. Compute the effect on this new sample. Repeat ten thousand times. You get ten thousand plausible values of the effect. The middle ninety-five percent of them, between the two and a half and ninety-seven and a half percentiles, form a percentile bootstrap confidence interval.

Picture a courtroom asking not just "guilty or not?", but "how much damage was done, and how sure are we of that amount?" The bootstrap estimates that uncertainty by replaying the trial many times with the witnesses we have.

A key rule: resample the units that are independent. In most E-E-G studies, that is participants, not trials. Resampling single trials as if they were independent underestimates the uncertainty, because trials from the same person are correlated.

The bootstrap works with almost any statistic: a mean, a median, a peak latency, a correlation, a decoding accuracy. For a latency or a time course, you can draw a confidence band around the whole curve.

Some cautions. With very small samples, bootstrap intervals can be too narrow. Improved versions, like the bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap, help. Guillaume Rousselet, Cyril Pernet and Rand Wilcox have written practical guides on robust estimation and bootstrap methods for neuroscience.

And remember: an interval that excludes zero does not make the effect important, and an interval that includes zero does not prove the absence of an effect. A wide interval that includes zero simply says that the data cannot tell. That is where the next episode comes in.

In Python, SciPy provides a bootstrap function with percentile and bias-corrected methods, and effect sizes are easy to compute with NumPy.

To recap. p-values do not measure the size or precision of an effect. Report raw or standardized effect sizes with confidence intervals. The bootstrap resamples participants with replacement to estimate the uncertainty of any statistic. Resample independent units, prefer improved methods for small samples, and remember that wide intervals mean the data cannot decide.

Next time: Bayesian statistics, in brief.

PLATE 15.74:09

Bayesian statistics in brief

A non-significant p-value cannot show that an effect is absent. The Bayes factor compares how well the alternative and the null predict the data, and can support either or show the data are inconclusive. It depends on the prior, so the prior must be justified and tested for sensitivity, and Bayesian methods do not rescue poor designs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Bayesian statistics, in brief. Evidence for an effect, and evidence for its absence.

A classic test gives a p-value. If it is below zero point zero five, you reject the null hypothesis. If it is above, you cannot conclude much. A non-significant result does not show that there is no effect. It might simply mean that the study was too small or too noisy.

This is a real problem in science. Sometimes the absence of an effect is the interesting finding. Does a drug leave the P3 unchanged? Is the response to two stimuli really the same? A p-value cannot support such claims.

Bayesian statistics offers a different approach. Instead of asking "how surprising are the data, if there is no effect?", it compares two hypotheses directly: how well does each one predict the data we observed?

The central quantity is the Bayes factor. Bayes factor one zero, written B-F one zero, is the ratio of how well the alternative hypothesis predicts the data, to how well the null hypothesis does. A B-F one zero of ten means the data are ten times more likely under the alternative. A B-F one zero of one tenth means the data are ten times more likely under the null: evidence for the absence of an effect. And a value close to one means the data do not favour either: they are not informative enough.

Picture a courtroom with three possible verdicts: guilty, innocent, and "the evidence is insufficient". The p-value only offers two: guilty, or "not proven". The Bayes factor can also say "innocent", when the evidence supports it.

Common rules of thumb, following Harold Jeffreys, call Bayes factors between three and ten moderate evidence, and above ten strong evidence, in either direction. Values between one third and three are considered inconclusive. These are conventions, not strict thresholds.

Christian Keysers, Valeria Gazzola and Eric-Jan Wagenmakers explained in 2020 how Bayes factors can establish evidence of absence in neuroscience.

Now, the catch: the prior. To compute how well the alternative hypothesis predicts the data, you have to specify what effect sizes it expects. This is the prior distribution. A common default for t-tests is a Cauchy distribution with a scale of about zero point seven. But the Bayes factor depends on this choice. A prior expecting huge effects will favour the null when the effect is small but real.

So, good practice: justify the prior, ideally from previous studies, and run a sensitivity analysis showing how the Bayes factor changes with other reasonable priors.

Bayesian methods have other advantages. You can monitor the Bayes factor as data come in, and stop when the evidence is strong enough, without the penalties that sequential testing requires in classical statistics, as long as you report the procedure. Bayesian estimation also gives credible intervals: ranges that contain the effect with a given probability, given the model and the prior.

But they are not a shortcut. Bad data and flexible analyses produce misleading Bayes factors too. And Bayes factors for many channels and time points still raise the question of multiple comparisons, in a different form.

In Python, pingouin computes Bayes factors for t-tests and correlations with default priors, and PyMC and bambi allow full Bayesian models, including mixed models.

To recap. A non-significant p-value is not evidence of absence. The Bayes factor compares how well two hypotheses predict the data, and can support the alternative, support the null, or show that the data are inconclusive. It depends on the prior, so justify it and test its sensitivity. Bayesian methods allow monitoring and credible intervals, but do not rescue poor designs.

Next time: double dipping.

PLATE 15.83:50

Double dipping

Double dipping means selecting a window or electrode because conditions differ there, then testing the difference in that same place. On pure noise, such circular analysis pushes false positives far above five percent, like a prosecutor who shows only incriminating evidence. Remedies are choosing parameters in advance, using independent or orthogonal data for selection, testing the whole map with correction, and labelling exploration honestly.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Double dipping. Selecting and testing on the same data.

Here is a scenario that happens in many labs. You compare two conditions. You look at the grand average waveforms, and see that they differ most between three hundred and fifty and four hundred and fifty milliseconds, at electrode P z. So you measure the mean amplitude in that window, at that electrode, and run a t-test. It is significant.

The problem: you chose the window and the electrode because the difference looked large there. Then you tested the difference in exactly that place. The test was bound to look good.

Picture a courtroom where the prosecutor reviews all the evidence, keeps only the pieces that point to the defendant, and then shows the jury only those. Every piece shown is real. But the selection guarantees a convincing case, whether the defendant is guilty or not.

This is called double dipping, or circular analysis. Nikolaus Kriegeskorte and colleagues described it in neuroimaging in 2009, showing how it can create or inflate effects. Steven Luck and Nicholas Gaspelin showed for E-R-Ps in 2017 that with random data, choosing the window and electrodes where the conditions differ most produces significant effects far more often than five percent of the time.

Here it is with pure noise. Simulate two conditions with no true difference. Find the window and electrode with the largest difference in the grand average. Test the difference there. Repeat many times. The false positive rate climbs far above five percent, sometimes above fifty percent, depending on how many windows and electrodes you scan.

Double dipping comes in many forms. Choosing electrodes based on the effect of interest. Choosing frequency bands where conditions differ most. Selecting participants or trials based on the outcome. Reporting the peak accuracy of a decoding curve as if it were a single test. Choosing the analysis pipeline that gives the best result.

What are the solutions?

First, choose the window, electrodes and frequencies in advance, based on previous literature or theory, and write them in a preregistration.

Second, use independent data for selection and testing. For example, select on half of the trials or participants, and test on the other half. Or use a separate localizer task.

Third, select on a contrast that is orthogonal to the one you test. A common example is the collapsed localizer: average all conditions together, find where the overall response is largest, and test the difference between conditions there. This works when the conditions are balanced, though Luck and Gaspelin note it can still be biased in some designs.

Fourth, use methods that test the whole map with proper correction, like cluster-based permutation tests, instead of picking a spot first.

And when exploration is part of the work, which is normal and valuable, say so clearly. Label exploratory analyses as exploratory, and confirm them in new data.

In Python, these safeguards are mostly about workflow: split data with NumPy or scikit-learn, define windows before analysis, and use M-N-E's cluster tests for whole-map inference.

To recap. Double dipping means using the same data to select where to look and to test what you find there. It inflates effects, and can create them from noise. Choose analysis parameters in advance, select on independent or orthogonal data, or test the whole map with proper corrections. Label exploration honestly.

Next time: the garden of forking paths, and multiverse analysis.

PLATE 15.94:05

The garden of forking paths · Multiverse analysis

Every EEG analysis is a path of forks, such as filter, reference, artifact method and window, and decisions made after seeing data can drift toward interesting results. The EEGManyPipelines project, with 168 teams on one dataset, showed how much results vary. Multiverse analysis runs all defensible combinations, and a specification curve shows which choices drive the effect; robust results survive most paths.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The garden of forking paths. Multiverse analysis.

An E-E-G analysis is a long path of decisions. Which high-pass filter? Which reference? How to reject artifacts? I-C-A or not? Which baseline? Which time window and which electrodes? Mean or peak amplitude? Which statistical test? At each step, several choices are reasonable.

Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken called this the garden of forking paths. Even without any bad intention, if decisions are made after seeing the data, each fork can drift toward a more interesting result. And the final p-value hides all the paths that were not taken.

Picture a courtroom where the same evidence is handed to many independent juries. If they all reach the same verdict, you can trust it. If half say guilty and half say innocent, the verdict depends more on the jury than on the evidence.

This is not just theory. In the EEGManyPipelines project, one hundred and sixty-eight teams analysed the same E-E-G dataset to test the same hypotheses. No two teams used the same pipeline, and their results did not always agree. Peter Clayson and colleagues also showed in 2021 that common processing choices for E-R-Ps change the resulting measures in meaningful ways.

So how do we handle this? Multiverse analysis, proposed by Sara Steegen and colleagues in 2016, takes the forks seriously. Instead of picking one path, list all the reasonable choices at each fork, and run every combination. Two filters, three references, two artifact methods and two windows give twenty-four analyses. Then look at the distribution of results across the whole multiverse.

A related tool is the specification curve, from Uri Simonsohn and colleagues. Sort all the analyses by the size of their effect, and plot them as a curve, with a panel below showing which choices each analysis used. You can see at a glance whether the effect is robust, and which choices drive it.

A robust result survives most reasonable paths: the effect keeps the same direction and a similar size across the multiverse. A fragile result appears only under a narrow set of choices. Fragility does not prove that an effect is false, but it should lower our confidence, and it shows where the result is sensitive.

There are practical considerations. The multiverse should contain only choices that are genuinely defensible, not every possible setting. It can grow very fast: five forks with three options each already give two hundred and forty-three analyses. Computation can be heavy, so automated pipelines help. And interpreting many results needs care: the multiverse is a description of robustness, not a new significance test that combines all paths.

Multiverse analysis also pairs well with preregistration. You can preregister one main pipeline and a multiverse as a robustness check, or preregister the multiverse itself.

In Python, the multiverse is easiest with a scripted pipeline: M-N-E steps wrapped in functions with parameters, a loop over the combinations, and a table of results. Packages for workflow management and parallel computing help when the multiverse is large.

To recap. Every analysis passes through many forks, and flexible choices can drift toward interesting results. Many-teams projects like EEGManyPipelines show how much results can vary. Multiverse analysis runs all reasonable combinations and examines the distribution of results. Specification curves show which choices drive the effect. Robust results survive most paths. Keep the multiverse defensible, and report it alongside your main analysis.

Power analysis for E-E-G. How many participants, and how many trials?

Statistical power is the probability that a study detects an effect, if the effect is really there. A study with eighty percent power will find a true effect four times out of five. A study with twenty percent power will miss it four times out of five.

Low power is not just a waste of time. When an underpowered study does find a significant effect, that effect is likely to be exaggerated, because only the lucky overestimates cross the threshold. Katherine Button and colleagues estimated in 2013 that the median statistical power of neuroscience studies was around twenty percent.

Picture a courtroom with a weak investigation. Most guilty defendants go free for lack of evidence. And the few convictions that happen tend to rest on exaggerated or lucky evidence.

For E-E-G, power depends on two kinds of numbers: how many participants, and how many trials per participant. More trials reduce the noise of each participant's estimate. More participants reduce the uncertainty of the group result. They are not interchangeable: beyond a certain point, adding trials stops helping, because differences between participants dominate.

Megan Boudewyn and colleagues explored in 2018, by resampling real E-R-P data, how the number of trials and participants affect power for different components. Daniel Baker and colleagues proposed power contours in 2021: plots of power as a function of both numbers at once, which make the trade-off visible.

How do you compute power for your own study? For a simple t-test, there are formulas. But E-E-G analyses are rarely simple: they involve preprocessing, trial rejection, windows, and corrections for multiple comparisons. So the most honest approach is simulation.

Here is the recipe. First, choose a plausible effect: its size, its timing, and its topography. Second, simulate many datasets with that effect, the right amount of noise, the right trial counts, and the right variability between participants. Third, run your full analysis pipeline on each simulated dataset, including the multiple-comparisons correction. Fourth, count how often the effect is detected. That proportion is your power. Repeat for different numbers of participants and trials, and you get a power curve.

The hardest part is choosing a plausible effect size. The temptation is to use the effect you hope for, or the effect from one exciting published study. But published effects tend to be inflated, for the reasons above. Effects from small pilot studies are very noisy: Casper Albers and Daniel Lakens showed in 2018 that they are unreliable for planning sample sizes. Better sources are meta-analyses, large datasets, or the smallest effect that would still be meaningful for your question.

Noise estimates, on the other hand, can come from existing data: open datasets like ERP CORE, or your own previous recordings, give realistic trial-to-trial and participant-to-participant variability.

And plan for losses: participants excluded, trials rejected. If you expect to reject twenty percent of trials, plan for more.

In Python, statsmodels provides power calculations for simple tests. For realistic E-E-G power analysis, simulate data with NumPy or M-N-E's simulation tools, and run your actual pipeline in a loop.

To recap. Power is the probability of detecting a true effect. Underpowered studies miss real effects and exaggerate the ones they find. E-E-G power depends on both participants and trials, and power contours show the trade-off. Simulate your full pipeline with a plausible effect size, taken from meta-analyses, large datasets or the smallest meaningful effect, not from a hopeful guess or a small pilot. Plan for losses.

Report it so others can redo it. The last episode of the main path.

A result is only as good as our ability to check it. If another lab cannot understand exactly what you did, they cannot reproduce it, build on it, or find its errors. So the final step of any study is reporting: not just the results, but every choice that led to them.

Picture a courtroom where the verdict is published with the full case file: every piece of evidence, every procedure, every decision. Anyone can review it. A verdict that cannot be reviewed is not much of a verdict.

What should a report contain? The Committee on Best Practice in Data Analysis and Sharing of the Organization for Human Brain Mapping published recommendations specific to M-E-G and E-E-G, led by Cyril Pernet, in 2020. It is often called COBIDAS-MEEG. Andreas Keil and colleagues also published publication guidelines for E-E-G and M-E-G studies in 2014. Together, they cover the full chain.

Here is a short checklist, following the path of this series.

The participants: how many, how they were recruited, who was excluded and why.

The recording: the system, the number and positions of electrodes, the reference, the sampling rate, the online filters, and the impedances.

The design: the task, the timing of trials, the number of trials per condition, the randomization.

The preprocessing: every step, in order, with its parameters. Filters with their type, order and cut-offs. The re-referencing. The method and criteria for bad channels, I-C-A and artifact rejection. How many trials remained per participant and condition.

The measures: windows, electrodes, frequencies, and how they were chosen, ideally in advance.

The statistics: the exact test, the correction for multiple comparisons, the effect sizes and their confidence intervals.

The software: names and versions of every package, because defaults change between versions.

Then, share. Data in E-E-G-BIDS format, which we met in episode six point five, on a public repository such as OpenNeuro. Code on a public platform, with a permanent identifier, for example through Zenodo. And, when possible, a preregistration made before the data were collected or analysed, with any deviations reported.

Sharing has real benefits for you too. Other people find your errors before they become a problem. Your data can be reused, and cited. And the act of preparing data and code for others usually makes your own analysis cleaner.

Of course, there are constraints: participant privacy, consent forms, and institutional rules. Plan for sharing when you write the consent form, not after the study.

And here is the best possible ending for any study. Another lab takes your shared data and code, runs them, and gets the same result. Then they collect new data, follow your preregistered protocol, and find the effect again. Reproduced, then replicated.

That is the whole path of this series: from the synaptic current to the scalp potential, from the sampled number to the clean signal, from the measure to the statistic, and finally to a result that others can verify.

In Python, M-N-E-BIDS writes and reads E-E-G-BIDS datasets, M-N-E reports can document your pipeline, and scripts under version control make every step reproducible.

To recap. Report every step, with its parameters, following COBIDAS-MEEG and the E-E-G and M-E-G publication guidelines. List software versions. Share data in E-E-G-BIDS, code with a permanent identifier, and preregistrations with deviations. Plan sharing in the consent form. The goal is a result that others can reproduce, and then replicate.
