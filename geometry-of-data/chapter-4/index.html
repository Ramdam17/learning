PLATE 4.16:14

Sample by sample

Comparing signals sample by sample treats one thousand samples as a vector with one thousand columns. A narrow bump fifty milliseconds late is 5.95 from itself yet only 4.21 from a flat line. Sliding up to one hundred milliseconds fixes a single global delay, bringing it to zero, but a delay that drifts, at twenty, fifty and eighty milliseconds, stays at 8.0.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the recording studio. Every shelf in here holds the same kind of object: a signal. A signal is a list of numbers, taken one after the other in time.

Until now our objects were people, texts and sets. Signals bring something new: time. And time brings delays, stretches, rhythms and an order of reading. This whole chapter asks one question. What do we forgive to time?

Let us start with the most naive answer. Forgive nothing.

Take one second of signal, measured one thousand times per second. That is one thousand samples, so one thousand numbers. And a row of one thousand numbers is a vector with one thousand columns. So the straight line of episode two point one applies, exactly as it stands. Subtract the first sample from the first sample, the second from the second, square, add, take the root. Sample by sample.

Picture two tapes side by side, and one playhead that reads both at the same instant. At each instant the playhead measures the gap between the two tapes. The distance is the total of all those gaps.

Our first signal is a narrow bump, ten milliseconds wide, with height one. Here is the same bump again, fifty milliseconds later. Same shape, same size, only late. The straight line says they are five point nine five apart.

Now compare the bump with a flat line, a recording of nothing at all. Four point two one.

Read that again. For the straight line, the bump is closer to nothing than to itself, fifty milliseconds late. The reason is simple. At every instant, the playhead sees a bump facing a flat stretch, or a flat stretch facing a bump. It never sees the two bumps meet. Each instant is judged alone, and each one adds a gap.

Our clock, TICK, is not surprised. For a bump this narrow, fifty milliseconds is a very long time.

A wave makes it even clearer. Take an oscillation at ten cycles per second, ten hertz. Each cycle lasts one hundred milliseconds. This is the speed of the alpha rhythm in an EEG, and the EEG series shows these oscillations in its episode two point one. Now delay the wave by fifty milliseconds. That is half a cycle. Wherever one wave is up, the other is down. Their correlation is minus one. The straight line gives forty-four point seven. These are as far apart as two such waves can be, and yet they are the same wave, just late.

Let us be systematic, with the row of changes. Here is one simulated signal: a bump riding on a slow wave. We make five copies, and each copy changes one thing.

The first is shifted: every sample goes up by zero point five. The second is amplified: every sample is multiplied by one point six. The third is delayed: the whole signal arrives fifty milliseconds late, and what falls off the end comes back at the start. The fourth is shuffled: the same samples, in a random order. The fifth is noisy: a little random noise is added to every sample.

The straight line sees all five. Shifted, fifteen point eight. Amplified, four point four. Delayed, seven point zero. Shuffled, ten point six. Noisy, three point two.

Look at the delayed copy. Fifty milliseconds of delay count for more than noise added to every single sample. The straight line ignores nothing. In this chapter, that is not always a virtue.

So here is a first repair, the one we met in episode one point five. Slide one signal along the other. Try every slide from minus one hundred to plus one hundred milliseconds. That is two hundred and one tries. Keep the smallest distance.

Go back to the two bumps. As we slide, the distance falls, and at exactly fifty milliseconds the bumps land on each other. The distance is zero. The oscillation does the same. Slid back by half a cycle, the minus one becomes a perfect match, and the distance is zero.

Now the row again. The voyant under the delayed copy goes out. Zero. The others stay lit. Shifted, still fifteen point eight. Amplified, still four point four. Noisy, still three point two. Shuffled, ten point one: no slide can put a random order back in order.

So we can fill the card. The object is a signal: one thousand samples. The distance is the smallest straight line over all the slides. What it ignores is a delay of up to one hundred milliseconds. What it costs is two hundred and one straight lines, about two hundred thousand subtractions, instead of one thousand. And its trap? It allows one single delay for the whole recording.

Let us test that trap. Here are three bumps in a row, at two hundred, five hundred and eight hundred milliseconds. In the copy, the first bump is twenty milliseconds late, the second fifty, the third eighty. The timing drifts along the way.

The straight line gives nine point seven. Now slide. The best single slide puts the middle bump back, and spoils the other two. The distance only falls to eight point zero.

Compare with a copy that is late by fifty milliseconds all the way through. The straight line says ten point three. One slide, and it is zero. A global delay is forgiven cheaply. A delay that changes along the way is not forgiven at all.

If we slid each bump on its own, we would find zero again. But then we would be choosing a different delay for every instant. That is a different tool, and it needs a different table.

Here is the idea to keep. Comparing sample by sample is a bet. It bets that instant five hundred of one signal corresponds to instant five hundred of the other, and to nothing else. We met the same bet in episode three point two, with Richard Hamming, who compared words box by box in nineteen fifty. A slide lets us break the bet once, for the whole recording. It cannot break it again halfway through.

What if the delay itself keeps changing? That needs a way to stretch and squeeze time, instant by instant.

PLATE 4.27:52

Warping time

Two takes of a three-syllable word, one fast and one slow, are 2.12 apart sample by sample. Dynamic time warping finds the cheapest road through a table of gaps, never going back, and reaches 0.55, also matching each syllable. It is not a distance: it can be zero for different sequences and breaks the triangle rule. A band limits how much delay it forgives.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Say the same word twice. Once fast, once slowly.

It is the same word. A listener has no doubt about it.

But record both, and compare them sample by sample, the way we did last time. Instant by instant, the fast take and the slow take disagree almost everywhere. The syllables simply do not arrive at the same moment.

Last time we slid one recording under the other. That repaired one delay, the same everywhere. Here the delay keeps changing. So today we let time stretch.

Here are two takes of one made-up word, with three syllables. They are illustrative curves, thirty-six samples each.

The pink take is fast. The syllables crowd together at the start, and then there is silence. The blue take is slow. The same three syllables, spread over the whole recording.

Compare them instant by instant, as before. Each pink sample is judged against the blue sample at the same position. The first syllable roughly meets itself. After that, the syllables land on silence.

Pile up the squared gaps, take the root, and you get 2.12. That is the straight line of episode 2.1, applied to the whole recording.

Now draw a table. One row for each instant of the pink take. One column for each instant of the blue take. In each cell, write the gap between the two values.

The darker the cell, the bigger the gap. Where a syllable meets a syllable, the cell is pale. Where a syllable meets silence, it is dark.

Sample by sample comparison walks along the diagonal of this table, from the top left corner to the bottom right corner. It is the only road it ever takes. But the pale cells are somewhere else.

So look for a better road. It starts in the top left corner, and it ends in the bottom right corner. At each step it may go right, go down, or go diagonally. It never goes back.

Going right means that one instant of the pink take is matched to one more instant of the blue take. The pink take waits. Going down does the opposite. The blue take waits.

The cost of a road is the same as before. Square the gap in every cell it crosses, add them up, and take the root. We want the cheapest road. That is dynamic time warping.

Watch the road walk. It leaves the diagonal to wait for the slow syllables, and it stays on the pale cells. The cost grows slowly.

At the end, the cost is 0.55. The straight line said 2.12. The road says 0.55.

And the road tells us more than a number. It says which instant of one take corresponds to which instant of the other. Draw a thread for every cell on the road, and each syllable finds its partner.

The idea was born in speech recognition. Vintsyuk described it in 1968, and Sakoe and Chiba published their version in 1978.

How do we find the cheapest road without trying them all? We fill the table from the top left. In each cell, the cheapest way to arrive is the cheapest of three neighbours: the one on the left, the one above, and the one on the diagonal. Add the squared gap of the cell itself, and move on.

One pass over the table, each cell visited once.

Back to the bump of last time. A narrow bump, and the same bump fifty milliseconds later. The straight line put them at 5.95. A slide put them at zero, but only because the delay was the same everywhere.

With the road, they are at zero too. The road bends by fifty samples, and every sample finds its twin.

Now the bump and a flat line. The straight line said 4.21. The road says 4.21 as well. A flat line has nothing to line a bump up with, so every sample of the bump still pays its own gap.

So the bump now looks like itself, late, and not at all like nothing. That is what the straight line got wrong.

Now the row of changes. The same simulated signal as last time, and the same five changes.

Shifted up by half a unit: the road cannot help. It sees the change, at 12.2. Amplified by one point six: it sees it, at 3.4.

Delayed by fifty milliseconds: the road falls to 1.0. Not exactly zero, because this delay wraps the slow wave around the end, and no warping can invent the piece that fell off.

Shuffled: 7.1. The road cannot put a random order back. Noisy: 3.1.

So the road forgives delays and stretches, and nothing else. A vertical shift or a gain, it still sees. That is why people put signals on the same scale first, subtracting the mean and dividing by the spread. After that, the first two fall to zero. But that was the rescaling talking, not the warping. And rescaling is a choice you make, and write down.

Time to fill in the card. The object: a signal. The distance: the cheapest road through the table of gaps. What it ignores: local speeding up and slowing down.

What does it cost? One cell for each pair of instants. One second at a thousand samples a second is a million cells. The straight line needs a thousand subtractions.

We can forbid the road from wandering far from the diagonal. Then only a band of the table is filled. With a band of a hundred milliseconds on each side, that is 191,000 cells, about as many as the 201,000 subtractions of last time's slides.

Now the four rules. Never negative: yes. Each squared gap is positive. The same both ways: yes. Swap the two signals, the table turns over, and the cheapest road is the same road.

Zero only for itself: no. The sequences zero, one, and zero, zero, one are different. Yet they sit at zero, because the road lets the first zero wait.

No shortcut: no. Take the single value zero, then the pair zero, one, then five ones. The first two are at 1. The last two are at 1. But the single zero and the five ones are at 2.24, more than 1 plus 1.

A paper on indexing this distance, published in 2005, stated it plainly: it does not obey the triangle inequality.

Now the trap. Take a short pulse near the start of a recording, and the same pulse near the end. The straight line puts them at 1.41.

The road puts them at zero. With no limit, the road runs along the edge of the table. One instant of the first signal is glued to a long stretch of the second, and then the other way round.

A delay of twenty-five samples is forgiven like any other. Signals that barely resemble each other become neighbours.

So we put the limit back. With a band of four samples, the pulses return to 1.41. The same band also changes our two takes of the word. Their distance climbs from 0.55 to 1.84, because the slow take is more than four samples late on its last syllable.

The band is a real parameter. It says how much delay you agree to forgive. Choose it from what you know about the signal. If a reaction cannot vary by more than a hundred milliseconds, a band of a hundred milliseconds is enough.

So is warping a distance? No. And that is fine, as long as you know it.

Searching for nearest neighbours works. The shortcuts that rest on the triangle rule do not.

What it forgives is yours to set. The width of the band says which delay you accept.

Remember: the road forgives local stretches, it is not a distance, and the band is the dial.

PLATE 4.37:01

Elastic distances that keep the rules

Dynamic time warping breaks the triangle rule in 2.35 percent of random triples. Pricing each stretch, as the real-penalty edit distance does, restores it in all twenty thousand. An envelope bound skips seventy-two percent of candidate tables in nearest-neighbour search without changing answers. Warped averaging keeps one full bump. Elastic distances beat the straight line mainly with few training examples.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Dynamic time warping answers one question: what do we forgive to time? It forgives stretches. Last time it left us with three problems.

Can we repair the triangle rule? Can we make it faster? And can we take an average with it?

Today each problem gets an answer. And then a measurement, to see whether the answers matter.

First, the rule. Take the three tiny signals from the last episode. Zero. Zero, one. And five ones. Dynamic time warping, as defined last time, puts them at one, one, and two point two four. Two point two four is more than one plus one. The shortcut is longer than the detour. The triangle rule is broken.

The repair comes from text. In an edit distance, every edit has a price. In 2004, Chen and Ng brought the idea to signals, as the real-penalty edit distance. A sample that finds a partner pays the gap between the two values. A sample that finds none is a stretch, and a stretch pays its distance to a reference value. Here, zero. Think of it as a toll: a stretch is allowed, but you pay at the booth, and the total of the tolls is part of the distance.

Same three signals. One, four, and five. Five is not more than one plus four. The rule holds, just barely. Chen and Ng present it as a true distance.

I checked on twenty thousand random triples of short signals. Dynamic time warping broke the rule in four hundred seventy of them, two point three five percent. The real-penalty edit distance broke it in none.

One detail. With a reference value of zero, a stretch of zeros is free. Zero, one and zero, zero, one are at distance zero, although they differ.

In 2009, Marteau closed that gap, with the time warp edit distance. Each sample carries its time stamp. A stretch pays for the time it spans, through a stiffness you choose, plus a flat penalty. Now zero, one and zero, zero, one are at distance two, with our settings. Marteau presents it as a true distance too. And the stiffness is a dial. Infinite stiffness is the straight line. No stiffness is dynamic time warping.

So here is its card. It ignores nothing for free: a stretch always pays. It costs the same table as before. Its trap: the price of a stretch is yours to choose.

Second, speed. The table has one cell per pair of instants. For signals of one hundred twenty-eight samples, that is sixteen thousand cells for each pair. Keeping the path within a band cuts that to three thousand. Still, a search through thousands of signals pays it again and again.

In 2002, Keogh had an idea. Wrap one signal in an envelope. At each instant, the envelope spans the highest and lowest values it reaches inside the band. Now take a candidate, and add up how far it sticks out of the envelope. That costs a single pass, one subtraction per sample, nothing like a table.

And warping cannot beat it. Every sample of the candidate must be matched to a sample of the query inside the band, so it pays at least its distance to the envelope.

On our pair, the straight line says four point five. Warping says two point one. The envelope says one point seven. In that order, always.

Now a search keeps the best distance found so far. For a new candidate, compute the envelope bound first. If it already exceeds the best, skip the candidate. No table at all. The answer is exactly the same as without skipping, in all one hundred searches of my simulation. And in that simulation, seventy-two percent of the candidates never needed their table.

What about the worst case? In 2015, two teams asked whether something truly faster than the table exists. Bringmann and Künnemann. And Abboud, Backurs, and Vassilevska Williams. Their answer: a truly faster algorithm would break a widely believed conjecture of complexity theory. So the envelope helps on typical data. In the worst case, we have a reason to think that the table is the price.

Third, the average. Take two bumps with the same shape, one early and one late. Average them sample by sample. You get two half bumps, each at half the height. A shape that nobody produced.

Now average with warping. Align every signal to the current average. Then each sample of the average takes the mean of the samples aligned to it. Repeat. Petitjean, Ketterlin, and Gançarski proposed this in 2011.

On our two bumps, after fifteen rounds, there is one bump, at full height. The warped distance to the two signals adds up to two point eight five for the sample-by-sample average, and zero for the warped one. One detail: it keeps the timing of the signal it started from.

In 2017, Cuturi and Blondel replaced the minimum over alignments with a smooth minimum. The distance becomes differentiable. So you can follow its gradient, to compute averages, or to train a model. The price is still one table.

Now the measurement. Do these distances matter? In 2008, Ding and colleagues compared nine distances on thirty-eight datasets. Among them, dynamic time warping and the real-penalty edit distance. When training examples are few, the elastic distances beat the straight line, significantly. As the examples grow in number, the gap closes. They found no general threshold: on one dataset the gap vanished near four hundred examples, on another it took a few thousand.

I simulated the same effect, on signals I made up, so take it as an illustration and not as a law. Two kinds of signal: one wide bump, or two narrow ones, each shifted and warped at random. With eight training examples, the straight line is right seventy-four percent of the time. Warping, ninety-two. With sixty-four, the straight line is at ninety-eight point seven, warping at ninety-nine point two. With two hundred fifty-six, the order flips: ninety-nine point nine for the straight line, ninety-nine point five for warping.

Why? With many examples, some neighbor almost always has the right rhythm. The straight line only needs to find it.

In 2017, Bagnall and colleagues compared eighteen algorithms on eighty-five datasets. Nearest neighbor with warping remains a hard baseline to beat. Only nine algorithms were significantly better than it, and than a second baseline.

So forgiving stretches helps most when examples are few. With many, someone has the right rhythm, and the straight line is enough. The choice of a distance depends on how much data you have.

Three answers. Price the stretch, to keep the rules. Bound the table, to skip it. Align before averaging.

Next time: Walking the dog.

PLATE 4.46:56

Walking the dog

A person and a dog walk two curves, each at their own pace and never backward; the shortest leash is the Fréchet distance. Letting each choose their pace shortens the leash from 2.1 to 0.8. It ignores pace but sees order: a path going forth, back and forth, beside a line, has Hausdorff distance 0.1 and Fréchet distance 5. One bad step sets the value.

TRANSCRIPTEN

So far in this chapter, the objects we compared were lists of numbers, laid out in time. This time the object is a curve. A trip across a room. The route taken by your eyes over a screen. The state of a system as it evolves. What matters is the line it draws, and the order in which it draws it. In this chapter, every episode asks what we forgive to time. Today the answer is short: the pace, and nothing else.

Here is the picture. A person walks along one curve. Their dog walks along another. Each of them goes at their own pace, and neither of them may ever step backward. They are joined by a leash. What is the length of the shortest leash that lets both of them finish their walk?

That length is the Fréchet distance. The definition is in Fréchet's work of 1906, the text we met in episode 1.2. The picture of the person and the dog is usually credited to Alt and Godau, who gave the first algorithm for it in 1995. The version for lists of points, where the person and the dog hop from one point to the next, is due to Eiter and Mannila, in 1994.

Let us watch. The person follows the blue path, the dog follows the pink one. The leash stretches and shrinks, and the number on the screen is its length right now. At the end, the longest it ever had to be is 0.8. No shorter leash would have worked, however they chose to walk.

Now suppose they do not choose. Suppose that at every moment, both are at the same fraction of their way. That is the sample by sample comparison of episode 4.1. The leash now has to reach 2.1, instead of 0.8. Letting each one pick their own pace is worth a lot of leash.

You have seen the machinery before. In episode 4.2, we built a table. One row for each instant of the first signal, one column for each instant of the second, and in every cell, the gap between the two. We looked for the path from corner to corner that never goes back.

Here is the same table, for the person and the dog, with twenty points each. Darker means farther apart. Walking the dog is the same search, with one change. Dynamic time warping adds up the gaps along the path. The Fréchet distance keeps only the biggest one. So the best path is the one whose worst cell is as light as possible. Here, that cell is 0.8.

The path with the smallest total, the one from episode 4.2, is a different path. Its total is 10.0, and its worst gap is 0.92. Each question picks its own path.

Now compare it with episode 3.5, the worst point. Take a straight line of length 10. Beside it, at a distance of 0.1, a path that goes forth along the line, back again, and forth again. Every point of the path is within 0.1 of the line. Every point of the line is within 0.1 of the path. The Hausdorff distance is 0.1.

Now walk the dog. The person follows the line. The dog goes forth, back, and forth. When the dog reaches the far end, the person must be somewhere. When the dog is back at the start, the person cannot have stepped backward. The best the person can do is to wait in the middle. The leash then stretches to 5 at each turn. The Fréchet distance is 5.0.

That is fifty times the Hausdorff distance, for two paths that go through exactly the same places.

Let us fill in the card. The object is a curve. The distance is the shortest leash that lets both walk. What does it ignore? The pace. Walk the same route slowly, or fast, or sit for ten minutes on a bench, and the distance to the original is zero.

Be careful about what that means. This is a distance between curves, not between signals. A signal says what happens at each instant. A curve only says where you went, and in what order. Two walks of the same route, at different paces, are the same curve.

Here is the row of changes, adapted to curves. Move the curve sideways by 1, and the distance is 1.0. Blow it up by 1.6, and the distance is 2.6. Walk the same curve at another pace, and the distance stays at zero. There is no sensible way to shuffle a curve, so the fourth copy is walked backward. Hausdorff stays at zero, because the places are the same. Fréchet sees the order reversed, and says 8.3. Add a little noise at every vertex, and the distance is 0.17.

What does it cost? The table. Two curves of a hundred points need ten thousand cells. Two curves of a thousand points need a million.

In 2014, Bringmann showed that it is hard to do much better. A method that grows noticeably slower than the square of the number of points would break a widely believed conjecture of complexity theory, the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis. This holds for the exact answer, and even for an answer that is off by only a tenth of a percent.

And the trap? Like Hausdorff, the Fréchet distance listens only to the worst moment. Suppose the dog stays within 0.1 of the person for the whole walk, except for one step, when it chases a squirrel, and ends up 1.8 away. The distance becomes 1.7. The whole walk is summed up by one detour. Hausdorff has the same weakness, as episode 3.5 showed: one stray point decides everything. Here it is worse, because the leash is a worst case by construction. One bad moment gives its value to the whole walk, and the calm steps around it do not count at all.

And it forgets time entirely. Two recordings of a gaze on a screen, one hesitant and slow, one quick and sure, are the same curve if the eyes visited the same places in the same order. If the question is about rhythm, this distance cannot see it.

Sometimes you want to separate the shape from the rhythm, without throwing the rhythm away. Then you turn to elastic distances between curves. Srivastava and his colleagues, in 2011, replace each curve by its velocity, divided by the square root of its speed. After that change, the elastic comparison of two curves becomes the plain straight line of episode 2.1, and re-timing both curves in the same way leaves it unchanged.

So the card is full. Object, a curve. Distance, the shortest leash. Ignores, the pace. Costs, a table, and probably no better. Trap, only the worst gap counts.

Hausdorff asks whether the two curves pass through the same places. Fréchet asks whether they pass through them in the same order. For a trajectory, the order is almost always the question.

PLATE 4.57:01

Comparing spectra

Spectra compare what signals contain, not when it happens. A chord of three oscillations at four, ten and twenty-three hertz keeps its spectrum when phases are scrambled, while its shape changes completely. Taking logarithms stops one frequency carrying ninety-two percent of the distance. Welch averaging cuts the spread of noise spectra, but a one hertz peak move looks as far as a twenty hertz jump.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the recording studio.

So far, we compared signals moment by moment, then with a delay allowed. Today we change the question. We stop comparing the signals themselves. We compare what they contain.

Any signal can be written as a sum of oscillations, one for each frequency. Think of a chord on a piano. Three notes, played together, make one sound. Here are three oscillations, at four, ten and twenty-three hertz. Add them up, and you get the chord.

Each oscillation comes with two numbers. Its power says how much it weighs in the sum. Its phase says where it stands in its cycle at the very start.

The spectrum is the list of the powers, one per frequency. Here is the spectrum of our chord. Three bars. Two hundred fifty, ninety, and twenty-two point five, in arbitrary units. The EEG series builds this spectrum in its episodes three point two and three point four.

The phases are not in it. Turn all three phases to other values, and the bars do not move at all. The shape of the sound, though, changes completely. The correlation with the first chord is minus zero point four one.

Let us make this systematic, with the row of changes. Here is the simulated signal of episode four point one. We compare each copy with the original through their spectra, between one and forty hertz. At each frequency, we take the ratio of the two powers, on a logarithmic scale, in decibels. Then we take the root mean square over the frequencies.

Shifted: every sample goes up by zero point five. Only the zero hertz line, the average, moves. We leave that line out, so the voyant stays at zero.

Amplified by one point six: every power is multiplied by two point five six, and the distance sees it. Four point zero eight decibels.

Delayed by fifty milliseconds: zero. A delay changes the phases and nothing else.

Shuffled: ten point seven. Noisy: three point zero.

Now two more copies. Read the signal backwards, from the last sample to the first. The spectrum is exactly the same. Zero. Yet the correlation with the original is minus zero point six nine.

Last, keep every power, and draw every phase at random. The shape is unrecognizable. The bump is smeared into noise. The spectrum is still the same. Zero. The correlation with the original is zero point six six for this draw. With other draws, it goes anywhere from about minus zero point eight to plus zero point eight.

The spectrum ignores the phase, and with it, everything that says when things happen.

Now, how do we compare two spectra? The plain idea is to subtract the powers, bar by bar, and take the straight line. There is a problem. Power falls as frequency rises. In this model spectrum, the power at one hertz is sixteen hundred times the power at forty hertz.

Make every frequency five percent louder. The straight line says five point two. Now triple the power between fifteen and thirty hertz, and leave the rest. The straight line says one point nine seven. Five percent more everywhere counts for more than three times more power in a whole band. Ninety-two percent of the first distance comes from a single frequency.

So take the logarithm first. Now every frequency counts by its ratio, whatever its power. The five percent change becomes zero point two one decibels. The tripled band becomes three point zero two. This is the log-spectral distance, one of the measures that Gray and Markel reviewed in nineteen seventy-six.

Another measure also compares ratios. It is the Itakura-Saito divergence, from nineteen sixty-eight. At each frequency, take the ratio of the two powers, subtract its logarithm, subtract one, and average. But it is not symmetric. From the model spectrum to the tripled band, zero point one seven. From the tripled band back to the model, zero point three six. It is a divergence, in the sense of episode one point three.

What does it cost? The spectrum itself is cheap. Computed directly, one thousand twenty-four samples need about a million multiplications. The fast Fourier transform of Cooley and Tukey, in nineteen sixty-five, needs about ten thousand. On this machine, two hundred sixty-two thousand samples take about one millisecond. The idea is older, though: later historians trace it back to Gauss.

But a spectrum is also an estimate, and an estimate can be poor. Take pure noise. The raw spectrum has a spread equal to its average. About one, with one thousand twenty-four samples. About one again, with two hundred sixty-two thousand. A longer recording gives more frequencies, not better ones. Take two recordings of the same noise. Their spectra are eight decibels apart on average. The same noise, eight decibels apart.

The remedy comes from Welch, in nineteen sixty-seven. Cut the recording into windows. Compute one spectrum per window. Average the spectra. With five hundred eleven overlapping windows, the spread falls to zero point zero five, and two spectra of the same noise are now zero point three decibels apart.

The price is resolution. With only one thousand samples, fifteen windows of one hundred twenty-eight samples give a spread of zero point three two, and one bar every eight hertz. The EEG series details this compromise in its episode three point six.

Now the trap. We compare frequency by frequency. Here are three recordings. One has a peak at ten hertz. One has a peak at eleven hertz. One has a peak at thirty hertz. Ten and eleven are neighbours. Ten and thirty are not.

Look at the table. Ten against eleven, nine point zero decibels. Ten against thirty, eight point six. Eleven against thirty, eight point four. The neighbours are not closer than the strangers.

For this distance, a peak that moves by one hertz has moved as far as a peak that jumps by twenty. It does not know that ten and eleven are neighbours. Pushing a pile of power along the axis, a little or a lot, is a different idea. Chapter six does exactly that.

Here is the card. The object is a spectrum. The distance is the root mean square of the log power ratios. It ignores the phase, so any delay, any reversal, any timing. It costs one fast Fourier transform per signal, plus an average over windows. And its trap is comparing frequency by frequency.

So a distance between spectra compares what two signals contain, not when it happens. It suits spontaneous activity, where delays mean nothing. It does not suit the response to an event, where the timing is the whole point.

PLATE 4.66:16

Trains of spikes

Two spike trains, at ten, twenty and thirty milliseconds versus twelve, twenty and thirty-one, are compared by deleting, adding or sliding spikes at a cost q per millisecond. The inverse of q is the timing precision you grant the neuron, so q is the scientific question. Choosing the best of seventeen q values on identical conditions lifted accuracy from forty-eight to sixty-one percent, an artefact.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A neuron talks in tiny electrical pulses. We call each one a spike.

The shape of a spike hardly matters. What may matter is when it happens. So the record of a neuron is a list of times, in milliseconds.

Record the same neuron twice, and the spikes never fall at exactly the same times. So how far apart are two such lists? And how much of the difference should we care about?

Take two short trains. The first has spikes at ten, twenty and thirty milliseconds. The second has spikes at twelve, twenty and thirty-one.

These are not vectors. One train could have three spikes and another could have seven. There is no column to compare with no column.

In nineteen ninety-six, Jonathan Victor and Keith Purpura found a way. They treat the first train as a string of beads on a line, and ask how to turn it into the second.

You have three moves. You can delete a bead. That costs one. You can add a bead. That costs one. Or you can slide a bead along the line. Sliding costs q, times the distance slid in milliseconds.

The distance is the cheapest way to turn one train into the other. A small table finds it, and the table is the same one we used for words in episode three point three.

Think of q as the stiffness of the line. A loose line lets the beads slide freely. A stiff line keeps them where they are.

Now turn the knob q.

At q equal to zero, sliding is free. Every bead goes where it wants, and the distance is zero. The trains have the same number of spikes, and that is all this distance can see.

At q equal to a tenth per millisecond, sliding costs a little. Two milliseconds, plus one, make three milliseconds in all. Three milliseconds times a tenth: the distance is zero point three.

At q equal to two per millisecond, sliding is expensive. Moving the first spike by two milliseconds would cost four. Deleting it and adding a new one costs two. So the cheapest plan throws the bead away and puts a new one back. The distance is four.

Here is the whole curve, for every q from zero to three. It climbs, bends, and then stays flat at four. The first bend is at one per millisecond, where a two millisecond slide costs as much as a deletion and an addition. The second is at two, where even a one millisecond slide does. Past two, sliding never beats deleting and adding.

So what does q mean? Sliding a spike is only worth it when it costs less than two. That happens when the spikes are less than two over q milliseconds apart. The inverse of q gives the order of magnitude of the timing precision you lend to the neuron.

That is why q is not a setting. It is the scientific question. How precisely does this neuron write in time?

Victor and Purpura asked it of cells in the visual cortex. For some properties of a picture, the answer was around ten to thirty milliseconds. For others, closer to a hundred.

There is a second way to build the same idea. Mark van Rossum, in two thousand and one, replaces each spike by a small bump that decays. Then he measures the straight line between the two curves, just as in episode four point one.

The time of the decay plays the role of q. A short decay sees timing. A long decay sees only how many spikes there are. For our two trains, the distance falls from one point two at one millisecond, to zero point one seven at a hundred.

A third family needs no parameter at all. It was proposed by Thomas Kreuz and colleagues, in two thousand and seven and two thousand and thirteen. It compares the two trains moment by moment, from the gaps between spikes and the nearest spikes. It gives up the question about precision, and it also gives up the choice.

Now fill in the card.

The object is a list of times. The distance is the cheapest edit.

What does it ignore? That depends on q. We can show it with the row of changes, adapted to lists of events. Amplitude means nothing for a spike, so the copies are different. Everything is shifted. The time is stretched. One spike is added. The gaps between spikes are shuffled. Each spike is jittered.

At q equal to zero, the eye closes four times. Only the added spike is seen.

At a tenth per millisecond, every change is seen. The shift gives eleven. The stretch gives twelve point eight. The added spike gives one. The shuffled gaps give eleven point six. The jitter gives three point nine.

So the distance ignores nothing, or almost everything. Q decides.

For the rules: a true distance needs q above zero. At q equal to zero, two different trains are at distance zero, so that voyant goes out.

What does it cost? One cell of the table for each pair of spikes. For two trains of a hundred spikes, that is ten thousand cells.

Now the trap.

The parameter is a hypothesis about the code. And it is tempting to try many values of q, and keep the one that separates your conditions best.

Here is what that does. We simulated two conditions that are, by construction, exactly the same. Thirty-two trains, half a second long, twenty spikes per second. A nearest neighbour guesses the condition. At a q chosen in advance, it is right forty-eight percent of the time. That is chance.

Now try seventeen values of q, and keep the best. The score rises to sixty-one percent. The data held nothing. We chose the distance after seeing the result.

The honest way is to choose q on one half of the trials, and score on the other half. That gives forty-five percent. Chance again.

So here is the point. In this episode, the parameter of the distance is the question we ask of the system. And the same distance works for any list of events. Heartbeats. Eye movements. Turns of speech.

Remember: a spike train is a list of times, q is the price of a slide, and its inverse is the precision you grant. Choose it before you look.

Back in the recording studio. So far this chapter has compared signals sample by sample, then with slides, then with warped time. Each time we asked the same question. What do we forgive to time?

Today the answer is bold. Almost everything.

Here is what most analyses do, often without saying so. They do not compare the signals. They summarize each signal by a few numbers, and then they measure a straight line between the summaries.

Take one second of signal, one thousand samples. Compute its power in four frequency bands: delta, theta, alpha and beta. Add its variance, which says how much it moves. Add its spectral slope, which says how fast the power falls as the frequency rises. That is six numbers. The signal has become a vector with six columns.

And a vector, we know how to compare. Subtract, square, add, take the root. Six subtractions instead of one thousand.

Let us look at what this distance forgives. Here is the row of changes, with the same simulated signal as in episode four point one, and the same five copies. Each copy changes one thing.

Shifted: every sample goes up by zero point five. Under the straight line on the samples, fifteen point eight. Under the summary: zero. The offset is not in the summary. The bands start above zero hertz, the variance ignores a constant, and the slope too.

Amplified, times one point six. The summary does see it, but only barely: zero point one. All the powers grow, and they are small numbers to begin with.

Delayed by fifty milliseconds. On the samples, seven point zero. Under the summary: zero. A delay does not change which frequencies are present, nor how strong they are. The summary never knew when anything happened.

Shuffled: the same samples in a random order. The straight line on the samples said ten point six. The summary says two point eight, the largest of the row. Shuffling flattens the slope, and the slope counts more than the powers. We will come back to why.

Noisy: zero point five. It is the noise that moves the summary a little, in every band.

What it costs: the price of computing the summaries. For this one, a single Fourier transform per signal, well under a millisecond for one second of signal. After that, each comparison costs six subtractions. The trap: all the questions of chapter two come back.

First, though, how many numbers should a summary hold?

You can summarize a lot. The toolbox called hctsa, by Fulcher and Jones in twenty seventeen, computes more than seven thousand seven hundred features for every signal. You can summarize a little. Lubba and colleagues, in twenty nineteen, started from a filtered set of four thousand seven hundred ninety one of those features and kept twenty two, which they called catch twenty two.

There is also a way to summarize by symbols. Lin and colleagues proposed it in two thousand three, under the name SAX. Rescale the signal to mean zero and spread one. Cut it into twenty slices and average each slice. Then give each average a letter, from a to d, according to which quarter of a bell curve it falls in. The signal becomes a word of twenty letters.

And now we are back in chapter three. Two words of the same length, compared letter by letter: that is the Hamming distance of episode three point two.

Look at the row again, through the words. Shifted and amplified: the same word, because the rescaling removes both. Zero letters differ. Delayed: the word slides by one letter, and twelve letters out of twenty differ. Shuffled: thirteen out of twenty. But that distance is not a true distance: it is zero for letters that are neighbors in the alphabet.

Now the trap, in three parts.

The first part is units. Take forty simulated recordings. Summarize each by two numbers: its alpha power and the frequency of its alpha peak. With the power in microvolts squared, the power carries almost all of the squared distance, and the frequency hardly counts. Now write the power in millivolts squared. Nothing in the signals changed, but the frequency now carries everything. For twenty six recordings out of forty, the nearest neighbor changes. That is the story of episode two point one, in a new costume. It is also why the slope won the row a moment ago.

The second part is correlation. Take the four band powers and the variance. They all grow when a recording is louder, so they are correlated. Across our forty recordings, the average correlation between two of these columns is zero point four six. After standardizing every column, a single direction, how loud the recording is, still carries fifty nine percent of the squared distances. Five columns were meant to share the weight equally, which would be twenty percent each. The straight line counts loudness several times over.

The third part is the number of columns. With twenty two numbers, or seven thousand, the concentration of distances from episode two point six is waiting for us.

So what is the lesson? A distance between summaries is a distance between signals that ignores almost everything. And that is very often exactly what we want. If the question is how much alpha there is, then when the alpha appears is not our concern.

But choosing the numbers of the summary is choosing the distance. That choice must be defended as a choice, column by column: why this band, in this unit, and why twice the same thing.
