Look before you compute

Four fishing crates carry identical labels, yet drawing them reveals a cloud, a curve, a line with one stray point and a single point making the whole line. Anscombe's four sets, the dinosaur's twelve cousins, a two-humped histogram whose mean sits in the valley, and one patient creating a correlation all teach one habit: draw first, then compute.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the fishing quay. Crates arrive all morning, and each one has a label stuck on its lid.

Today's rule is short. Look before you compute. A number is a summary, and a summary leaves things out. The only way to know what was left out is to look at the data.

In nineteen seventy three, the statistician Francis Anscombe built four small datasets. Each has eleven points, and each point has an x and a y. Here are the labels on the four crates. The mean of x is nine. The mean of y is seven point five. The spread of x, its variance, is eleven. The spread of y is four point one. The correlation is zero point eight two. And the best straight line is y equals three plus one half x. The four labels are the same, to the digit.

Guess before you look. If we lift the lids and draw the points, how many different pictures will we see? One? Two? Four? Lift the lids.

Crate one is a cloud around a rising line. The label is a fair summary here. Crate two is a smooth curve, a hill. A straight line fits it badly, and the correlation does not tell you so. Take x equal to fourteen. The line predicts ten. The crate holds a point at eight point one. The line is off by almost two units, and the label never said so. Crate three is a perfect line, plus one point that sits off by itself. Without that point, the correlation is one, and the line is flatter. Crate four has ten points stacked at the same x, and one point far to the right. That single point makes the whole line. Take it away, and there is no line to compute at all, because every x is the same.

Now a common misreading. The numbers are the same, so the data are the same. Not so. The same numbers only tell you that the numbers cannot tell the crates apart. And the picture is not a decoration added after the analysis. Anscombe wrote against the habit of treating numbers as exact and graphs as rough.

But these crates are tiny. Could it happen with a hundred points? In two thousand sixteen, Alberto Cairo shared a dataset that, once drawn, is a dinosaur. A year later, Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice, at Autodesk, started from the dinosaur. They moved the points a tiny bit at a time, steering toward a target shape, and always keeping the statistics the same to two decimals. They made twelve cousins. A star, a circle, a bullseye, an X, slanted lines.

Every set has one hundred forty two points. The mean of x is fifty four. The mean of y is forty eight. The spreads are about seventeen and twenty seven. The correlation is about minus point zero six. Across all thirteen shapes, each of these differs by no more than one hundredth. Guess first. Does the star have a lower mean of y than the dinosaur? No. The same, to a hundredth. The numbers hold still, and the picture changes every time.

These shapes were built on purpose, and real data are rarely so devious. The lesson is smaller and more useful. A handful of numbers cannot describe a hundred and forty two points, and looking costs almost nothing.

Now a different kind of look. A histogram counts how many values fall in each bin, and the width of the bin is your choice. Here are three hundred patients, with a symptom score from zero to a hundred. Lower is better. These scores are simulated. The mean score is forty two point four.

Guess first. Which bin width shows the truth? Two bars of width fifty say almost nothing. At width twenty, a bump appears. At width ten, two humps. At width five, two humps clearly. At width two, the bars turn ragged. At width one, it is a comb of tiny bars, and you are mostly looking at noise. No single width is the right one. And the edges matter too. Keep width ten, but slide every edge by five. The tallest bar drops from seventy six to sixty four, and the bars rearrange. One histogram is one opinion. Try several.

Better still, draw every patient as a dot under the bars. Now you see two groups, one near twenty five and one near sixty. And look where the mean sits. It sits in the valley between them. Only seventeen of the three hundred patients are within five points of it. The average patient does not exist.

A third example comes from a classroom survey. Twenty students rate a course from one to seven. Here are two courses. In the first, twelve students say four, and the rest are close. In the second, ten say three and ten say five. Nobody says four. Both courses average exactly four, with the same spread, one point oh three. A report with only the mean and the spread cannot tell a shrug from a split room. A bar chart can, in one second. And the two courses need different decisions. The first needs polish. The second needs two different lessons.

One more example, this time with a correlation. Twenty patients, a dose and a response. By construction they are unrelated. The correlation is minus point zero seven, close to nothing.

Guess first. One new patient arrives, far above average on both. How big a correlation can one patient make? The correlation jumps to point four one. One patient did that.

Was that a lucky draw? Repeat it five thousand times. With twenty unrelated patients, the middle correlation is zero. Add one far patient, and the middle correlation becomes point four five. In about a third of the studies, it passes point five. Now imagine a paper that reports r equals point four five, and no scatterplot. You cannot tell a trend from a single patient. The picture can.

What to do with such a patient is a question for later. For now, only one habit. Draw first, then compute.

The habit has three steps. Draw every point. Then summarize. Then draw the summary on top of the points, to see what it kept and what it lost. When there are too many points to draw one by one, draw their density, or a histogram with several bin widths. The principle stays the same. The summary comes second. In a real study, this has a practical meaning. When a paper gives you a table of means and spreads and no figure, ask for the figure. When you write your own, put the individual points in. It costs one line of code, and it shows your reader what a table hides.

Remember: a number is a compression. Draw the data first, and keep the picture next to the numbers.

Where is the middle?

Three honest centres exist: the mean is the balance point of a plank, the median the middle rank, and the trimmed mean a dial between them. Sliding one trial far right drags the mean anywhere while the median stays put. The mean suits the manager's total waiting hours, the median the patient's typical wait; say which you took.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Someone asks you how long people take to react. You have to answer with one number. Which one?

There are at least three honest candidates. They are not the same number, and today we find out why.

Start with eleven reaction times, simulated. Each one is a small weight, placed on a plank at its value in milliseconds. The fastest is 186. The slowest is 751.

Now slide a support under the plank, and find the spot where it stays level. That spot is the mean: 404 milliseconds.

Why there? Because every weight pulls its side down by its distance from the support. At the mean, the pulls on the left and the pulls on the right cancel exactly. Add up the distances on the left, subtract the distances on the right, and you get zero. So the mean listens to how far each value is, not only to which side it sits on.

Here is a different idea. Line the eleven dots up from fastest to slowest, and take the one in the middle. Five dots on each side. That is the median: 366. Notice that it sits below the mean. Seven of the eleven dots are faster than the mean, because the slow tail pulls the balance point to the right.

A third centre lives between them. Drop the two fastest and the two slowest values, a fifth at each end, and average the seven that are left. This is the twenty percent trimmed mean: 379. The twenty percent is a choice made for this video, not a law.

Turn that choice like a dial. Drop nothing, and you get the mean, 404. Drop one value at each end, and you get 389. Keep dropping, down to a single dot in the middle, and you get the median, 366. The trimmed mean is a dial between the other two.

Before we go on, a question. People often say that the median ignores most of the data. Does it?

Move the fastest dot, from 186 down to 150. It was already below the middle, so the median stays at 366. Now carry the same dot across to the far right, past the slowest one. The median steps up to 373, because a different dot is now in the middle. So the median does use every value. It uses only its rank. The mean, meanwhile, jumps from 400 to 466.

Now guess before you see. Take the slowest trial, 751, and slide it to the right. Maybe the volunteer looked away from the screen. What happens to the three centres?

Watch. The median does not move. Neither does the trimmed mean. The mean follows the dot. At three thousand milliseconds, the mean is 608. At six thousand, it is 881, even though ten of the eleven trials are faster than 600. The mean has no ceiling. One value can take it anywhere.

The same story happens with money. Here is a street of ten houses, in thousands of dollars, illustrative. Nine cost between 227 and 302. One mansion costs 2,400. The mean price is 478, and nine of the ten houses cost less than that. The median is 272. Which one describes the street? Ask who is buying. A tax office adding up the street wants the mean. A family looking for a typical house wants the median.

How much of the data must go wrong before a centre gives way? Take twenty simulated values, and replace some of them with a million. With one bad value, the mean jumps from 437 to more than fifty thousand. It is gone.

The median holds. With nine bad values it has drifted to 717, but it is still a believable reaction time. At ten bad values, half of the data, it finally follows them. The trimmed mean holds while the bad values are no more than the four it throws away at the top. The fifth one gets through.

Statisticians call this the breakdown point: the share of bad values a centre can survive. For the mean it is close to zero. For the median it is one half. The idea comes from Hampel, in 1971.

Now no bad values at all, just a skewed crowd. Here are two thousand simulated reaction times, with a long tail of slow trials. The median lands at 394, the trimmed mean at 406, and the mean at 450.

They disagree, and nothing is wrong. The long tail drags the balance point to the right. Only four trials in ten are slower than the mean. So which centre is right? Ask what the number is for.

A second world. Waiting times in an emergency department, two hundred patients, illustrative. The median wait is 103 minutes. The mean is 129.

The manager must staff the waiting room. She needs the total number of waiting hours: 431. That is the mean, times two hundred patients, divided by sixty. The median would give 343 hours, and the room would be short by 88 hours. A patient asks something else: how long will I typically wait? The median answers that. For the budget, the mean. For the patient, the median. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions.

One more guess. If the data are symmetric, where do the three centres sit? Here are two hundred simulated values shaped like a bell. The mean is 397, the median 392, the trimmed mean 396. They agree within five milliseconds. So the choice matters when the data are skewed or contaminated. And that is exactly when you should look at the picture first, as in the last video.

Last, what this does to a study. Two groups of twelve volunteers, simulated reaction times. Group B is slower: the means differ by 107 milliseconds, the medians by 72. Now one volunteer in group A has a lapse, a single trial of three thousand milliseconds. The difference of means turns negative, minus 86. Group A suddenly looks slower. The difference of medians is still 72. One trial reversed the story for the mean.

That is not a reason to throw the mean away. It is a reason to look at the data, and to decide before the results which centre you will report.

So, where is the middle? The mean is the balance point. It uses distances, and one value can drag it anywhere. The median is the middle rank. It uses order, and it takes half of the data to move it. The trimmed mean sits between the two. No centre is the right one in the absolute. Each answers a different question.

Remember: the middle is a choice, so say which one you took.

PLATE 2.37:16

How wide?

Three widths measure how wide a pile is: the standard deviation, the median absolute deviation and the interquartile range. Five lapses lift the standard deviation from 62 to 83 and barely move the others. Dividing squared distances by n runs short by a share of n minus one over n, so dividing by n minus one fixes the variance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we chose a middle. Now a harder question: how wide is the pile?

Two boats bring back the same average catch. One is always close to it. The other swings from an empty hold to a full one. The centre cannot tell them apart. The width can.

Today we measure width in three ways. Then we meet a small trick hidden in one of them: dividing by n minus one.

Start with ten resting heart rates, in beats per minute. They are simulated, for illustration. The mean is 69.

Every person sits some distance from the mean. One pulse is 19 beats below it. Another is 8 above. We want one number for all those distances.

Adding them gives zero, because the plus and the minus cancel. So we square each distance first. A distance of 2 builds a square of 4. A distance of 19 builds a square of 361.

Look at that big square. One slow pulse owns almost two thirds of all the square area: 361 out of 558.

Now average the squares. You get 55.8. This is the variance. Its unit is beats squared, which nobody can picture. So we take the square root: 7.5 beats per minute. That is the standard deviation.

A common misreading: the standard deviation is the average distance from the mean. It is not. The average distance here is 5.4 beats. The standard deviation is larger, because squaring gives the far pulse a bigger vote.

There are two other recipes, and neither one squares anything.

The first ignores the signs, lines the ten distances up, and takes the middle one. That is the median absolute deviation. Here it is 4.0.

The second cuts the data into quarters and measures the middle half. That is the interquartile range: from 66 to 75, so 8.5.

Three recipes, three different numbers. They are not on the same scale yet.

To compare them fairly, there is a rule for bell-shaped data. Multiply the median absolute deviation by 1.4826. Divide the interquartile range by 1.349. Now all three are on the same scale.

Here are 400 simulated reaction times, bell-shaped. The standard deviation is 62. The interquartile range gives 61. The median absolute deviation gives 62. They agree.

Before you look: add five lapses of 900 milliseconds, five trials out of 405. Which of the three widths will notice?

The standard deviation jumps from 62 to 83. The other two move to 64. Five trials made one width a third bigger, and left the other two almost alone.

Is the standard deviation wrong? No. It counts big gaps heavily. Whether you want that depends on what the lapses mean. Say which width you used.

One more knob: the unit. Here are 40 adult heights, illustrative. The standard deviation is 7.5 centimetres. Switch to inches, and it becomes 2.9. The median absolute deviation goes from 4.5 centimetres to 1.8 inches. Every width follows the unit.

Now add 20 centimetres to every person. Nothing moves. 7.5 and 4.5, as before. A width measures spread, not position.

Now the trick. To average the squares, we divided by n. A second recipe divides by n minus one. Which is right? Let us ask the machine.

The population is 10,000 reaction times, simulated, with a true variance of 3,677. SCOOP draws five people. We compute the variance dividing by n, and drop the tile.

Again. And again. After 2,000 samples, the pile has an average of 2,948. That is 80 percent of the truth. Seventy-two percent of the tiles land below it.

Now the same samples, dividing by n minus one. The average is 3,685. Right on target.

Guess before you see. If we change the sample size, does the shortfall change? Here is the recipe dividing by n, as a share of the truth. With two people: 50 percent. With three: 66. With five: 80. With ten: 90. With thirty: 97. The shortfall is exactly n minus one over n. The other recipe sits on 100 every time.

A tiny case. Two values: 400 and 500. The mean is 450, and each is 50 away. Two squares of 2,500 make 5,000. Divide by two, and you get 2,500. Divide by one, and you get 5,000.

And a sample of one? Its distance from its own mean is zero. Dividing by n claims there is no spread. Dividing by n minus one says zero over zero: no answer, which is honest.

So why does the first recipe fall short? Take one sample of five: 396, 325, 292, 434 and 287. Its mean is 347. The true centre is 400, but in real life, nobody knows it.

Measure the squares from the sample mean, and the total is 17,079. Slide the flag toward the true centre, and every square grows. At 400 the total is 31,230.

The sample mean is the one point where the squares are smallest. It is always a little too well placed for its own sample. The true centre is somewhere else, so the real total is larger, by n times the gap squared. Here, that extra is 14,151.

So measuring from the sample mean always falls short, by a predictable share. Dividing by n minus one gives it back. Another way to say it: once the mean is fixed, only four of the five distances are free. The fifth is forced.

A common misreading: n minus one makes the standard deviation exact. It does not. It makes the variance exact on average. The square root is still a little short: about 6 percent at n equals five, and 20 percent with two people. The shortfall fades as n grows.

And check your software. In numpy, the standard deviation divides by n by default. In pandas it divides by n minus one. R's sd divides by n minus one. On our five numbers, that is 58 against 65.

This matters in real studies. A small pilot study often lends its spread to the planning of a bigger one. Divide by n, and the plan inherits the shortfall.

Remember: a width is a recipe, so say which one you used. And when the centre is the sample's own mean, divide by n minus one.

Skewness: which way the tail leans

Skewness is the side of the tail, measured by the moment recipe with cubed distances or by the quartile recipe, and the two react differently when the longest wait stretches. Samples of twenty from a perfectly symmetric population often look skewed, and about 5,000 values are needed to estimate skewness as precisely as the mean at twenty.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a long tail dragged the mean above the median. Today: which way does a tail lean, and by how much? And how much can a small sample tell us about it?

Here are three simulated populations of ten thousand values each. The first leans left: its tail trails off toward small values. The second is symmetric. The third leans right, with a long tail of large values. That lean is called skewness, and the side is the side of the tail, not the side of the crowd.

Look at the mean and the median. Leaning left: mean 57.7, median 60.0. Symmetric: 50.0 and 49.8. Leaning right: 42.3 and 40.0. The tail pulls the mean toward itself. We will test that rule of thumb later.

Now we want one number for the lean. Recipe one uses every value. Take ten waiting times in minutes, illustrative: 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11 and 29. The mean is 9.

Picture a tug of war on the quay. Every wait pulls the rope from the mean, toward its own side. Pulls equal to the distances would cancel exactly, since that is what a mean is. So we cube each distance instead.

Cubing keeps the sign: minus five cubed is minus 125. And it exaggerates: twice the distance, eight times the pull.

The wait of 29 minutes is 20 from the mean. Its cube is 8,000. All the waits below the mean together pull minus 316. That one wait supplies 96 percent of the pulling.

Average the ten cubes: 769. Divide by the standard deviation cubed, 337, to drop the unit. The result is 2.28. That is the moment skewness. Positive: the tail leans right. Negative: left.

An average of a power of the distances is called a moment. The mean is the first, the variance the second, and this is the third.

Recipe two uses only three values, the quartiles. For the same ten waits: 5.25, 6.5 and 8.75. Two gaps: from the first quartile up to the median, 1.25; from the median up to the third quartile, 2.25. A longer gap on the right means a lean to the right.

Divide the difference of the gaps by the width of the middle half, 3.5: 0.29. This is the quartile skewness, named after the statistician Arthur Bowley. It stays between minus one and one.

On the three populations, the moment skewness is minus 1.01, 0.04 and 1.01. The quartile skewness is minus 0.10, 0.02 and 0.10.

Now stretch the tail. Move the longest wait from 29 minutes to 49. The quartiles do not budge, so the quartile skewness stays at 0.29. But the cube of that one wait grows almost seven times, from 8,000 to 54,872. The moment skewness climbs from 2.28 to 2.55, no more, because the spread grows too. One recipe listens to every distance, the other to the middle half. Video 2.6 tests both against outliers.

Here is a population of ten thousand values that is perfectly symmetric. Its skewness is exactly zero, by construction. SCOOP draws a sample of 20 and computes its skewness.

The first says minus 0.25. The next, minus 0.33. Then minus 0.16. Ten thousand samples.

The estimates run from minus 2.18 to 2.02. The typical spread is 0.45. In 25 percent of the samples, one in four, the skewness is beyond 0.5 in either direction, a threshold chosen for this video. Roughly half of those say right, half say left.

Guess before you see. Here are 20 values. Skewness 0.92, mean 48.0, median 43.7. The tail is on the right. Does the population lean right?

No. It came from the symmetric population. It is draw 29, the first above 0.8. Samples like this turn up in about 4 percent of draws.

Turn the knob on the sample size. With 10 values, the spread is 0.57, and 36 percent of samples look skewed. With 20, 0.45 and 25 percent. With 50, 0.30 and 10 percent. With 200, 0.16 and 0.2 percent. With 1,000, 0.07 and none.

Now a population that really leans right. Its skewness is 1.01. Draw samples of 20. On average, the estimate is 0.68, below the truth. Only 59 percent of the samples look clearly right. The other 41 percent look symmetric, or even lean left.

Now compare with the mean, in this population, with 20 values. The estimated mean is typically off by about 8 percent of its true value. The estimated variance, 44 percent. The estimated skewness, 55 percent. The higher the order of the moment, the more data it needs.

How much more? To estimate the skewness as precisely as the mean at 20 values, you need about 5,000 values. At 5,000, it is 6.5 percent. At 20, a sample's skewness says almost nothing about the population's.

Back to the rule of thumb: mean above median means right skew. Often true, not always. Paul von Hippel showed in 2005, in the Journal of Statistics Education, that it fails more often than textbooks say, especially for discrete data: a survey variable, and a Poisson law, have a positive skew and a mean left of the median.

A small constructed set: five ones, five fives and a fifteen. The mean is 4.09, below the median, 5. Yet the moment skewness is plus 1.71, because the lone 15 dominates the cubes. And the quartile skewness is minus 1, because the middle half is squeezed against its top. The tail leans right, the crowd leans left. So draw the data first.

Where do tails lean in real life? Reaction times lean right: there is a floor, and no ceiling for a lapse. House prices: none goes below zero, and a few mansions stretch the tail. Hospital stays: most people go home in days, a few stay for months. So do waiting times, and spike counts, since a count cannot go below zero.

Some lean left. Exam scores with a ceiling pile up near 100, and the failures trail off to the left. Age at death: most people die old, and early deaths form the tail. Reviews out of five stars: most people give five. All these shapes are illustrative.

Remember: skewness is the side of the tail, and a small sample says very little about it. Draw the data first.

Tails and kurtosis

Kurtosis is the weight of the tails, not the sharpness of the top. In a quarry of stones weighted by z to the fourth, the heavy crowd's 124 stones beyond three standard deviations carry 74 percent of the weight. Small samples badly underestimate it, and the wobble shows up in the standard deviation more than in the mean.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we asked which way a tail leans. Today we ask how much a tail weighs. The number is called kurtosis.

Here are two simulated crowds of ten thousand values each. Both have a mean of exactly zero, and a standard deviation of exactly one, by construction. The first is a normal law. The second has heavy tails: a Student t law with five degrees of freedom, rescaled.

Same mean, same spread. Guess before you see: how many values out of ten thousand fall beyond three standard deviations?

In the normal crowd: 23. The exact expectation is about 27, computed from the law. In the heavy crowd: 124. The exact expectation is about 117. That is more than four times as many.

The most extreme value: 3.6 standard deviations from the mean in the normal crowd, 9.3 in the heavy one.

The standard deviation cannot see the tails. Let us build a number that can.

Measure each value's distance from the mean in standard deviation units: z. Raise it to the fourth power. Average those over all the values. That average is the kurtosis.

Picture a quarry. Each value is a stone, and its weight is z to the fourth. Screens sort the stones into four trays: within one standard deviation, one to two, two to three, and beyond three.

Normal crowd. The first tray holds 6,836 stones, and weighs 4 percent of the total. The second, 2,697 stones, 42 percent. The third, 444 stones, 46 percent. The last, 23 stones, 8 percent.

Heavy crowd. 7,549 stones in the first tray, and 1 percent of the weight. Then 1,971 stones, 10 percent. Then 356 stones, 15 percent. And in the last tray, 124 stones weigh 74 percent of everything.

The kurtosis of the normal crowd is 2.95. The exact value for the normal law is 3. The heavy crowd gives 8.15. The exact value for this law is 9. The crowd of ten thousand falls short, because a few of the rarest stones never showed up.

Count the stones beyond two standard deviations. Normal: 467. Heavy: 480. Nearly the same number. But they supply 54 percent of the kurtosis in the first crowd, and 88 percent in the second. In the heavy crowd, the single heaviest stone alone is 9 percent of the total.

There are two ways to write kurtosis. In the first, the normal law scores 3. In the second, called excess kurtosis, we subtract 3, and the normal law scores 0.

Software disagrees. In SciPy, the function kurtosis returns the excess by default. In pandas, the method kurt returns the excess too. In the R package e one zero seven one, the function kurtosis also subtracts 3. The R package moments says it computes Pearson's measure, the plain form. So before comparing two numbers, check which convention made them.

Now the big misunderstanding. Many textbooks say kurtosis measures how peaked the top is. Peter Westfall, in The American Statistician in 2014, argued that kurtosis tells you virtually nothing about the peak. What it measures is tail extremity.

Check, by exact computation: a law whose density falls off as one minus the distance, squared, between minus one and one. Its top is about 19 percent taller than the normal's, at the same spread: 0.474 against 0.399. Yet its kurtosis is 2.86, below 3. A sharper top, a lower kurtosis.

Now take the heavy law and cut off only the far tails, beyond three standard deviations. That removes 1.2 percent of the values. The top barely moves: its height rises by 1.2 percent. The kurtosis falls from 9 to 3.5.

So kurtosis is the weight of the tails.

SCOOP draws samples of 50 from the heavy crowd and computes the kurtosis of each. The first says 5.17. Then 3.14. Then 8.57, 4.37, 5.03. Ten thousand samples.

The truth is 8.15. The typical sample says 3.96. And 53 percent of the samples land below half of the truth. Only 10 percent are above it. From the normal crowd, the typical sample says 2.77, within a narrow band.

Turn the knob on the sample size. With 20 values, the typical estimate is 3.11, and 74 percent of the samples are below half of the truth. With 50, 3.96 and 53 percent. With 200, 5.26 and 20 percent. With 1,000, 6.92 and 0.6 percent.

Now the mean and the standard deviation, side by side, with 20 values. You might expect the mean to wobble more under heavy tails. It does not. Its spread is 0.226 in the normal crowd and 0.222 in the heavy one: the same variance gives the same spread. What shakes is the standard deviation. Its spread goes from 0.157 to 0.245. And a sample's standard deviation lands more than 25 percent away from the truth in 12 percent of normal samples, against 30 percent of heavy ones.

With 100 values: 0.070 against 0.127, and off by more than 25 percent in none, against 4.7 percent. A wobbly standard deviation means a wobbly standard error, and intervals built from it.

Where do heavy tails live? Daily stock returns: calm days, and a few stormy ones. Insurance claims: a few huge disasters. Earthquake energy: size multiplies from one event to the next. Reaction times: attention sometimes drifts away, a lapse. EEG amplitude: blinks and jaw clenches. A sensor with an occasional glitch. And city sizes: growth compounds.

These seven are simulated and illustrative, not real data sets. Out of ten thousand values, the counts beyond three standard deviations are 178, 182, 154, 310, 66, 48 and 89. All of them are above the normal's 27. For city sizes, the kurtosis is 1,511 even though only 89 values pass the line. Kurtosis counts how far a value is, not only how many.

Remember: kurtosis is the weight of the tails, not the sharpness of the top. In a heavy crowd, a few extreme values carry most of it.

One outlier, four estimators

Sliding one reaction time to the right breaks every classical estimator (mean, standard deviation, moment skewness, kurtosis) while the median, scaled deviation, quartile skewness and Moors' measure hold, like a fuse that does not blow. Robustness costs precision on clean data but pays when data are contaminated. Decide how to exclude outliers by a rule written before seeing results.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a few extreme values carried most of the kurtosis. Today we put one extreme value in front of every estimator of this chapter, and watch which ones give way.

Here is the chapter in one table. Four questions: where is the centre, how wide is the pile, which way does it lean, how heavy are its tails. For each, a classical answer on the left and a robust answer on the right.

Mean, and median. Standard deviation, and the scaled median absolute deviation. Moment skewness, and quartile skewness. Kurtosis, and Moors' octile measure, which reads only the eighths of the data: the cut points that split it into eight equal parts.

The data are thirty reaction times, simulated. The mean is 416 milliseconds and the median 414. The widths are 65 and 56, near the true 60. It is only a sample.

Take one value, 389, and slide it to the right. A cell turns pink when it has moved by more than three of its own wobbles, the spread it shows from sample to sample on clean data.

At 1,000 milliseconds, the mean has moved to 437. The standard deviation has nearly doubled, to 125. The skewness has jumped from 0.29 to 3.15, and the kurtosis from 3.3 to 15.2.

Look at the right column. The median is 418. The width is still 56. The quartile skewness barely moved: minus 0.14, minus 0.13. Moors' measure took one step, from 0.99 to 1.32, at one of its cut points, then stayed.

At 2,400 milliseconds, the mean is 483, the standard deviation 368. Skewness and kurtosis flatten, far from where they began. Four cells are pink: every classical one. No robust cell is.

How many bad values can each cell take? This is the breakdown point from 2.2. For the mean, the standard deviation, the skewness and the kurtosis, none: one bad value is enough. For the median and the width, half of the data. For the quartile skewness, a quarter. For Moors' measure, an eighth, because it reads the first and the last eighth.

We checked on 40 values, with bad ones at a million: the median and the width broke at 20, the quartile skewness at 10, Moors' measure at 5.

So the robust column is a fuse that does not blow. What does it cost? Insurance has a premium. Let us measure it on clean data.

SCOOP draws 25 values from a clean simulated population, and records the mean and the median of each sample. Ten thousand samples. The mean wobbles by 12.0 milliseconds, the median by 14.7. That is 22 percent more wobble: 67 percent of the precision.

In theory, for large samples, the median keeps 2 over pi of the mean's precision: 64 percent. At 400 values we measure 66. The median needs about one and a half times as many values.

The width pays more. The scaled median absolute deviation wobbles by 13.7, the standard deviation by 8.6. At 400 values it keeps 38 percent of the precision, close to the 37 percent that Rousseeuw and Croux reported in 1993.

For the lean and the tails, the twins live on different scales. So we ask: take one sample of 50 from a shaped crowd and one from a normal crowd. How often does the statistic rank the shaped crowd higher? For the lean, the moment skewness is right 96 percent of the time, the quartile skewness 66. For the tails, kurtosis 82 percent, Moors' measure 60. Here the premium is not small.

Now the payout. Contaminate the crowd: 1,057 of the 10,000 values come from a law ten times wider. The mean's wobble jumps to 40.8, 3.4 times what it was. The median's goes to 16.7, only 13 percent more.

The typical sample's standard deviation reads 170, when the bulk is 60. The scaled median absolute deviation reads 66. And the rankings: moment skewness falls to 55 percent, almost a coin toss, kurtosis to 53. The robust twins keep 68 and 62.

So a robust estimator is insurance. A premium on clean data, small for the centre, larger for the width, large for the lean and the tails. And a big payout when the data are contaminated.

Is the extreme value an error, or a real member of the population? Six cases, all illustrative.

Ten patients' weights, and one entry reads 750 instead of 75. An error. The mean says 139 kilograms, the median 73. Fix the entry.

A temperature log, and one reading is minus 127: the sensor failed. An error. The mean says 13.8 degrees, the median 19.8. Exclude it with a physical limit.

Forty EEG epochs, and three contain a blink, twelve times the usual amplitude. An error for the brain question. The mean says 16.3, the median 7.95. Reject by a threshold fixed in advance.

Twenty reaction times, and one sneeze: 3,000 milliseconds. The mean is 517, the median 405. The sneeze is not what we measure. If lapses are the question, it is the data.

A town survey of 1,000 incomes, and one billionaire. Real, not an error. The mean says 2,047,700, the median 42,150. For the town's total income, the mean. For a typical resident, the median.

Fifty years of yearly river peaks, and the last year is a record, 1,100 centimetres, when the previous worst was 591. Real, and it must not be removed: the barrier must be built for it. Neither the mean nor the median answers that. The extreme value does.

Now the danger: deciding what is an outlier after seeing the result. We simulate 10,000 studies, two groups of 20, with no true difference. An analyst who always uses all the data gets a false alarm 4.9 percent of the time.

An analyst who drops points beyond two standard deviations, but only when that lowers the p-value, gets 10.4 percent. Twice as many. A rule written in advance, dropping any point more than three scaled deviations from the pooled median, without looking at the groups, gets 4.6 percent. Write the rule first. Preregistration is the subject of 22.1.

Remember: a robust estimator is insurance, a premium on clean data and a payout on contaminated data. The rule for excluding values is written before the results.
