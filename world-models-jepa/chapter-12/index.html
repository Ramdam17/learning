H-JEPA: predicting at several time scales

A hierarchical JEPA stacks one JEPA on another, like the second and minute hands of a clock. The low level predicts the next moment in detail, while the high level predicts much further ahead with a short, abstract embedding, because forgetting unpredictable detail makes distant prediction possible. Planning then splits a goal into subgoals, with each claim dated as proposed or shown.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the airport, the world of chapter twelve, and a good place to think about time. A controller in the tower follows a flight that lasts hours. A pilot on the runway watches the next few seconds. They look at the same journey, yet they do not keep the same details. Today we ask how a world model can do the same. We stack two levels of JEPA. The low level looks a short time ahead, in detail. The high level looks much further ahead, in less detail.

A warning before we start. This chapter describes mostly a proposal. So every element will carry a tag: proposed, shown in simulation, or shown on a robot. And each tag will carry its date.

First, the problem with a single time scale. The world model of chapter ten takes a state and an action, and predicts the next state. To look far ahead, we run it again and again. Each step adds a little error, and the errors pile up, as we saw in episode 9.3. The search for good actions also grows with every extra step. The authors of H-JEPA add a second problem. A single space that keeps every fast detail predicts the next moment well. But it is a poor way to say whether you are close to a distant goal.

Let's picture a clock with two hands. The second hand ticks. At each tick, the low-level JEPA looks at the world and predicts the next second. Its necklace is long, because it keeps many details: the pose of every limb, the flicker of every light. This is the JEPA of chapter eleven. The time scales here are illustrative.

The minute hand moves slowly. Above the first JEPA, we stack a second one. It does not look at the raw scene. It takes as input the necklaces made by the first. And it predicts much further ahead, a minute instead of a second, but with a short, abstract necklace. This is the hierarchical JEPA that Yann LeCun sketched in section 4.6 of his 2022 position paper, and he calls it H-JEPA. In the paper's words, the first JEPA extracts low-level representations and makes short-term predictions. The second takes those representations as inputs, and makes longer-term predictions.

The paper gives a concrete example, with a car. Given the moves on the steering wheel and the pedals over the next several seconds, a driver can predict where the car will be. Over longer periods, the details depend on other cars, traffic lights and pedestrians, and they are somewhat unpredictable. But at a higher level of abstraction, the driver can still say that the car will probably arrive within a predictable time. The detailed trajectory is absent from that description. The approximate route, as drawn on a map, is kept.

Now the strong moment. The further ahead you predict, the more detail you must forget, and that forgetting is what makes the prediction possible. Think of a bead that encodes a flicker nobody can foresee a minute from now. The high level should not carry that bead. The paper says that a JEPA can train its encoders to eliminate irrelevant details, so that the representations become more predictable. Compare the painters of chapter six. They had to predict every pixel, and the details they could not foresee forced them to blur. A JEPA does not average the unpredictable detail. It drops it.

H-JEPA, the 2026 system we will meet in a moment, lets us check this. Its authors trained it on a simulated four-legged ant walking through a maze, and asked a probe, in the sense of episode 4.3, to read the ant's state from each level. At the higher levels, the state of the body became harder to read, while the position of the ant stayed easy to read. The legs moved too fast to be predictable far ahead. The position did not.

The same stack also cuts a big goal into small ones. The 2022 paper takes a commute. Commuting to work decomposes into driving to the train station and catching a train. Driving to the station decomposes into walking out of the house, starting the car, and driving. Getting out of the house requires standing up, walking to the door, and opening it. The decomposition goes all the way down to the control of muscles, millisecond by millisecond. A lecture by LeCun in 2026 draws a trip to Paris the same way: the distance to Paris, then the distance to the airport, then grab a taxi. Each level hands subgoals to the level below.

How does planning use this? In the paper's hierarchical planning, the top level looks for a sequence of high-level actions. These are not real actions. They are conditions that the level below has to satisfy. The lower level then finds the actions that meet those subgoals. It is the model-predictive loop of episode 9.4, run once per level.

So where do implementations stand, as of November 2026? Four dates.

June 2022: the position paper itself. Proposed. It describes a possible architecture.

April 2026: HWM, Hierarchical Planning with Latent World Models, from a group that includes LeCun. Two time scales, but in one shared space. On a real Franka robot arm, from a single goal image, it solves pick and place at seventy percent success with a cup and sixty with a box. The same planner without hierarchy scores zero. Shown on a robot.

October 2026: H-JEPA. Here each level has its own space, and all the levels are trained end to end, each one striding twice as far as the level below. On a simulated maze, a three-level stack raises the success rate from eighteen to seventy-three percent, with less planning compute. On recorded real-robot videos from DROID, it improves offline planning. Closed-loop control on a physical robot is left as the next step. Shown in simulation.

November 2026: WARP-JEPA, from another group. It works on thirty H-JEPA models kept frozen, and replaces the slow search by learned proposers of trajectories. On long tasks, inference is between nine point six and eighteen point eight times faster, and success rises by up to thirty-three point three points. Shown in simulation.

And the limits? The strides between levels are set by hand. On one task, pushing a block, three levels did poorly. The authors think that short episodes left too little data to train the third level. And all of this needs actions in the data: learning a hierarchy from video without actions is, for the authors, a direction for later.

To sum up. A hierarchical JEPA stacks one JEPA on top of another. The low level predicts the next second, in detail. The high level predicts the next minute, with a shorter, more abstract necklace. The further ahead you predict, the more detail you must forget, and that is what makes the prediction possible. As of November 2026, the idea is proposed since 2022, and shown in simulation and on a robot in 2026. Next time: episode 12.2, costs, critics and memory.

PLATE 12.26:38

Costs, critics and memory

The agent's dashboard has four parts: a hard-wired intrinsic cost, a learned critic that predicts future cost, a short-term memory, and guardrails written inside the cost. The agent acts by minimising cost at inference time, searching action sequences with its world model: objective-driven AI. Most parts are only proposed, and critics doubt that good objectives can be specified well enough.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the airport. Episode 11.1 named the six modules of Yann LeCun's 2022 proposal. Today we climb the control tower and read one screen: the dashboard that tells the agent what is good and what is bad. It has four parts. A fixed cost. A learned critic. A short-term memory. And guardrails. Each part comes with a status, proposed or shown, and a date: as of November 2026.

First, the fixed cost. The paper calls it the intrinsic cost. It looks at a state of the world and returns one number, the energy, which measures the discomfort of the agent. Think pain, hunger, danger. For a robot, the paper lists force overloads, dangerous heat, excessive power consumption, and low energy reserves. The cost is a sum of small terms, and each term is a drive. The key word is hard-wired. The paper says that, to prevent an uncontrolled drift towards bad behaviours, this cost must be immutable and not subject to learning. Status: proposed.

Second, the critic. The fixed cost only judges the present moment, yet the agent needs to know where a state is heading. The critic is a trainable module that predicts future intrinsic energy. Its role, says the paper, is to anticipate long-term outcomes without running the heavy world model every time. It learns from the memory. It reads an old state and the energy measured later, and is trained to predict it, much like critics in reinforcement learning. That idea is shown: the Dreamer agent of episode 10.3 learns values of imagined states. In the JEPA family, a December 2025 preprint, co-authored by LeCun, shapes the latent space so that distance to the goal approximates a learned goal-reaching value, in two simple simulated worlds. Status: shown in simulation, for reaching a goal.

Third, the short-term memory. It stores past, current and future states of the world, with their costs. It has two jobs. It gives the critic its training pairs, and it keeps the state of the world up to date. The paper's example is a bottle carried from the kitchen to the dining room. Only three entries change: the bottle, the kitchen, and the dining room. The paper says this module plays some of the roles of the hippocampus, and also that its precise architecture is somewhat fuzzy. Status: proposed. As of November 2026, no published JEPA planner is known to include one, so episode 12.3 leaves it grey.

Fourth, the guardrails. They are not a separate module. They are terms written inside the fixed cost, immutable like the rest, that make dangerous states expensive. The 2022 paper mentions them once, to warn that letting the configurator reshape the fixed cost too freely could make the basic drives, including the cost terms that implement safety guardrails, difficult to control. Status: proposed. The nearest shown thing lives elsewhere. In 2023, SafeDreamer added safety costs to a Dreamer world model, and its authors report nearly zero cost on simulated benchmarks. It is not LeCun's architecture.

Now the strong moment. Most systems you know produce an answer in one pass. Here, the answer is searched for. The agent proposes a sequence of actions, the world model imagines what follows, the cost adds up the energy, and the actor changes the sequence to lower it. The paper calls this reasoning as energy minimisation, and it is the model-predictive control of episode 9.4. The optimisation happens while the agent acts, at inference time. LeCun calls this approach objective-driven AI. The 2022 paper never uses that phrase. The earliest documented use is a talk at MIT on 21 July 2023, whose slides carry that title.

Here is the safety argument he draws from it, paraphrased from his slides of 2023 and January 2024. Behaviour comes from minimising objectives, so controllability can be designed into the objectives, by design rather than by fine-tuning. Guardrail objectives, he says, can make such systems safe by construction. He compares it to turbojets: there are infinitely many ways to build an unreliable one, yet safe ones exist, and safer AI is simply better AI with the proper objectives and guardrails. For alignment, he points to trial and error in sandboxed systems, and he compares designing objectives to law making.

The critics do not attack the idea of a cost. They question whether the cost can be written. In May 2023, Steven Byrnes argued, in a post about this very paper, that it contains an unsolved technical alignment problem. The fixed cost is only sketched, and the critic must judge plans it has never seen, so a planner that searches for low cost can find exactly the plans where the critic is wrong. In July 2024, Yoshua Bengio wrote, without naming LeCun, that nobody can give a complete formal specification of what is unacceptable, and that an optimiser can satisfy the letter of a rule and miss its spirit. So the disagreement is about whether the objective can be specified well enough. No published system implements the fixed cost yet, so the question stays open.

Here is the board, as of November 2026. Fixed cost: proposed. Guardrails: proposed. Critic: shown in simulation, for goal reaching only. Memory: proposed, and not placed. One caution about the only cost that exists. An August 2026 preprint reports that, in released JEPA world models, closeness to the goal in latent space often ranks candidate actions wrongly. It is a preprint, not a settled result. This mix of shown and proposed is why the cost gets an orange light in the next episode.

To sum up. The cost has a fixed part, with guardrails written inside, and a learned critic. The memory keeps the state of the world and trains the critic. The agent acts by minimising the cost at inference time: objective-driven AI. Whether good objectives are enough is a live debate. Next time: episode 12.3, the full cognitive architecture.

PLATE 12.37:51

The full cognitive architecture

The cognitive architecture is drawn as an airport with six modules: configurator, perception, world model, cost, short-term memory and actor. It runs in a fast reactive mode or a slow planning mode that loops through the world model and the cost. A dated audit finds perception, action-conditioned prediction and planning built, the cost and hierarchy partial, and the configurator only proposed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

The lights go down on the shadow theatre, and the runway lights come up. Welcome to the airport, the world of chapter twelve. Episode 11.1 ended on a promise. We named the six modules of the 2022 proposal, and we stopped there. Today we open the whole architecture. And then we ask what a researcher should ask of any blueprint: how much of it exists, as of November 2026?

First, the map. The blueprint is Figure 2 of Yann LeCun's position paper, version 0.9.2. The paper assumes that every module is differentiable, so that gradients of the cost can flow back through the arrows. We will draw it as an airport plan. The buildings are ours. The roles are his.

The control tower is the configurator. The paper calls its role executive control. Given a task, it pre-configures the perception, the world model, the cost and the actor for the task at hand.

The arrivals hall is perception. It estimates the current state of the world. For a given task, the paper says, only a small subset of that state is relevant, so the hall keeps what the task needs.

The flight simulator hangar is the world model, the most complex piece of the architecture. Its role is twofold: to estimate missing information about the state of the world, and to predict plausible future states. It is a kind of simulator, and it predicts inside an abstract representation space. In our series, in necklaces, not in pixels.

The safety office is the cost. It measures the discomfort of the agent as one number, which the paper calls the energy. It has two parts. The intrinsic cost is hard-wired and cannot be trained: think pain, hunger, danger. The trainable critic learns to predict future intrinsic energy.

The departures board is the short-term memory. It stores the past, current and future states of the world, with their costs. The paper says it can be seen as playing some of the roles of the hippocampus.

And the runway is the actor. It proposes sequences of actions, and sends the chosen ones to the effectors, the parts of the machine that move.

Now, the traffic on the map. The paper describes two ways to run it.

Mode-1 is reactive. The perception module estimates the state, and a policy module, part of the actor, turns that state straight into an action. In the paper's words, this reactive process does not make use of the world model nor of the cost. It is the short corridor.

Mode-2 is reasoning and planning, and it takes the long loop. The actor proposes a sequence of actions. The world model predicts the states that would follow. The cost adds up the energy along the way. Then the actor proposes a better sequence, and so on. When it settles, the actor plays the first action, and the episode starts again. You know this loop. It is the model-predictive control of episode 9.4, and the paper uses that very name. What changes, the paper notes, is that the world model and the cost are learned.

LeCun names the two modes by analogy with Daniel Kahneman. Mode-1 is similar to Kahneman's System 1, and Mode-2 to System 2, from the book Thinking, Fast and Slow, published in 2011.

Why keep both? Because Mode-2 is onerous. The agent has a single world model engine, so, like us, it can focus on one complex task at a time. So the paper proposes to train a policy to imitate the result of Mode-2 planning. A skill is, in his word, compiled into a reactive policy that no longer requires careful planning.

Now the strong moment. A traffic light on each building, as of November 2026. Green means that published systems do it. Orange means that it is shown in simple cases. Red means that it is still only proposed. And a grey, for a module no published system fills yet. Where nothing can be placed, the table says so rather than guessing.

Perception: green. A JEPA encoder does exactly this job. I-JEPA, in January 2023, for images. V-JEPA 2, in June 2025, trained on over one million hours of video. And V-JEPA 2.1, in March 2026.

World model: green, for the part that predicts the effect of actions. V-JEPA 2-AC, from June 2025, adds an action-conditioned predictor trained on less than sixty-two hours of robot video. LeWorldModel, in March 2026, learns one end to end from pixels, with about fifteen million parameters. But look at the horizon. It plans five steps ahead, and its authors write that planning remains restricted to short horizons.

The hierarchy, the part of the world model that predicts at several scales: orange. In October 2026, a preprint called H-JEPA trains a stack of action-conditioned JEPAs end to end. On a simulated maze, a three-level stack raised the success rate from eighteen to seventy-three percent. Its abstract reports offline evaluation on recorded real-robot DROID videos, and closed-loop control on a physical robot is left as future work.

Cost: orange. What exists is a goal cost. V-JEPA 2-AC scores every imagined future by its distance, in representation space, to a goal image. A December 2025 preprint shapes the latent space so that distance approximates a learned goal-reaching value, in two simple simulated worlds. The hard-wired drives and guardrails of the intrinsic cost? As of November 2026, no published system shows them.

Actor: green, for planning. V-JEPA 2-AC and LeWorldModel both plan with the cross-entropy method of episode 9.4, and play only the first step. V-JEPA 2-AC did it on Franka robot arms in two labs. A November 2026 preprint, WARP-JEPA, replaces online gradient optimisation with learned trajectory proposers on frozen H-JEPA models. That is amortised planning, close to the Mode-1 shortcut but not one distilled policy, so no light yet.

Short-term memory: grey. The paper itself says that its precise architecture and function are somewhat fuzzy. As of November 2026, no published system clearly fills this module, so, no light.

Configurator: red. The paper calls it the most mysterious module, and says that how to find sequences of subgoals is not specified. In the pick-and-place tests of V-JEPA 2-AC, the authors show the model two sub-goal images, chosen by hand. A person played the control tower.

So the blueprint is not yet a system. Pieces exist, one at a time, mostly on short horizons, and nobody has built the whole airport. These lights are dated for a reason: they change quickly.

To sum up. The architecture has six modules: configurator, perception, world model, cost, short-term memory and actor. It runs in two modes, a reactive Mode-1 and a planning Mode-2, by analogy with Kahneman's System 1 and System 2. As of November 2026, perception, the action-conditioned predictor and the planner are green. The cost and the learned hierarchy are orange. The configurator is red, and the memory is not placed.
