PLATE 10.15:15

Compared to what?

The bird quarrel was about what to compare with. On the US airline network, clustering of 0.4 is about ten times higher than in random networks of the same size, but only one and a half times higher than in networks keeping every degree. The same value can look remarkable against one reference and modest against another. Choosing the reference chooses the hypothesis.

TRANSCRIPTEN

In nineteen seventy-five, the ecologist Jared Diamond studied the birds of the Bismarck Archipelago, a chain of islands off the coast of New Guinea.

He noticed that some pairs of species never lived on the same island. One bird here, the other bird there, like the black and white squares of a checkerboard. He read it as competition.

Four years later, Edward Connor and Daniel Simberloff replied. Patterns like these, they said, also appear by chance.

Chance, but which chance? They drew random tables where every island kept its number of species, and every species kept its number of islands.

It does not look like a graph. But a table of species and islands is a table of links.

Put a dot on every species, a dot on every island, and a line wherever a species lives on an island. And there is the hidden graph.

Two kinds of nodes, as in episode one point four. And the quarrel was about the reference. Compared to what? That is the question of this chapter.

Here is a network. This is the airline network of the United States in nineteen ninety-seven: three hundred thirty-two airports, and two thousand, one hundred twenty-six routes between them.

We measure its clustering. Take two routes that meet at an airport, A to B and B to C. Is there also a route between A and C?

There are ninety-two thousand, one hundred eighty-nine such pairs of routes. Thirty-six thousand, five hundred forty-three of them close into a triangle. That is zero point four: about four in ten.

Is zero point four a lot? The number alone cannot say. It needs something to be compared with.

Start with the random network of episode nine point one. Keep the three hundred thirty-two airports, keep the two thousand, one hundred twenty-six routes, and wire them at random.

Do it a thousand times, and measure the clustering each time.

The thousand answers sit around zero point zero four. The highest is zero point zero four six. Our network is about ten times higher, and not one of the thousand comes close.

So against pure chance, the airline network is extremely clustered. Or is it?

This reference forgot something. Airports are not alike. The typical airport has five routes. The biggest hub has a hundred thirty-nine.

A hub with that many routes owns nearly ten thousand pairs of routes, and among so many pairs, many will close for no special reason. The ten biggest hubs hold forty-nine percent of all the pairs.

So change the reference. Keep every airport's degree. The hub still has a hundred thirty-nine routes, and the airport with one route still has one.

We met networks of this kind in the tombola of chapter eight. How to build them well is the business of the next episodes. Here we only ask what they give.

A thousand of them again. Their clustering sits around zero point twenty-six. The largest is zero point two seven five.

Same airline network, same measure, another reference. Against the first, ten times higher. Against this one, one and a half times.

The degrees alone lift the expected value from zero point zero four to zero point twenty-six. That is sixty-two percent of the way to the real value.

Not all the way, though. Not one of the thousand reaches zero point four. The degrees explain most of the clustering, not all of it.

Look at what happened. The same value was spectacular against one reference, and much less so against another. Nothing changed in the network. Only what the reference kept fixed.

Comparing with a random network of the same size is often a way to rediscover the degrees.

Does the second reference always shrink the gap? No.

Take the American college football season of two thousand: one hundred fifteen teams, six hundred thirteen games. Clustering: zero point four one.

At random, with the same numbers of teams and games, zero point zero nine. With the same degrees, zero point zero eight.

Here every team plays between seven and twelve games, so keeping the degrees changes almost nothing. The clustering comes from something the degrees cannot see.

So every reference is a question. Same nodes and links asks: is it more clustered than chance? Same degrees asks: more than popularity explains?

A reference network is a null hypothesis. The world as it would be without the thing you want to show, and your measure is judged against it. Choosing the reference is choosing the hypothesis you test.

That was the quarrel of the archipelago. Connor and Simberloff chose what to keep fixed. Some critics, Diamond and Gilpin among them, answered that keeping so much fixed makes the reference too hard to beat.

To sum up. A number means little alone. Compare it with a reference network, and ask what that reference keeps fixed.

One value can be remarkable against one reference, and much less so against another.

Next time, how do we build networks that keep every degree, without cheating?

PLATE 10.24:33

Rewiring without cheating

To build references that keep every degree without cheating, swap the ends of two links, refusing swaps that would create a loop or a double link. On the airline network, clustering falls from 0.4 to a plateau near 0.26 after about one swap per link. Counting must start only after that plateau, since earlier references still remember the original network.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: how do we build networks that keep every degree, without cheating?

Cheating has a name. In episode nine point four, blind pairing made self-loops and double links, and erasing them changed the degrees.

Here is a way that cannot cheat. We do not build a network from nothing. We start from the real one, and change it by tiny moves that keep every degree.

Here is the move. Pick two links: A to B, and C to D. Cut them both. Now join them crosswise: A to D, and C to B.

Count the links at each node. Before, the degrees are two, two, two, two, three and three. After, they are the same.

We gave away two ends, and took two ends back. Every degree is kept.

This is the move of Maslov and Sneppen, in two thousand and two. They rewired networks of proteins this way, to ask whether the hubs link to each other more than the degrees explain.

But some swaps are forbidden. Say the two links share a node: B to A, and A to E. Joining crosswise would link A to itself. A self-loop. Refused.

Or say E and F are already linked. Swapping B to F and E to D would draw a second link between E and F. A double link. Also refused.

When a swap is refused, we do nothing, and draw another pair.

So this method never cheats. No loop and no double link ever appears, so there is nothing to erase. And it loses nothing: starting from any simple network, swaps can reach every simple network with the same degrees.

The same move works on the archipelago. Take two species and two islands in a checkerboard: each species lives where the other does not. Exchange them.

Every island keeps its number of species, and every species keeps its number of islands. If one species already lives on both islands, the swap is refused.

So swaps draw random tables of the kind Connor and Simberloff described.

Here is our made-up table, with its checkerboard pair, A and B. We make a thousand random tables, a hundred and fifty swaps each.

On average they have one point three pairs of species that never share an island. Our table has one. More than three random tables in four have at least as many.

So in this invented table, the checkerboard is no surprise.

Now the question of this episode: how many swaps?

One swap changes two links. The airline network has two thousand, one hundred twenty-six links, so one swap is nothing.

We swap, and after every few swaps we measure the clustering. We start at zero point four.

We count swaps per link: the swaps made, divided by the number of links.

After a tenth of a swap per link, the clustering has fallen to zero point three three. After half a swap per link, zero point two seven. After one, zero point two six.

And then it stops falling. After two swaps per link, all one hundred runs end between zero point two five and zero point two seven. That is the plateau.

Ninety-two percent of the fall happens in the first half swap per link. Ninety-eight percent, by one.

Look at the links themselves. After one swap per link, forty-one percent of the original links are still there. Then the share settles near thirty-eight percent.

Before the plateau, the network still remembers where it came from.

Stop after a tenth of a swap per link, and the reference has clustering zero point three three. Our network would look only zero point zero six above it, instead of zero point one four. That reference is too close to the original.

So we start counting only after the plateau.

The plateau depends on the network and on the measure. So check it on yours, and keep a margin. In episode ten point one, we used ten swaps per link, ten times past the plateau.

Milo and colleagues, in two thousand and three, saw a plateau near one swap per link on a gene network. They still found that around a hundred per link was more than enough.

In our runs, about six attempts in ten were refused, almost all of them because of a double link.

To sum up. Swap the ends of two links, refuse loops and double links, and keep going until the measure stops moving. Then start counting.

Next time, a swap keeps every degree. But what about the weights, and the space?

PLATE 10.35:17

Keeping weights and space

A plain swap breaks node strengths and ignores space. On a brain network of eighty-three regions, the correlation of strengths with the original is only 0.51, rising to 0.98 once the weights are dealt back out. Keeping link lengths holds the wire near 62 metres. Clustering stays above every reference, but the rich club almost vanishes once degrees are kept.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a swap kept every degree. But two things were left behind: the weights, and the space.

Start with the weights. Here is a small network where every link carries a number. A to B carries four. C to D carries one.

Swap them, and let each weight travel with its link. Now A to D carries four, and C to B carries one.

The degrees are kept. But look at the strengths. The strength of a node is the sum of the weights on its links.

Before, B has strength six. After, B has three. And D goes from four to seven.

Two nodes changed, and nothing in the swap said they should.

Now a real case. A human brain network, with eighty-three regions and one thousand and sixty-seven links. It comes from Griffa, Alemán-Gómez and Hagmann. Here the weight of a link is a density of fibres. The strongest link is nearly ninety times the median one.

We swap its links, ten times per link as in episode ten point one, and we carry the weights along. Then we compare the strength of each region with its original strength.

Over a hundred references, the correlation averages only zero point five one. About a quarter of the differences between regions survives. A typical region is off by forty-six percent.

Rubinov and Sporns, in two thousand eleven, proposed a fix. First, switch the links, as before. Then deal the original weights back out.

Rank the original weights, heaviest first. Rank the new links by the weight their two ends would expect, given the strengths. Pick a weight at random, and give it to the link of the same rank. Update the strengths that remain, rank again, and repeat.

Every original weight is used once. The strengths are only kept approximately. In our runs, the correlation rises to zero point nine eight, and the typical error falls to eight percent.

So weights have a reference. Now, the space.

A brain is not an abstract graph. Its regions sit somewhere, and long cables cost more. In episode nine point five, we met this as wire.

Here, the length of a link is the straight distance between the centres of its two regions. The median link is fifty-four millimetres long. Added up, all the links make sixty-two metres.

Now swap the links, keeping every degree, and add up the wire again. It grows to seventy-seven metres. A quarter of the links are longer than ninety-two millimetres, against a tenth in the real brain.

No brain could pay for that.

So we change the rule. We cut the lengths into ten classes of equal size. A swap is accepted only if the two new links fall in the same two classes as the two old ones.

Every degree is kept. The whole histogram of lengths is kept too. The wire stays near sixty-two metres.

This is a simple version, written for this episode. The published ones are finer.

Roberts and colleagues, in two thousand sixteen, measured how much geometry contributes to the human connectome. We read their abstract only. It calls the contribution major, but not definitive.

Betzel and Bassett, in two thousand eighteen, built a null model that keeps the degrees and the weight distribution exactly. It keeps the length distribution, and the link between length and weight, approximately.

Now the test. In episode seven point four we met the rich club. In episode nine point two, the small world. Do they survive these references?

We measure both on this brain, against three references of five hundred networks each.

First reference: the same number of links, placed at random.

The brain clusters at zero point five three. Random links give zero point three one. The ratio is one point seven. The small-world index, sigma, is one point six six.

Second reference: the same degrees. The reference now clusters at zero point four two. The ratio falls to one point two seven. Sigma falls to one point two four.

Third reference: the same degrees, and the same lengths. The reference clusters at zero point four five. The ratio falls to one point one eight. Sigma falls to one point one six.

The effect shrinks at each step. But it does not vanish. Among all the references, not one reaches the clustering of the brain.

The rich club tells another story. Take the fifty regions with more than twenty links. Against random links, rho is one point five seven. Against the same degrees, it is one point oh two.

It was the degrees all along. Add the lengths, and it hardly moves: one point oh two again.

Among the twenty-four regions with more than thirty links, a small excess remains. Rho is one point oh eight, then one point oh seven.

So here is what the data say. Clustering keeps a margin that degrees and distance do not explain. The rich club almost does not.

To sum up. After a swap, check the strengths, and deal the weights back. To keep the wire, keep the lengths. And read each effect against every reference, one step at a time.

PLATE 10.44:32

Which reference for which question?

Three references answer three questions: is it more than a density, more than popularity, more than cheap wiring? Applied to one brain network, clustering of 0.53 stays above all three, with ratios of 1.7, 1.27 and 1.18. The rich club gets one yes and two noes. There is no neutral reference, so write the question before picking one.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time we asked: which reference should we use, and for which question?

Here is the answer, in one table.

Three rows, one for each reference of the last episodes. Three columns: what the reference keeps, what it scrambles, and the question it answers.

First row: the same links. We keep the nodes and the number of links, and we wire them at random.

Everything else is scrambled. The degrees, the triangles, the lengths. The busiest region of the brain has fifty-five links. With random links, the busiest has about thirty-six.

It answers a modest question: is it more than a density?

Second row: the same degrees. Every node keeps its number of links.

Who links to whom is scrambled, and so are the lengths. A tenth of the brain's links are longer than ninety-two millimetres. With the same degrees, about a quarter are.

It answers: is it more than the popularity of each node?

Third row: the same degrees, and the same space. Each node keeps its degree, and the lengths of the links keep their whole distribution.

Only the choice of partners is scrambled. This one answers: is it more than what cheap wiring explains?

Read down the table. Each row keeps more than the one above, so each row asks for more.

Now let us read the brain of the last episode through this table. Eighty-three regions, one thousand and sixty-seven links. Its clustering is zero point five three.

Question one: is it more than a density? The random reference clusters at zero point three one. The brain is one point seven times higher. Yes.

Question two: is it more than popularity? The reference with the same degrees clusters at zero point four two. Here the ratio falls to one point two seven. Yes, but by less.

Question three: is it more than cheap wiring? The reference clusters at zero point four five. Now the ratio is one point one eight. Still yes.

Out of five hundred references of each kind, not one reaches the brain.

Ask the same three questions of the rich club: the fifty regions with more than twenty links. Against random links, rho is one point five seven. Yes, more than a density.

Against the same degrees, rho is one point oh two. Add the space, and it is one point oh two again.

Same brain, same data, one yes and two noes. The rich club is more than a density, but it is not more than popularity. Neither answer was wrong. Each reference answered its own question.

Now back to the archipelago.

Connor and Simberloff kept two things fixed: how many islands each species lives on, and how many species each island holds. Diamond's Bismarck table was never published, so they used birds of the West Indies and of the New Hebrides.

Critics, Diamond and Gilpin among them, called this circular. Some of them added that competition itself may have shaped those totals.

By nineteen ninety-six, Gotelli and Graves wrote of an acrimonious debate that had spanned more than twenty years. They also wrote that the claim about competition had never been validated.

The quarrel was not about arithmetic. It was about what to keep fixed.

Keep the totals, and we ask: beyond what the totals say, is there a pattern? Free the totals, and we ask: does competition explain the totals themselves? Gotelli and Graves call these two hypotheses, each deserving a test in its own right.

So there is no neutral reference. Keep little fixed, and we test a modest hypothesis. Keep a lot fixed, and we test a demanding one.

In twenty twenty-two, Váša and Mišić reviewed null models for brain networks. Their abstract describes a spectrum, from liberal models that control few properties, to conservative ones that recapitulate several. We read the abstract only.

Neither end is the true one. Choosing what to keep fixed is choosing the hypothesis you test.

So before comparing, write the question, and then pick the reference that asks it.

The same idea has a chapter of its own in the Statistics series: chapter fourteen, What do we shuffle?

To sum up. Every reference keeps something fixed and scrambles the rest, and what it keeps decides the question it answers. There is no neutral reference.
