PLATE 1.16:47

Dots and lines

Harry Beck's London Underground map dropped geography and kept only what connects to what, which is exactly a graph: vertices and edges. The degree of a vertex counts its edges, and all degrees add up to twice the number of edges, the handshake lemma. Two drawings that look nothing alike can be the same graph, called isomorphic.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a map of an underground railway. The lines twist with the streets above. Stations crowd together in the centre and spread far apart at the edges. It is faithful to the city, and it is very hard to read.

In 1931, a draughtsman named Harry Beck had a strange idea for the London Underground. Throw the geography away. Draw every line straight, bend it only at forty-five degrees, and space the stations evenly. The publicity office first turned it down. A pocket edition reached the public in January 1933.

Why did it work? Because a rider does not need to know where a station is. A rider needs to know what comes after it, and where to change lines. Beck kept exactly that, and nothing else.

Now let us go one step further than Beck. Take away the colours. Take away the names. What is left is a set of dots, and a set of lines joining some pairs of dots. That is a graph.

Dots and lines may sound too simple to be interesting. But the same picture describes friends and who knows whom, web pages and their links, brain regions and the fibres between them, atoms and their bonds. Each time, the trick is Beck's trick: forget what the things look like, and keep only who is connected to whom.

The dots are called vertices, or nodes. The lines are called edges, or links. An edge joins two vertices, and that is all it does. It has no length, no shape, no colour. It only says: these two are connected.

Two vertices joined by an edge are called adjacent. They are neighbours. A vertex may also have no edge at all. It still belongs to the graph, like a guest who talks to nobody.

Our map is an invented metro. It has thirteen stations and thirteen links. That small pair of numbers already describes a network, and you will meet it again and again in this series: n, the number of vertices, and m, the number of edges.

Now look at a single station. Count the edges that touch it. That count is its degree. A station at the end of a line has degree one. A station in the middle of a line has degree two. The three interchanges have degree four: two neighbours on each of two lines. The degree is the simplest thing you can measure on a vertex, and it is already useful. The places where the network branches are the vertices of high degree.

Let us leave the metro for a party. Six guests: Ana, Ben, Cleo, Dev, Eli and Fay. Some of them shake hands. Each guest is a vertex, and each handshake is an edge. Ana shakes three hands. Ben shakes two. Cleo three, Dev three, Eli two, and Fay, who arrived late, only one.

Add up those counts: three, two, three, three, two, one. Fourteen. And how many handshakes were there? Seven. Fourteen is exactly twice seven. That is not luck. Every handshake involves two people, so when each guest reports their own count, every handshake is counted twice, once from each end. In any graph, the sum of all the degrees equals twice the number of edges. This rule goes back to Euler, and it is called the handshake lemma.

Check it on the metro. Six end stations of degree one, four stations of degree two, three interchanges of degree four. Six, plus eight, plus twelve: twenty-six. Thirteen links, each counted twice.

The lemma hides a small surprise. The total of the degrees is always even, because it is twice something. So the guests with an odd number of handshakes must come in pairs. At our party, Ana, Cleo, Dev and Fay shook an odd number of hands. That makes four, an even number. At any party, on any network, the number of vertices with odd degree is even. You know it before counting a single handshake.

Try one yourself. Could a party end with exactly three guests who shook an odd number of hands? Pause and think. The answer is no. Three is odd, and the lemma says that count is always even. Somebody has miscounted.

Now the most important idea of this episode. Here are two drawings. On the left, six dots on a hexagon, joined around the rim, with the three long diagonals across the middle. On the right, two rows of three dots, where every dot on top is linked to every dot below. They look nothing alike. One is a starred wheel, the other a woven ladder.

How would you even check? Start with what any drawing must keep. Count the vertices: six and six. Count the edges: nine and nine. List the degrees: every vertex has degree three, on both sides. If any of these counts differed, the graphs would differ. But matching counts prove nothing. A ring of six dots and a pair of triangles share all three counts, and they are different graphs: one is in a single piece, the other in two.

The real test is a matching of the vertices themselves. Give each dot a name, and move the dots one by one. Every edge on the left lands on an edge on the right, and no edge is left over. Here it works: these two drawings are the same graph. On large graphs, finding such a matching can be hard work, even for a computer. Two such drawings are called isomorphic, from the Greek for "same shape".

This is Beck's lesson, pushed all the way. The position of a dot means nothing. The length of a line means nothing. A drawing is only a way to show the graph, and the same graph has endless drawings. Some drawings make a structure obvious. Others hide it completely. Much of this series is about finding a good drawing, or about reading the graph without trusting any drawing at all.

Two notes of vocabulary before we go. First, Euler's seven bridges of Königsberg. Two bridges joined the island to the same bank, and two more joined it to the other bank. So between one pair of vertices there are two parallel edges. A graph that allows this is a multigraph. In this series we will mostly work with simple graphs: at most one edge between two vertices, and no edge from a vertex back to itself.

Second, the word itself. It was coined in 1878 by the mathematician James Joseph Sylvester, in a short note in the journal Nature. He took the idea from chemists, who drew molecules as atoms joined by bonds. A molecule is a graph too.

So here is the whole toolkit of this episode. Vertices and edges. The degree of a vertex. The sum of the degrees, equal to twice the number of edges. And one rule of reading: the links are the graph, the drawing is not.

Next time, the edges get a direction. Some streets are one way, and the question "who is connected to whom?" splits in two.

PLATE 1.25:40

One-way streets

When streets are one way, edges become arrows, and degree splits into in-degree and out-degree. Total in-degree always equals total out-degree. In the worm's map of 279 neurons, only 233 of 1961 connected pairs talk both ways, and erasing the arrows invents routes the worm does not have. Say what your arrows mean.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a graph was a set of dots and lines. A line between two dots worked both ways. If you can ride from one station to the next, you can ride back.

Now step out of the metro, into the old town. Here many streets are one way. An arrow is painted on the road, and you must follow it.

Our walker starts at the bakery. Bakery, market, park, station. Easy. Now the walk home. From the station, the only street leads to the library. From the library, back to the market. From the market, to the school or the park, and round again. No street ever leads back to the bakery. You can go from the bakery to the station, but you cannot come back.

So the lines become arrows. A graph whose edges have a direction is a directed graph. Each arrow leaves one vertex, its tail, and enters another, its head. Going from A to B no longer tells you anything about going from B to A.

The degree splits in two. The in-degree of a vertex counts the arrows that arrive. The out-degree counts the arrows that leave. The market has three streets coming in and two going out. The bakery has none coming in, and one going out.

The handshake rule from last time still holds, in a new form. Every arrow has exactly one tail and one head. So if you add up all the in-degrees, you count each arrow once, and the same for all the out-degrees. In our town: nine arrows, a total in-degree of nine, a total out-degree of nine.

Try one yourself. A friend draws a directed graph, and reports a total in-degree of ten and a total out-degree of nine. Pause and think. Something is wrong. Both totals count the same arrows, so they must be equal. One arrow was counted at one end, and not at the other.

Arrows are everywhere once you look for them. On a social network, Ana follows Ben. That is an arrow from Ana to Ben. Ben's in-degree counts his followers.

In science, a paper cites older papers. The arrow goes from the citing paper to the cited one. A paper can only cite work that came before it, so following the arrows is travelling back in time.

In a food web, the rabbit eats the grass and the fox eats the rabbit. Biology textbooks draw the arrow from the grass to the rabbit, and from the rabbit to the fox: the way energy flows, from prey to predator. A first-time reader often guesses the opposite, with the hungry animal pointing at its meal.

That is the trap. Even on the social network, the arrow could point the other way: Ana follows Ben, but Ben's posts travel to Ana. Both drawings are correct. They answer different questions. So when you draw a directed graph, say what an arrow means, and say it out loud.

Sometimes the arrows come in pairs. Ben follows Ana back. In our town, the park and the school are joined by a two-way street: one arrow each way. Two opposite arrows between the same pair of vertices make a reciprocal link. Reciprocity asks how many linked pairs go both ways. In the town, two pairs out of seven.

Now a real nervous system. The tiny worm Caenorhabditis elegans has 302 neurons. In 1986, John White and his colleagues published its wiring, traced on electron micrographs of thin slices, one after another. They found about five thousand chemical synapses, about six hundred gap junctions, and about two thousand junctions onto muscles.

The two kinds of link are different. A chemical synapse has a direction: one neuron releases a messenger, the other receives it. That is an arrow. A gap junction is a small channel between two cells, and current can flow either way. That is a plain line. The same neurons carry two graphs at once.

Here is a small piece of the real wiring: the circuit that makes the worm move away when it is touched. The touch neurons send arrows to four kinds of interneurons. Not one arrow comes back to them. Among the interneurons, many arrows do come in pairs.

Across the whole network, in a later, corrected map of 279 of these neurons, there are 2194 chemical connections. They link 1961 pairs of neurons. Only 233 of those pairs talk both ways: about one in eight.

Why keep the arrows? Pick any neuron A and any neuron B. Following the chemical synapses in their true direction, there is a route from A to B in about 85 percent of cases. In the other 15 percent, there is no route at all. Now erase the arrows. Every neuron can reach every other. Throw the direction away, and you invent routes the worm does not have. Chapter 2 will measure what can reach what.

The opposite mistake exists too. Many brain networks are not traced, they are computed. Two signals are recorded, and we ask how strongly they rise and fall together. That number, a correlation, is the same from A to B and from B to A. So a network built from correlations has no arrows, by construction. It cannot tell you who drives whom. Chapter 14 comes back to it.

So here is the toolkit of this episode. Arrows, with a tail and a head. In-degree and out-degree, which add up to the number of arrows. Reciprocal links. And one rule of reading: say what your arrows mean, and never erase them without a reason.

Next time, the links get a size. A link can be strong or weak, short or long, and the same number can mean two opposite things.

PLATE 1.35:22

How strong is a link?

Edges can carry weights, drawn as thickness, but a weight is either a distance or a strength. A big number means far on a road map and close between friends. Shortest-path methods assume distances, so strengths such as hours together must be converted first. Negative weights and thresholds add trouble: five synapses keeps only 382 of 2194 worm connections.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the lines became arrows. Today the lines get a size. Back on the platform, every line on the map looks the same. But some rides take two minutes, and others take twenty.

Look at three maps. On a road map, each road carries a number: the kilometres from one town to the next. In a group of friends, each friendship carries the hours two people have spent together. In the worm from last time, each connection carries the number of synapses between two neurons.

That number is a weight. A graph whose edges carry weights is a weighted graph. On paper, we draw the weight as the thickness of the line: a thin line for a small weight, a thick line for a big one.

Weights change what you see. In the worm, there are 2194 chemical connections between neurons. Almost half of them are a single synapse. The strongest one, between two motor neurons along the body, is made of 37 synapses. Without weights, a single synapse and a bundle of thirty-seven look exactly alike.

Now the friends. A study by Jeffrey Hall estimated that it takes about fifty hours together to become casual friends, and more than two hundred to become close friends. Ana and Ben have spent two hundred and forty hours together. Ana and Cleo, only forty. They barely know each other.

Here is the twist. On the road map, a big number means far. Thirty kilometres is a long drive. With the friends, a big number means close. Two hundred and forty hours is a deep friendship. The same kind of number, a bigger weight, and two opposite meanings.

So every weight is one of two things. It is a distance, where bigger means farther apart. Or it is a strength, where bigger means closer together. Road lengths, travel times and costs are distances. Hours together, synapse counts and correlations are strengths.

Picture each edge as one of two objects. A ruler: its number is its length. Or a rubber band: its number is how hard it pulls, so a strong band holds the two ends close. Take one triangle with the weights two, three and four. Read them as rulers, and the side marked four is the longest. Read them as rubber bands, and the very same side becomes the shortest. Same numbers, opposite shapes.

Every algorithm assumes one of the two readings. Finding the shortest path assumes distances. Watch what happens if you forget. Ana wants to pass a secret to Eve, through friends. We ask for the shortest path, and feed it the hours. It adds them up, and picks the smallest total: Ana to Cleo, forty hours, then Cleo to Eve, fifty. The route runs through the weakest ties in the group. That is the opposite of what we wanted.

The fix is to turn strengths into distances first. A common choice is one over the weight: two hundred and forty hours become a short distance, and forty hours a long one. Now the shortest path goes through Ben, the close friend. Brain network toolboxes ask for this step before they compute paths. Other choices exist, like a logarithm, and they can give different paths. The algorithm never warns you. It simply answers the question you gave it. Chapter 2 finds shortest paths, and chapter 6 builds measures of importance on top of them. Both depend on this choice.

Weights can also be negative. Many brain networks are computed from signals. Here are three simulated signals. A and B rise and fall together: their correlation is 0.70, a positive weight. A and C do the opposite: when one goes up, the other goes down. Their correlation is minus 0.64. That is an anticorrelation, and we draw it as a dotted blue line.

Real brains show them too. In 2005, Michael Fox and his colleagues found two large networks in resting brain scans whose signals were anticorrelated. How much of this is real, and how much comes from a cleaning step applied to the data, is still debated.

A negative weight fits neither reading. It is not a short road, and not a strong friendship. Shortest path methods can give wrong answers with negative weights, and most measures in this series were designed for positive ones. Some studies drop them, some keep them apart. Whatever you choose, say it.

There is one last way to lose information. Many analyses keep only the links above a threshold, and turn them into plain lines. In the worm, keep only the connections with at least five synapses. Of the 2194, only 382 survive. The other connections vanish. And among the survivors, a link of five synapses and a link of thirty-seven now look exactly the same. A threshold is a choice, and a different threshold gives a different graph. Chapter 14 comes back to it.

So here is the toolkit of this episode. A weight is a number on an edge, drawn as thickness. Before using it, ask what it means: a distance, where big is far, or a strength, where big is close. Turn strengths into distances before looking for short paths. Draw negative weights apart, and decide what to do with them. And remember that a threshold throws the weights away.

PLATE 1.46:31

Two kinds of nodes

A bipartite graph has two kinds of nodes, and every link crosses between them. Painting nodes in two colours along the links tests it, and fails exactly when a loop of odd length exists. In the Natchez data, 18 women and 14 events give 89 attendances. Projecting onto the women makes each event a clique, so some closeness is built, not found.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, every link got a number. Today, the nodes get something new: a kind.

Look at three lists you have seen a hundred times. Actors and films. Bees and flowers. Volunteers and time slots. All three are illustrative.

In each one, there are two kinds of things. An actor is linked to a film she played in. A bee is linked to a flower it visits. A volunteer is linked to a slot where she is free.

Notice what never happens. No actor is linked to another actor. No bee is linked to another bee. Every link crosses from one kind to the other.

A graph like this is called bipartite. Its nodes split into two groups, and every link goes from one group to the other. The word simply means two parts. You find such graphs wherever a table says who did what.

Drawn in two tidy columns, a bipartite graph is easy to spot. But you know from episode one point one that the position of the points means nothing. Below is the same kind of graph, scrambled. Can you tell whether it is bipartite?

Here is a test that always works. Pick any node and paint it pink. Paint all its neighbours blue. Then paint their neighbours pink. Keep going, always alternating. Let us follow it on this graph. The first node turns pink. Its three neighbours turn blue. Their other neighbours turn pink. The last node, which only pink nodes touch, turns blue.

If you reach the end without ever joining two nodes of the same colour, you have won. Pink on one side, blue on the other. Slide the nodes apart, and the two columns appear. The graph was bipartite all along.

Now add one more link, between two pink nodes. The painting breaks. That link joins two nodes of the same colour, and nothing can repair it.

Why does it break? Walk around a loop. At each step, the colour changes. Pink, blue, pink, blue.

To close the loop, you must come back to the colour you left. That works after an even number of steps. It fails after an odd number.

Here the length of a loop is its number of links. A triangle has three. A square has four. A pentagon has five. A hexagon has six.

The square and the hexagon can be painted. The triangle and the pentagon cannot. A graph with no loops at all passes the test too.

That is the whole story. A graph is bipartite if and only if it has no loop of odd length. It is a classical result of graph theory. It speaks about loops, not about drawings, so it holds however the nodes are placed. The painting is the test, and an odd loop is the only thing that can stop it.

Now, a real network. In the nineteen thirties, a team of anthropologists studied social life in Natchez, Mississippi. They noted which women went to which social events. Their book came out in nineteen forty-one, and the table of attendances is shipped with NetworkX, so you can load it yourself. The women are numbered W on screen, and the events E.

There are eighteen women and fourteen events. Together they make eighty-nine attendances, and each one is a link.

On screen, the women are on the left, the events on the right, and eighty-nine lines cross between them. Not one goes from a woman to a woman. So the graph is bipartite, and the painting confirms it.

A woman went to between two and eight events. The busiest evening gathered fourteen women, and the quietest only three.

Count the links from the left, woman by woman, and you get eighty-nine. Count them from the right, event by event, and you get eighty-nine again. Every link has two ends, one in each column. This is the handshake count from episode one point one, now with two sides.

Now comes the trap. Often we do not want two kinds of nodes. We want only the women, with a link between two women who met at the same event. This is called a projection.

Back to the women. Think of a lamp shining on the graph. The events are squeezed away, and only a shadow of the women remains.

Start small. One film, with four actors. Each pair of actors shares that film. Four actors make six pairs: each of the four has three partners, and every pair is counted twice, as in the handshakes. So the film turns into a group where everybody is linked to everybody. Such a group is called a clique.

Now project the real network. Of the one hundred fifty-three possible pairs of women, one hundred thirty-nine are linked. That is ninety-one percent. The shadow looks tightly knit, as if almost everyone knew everyone.

But part of that is made by the projection itself. The biggest evening had fourteen women. That single evening creates ninety-one links, more than half of all the possible pairs. The closeness was built by the construction. It was not discovered in the people.

The projection can keep one number: how many events two women shared. That is a weight, like last time. Forty-four pairs shared exactly one event, and one pair shared seven.

The shadow also forgets. Here are two different stories about three people. In one, all three met at a single event. In the other, each pair met at its own event. Project them, and you get the same triangle.

So a triangle in a projection can mean one evening, or three. The projection cannot tell you which.

You can also project the other way, onto the events. Two events are linked when one woman went to both. Sixty-six of the ninety-one pairs of events are linked. The same two effects appear: every woman becomes a clique of events, and the shadow forgets who was where.

Both shadows are useful, as long as you remember they are shadows. Chapter seven measures how tightly a network clusters, and it comes back to this trap. Chapter four uses bipartite graphs to match volunteers with slots. And chapter sixteen gives the nodes many kinds. Whenever you meet a table of who attended what, or who owns what, ask the same question. Are there two kinds of nodes?

To sum up, a bipartite graph has two kinds of nodes, and every link crosses between them. To test one, paint along the links with two colours. It fails exactly when there is a loop of odd length. And when you project onto one kind, every event becomes a clique, so some of the closeness you see is built, not found.

Next time, we write a graph as a table of ones and zeros, the adjacency matrix, in episode one point five.

PLATE 1.56:54

The graph as a table

A graph can be written as an adjacency matrix: rows are where links start, columns are where they end, and a row sum is a degree. Without arrows the table is symmetric. The worm's 279 neurons fill only 2.8 percent of the cells, so the table is sparse. Reordering the rows gives different tables for one graph and can reveal blocks.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we sorted nodes into two kinds. Today, we turn a whole graph into a table.

A drawing is fine for six nodes. But a computer does not look at drawings. It reads numbers, in rows and columns. So we need a way to write a graph as a table.

Here is a small graph, made up for the purpose. Six nodes, called A to F. Two triangles, joined by one bridge.

Think of a wall of pigeonholes in a post office. Every node owns one row and one column. When two nodes are linked, a letter goes into the hole where their row and their column cross.

Take row A and column B. Are A and B linked? Yes. So a one goes into that cell. Now take row A and column D. They are not linked. So the cell stays at zero.

Do this for every pair, and the wall is full. We call it the adjacency matrix. Adjacent just means next to each other. A one means that two nodes are neighbours, and a zero means they are not.

Look at the pattern. Fold the table along its diagonal, from the top left to the bottom right. The two halves match. Each link appears twice, once in row A and once in row B. So when links have no direction, the table is symmetric.

And the diagonal itself is empty. No node is linked to itself.

Now add up the ones in a row. Row C holds three ones, and C has three links. The sum of a row is the degree of the node. Add up the whole table, and you get fourteen. That is seven links, each counted twice. It is the handshake rule from episode one point one, seen in a table.

Now give the links directions, as in episode one point two. An arrow from A to B is not an arrow from B to A. So the table needs a rule, and here it is. The row is where the arrow starts. The column is where it ends. Row is from, column is to.

Read row A. It has a one in column B, so an arrow goes from A to B. Now read row B. Its cell in column A is a zero, so no arrow comes back. The symmetry is broken.

Be careful. Some books use the opposite rule. Whenever you read a matrix, check which way it was written. In this series, the row is always the start, and the column is always the end.

With arrows, a row sum counts the arrows that leave a node, the out-degree. A column sum counts the arrows that arrive, the in-degree. Row C adds up to two.

Next, the weights from episode one point three. The cells now hold the weights instead of ones. The link between A and B has weight three, so both of its cells hold a three. The darker the cell, the heavier the link.

What about the diagonal? A one there means that a node is linked to itself. That is called a loop. Here, node E has one.

There is another way to write the same graph. For each node, list its neighbours. A has B and C. C has A, B and D. The list holds the same information as the table, in another shape.

The table has a cell for every pair, filled or not. The list keeps only the links that exist. Remember this difference. It matters in a moment.

Now, a real network. The worm Caenorhabditis elegans has three hundred two neurons. A table with one row and one column for each of them would have ninety-one thousand two hundred four cells.

But in the wiring list we use, only two hundred seventy-nine neurons appear. Twenty belong to the pharynx, which forms a separate small network. Three more make no synapse with other neurons. So our table has two hundred seventy-nine rows and two hundred seventy-nine columns. That makes seventy-seven thousand eight hundred forty-one cells.

Each filled cell is a chemical synapse connection, from the neuron of the row to the neuron of the column. Only two thousand one hundred ninety-four cells are filled. The other seventy-five thousand six hundred forty-seven are zero. That is two point eight percent.

On average, a neuron sends to about eight others, out of two hundred seventy-eight possible. A table that is mostly zeros is called sparse. Many real networks are sparse, and this one is. It is not symmetric either. It is a directed network, with arrows.

So here is why the list of neighbours matters. For a sparse network, writing only the links that exist takes far less room than writing every zero.

Now comes the surprise. The order of the rows is our choice. Let us renumber the small graph: A, D, B, E, C, F. Nothing has changed. Same nodes, same links. But the table now looks scattered.

Put the nodes back in the first order, and the pattern returns. One block for the first triangle, one block for the second, and a single cell for the bridge.

The same thing happens on the worm. In the order of the file, the neurons are listed by name, and the table looks like a dust of points. Shuffle the order at random, and it looks like noise.

Now sort the neurons so that those that talk to each other end up side by side. An algorithm called the Louvain method does the grouping. It finds five groups. Order the rows and the columns by group, and blocks appear along the diagonal.

Count the connections that fall inside a block. Sixty-four percent of them do. If the groups had been drawn at random, with the same sizes, only twenty-three percent would.

But be careful. That algorithm was built to find dense groups, so it always finds some. Whether these groups mean anything is a question for chapter eight. The table only shows that the order matters.

Three tables, one graph. The graph is the same each time. Only the way we wrote it down has changed.

You may have met such tables already. In the EEG, fMRI and fNIRS series, a connectivity matrix is exactly this, a weighted adjacency matrix. It holds one number for each pair of regions or channels. Often that number comes from a symmetric measure, such as a correlation, so the table is symmetric.

The matrix with four blocks, from the hyperscanning series, returns in chapter fifteen.

One last remark, outside the main route. Multiply a table by itself, and each cell counts the walks of length two between two nodes. From A to C, there is one, through B. Chapter eleven shows why this is useful.

To sum up. A graph can be written as a table. Rows are where links start, and columns are where they end. Without arrows the table is symmetric, weights go in the cells, and a row sum is a degree.

Different orders give different tables for the same graph. And a sorted table can show blocks.
