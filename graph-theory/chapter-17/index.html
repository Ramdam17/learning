Messages between neighbours

A molecule is a graph of atoms and bonds. Each atom holds a card of numbers and mixes in its neighbours' cards with learned weights, so after k steps it knows everything within k bonds. In a toy of fifteen atoms the answer is 12.91 whatever the numbering, whereas a model reading the table as an image gave a thousand different answers.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. TransE gives each node one point, and never looks at who its neighbours are. What if a node could learn from what its neighbours say?

Welcome to the chemistry lab.

In 2020, a team at MIT and the Broad Institute trained a neural network on two thousand three hundred and thirty-five molecules. For each one, the lab knew whether it stopped a bacterium from growing.

Then the network judged a library of six thousand one hundred and eleven known molecules. One of them stood out. It had been built to treat diabetes, not infections.

Its name is halicin. In mice, it fought two kinds of infection. The team then let the network judge more than a hundred and seven million other molecules.

Here is the question for this chapter. How did the network read a molecule? Not as a picture. As a graph.

Look at the sketch on the wall. Every corner and every letter is an atom, and every line is a bond between two atoms.

Take away the paper, and a graph is left: fifteen atoms and sixteen bonds. Hydrogens are left out, as chemists do.

That is where the word graph comes from, as Sylvester wrote in eighteen seventy-eight, and as we saw in episode one point one.

Give each atom a small card of numbers. Four slots say its type: carbon, nitrogen, oxygen or sulphur. One slot says its charge.

Take the nitrogen on the left, the one with a plus charge. Its card has a one in the nitrogen slot, and a one in the charge slot.

Now every atom receives the cards of its neighbours. This nitrogen has three: a carbon, and two oxygens.

Add them up: one carbon, two oxygen, and a charge of minus one.

The atom mixes that sum with its own card, using fixed weights, and writes a new card of four numbers: zero point eight eight, zero point three eight, zero point one four, and minus zero point eight five.

Every atom does the same, at the same time, with the same weights.

That was one step. Do it again, and each card carries news from neighbours of neighbours.

Change the nitrogen's card, and watch which cards move. After one step, four cards change: its own, and its three neighbours'. After two steps, six. After three, eight.

I checked, and the cards that move are exactly the atoms within one, two, then three bonds.

So after k steps, a card summarises everything within k bonds.

The two farthest atoms are nine bonds apart. After nine steps, every card can have heard from every atom.

To judge the whole molecule, add up all fifteen cards. That gives four totals. One last set of four weights turns them into a single number.

In this toy, the number is twelve point nine one. It means nothing, because the weights are random: they were drawn with a fixed seed.

What a real model learns is its weights. This toy has one hundred and twenty of them. Training moves them until the output agrees with what the lab measured.

Does this ring a bell? In episode thirteen point three, we built a filter on a graph from neighbours alone.

With one number per node, a step here is one weight times my value, plus another weight times the sum of my neighbours.

Three steps make one polynomial filter. I computed both routes, and they agree to fifteen decimal places.

The difference is that here the coefficients are not chosen. They are learned.

In 2017, Gilmer and his co-authors noticed that several models for molecules already worked this way. They gathered them under one name: message passing neural networks.

The model behind halicin is one of them, a directed message passing neural network.

Now the test that matters. In episode one point five, we renumbered the vertices of a graph. The table changed, and the graph did not.

Do it here. Number the fifteen atoms from one to fifteen, then number them again, at random. Fifty-two of the two hundred and twenty-five cells of the table change.

Take a model that reads that table like an image: one fixed weight for each cell, then the cards in order.

Before renumbering, it answers zero point three four. After, zero point two nine. Same molecule, different answer.

Over a thousand random renumberings, it gave a thousand different answers, from minus zero point nine to zero point eight eight.

Message passing answers twelve point nine one every time. The largest difference over a thousand renumberings was smaller than ten to the minus fourteen, which is rounding noise.

Why? Each atom adds the cards of its neighbours, and a sum has no order. An atom keeps its card whatever its number, and the final sum over atoms has no order either.

So the answer depends on the molecule, and not on how someone wrote it down. That is what separates a network on graphs from one that reads the table as an image.

To sum up. Each atom holds a card, and at every step it mixes in its neighbours' cards, with weights that are learned. After k steps it knows its neighbourhood of radius k, and renumbering the atoms changes nothing.

What the model cannot see

Message passing cannot separate a ring of six carbons from two rings of three: every atom hears identical cards, so the two answers differ by exactly zero across a thousand sets of weights. Colour refinement has the same blind spot, and two teams proved the link in 2019. Adding triangle counts to the cards lifts the limit.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. Fifteen atoms, one rule everywhere: is there a difference between two molecules that the cards can never show?

Back in the chemistry lab, the answer is yes. Here are the two molecules.

The first is a ring of six carbon atoms. Benzene has this shape. The second is two separate rings of three carbon atoms, as in cyclopropane.

I drew only the carbon skeletons. Hydrogens are left out, and so are the bond types. It is a toy, but it keeps what matters here.

Count them. Each drawing has six atoms and six bonds. And every atom has exactly two neighbours.

They are not the same graph. One is a single piece. The other is two pieces, with no bond between them. Chemically, a six-ring and two three-rings are very different molecules.

Now give them cards, and run the toy model from last time. Same weights, drawn with seed one hundred and seventy-one. Same three steps.

Every atom is carbon, so every atom starts with the same card: a one in the carbon slot, and zeros elsewhere.

Step one. Each atom receives two cards, both identical, so each atom writes the same new card. That holds in the hexagon, and in the triangles.

Step two, and step three, do the same again. After three steps, all twelve atoms hold one and the same card.

Add up the six cards of each molecule, and apply the last weights. The hexagon gets minus seven point nine one. The two triangles get minus seven point nine one. The difference is exactly zero.

Was that bad luck with the weights? I drew a thousand other sets of random weights, and compared the two answers each time. The largest difference was zero.

The reason fits in one line. Every atom hears two identical cards, whatever the weights are. So all the cards stay equal.

The limit comes from how the model is built. Training only chooses weights, and no weights can pull these two molecules apart.

There is an older test with the same blind spot. It is called colour refinement. Give every atom a colour from its type. Then repeat one rule: the new colour of an atom depends on its own colour, and on the list of its neighbours' colours. A list has no order.

Try it on a chain of four carbons. At first, every atom has the same colour. After one round, the two ends have heard one neighbour, and the two middle atoms have heard two. So the ends get one new colour, and the middle atoms another.

A second round changes nothing, so the test stops. It can see something here: the ends are not the middle.

Now the hexagon and the triangles. Every atom has the same colour, and hears two neighbours of that colour. Everyone gets the same new colour, in both molecules. The round changes nothing, and the test stops at once.

Count the colours. Six atoms of one colour on the left, and six of the same colour on the right. The two counts are equal, so the test cannot tell the molecules apart.

This method carries the names of Weisfeiler and Leman. Their paper, in Russian, dates from nineteen sixty-eight, and it reduces a graph to a canonical form. What we just ran is the simplest version of it.

In twenty nineteen, two teams proved the link with neural networks. Xu, Hu, Leskovec and Jegelka were one. Morris and his co-authors were the other.

Their result is this: message passing cannot tell apart two graphs that colour refinement cannot. Xu and colleagues also showed a design that reaches exactly that limit.

So the ceiling of the simplest model is this test. The hexagon and the triangles show where it sits.

How do stronger models get past it? They give the cards information that message passing cannot see. One example is the number of triangles that each atom belongs to.

Add it as a sixth slot. In the hexagon, every atom belongs to zero triangles. In the two triangles, every atom belongs to one.

Run the toy again, with fresh weights, drawn with seed one hundred and seventy-two. From the first step, the cards differ. The two answers are seven point two seven, and zero point four seven.

With a thousand other sets of weights, the two answers differed in every one.

Bouritsas and colleagues gave counts of small substructures to the model, and showed that it then goes beyond the colour test. Morris and colleagues took another road: networks that look at small sets of atoms together, not one atom at a time.

To sum up. A message passing model sees each atom only through the cards of its neighbours. So it cannot separate a six-ring from two three-rings, and no training changes that. Adding information, such as triangle counts, lifts the limit.

PLATE 17.36:34

Pitfalls

Stacking many message-passing steps has three pitfalls. Cards level out like heat, so the largest distance between cards falls from 2.45 to 0.02. A far message gets squeezed through a bottleneck. And when connected nodes tend to differ, trusting neighbours fails. Each is an old idea: heat, a fence with few gates, and negative assortativity.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. More steps let a card see further. What could go wrong when we stack many of them?

Back in the chemistry lab, there are three answers. Each one is a pitfall, and each one comes with a measurement.

The first pitfall is that the cards become alike.

Take halicin again: fifteen atoms, sixteen bonds. Every atom holds a card of five numbers. Four slots say carbon, nitrogen, oxygen or sulphur, and one slot says the charge.

To see the mechanism alone, I made the plainest rule I could. No weights, and no squashing function. At each step, every atom replaces its card with the plain average of its own card and its neighbours' cards.

Watch the sulphur slot. Three atoms carry sulphur, so their slot holds a one. Every other atom holds a zero.

After one step, each sulphur shares its slot with its neighbours. After nine steps, which is the diameter, every card has heard from every atom. The sulphur slot now runs from zero point zero nine to zero point two four.

Keep going. After a hundred steps, every atom holds between zero point one eight eight and zero point one nine five. The three sulphurs are no longer special.

Now measure it for the whole card. Take the largest distance between any two cards. At the start it is two point four five. After a single step, it is one point one four, less than half. At nine steps, zero point three six. At a hundred, zero point zero two, under one percent of where it began.

Does this look familiar? It is heat. In episode twelve point two, heat crossed every edge from hot to cold, and the graph levelled out. One averaging step is exactly one step of that flow, with each atom's pace divided by its number of bonds plus one. I checked, and the two agree up to rounding noise.

Remember the roughness of episode eleven point one, the sum of squared gaps over the bonds. Here it fell at every step: thirty-four, then three point eight, then zero point nine.

In twenty eighteen, Li, Han and Wu showed that the graph convolution of a popular model is a form of Laplacian smoothing. They warned that stacking many layers risks over-smoothing. That is this pitfall.

The second pitfall is that a far message gets lost.

Here is an invented graph, and it is illustrative: two clusters of seven nodes. Inside a cluster, every node is linked to every other. Between the clusters, there is one single link.

Pick a source S in the left cluster, and a target T in the right one. T needs three steps to hear from S.

After three steps, how much of T's card is made of S's card? With averaging, the answer is a share. It is also how much T's card moves when S's card moves.

With one link across, the share is zero point two two percent. A cluster-mate of T, one link away, makes up thirteen point eight percent.

Now add links between the clusters, one at a time, up to six. S and T stay three steps apart. The share grows with every link, from zero point two two percent to one point three four percent. That is exactly six times as much.

This is the fence of episode three point three. A fence has a capacity, which adds up its gates. Here the fence is the set of links between the clusters. Its gates limit how much of S can reach T.

And T has only five numbers to hold it all. In three steps, T hears from the thirteen other nodes.

In twenty twenty-one, Alon and Yahav called this over-squashing. A growing amount of information must be squeezed into a card of fixed size. They showed that this bottleneck keeps popular models from fitting signals that travel far.

The third pitfall is that neighbours are not always alike.

Take the plainest guess a model can make. Hide a node's label, and pick the most common label among its neighbours.

Here are two invented networks, each with twenty-four nodes in two teams, pink and blue. In the first, links mostly stay inside a team. In the other, most links join the two teams.

Compute the assortativity of episode seven point three on the team labels. It is plus zero point eight seven for the first network, and minus zero point seven four for the second. It would reach minus one if every link joined the two teams, as in the bipartite graphs of episode one point four.

Now run the guess. On the first network, it is right for twenty-four nodes out of twenty-four. On the second, it is right for none. That is worse than a coin flip.

Was that a lucky draw? I drew a thousand networks of each kind, with seed one thousand seven hundred thirty. The first kind averages ninety-nine percent right. The second averages less than one percent.

In twenty twenty, Zhu and colleagues found the same failure in popular graph models. When connected nodes tend to have different labels, which is called heterophily, many of them do poorly, and some do worse than models that ignore the graph.

They also found three designs that help. Keep a node's own card apart from its neighbours' cards. Look beyond the nearest neighbours. Combine the cards from several steps.

Try the second one on our toy. Let each node vote among the neighbours of its neighbours. Here, that vote is right for twenty-two nodes out of twenty-four, on the second network.

Chemistry itself can behave this way. Look at halicin, and take the element as the label. Only two of the sixteen bonds join the same element. The assortativity is minus zero point three, and the neighbour vote names the element of two atoms out of fifteen.

Now look at the three pitfalls together. They are old friends under new names.

Over-smoothing is the heat of chapter twelve, levelling out. Over-squashing is the bottleneck of chapter three, a fence with few gates. Heterophily is the assortativity of episode seven point three, with a negative sign.

So understanding the graph tells you where the model will fail.

To sum up. Stack many steps, and the cards level out like heat. Put a bottleneck between two nodes, and only a small share crosses it. Link unlike nodes together, and a model that trusts its neighbours trusts the wrong ones.

Predicting links

Counting common neighbours predicts links. On a physics collaboration network of 4,158 authors, it puts 87 percent of hidden links in the first ten against random pairs, but 57 percent when rivals also share a neighbour. The refined score reaches 69. The 14 percent of hidden links with no shared neighbour stay unreachable, so grade on hard negatives.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we ended on a question. In episode sixteen point five, we looked for a missing link. How would a graph model find one?

Welcome to the last episode of the main series. We start with the plainest answer.

Two people who share many friends are probably friends themselves. Count the neighbours that two nodes have in common, and call that count their score.

Does it work? Let us test it on a real network. Every node is an author on the archive of physics papers, in general relativity and quantum cosmology. Two authors are linked if they wrote a paper together.

Leskovec, Kleinberg and Faloutsos published this network in 2007. A paper with several authors links all of them to each other. I kept the largest connected piece: 4,158 authors and 13,422 links.

Now hide ten percent of the links, picked at random, with seed 174. That is 1,342 links. Scores are computed from the other links only. The question is where the hidden links land.

To grade a rule, I line up each hidden link with a hundred pairs of authors who are not linked, and rank all hundred and one by score. As in episode sixteen point five, the grade is the share of hidden links that land among the first ten.

First, an easy line-up. The other hundred are pairs of authors picked at random.

Take one hidden link. Its two authors have two neighbours in common. Not one of the hundred random pairs has any. The link comes first.

That is no accident. Of all the pairs that are not linked, only 0.62 percent have a neighbour in common.

Over the whole network, the plain count puts 87 percent of the hidden links among the first ten. That looks excellent.

But look at what the test really asks. It asks the rule to tell a linked pair from a stranger. And 86 percent of the hidden links still have a neighbour in common after the hiding. They close a triangle.

Now make the line-up harder. The hundred others must also share at least one neighbour, as the real link does. Same rule, same hidden links.

For the link we just saw, four rivals beat it, and six tie with it. Across the whole network, the same rule now puts 57 percent in the first ten. It was 87.

In 2023, Li and colleagues pointed at this very trap. In three large benchmarks, almost all the negative pairs of the test sets had no common neighbour. They proposed harder negatives. Mine are a simpler version of the same idea.

Can a better rule do better? Here is the refinement of Adamic and Adar. A shared neighbour who knows everybody tells you little. One with few links tells you more. So each shared neighbour counts one, divided by the logarithm of its number of links.

Look at two pairs. Both have two neighbours in common, so plain counting gives them the same score. In the first pair, a hidden link, the shared neighbours have two links each. In the second pair, not linked, they have seventy-two and seventy-five. Their refined scores are two point eight nine, and zero point four seven.

Adamic and Adar wrote this idea in 2003, to judge when two personal home pages are related. Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg applied it to links between people in 2007, on collaboration networks of the archive. They asked a harder question than ours: from 1994 to 1996, which new pairs would write together in 1997 to 1999? They found that the network alone carries information about the future, and that fairly subtle measures can beat more direct ones.

Back to our test. On the hard line-up, 69 percent of the hidden links reach the first ten with the refined score, against 57 for plain counting. On the easy one, the two were tied at 87, and so was a third rule, the Jaccard share, which falls to 53. The easy line-up could not tell three rules apart. The hard one ranks them.

What about a learned model? I did not train a neural network. I did build an embedding. Like the points of episode sixteen point five, each author gets 32 numbers, from a factorisation of the visible links, and the score is the product of two authors' numbers.

It reaches 72 percent on the easy line-up, and 41 on the hard one. Here, it does not beat the plain rules, on either. That is one network and one embedding, and no more.

I repeated everything on ten other random splits. The hard line-up gave the same order each time, the refined score first. The three neighbour rules stayed tied on the easy one. Results moved by about one point.

Now look at the links that these rules cannot see. Of the 1,342 hidden links, 186 have no neighbour in common. That is 14 percent. None of them reaches the first ten of the hard line-up, with any of the three neighbour rules. A rule that reads shared neighbours cannot find a link whose ends have none.

Remember episode sixteen point five. A high rank says that a pair looks like the pairs already linked. A candidate is a hypothesis, not a discovery.

So there are two lessons. First, the plainest rule is a baseline, and a graph model has to beat it. Second, a score means little until you know the negatives. Li and colleagues compared many graph neural networks with their harder negatives. Most datasets gave much lower scores, and simple models did better against complex ones than under the old test. They wrote that recent advanced methods may have benefited from the easier negative samples.

Chapter six asked which node matters most. Chapter three asked where a network breaks. Chapter twelve asked how something spreads.

In episode five point seven, we collected fourteen sentences that each hide a graph. The first one was: who knows whom? Today's rule turns that sentence into a prediction.

One idea carried the whole series: forget the shape, and keep what touches what. Counting common neighbours uses nothing else.

To sum up. Two nodes that share neighbours give you a baseline every model must beat. Give more weight to the rare ones, and it sharpens. Grade it on hard negatives, or every model looks excellent.
