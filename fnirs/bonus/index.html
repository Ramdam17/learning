An MNE-NIRS pipeline, end to end

A complete fNIRS analysis runs in Python with MNE and MNE-NIRS on a simulated recording with known ground truth. Steps are loading, optical density, scalp coupling index, motion correction with temporal derivative distribution repair, then two branches: short-channel regression, Beer-Lambert conversion and block averaging, or a general linear model with autoregressive noise. About thirty lines, with every decision in the code.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Bonus Episode! We have spent 7 chapters on ideas. Now let's run a complete fNIRS analysis in Python with MNE and its fNIRS extension, MNE-NIRS. Every line of code you will see here actually ran, on MNE version 1.13 and MNE-NIRS version 0.7. One honest detail, the recording is simulated so we know the ground truth. 10 minutes, 8 long channels over the left motor cortex, 2 short channels, 19 blocks of finger tapping, a few motion artifacts, and 1 channel with poor contact on purpose.

Step 1. Load. With real data, you would call Read Raw SNIRF on a SNIRF file or Read Raw NIRx on a NIRx folder. Here we load our simulated file. What you get is raw light intensity, 2 wavelengths per channel, plus annotations marking each tapping block.

Step 2. Optical Density. One function call converts intensity into changes in optical density, the log of the ratio to the mean. Everything that follows works on that.

Step 3. Quality. The scalp coupling index checks whether the heartbeat is visible and identical at both wavelengths. If the optode touches the skin, the cardiac pulse shows up clearly in both. Our good channels score around 0.84. Channel S3D1 scores 0.41 below the threshold of 0.5. It goes into the bads list, pure automation, and exactly the channel we broke.

Step 4. Motion. Temporal Derivative Distribution Repair, TDDR, finds the jumps and steps in the signal and corrects them, without you choosing a threshold. Here is one channel around a motion artifact, the step is gone.

Now the pipeline splits in two branches, like in Chapter 5.

Branch A Block Averaging. First, short channel regression. For each long channel, MNE-NIRS removes the part explained by its nearest short channel, the scalp and systemic signal. Then the modified Beer-Lambert Law with a partial path length factor of 6, turns optical density into HbO and HbR. We keep the long channels, band pass filter, cut epochs from 5 seconds before to 15 seconds after each block onset, with a baseline and an amplitude-based rejection. All 19 epochs survive. Average them and there is the classic shape. HbO rises over the motor channels, HbR dips and both head back toward baseline once the block ends.

Branch B, the general linear model. Same steps up to the Beer-Lambert law without filtering. Drop the bad channel. Build a design matrix. The tapping regressor convolved with a canonical response, cosine drift terms, and the mean of the short channels for HbO and for HbR as nuisance regressors. Then one call run GLM. MNE-NIRS uses an autoregressive noise model, so the serial correlations we discussed are handled.

The results per channel for tapping. The strongest channel, S1D1, HbOβ of 1.5 micromolar T equals 15.5 with a matching HbR decrease T of minus 10.6. Three neighbors follow. And the weakest channel, S3D2, which in the simulation only had a tiny response. T of 4.9 for HbO and for HbR a small positive value the wrong sign. A good reminder that HbO and HbR should agree before you get excited.

Notice what the pipeline did not do. It did not choose a threshold by eye, did not delete epochs by hand, did not cherry pick channels after seeing results. Every decision is in the code so anyone can rerun it.

From here the next steps. Repeat the subject level pipeline in a loop over a BIDS dataset. Collect the betas into a table. Fit a group model, for example, a linear mixed effects model with statsmodels, as in the MNE-NIRS group tutorial. Correct for multiple comparisons, and report it all with the code as we saw in Chapter 6.

Quick recap. load, optical density, scalp coupling index, motion correction, then either short channel regression, Beer-Lambert, epochs and average, or Beer-Lambert and a GLM with short channel regressors. About 30 lines of Python, from raw light to a defensible result.

That is the end of the fNIRS series. Thank you for watching and happy analyzing.
