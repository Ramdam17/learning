PLATE 15.17:09

Twenty tests, one false alarm

Each test on empty data is like a twenty-sided die with one alarm face. A tray of twenty tests, tipped 10,000 times, gave at least one false alarm 63.5 percent of the time, close to 0.95 multiplied by itself twenty times. A dead salmon seemed to read emotions. Always ask how many tests were run, including unreported ones.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the mushroom picking. This chapter is about what happens when we run not one test, but many. Picture a forest, and a picker with a basket and a magnifying glass. In a forest big enough, the picker always finds something strange: a spotted cap, a ring, a twisted stem. Today we count how often chance alone fills the basket.

Remember the rule from episode 6.4. A test run at the 0.05 level, on data where nothing happens, rings a false alarm five times in a hundred. One chance in twenty. That is a fair price for one test. But a study rarely runs just one. So we run twenty, on data where nothing is going on at all.

Here is a way to picture it. When nothing is going on, a p-value is equally likely to land anywhere between zero and one. A p-value below 0.05 is therefore one slice in twenty. A test behaves like a twenty-sided die with one alarm face and nineteen quiet ones. A study of twenty tests is a tray of twenty dice.

Here is the first tray. Twenty tests, twenty p-values, and one of them is below 0.05. A false alarm. The second tray: one again. The third tray: none.

Guess before you see. We tip ten thousand trays. In how many will at least one die show an alarm? One in twenty? One in five? More than half? 6,352 of the 10,000 trays have at least one false alarm. That is 63.5 percent. Only 3,648 trays stay quiet.

Now look at how many alarms each tray holds. 3,781 trays hold exactly one. 1,865 hold two. 559 hold three, and the worst tray holds six. On average, a tray holds 0.98 alarms: about one, which is twenty times 0.05.

We do not need the simulation to get the number. We need the complement rule of episode 3.1. At least one is the opposite of none. One test stays quiet with probability 0.95. Twenty tests all stay quiet with 0.95 multiplied by itself twenty times, which is 0.358. So at least one alarm has probability one minus 0.358, which is 0.642. About 64 percent. But read the small print: this exact number assumes the tests are independent. We will come back to that.

Now turn one knob: the number of tests. With one test, the share of studies with a false alarm is 5 percent. Guess before you see. How many tests until it is a coin flip? Each dot on this curve is ten thousand simulated studies, and the curve is the formula. With 14 tests, the share is already about 51 percent. With 50 tests, 92 percent. With 100 tests, more than 99 percent.

Take twenty EEG sensors that all pick up one shared signal, so their results move together. Each test still rings a false alarm 5 percent of the time. But the tests are no longer independent. We simulate it, with a correlation of 0.8 between sensors. Now only 22 percent of the studies have at least one false alarm, not 64. The formula is too high, because it assumed independence.

Look at the number of alarms per study. It is still about one on average: 1.02. Most studies have none. But when a study does ring, it often rings on many sensors at once, and in 446 studies on eight or more. Turn the correlation knob. At 0, 64 percent of studies ring. At 0.5, 41 percent. At 0.8, 22. At 0.95, 12. The risk falls, but the false alarms come in bunches, and a bunch looks like a real effect.

Now a famous case. In 2009, Craig Bennett and colleagues put a mature Atlantic salmon in a magnetic resonance scanner. The salmon was not alive. They showed it photographs of people in social situations, and asked it to say what emotion each person felt. Then they tested every voxel of the scan, every small cube of the picture, for a response to the photographs. They used a threshold of 0.001, with no correction for the number of tests. Their poster reports a search volume of 8,064 voxels, and 16 of them were significant. A dead fish, apparently reading emotions.

With a correction for the number of tests, no active voxel remained. The study won the Ig Nobel Prize in neuroscience in 2012.

We can imitate it. This is a slice of 568 simulated voxels, pure noise, each voxel one test. At 0.05, 30 voxels light up. At 0.01, three. At 0.001, none in this small slice.

This is the idea of a family of tests: all the tests we run on the same data, each asking a question of the same kind. Sixty-four EEG sensors: 64 tests, and 3.2 false alarms expected. Five hundred time points of an evoked response: 500 tests, and 25 expected. Five frequency bands at 64 sensors: 320 tests, and 16 expected. A questionnaire with 20 items: 20 tests, one expected. A drug trial with four outcomes and six subgroups, three age bands by two sexes: 24 tests, and 1.2 expected. A screen of 20,000 genes: 1,000 false alarms expected. These sizes are illustrative; the genome has about twenty thousand protein-coding genes. The rule is always the same: the number of tests times 0.05.

Now the most important question. A paper reports one result, with p equal to 0.027. How many tests were run? Suppose a lab measured twenty outcomes, and reported only the one that rang. Among our ten thousand trays, 6,352 have something to report. But the nineteen quiet dice stayed in the drawer. A family is every test we looked at, not only the ones we report.

So the first question to ask of a result is: how many tests were done, including those nobody reports? Correlation changes how alarms arrive, not the size of the family. And with enough tests, chance guarantees discoveries.

Remember: with twenty tests at the 0.05 level, chance alone gives at least one false alarm about 64 percent of the time, so always ask how many tests were done. Next time: Bonferroni and Holm, the first two ways to count the tests.

PLATE 15.27:22

Bonferroni and Holm

Bonferroni cuts the five percent pie into equal slices: with twenty tests, 0.05 divided by 20 is 0.0025, keeping false alarms at 4.88 percent. Holm's step-down version shares the pie among remaining tests after each find, never does worse, and found more in 5.6 percent of families. Both are too cautious when tests move together: at correlation 0.9, Bonferroni rang only 1.21 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mushroom picking. Last time we saw that twenty tests on empty data ring at least one false alarm 64 percent of the time. Today, the two oldest cures: Bonferroni and Holm. Picture the five percent of risk we allow as one pie.

Bonferroni's rule has one line: divide the threshold by the number of tests. With twenty tests, 0.05 divided by 20 is 0.0025. Cut the pie into twenty equal slices, and give each test one slice. A test rings only if its p-value is smaller than its own slice. In 1961, Olive Jean Dunn published this as a method for comparing several means.

Why does it work? The chance that at least one thing happens is never bigger than the sum of the chances of each thing. Twenty tests, each at 0.0025, add up to 0.05. So the whole family can never ring more than five times in a hundred, whether the tests are independent or not. That promise has a name: it controls the family-wise error rate.

Guess before you see. We tip ten thousand families of twenty empty tests, and give each test its new slice. How many families ring at least once? Without correction, the exact answer is 64.2 percent. With Bonferroni, 488 families out of 10,000: 4.88 percent. The exact calculation, one minus 0.9975 to the power twenty, gives 4.88 percent too.

The price is that the slice gets thinner as the family grows. Twelve subgroups in a trial: 0.0042. Five outcomes in a clinical study: 0.01. Sixty-four EEG electrodes: 0.00078. Five hundred time points of an evoked response: 0.0001. A genome scan with a million variants: 0.05 divided by a million, which is five times ten to the minus eight, the usual cut-off in those scans. All sizes are illustrative.

A common misreading: divide by the number of tests we reported. No: divide by the number we ran, drawer included, as in episode 15.1.

Bonferroni gives every test the same slice, even after some tests have already rung. In 1979, Sture Holm saw that we can do better, with the same promise. His rule is called a step-down procedure.

Here are ten simulated tests, three of them real. Sort the p-values, smallest first. Compare the smallest with 0.05 divided by 10, which is 0.005: Bonferroni's slice. It is tiny, so it passes, and we announce it. Now the pie is shared among nine tests only. The second p-value is tiny too. It passes its slice of 0.05 divided by 9.

The third p-value is 0.00549. Its slice is 0.05 divided by 8, which is 0.00625. It passes. Bonferroni's slice of 0.005 would have missed it. The fourth p-value is about 0.1. Its slice is 0.05 divided by 7, about 0.007. It fails, so Holm stops: that test and every larger p-value stay quiet.

The last rung, for the largest p-value, is the ordinary 0.05. Holm announces three tests. Bonferroni announces two. Each find removes one test from the sharing, so the pie is cut again among those that remain, and the slices grow. I chose this family because it shows a difference. Across ten thousand such families, the two rules agree in 94.4 percent. Holm finds more in 5.6 percent. It finds fewer in none.

Now the power. Twenty tests, four of them real, each shifted by three standard errors. Simulated, ten thousand families. With no correction, we find 85 percent of the real effects, but a false alarm rings in 56.6 percent of the families. Bonferroni finds 49.2 percent, with false alarms in 3.9 percent of the families. Holm finds 50.2 percent, with 4.5 percent. Correction costs about half the power. Holm wins back one point.

Turn a knob: the number of real effects among the twenty. With one real effect, Holm and Bonferroni find the same: 49.2 percent. With four, 49.2 and 50.2. With ten real effects, 49.0 and 52.4 percent, and Holm finds more in 28 percent of the families. The more tests succeed, the more the slices grow for the rest.

Now twenty sensors that share a signal, simulated. Each test is still a fair test, but they move together. Guess before you see: will Bonferroni ring more than 4.88 percent of the time, or less? Turn the correlation knob. With correlation zero, Bonferroni rings in 4.88 percent of the families. At 0.5, 3.56 percent. At 0.8, 1.81. At 0.9, only 1.21 percent. The promise holds, but nobody asked for 1.21 percent. We asked for five.

What does that cost? Put one real effect on one sensor. Bonferroni finds it 49 percent of the time at every correlation. The slice does not move, so the power does not move. I expected it to fall, and it does not. What grows is what we leave on the table. Choose the cutoff so that false alarms happen exactly five times in a hundred. At correlation 0.9, that cutoff is 0.0125, not 0.0025, and the same effect is found 69 percent of the time.

That is the price of ignoring the correlation. Bonferroni and Holm both treat twenty linked tests as twenty separate ones. Episode 15.4 builds a cutoff that uses the correlation.

Here is the strong moment. Holm always does at least as well as Bonferroni. It makes the same promise, and needs no extra assumption about how the tests relate. It costs a few more lines of arithmetic. When Bonferroni is an option, Holm is better.

One last example. A clinical study with five outcomes. These p-values are illustrative. Bonferroni's slice is 0.01, so one outcome passes. Holm lets the first pass at 0.01, and the second at 0.0125. The third fails its rung, 0.0167, so Holm stops. Two outcomes are announced.

Remember: Bonferroni divides the threshold by the number of tests, Holm does it step by step and never does worse, and both are too cautious when the tests move together. Next time: the false discovery rate, a different promise.

PLATE 15.37:10

False discovery rate

The false discovery rate is a different promise: among announced discoveries, few are false on average, without saying which. In a factory of 1,000 ideas with 100 real, no correction announced 143, with 51 false. Bonferroni announced 31 real ideas, while a ramp of sorted p-values announced 79. Over 10,000 batches the false share averaged 4.5 percent. Choose by what a false alarm costs.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mushroom picking. Last time we saw that, with enough tests, chance alone fills the basket: twenty tests at the 0.05 level give at least one false alarm 64 percent of the time. Today we choose a promise to keep, and then a rule that keeps it.

Take the factory of episode 6.3. A lab tests 1,000 ideas, and 100 of them are real. HUNCH believes one idea in ten is real. In this simulation we know which ones, so we can mark them. Each idea gives one p-value. A real idea shifts its test statistic by 3.5 standard errors, a solid effect. Here are the 1,000 tests, with the real ones marked.

First rule: no correction. Announce every idea with p below 0.05. That is 143 announcements. 92 are real, and 51 are false: 36 percent. This is the share of luck we met in 6.3.

Guess before you see. Repeat the factory on 10,000 fresh batches of 1,000 ideas. How many batches hold at least one false discovery? Every single one.

Second rule: Bonferroni. Divide the threshold by the number of tests. 0.05 over 1,000 is 0.00005. The promise is strong: in 95 percent of batches, not one false discovery in the whole family. We check it. In the 10,000 fresh batches, 4.5 percent hold a false discovery. The promise is kept. In our batch, 31 ideas are announced, all real. But 69 real ideas stay in the drawer.

In 1995, Benjamini and Hochberg made another promise. Not "no false discovery". Instead: among the ideas I announce, the share that are false will, on average, not exceed a level I choose, say 5 percent. That share is the false discovery rate. It is the very share we counted in 6.3, and now we control it.

Here is the rule. Sort the 1,000 p-values, smallest first, and give each a rank. Plot p against rank. Now lay a ramp. At rank k, its height is k over 1,000, times 0.05. At rank one, the ramp stands at 0.00005, Bonferroni's threshold. At rank 1,000, it reaches 0.05. Find the farthest dot that still sits under the ramp. Announce that idea, and every idea ranked before it.

In our batch, the farthest dot under the ramp is rank 79, with p equal to 0.0034. So 79 ideas are announced: 72 real, and 7 false. Why a ramp? It is an allowance. The longer the list of discoveries, the more room for a few false ones.

Three rules, the same 1,000 p-values. No correction: 92 real discoveries and 51 false. Bonferroni: 31 real, none false. The ramp: 72 real, and 7 false.

But 7 false among 79 is 8.9 percent, above the 5 percent we chose. Is the promise broken? No. It is a promise about the average over many batches, not about this one.

Guess before you see. Over the 10,000 fresh batches, what is the average share of false discoveries with the ramp at 5 percent? Without correction, it is 32 percent. With Bonferroni, 0.2 percent. With the ramp, 4.5 percent, under the level we chose. The proof gives a ceiling of 0.9 times 0.05, because 900 of our 1,000 ideas do nothing. That is 4.5 percent.

Now the spread. Each batch has its own share. In 39 percent of batches, it is above 5 percent. In 2.4 percent, it is 10 percent or more. The worst batch reaches 16.7 percent, and 3.6 percent of batches have no false discovery at all. The level is an average, not a guarantee for the batch in your hands.

Turn the knob. At a level of 1 percent, the average false share is 0.9 percent, and 51 real ideas are found. At 5 percent: 4.5 percent, and 73 real ideas. At 10 percent: 9 percent, and 81. At 20 percent: 18 percent, and 89. A cleaner list costs real discoveries.

Notice what the ramp does not promise. In 96 percent of batches, it announces at least one false discovery. It never said none. It said few, without saying which.

Tests often share a signal, like neighbouring sensors. Benjamini and Hochberg proved their rule for independent tests. In 2001, Benjamini and Yekutieli extended it to positively dependent tests, and gave a version for any dependence at all, which divides the level by about 7.5 for 1,000 tests. We simulate correlated tests, correlation 0.5 between all of them. The average false share is 3.4 percent, still under 5. The any-dependence version finds 46 real ideas instead of 73. That is its price.

So which promise do we want? Controlling the family-wise error is promising "no false alarm". Controlling the false discovery rate is promising "few false ones among my discoveries", without saying which. We choose by what a false alarm costs.

A screen of 20,000 genes. A false lead costs one follow-up experiment, and a missed gene may be the discovery. The false discovery rate fits: at 5 percent, a list of 500 genes holds at most about 25 false leads. An A/B test of 50 web page variants. Shipping a dud costs little, and most winners are real. The false discovery rate again.

A drug-safety screen of 300 side effects. A missed harm costs more than a false flag, so we accept a lenient level, like 20 percent, and recheck every flag. An EEG map of 64 sensors at 500 time points: 32,000 tests. At 5 percent, the map is mostly right, but we cannot say which pixels are wrong. Later episodes show how to say where.

The final trial before a drug is approved, with four main outcomes. Every claim goes on the label, and one false claim harms patients. We promise no false alarm, with Bonferroni. A questionnaire of 20 items, where the paper will headline the one item that differs. A false headline is costly, so again, no false alarm.

Remember: Bonferroni promises no false discovery in the whole family. The false discovery rate promises few false ones among the discoveries, on average, without saying which. Choose by what a false alarm costs. Next time: the maximum statistic, which counts the tests by shuffling.

PLATE 15.47:09

The maximum statistic

The maximum statistic builds one cutoff for twenty correlated sensors by shuffling. Flip each participant's sign across all sensors together, keep only the tallest bar of each shuffle, and take the 95th percentile: 3.15. Sensor seven, at 3.20, rings while Bonferroni misses it. False alarms stay near five percent, and the smoother the map, the more it finds.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mushroom wood. Last time, twenty sensors that share a signal made Bonferroni too cautious. At correlation 0.9, it rang in only 1.21 percent of the families, when we asked for five. Today we build a cutoff that uses the correlation, without ever modelling it. It is called the maximum statistic.

Here is a simulated study. Sixteen participants, twenty sensors, and each participant's sensors share a signal, with correlation 0.8. Each sensor gets a t statistic: the average over the sixteen people, divided by its own uncertainty. The farther from zero, the more surprising. One sensor, number seven, has a real effect. The other nineteen have none. This is the third draw of its seed, kept because Bonferroni misses it. We measure how often afterwards.

Now we shuffle, as in episode 14.3. We flip the sign of each participant, the whole row at once, so the twenty sensors are shuffled together. Each shuffle gives twenty new t statistics. We draw their heights, ignoring the sign. Then we keep only the tallest bar, and drop it on the curtain.

Shuffle one: the tallest bar is 1.73. Shuffle two: 1.93. Shuffle three: 1.23. Shuffle four: 2.63. Each shuffle leaves one tile, and the tiles pile up into the curtain of the maximum.

Ten thousand shuffles later, the curtain stands. Its 95th percentile, the value that only five shuffles in a hundred beat, is 3.15. That is the cutoff, one cutoff for all twenty sensors at once. Sensor seven stands at 3.20, above it. It rings. No other sensor does. Its adjusted p-value, the share of shuffles that reach 3.20, is 0.047.

A common misreading: the rule only tests the tallest sensor. No. Every sensor is compared with the same cutoff. The tallest bar only builds it.

Bonferroni's slice, 0.05 divided by 20, is 0.0025. On this scale, that is 3.62. Sensor seven is under it, so Bonferroni misses it. Put the new cutoff in p-value language: a sensor rings if its own p-value is below 0.0066. That is 2.6 times Bonferroni's 0.0025.

Why does it work? If nothing is going on, the real data are just one more shuffle. Their tallest bar has the same chance as any shuffle's of landing among the top five in a hundred. That is a statement about the whole family: the chance of at least one false alarm is five percent by construction.

Guess before you see. Ten thousand empty studies at correlation 0.8, a thousand shuffles each. How often does the rule ring at least once? 5.05 percent. Bonferroni rings in 2.13 percent. At the other correlations: 4.91, 5.11 and 4.79 percent. Always close to five.

Turn the correlation knob, as in 15.2. The cutoff, as a p-value: 0.0026 at correlation zero, 0.0034 at 0.5, 0.0071 at 0.8, and 0.012 at 0.9. In 15.2, the cutoff that gives exactly five percent at correlation 0.9 was 0.0125. Here it is 0.012: another test, sixteen people, same story.

Meanwhile Bonferroni's false alarms drift down: 4.81, 3.84, 2.13 and 1.44 percent.

Power: one real effect on one sensor. With sixteen people, this effect is found less often than in 15.2, since the standard error is itself uncertain. Bonferroni finds it 31 to 32 percent of the time, at every correlation. The maximum statistic finds it 32, 35, 46 and then 54 percent of the time at correlation 0.9.

Two limits. With independent sensors, the cutoff is 0.0026, next to Bonferroni's 0.0025. Sidak's sharper formula gives 0.00256. With perfect correlation, the twenty sensors are one sensor in costume, so the cutoff should be a single test's 0.05. Measured: 0.050.

Real maps are smooth in their own way. Same recipe, simulated, and we compare the cutoff with Bonferroni's slice. Sixty-four electrodes on a grid: twice as much. Five hundred time points of an evoked response: 2.1 times. A map of 1,024 voxels: only 1.3 times. Forty frequency bands: 1.1 times. Five hundred genetic markers in linked blocks: 1.8 times. Ten questionnaire items that share one factor: 1.2 times. The gain depends on the map.

How much depends on the smoothness. On the 500 time points, smooth the noise over 1, 5, 20 and then 100 samples. The cutoff is 1.0, 1.1, 2.1 and 8.7 times Bonferroni's. The smoother the data, the further the cutoff falls below Bonferroni's.

One rule from chapter 14: shuffle the whole map together. Suppose we flip the signs of each sensor separately. The twenty sensors lose their correlation, and the curtain is the one of independent sensors. At correlation 0.8, the cutoff falls back to 0.0025, Bonferroni's. False alarms are 2.51 percent, power 33 percent. The data were correlated, but the shuffle was not.

In 1993, Peter Westfall and Stanley Young published this resampling idea in a book, and in 2002 Thomas Nichols and Andrew Holmes explained it for brain imaging. Westfall and Young also described a step-down version.

Here is the strong moment: one shuffle for the whole map. We never wrote down how the twenty sensors are correlated. The correlation is in the data, the shuffle keeps it, and the maximum inherits it.

One caution. A sensor that rings is a sensor, not a region. To talk about neighbours that pass together, episode 15.5 builds clusters.

Remember: keep only the tallest statistic of each shuffle, and its 95th percentile is one cutoff for the whole map. It keeps five percent of false alarms, whatever the correlation, and the smoother the map, the more it finds. Next time: clusters, neighbours that pass together.

PLATE 15.57:29

Clusters

A sensor that rings is a sensor, not a region, so neighbours that pass a first threshold form clusters, each with a mass: the sum of absolute t values. Flipping whole participants, the largest mass of each shuffle builds the curtain. Clusters find broad, weak effects, but rejecting the null does not tell you where the effect starts or ends.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the mushroom wood. A sensor that rings is a sensor, not a region. But real effects are rarely one point wide. So today we test neighbours together. We build clusters.

Here is a simulated study. Sixteen participants, five hundred time points, and a real effect over the points 200 to 259. At each point we compute a t statistic, as in the last episodes. Now choose a first threshold: absolute t above 2.13, the five percent cutoff for sixteen people. Points that pass, touch each other and share a sign form a cluster. This study has twelve of them.

Each cluster gets a mass: the sum of the absolute t values inside it. Take the small cluster at points 106 to 108. Its t values are minus 2.31, minus 2.17 and minus 2.45. Add their absolute values: 6.93. That is exact arithmetic. The big cluster, points 200 to 259, has a mass of 265.95.

Is that mass surprising? As in chapter 14, flip the sign of whole participants, recompute every t, find every cluster, and keep only the largest mass. Shuffle one: 23.2. Shuffle two: 22.8. Shuffle three: 53.2. Shuffle four: 21.9. It is the curtain of episode 15.4, but each shuffle drops a mass, not the tallest bar.

Ten thousand shuffles later, the curtain stands. Its 95th percentile is 63.7. Our cluster, at 265.95, is far to the right. The largest mass in all ten thousand shuffles was 257.7, so none reached it. Counting the real data as one more shuffle, the p-value is one divided by 10,001: 0.0001.

The same recipe works wherever neighbours exist. These maps are illustrative, simulated, with a first threshold of 1.6. A time-frequency map: neighbours in time and in frequency. It forms eight clusters, and the heaviest has a mass of 180.3. A picture, where pixels are neighbours on four sides: ten clusters, the heaviest at 159.9. A slice of a brain image, with voxels as neighbours: thirteen clusters, the heaviest at 118.7.

The electrodes of a scalp, where neighbours are the ones that touch: one cluster passes, with a mass of 10.6. A run of days in a climate record, where a heat wave is a run of warm days: six clusters, and the longest lasts ten days, with a mass of 24.2. Markers along a chromosome, where neighbours are inherited together: nine clusters, and the longest covers twelve markers, with a mass of 34.1. In every map the question is the same: is the heaviest cluster heavier than the heaviest cluster of a shuffle?

A common misreading: the biggest cluster is the best-supported place. No. Mass mixes height and width. A tall, narrow peak and a low, wide hill can have the same mass, and the test treats them alike.

Why bother, if the maximum statistic exists? Take a broad, weak effect: size 0.4 over 150 points, in a simulated study. The tallest bar is 3.45. The maximum's cutoff is 4.79, so it finds nothing, with a p-value of 0.44. At threshold 2.13, the largest cluster has a mass of 74.6, against a curtain at 61.2. Its p-value is 0.0204.

Over ten thousand simulated studies with this effect, the maximum finds it in 32 percent, and the cluster test in 53 percent. On ten thousand empty studies, the cluster test raised a false alarm in 526 of them: 5.26 percent. That is five percent, within what ten thousand studies can tell apart.

But the cluster test does not always win. Take a narrow, strong effect, ten points wide. The maximum finds it in 98 percent of studies. The cluster test, with the same first threshold, finds it in 70 percent. Clusters trade sharpness for breadth.

The first threshold is a choice. Same broad study, three thresholds: 1.75, 2.13 and 2.95. The largest cluster has a mass of 91.3, then 74.6, then only 9.8, because a high threshold breaks a broad effect into crumbs. The p-values are 0.0124, 0.0204 and 0.3732. Two thresholds say yes, and one says no. Over ten thousand studies, the power is 59, 53 and 42 percent.

Each threshold alone keeps false alarms near five percent. But pick the best of the three after looking, and false alarms climb to about 8 percent. So choose the threshold before you see the data.

Here is the strong moment. The null hypothesis of the cluster test is: no difference anywhere. Rejecting it does not tell you where. We simulate an effect over points 200 to 259, size 0.9, ten thousand studies. The cluster test rejects in 9,832. Among those, the cluster lies entirely inside the true window in 95 percent. It starts before the true onset in only two percent, and five or more points late in 30 percent.

With a weaker effect, size 0.6, the test rejects in 66 percent. The median cluster starts 11 points late, and the average cluster is only 32 points long, against a true window of 60. That is what this simulation shows: here the cluster is rarely too early, but often too short.

Look inside our cluster. Of its 60 points, 38 would not pass the maximum's cutoff of 4.90 on their own. Its first point, number 200, has a t of 2.16. Alone, it would not be significant. The cluster test does not tell you when the effect starts. Sassenhagen and Draschkow made this point in 2019.

A caution about formulas. In 2016, Anders Eklund, Thomas Nichols and Hans Knutsson ran about three million analyses on real resting brain scans, where nothing was going on. Cluster methods based on formulas gave false alarms up to 70 percent, instead of five. The shuffle version was much closer to five.

In 2009, Stephen Smith and Thomas Nichols proposed threshold-free cluster enhancement, which removes the first threshold by adding up over all of them.

Remember: neighbours that pass together form a cluster, and the largest cluster mass of each shuffle builds the curtain. It finds broad, weak effects, but it says that something is there, not where it starts or ends.
