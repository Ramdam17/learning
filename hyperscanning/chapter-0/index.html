Two brains, one conversation

Hyperscanning records two brains at once while two people interact, but a single synchrony score cannot say where the synchrony comes from. Simulated pairs with phase locking values of 0.97 and 0.95 arise from mutual influence in one case and a shared beat in the other. A four-branch compass of stimulus, rhythm and movement, mutual prediction and body tracks which sources a design rules out.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Hello, and welcome. This series is about a simple idea with a hard question inside it. What happens when we record two brains at the same time, while two people interact?

For most of its history, neuroscience studied one brain at a time. One person, one chair, one screen. That taught us a great deal. But much of what people do, they do together. We talk, we play, we teach, we comfort each other. So let's get on the road, and look at two brains side by side.

The idea is older than it sounds. In 2002, Read Montague and colleagues linked two MRI scanners through the internet, and scanned two people playing a simple game of deception. They gave the method a name. Hyperscanning.

In 2010, Stephens, Silbert and Hasson used fMRI to record a speaker telling a story, and listeners hearing it. On average, the listener's brain activity mirrored the speaker's, a little later. And the more a listener's brain anticipated the speaker, the better that listener understood the story.

In 2017, Suzanne Dikker and colleagues recorded EEG from twelve high school students during their regular classes, across a whole semester. The more the students' brain activity moved together, the more engaged they were, and the more they liked their teacher and each other.

Here is what such a recording looks like. Two signals, one from each brain. These are simulated, for this video. For a while, they wander on their own. A standard synchrony score, the phase locking value, runs from zero to one. Here it reads 0.08.

Then something changes. The two signals begin to rise and fall together, and the score jumps to 0.95. It is tempting to say that we have caught the moment two minds meet.

But wait. Here are two other simulated pairs. In the first, the two signals really do pull on each other. In the second, nobody influences anyone. Both signals simply follow the same beat. The scores are 0.97, and 0.95. The same kind of number, from two very different causes.

So here is the question that runs through the whole series. Where does this synchrony come from? Two brains can move together because they respond to each other. Or because they hear the same sound, tap the same rhythm, or breathe at the same pace. A single number cannot tell you which.

To keep track, we will use a small compass with four branches. Stimulus. Rhythm and movement. Mutual prediction. And body. Every time we show a result, the compass will light up: which sources are still possible, and which ones the design has ruled out.

The road ahead follows the life of a study. We start at a café terrace, and ask why one brain is not enough. In the park, we take the four sources apart. In the board-game café, we choose a paradigm. On the film set, we record two brains with the same clock. In the laundromat, we clean both recordings in the same way. On the kite hill, we measure the coupling, over thirteen episodes. In the magician's theatre, we meet the traps. At the ball, we learn why the pair, not the person, is the unit of statistics. Then we look at the whole body, we try out models, and we end in a test basin, where we ask whether a study could have failed.

There are eighty-five episodes in all. If you only have four hours, follow the stars. Thirty-four episodes take you from designing a study to reading a paper with a critical eye.

For now, one habit. When you see two brains moving together, ask where it comes from.

Next time, we sit down at the café, and ask what changes when we stop watching people, and start talking with them. Episode 1.1. From watching to interacting.
