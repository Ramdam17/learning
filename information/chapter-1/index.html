PLATE 1.15:04

Random variables · What a distribution is

A jar of pink, blue and yellow candies introduces probability. A random variable is the quantity recorded from an uncertain draw, and its distribution lists each value with a probability between 0 and 1, adding up to 1. The distribution describes uncertainty before the draw, not one candy, and choosing the random variable means choosing the question asked of the data.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the Candy Shop. This is where our series begins with the grammar that every later episode is written in — probability. No formulas to fear today. Just jars of sweets and one very useful idea.

Look at this jar. Pink, blue, and yellow candies mixed together. Close your eyes, reach in, and pull one out. Before you open your eyes, you do not know its color. But you are not completely in the dark either. You know roughly how the jar is filled.

That situation, an outcome you cannot predict exactly but whose odds you know, is what probability describes.

Let's give it names. The act of pulling a candy is an experiment. Its result here the color is an outcome. And the quantity we record, the color written down as pink, blue, or yellow is called a random variable. By convention we write it with a capital letter X and a particular value with a small letter x.

A random variable does not have to be a number. It can be a color, a word, a button press. But very often it is a number. The voltage at an electrode, the interval between two heartbeats, a reaction time.

Now the key object. The label on the jar says, half of the candies are pink, three-tenths are blue, and two-tenths are yellow. That list of possible values, each with its probability is the distribution of X.

Two rules make a list of numbers a distribution. Every probability is between 0 and 1. And together they add up to exactly 1, something has to come out of the jar.

Where do the numbers on the label come from? Imagine drawing a candy, noting its color, putting it back and repeating a thousand times. About 500 draws will be pink, about 300 blue, about 200 yellow. The more you draw, the closer the proportions get to the label. That link between observed proportions and probabilities is what lets us estimate a distribution from data. We will come back to it at the end of this chapter and in a whole chapter on estimators.

Here is the first point to hold on to. A distribution does not describe one candy. It describes your uncertainty before you draw. Draw a single candy and you see one color. The distribution is the shape of everything that could have happened.

Different jars mean different distributions. A jar with only pink candies has no uncertainty at all. You know the color before you look. A jar with equal parts of each color is as unpredictable as three colors can be. Most jars sit somewhere in between. A jar can also hold more colors or fewer. A fair coin is a jar with two kinds of candy, in equal parts. A fair die is a jar with six. The same two rules apply to all of them. Keep this picture in mind. In the next chapter, we will put a number on how unpredictable a jar is, and that number will be the entropy.

Let's bring this to the lab. Take one EEG electrode. Every half second ask a simple question, is the alpha power high or low right now? That answer is a random variable with two values. Over a recording, you might find that it is high 60% of the time and low 40%. That is its distribution.

Now ask a different question about the same electrode. What is the exact voltage at this instant? That is another random variable with a continuum of possible values. Same recording, different question, different random variable.

This is the second point to hold on to. A random variable is a choice. It is the question you decide to ask of your data. And every information measure in this series will depend on that choice.

One more tool because we will need it constantly. The expected value. Suppose each candy color comes with a score. Pink is worth 1, blue is worth 2, yellow is worth 3. What score do you expect on average if you draw many times?

You weigh each score by its probability and add. 1 times 0.5 plus 2 times 0.3 plus 3 times 0.2. That gives 1.7, not a score any single candy can have, but the long-run average over many draws.

We write it E of X, the expectation, and we can take the expectation of any function of X, not just X itself. That small step is exactly how entropy will be defined, as the expected value of a quantity called surprise.

To recap, a random variable is the quantity you record from an uncertain experiment. Its distribution lists the possible values and their probabilities, which are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1. A distribution describes uncertainty, not a single observation. Choosing the random variable is choosing the question, and the expectation is a probability-weighted average.

Next time we put two jars side by side and look at two random variables at once, joint and marginal distributions.

PLATE 1.26:03

Joint and marginal

A table with candy color in rows and shape in columns is the joint distribution; summing rows or columns gives the marginals. Two EEG-heart tables share identical margins yet describe a dependent pair and an independent pair, so the joint cannot be rebuilt from the margins. The relationship between variables lives in the joint, which is why signals must be recorded together.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the candy shop. Last time each candy had one property, its color. Today each candy has two. A color pink or blue. And a shape round or star. Two random variables recorded on the same candy.

To describe them together we need a table. Rows for the color, columns for the shape. Each cell holds the probability of one combination, pink and round, pink and star, blue and round, blue and star. This table is called the joint distribution. We write it p of x and y.

The joint distribution follows the same two rules as before. Every cell is between 0 and 1. And all the cells together add up to exactly 1 because every candy has some color and some shape.

The joint table holds everything there is to know about the two variables together. Every other quantity in this episode and most quantities in this series can be computed from it.

Here is the first thing we can compute. Suppose you only care about color, and you ignore shape completely. What is the probability of pink? Add up every cell in the pink row, pink and round plus pink and star. That sum is the probability of pink whatever the shape.

Do the same for every row, and you get the distribution of color alone. Do the same down every column, and you get the distribution of shape alone. These are called the marginal distributions, because in the old days of paper tables they were written in the margins.

The operation has a name too, marginalizing. It means summing over the variable you want to forget.

With more than two variables, the joint table becomes a cube or a block with many dimensions. Marginalizing works the same way. You sum over every variable you want to forget and what remains is the joint of the ones you keep. This is also why joint tables grow so fast. Two variables with two values each need four cells. 10 such variables need 1024. Each extra variable doubles the table and the number of samples needed to fill it. Keep this in mind, it is the root of many estimation problems in chapter 5.

Now let's take the idea to the lab with an illustrative example. Two signals recorded at the same time in the same person. The first is EEG alpha power, cut into high or low. The second is heart rate, also cut into high or low. Every second of the recording gives one pair of answers.

Here is a possible joint table. Alpha high and heart rate high, 45% of the time. Alpha high and heart rate low, 15%. Alpha low and heart rate high, 5%. Alpha low and heart rate low, 35%.

Read the margins. Alpha is high 60% of the time, 45 plus 15. Heart rate is high 50% of the time, 45 plus 5. A quick check you can always make, each margin adds up to 1. 60 plus 40 for alpha. 50 plus 50 for heart rate. If a margin does not add up to 1, a cell is missing or mistyped.

Now here is a second joint table for a different person. Alpha high and heart rate high, 30%. Alpha high and heart rate low, 30%. Alpha low and heart rate high, 20%. Alpha low and heart rate low, 20%.

Read the margins again. Alpha is high 60% of the time. Heart rate is high 50% of the time. Exactly the same margins as before.

And yet the two tables tell very different stories. In the first, knowing that alpha is high makes a high heart rate much more likely. The two signals move together. In the second, knowing alpha tells you nothing about the heart. Each cell is just the product of its row margin and its column margin. 30% is 60% times 50%.

This is the key point of today. You cannot rebuild the joint distribution from the marginals. Many different joint tables share the same margins. The margins describe each variable on its own. Only the joint describes how they relate.

And that is precisely what mutual information will measure in Chapter 4. How far the joint table is from the table you would get by simply multiplying the margins.

The same picture works for continuous signals. Instead of a 2x2 table, picture a cloud of points, one dot per moment in time, one signal on each axis. The joint distribution is the shape of the cloud. The marginal of the first signal is what you see if you squash the whole cloud down onto the horizontal axis. The marginal of the second is what you see if you squash it onto the vertical axis.

Two clouds can cast exactly the same shadows on the axes and still have completely different shapes. A round cloud and a tilted one. A tilted one and a curved one. The shadows are the margins. The cloud is the joint.

A practical warning before we close. When you analyze two signals one at a time, you are only ever looking at shadows. Spectra, averages, histograms of each channel. These are all properties of marginals. Any question about how signals relate to each other needs the joint. This is also why recording signals together matters. Two signals recorded in separate sessions give you two margins and no joint at all. No amount of analysis can recover how they relate from separate recordings. The joint has to be measured sample by sample at the same moments.

To recap, the joint distribution gives the probability of every combination of values. Summing over one variable gives the marginal of the other. Different joint distributions can share the same marginals. The relationship between variables lives in the joint and nowhere else.

Next time, we zoom into a single row of the table and ask what happens when we already know one of the two values, conditioning.

PLATE 1.36:24

Conditioning · Zooming into a row

Conditioning keeps one row of the joint table and rescales it to sum to 1, giving the conditional distribution and the chain rule. Independence means conditioning changes nothing. A detector firing on 95 percent of true rare events is right only about 16 percent of the time when it fires, since A given B is not B given A.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back to the candy shop and back to our table of two variables. Today we ask what happens when someone tells you one of the two values before you guess the other. That move is called conditioning, and it sits underneath every information measure we will build.

Let's reuse the illustrative table from last time. Alpha power high or low. Heart rate high or low. Alpha high and heart rate high, 45%. Alpha high and heart rate low, 15%. Alpha low and heart rate high, 5%. Alpha low and heart rate low, 35%.

Without any other information, the probability that heart rate is high is 50%. That is the marginal.

Now someone tells you, right now alpha is high. What is the probability that heart rate is high?

Here is the move. Since you know alpha is high, the bottom row of the table is no longer possible. Cover it up. You are left with one row, 45% and 15%. But those two numbers no longer add up to 1. They add up to 60%, the probability that alpha is high.

So we rescale the row. Divide each cell by 60%. 45 divided by 60 gives 75%. 15 divided by 60 gives 25%. Now the row is a proper distribution again.

That is the conditional distribution of heart rate, given that alpha is high. Knowing alpha raised the chance of a high heart rate from 50% to 75%.

Do the same with the other row. If alpha is low, the row holds 5% and 35%, which add up to 40%. Divided by 40%, you get 12.5% and 87.5%. Knowing that alpha is low dropped the chance of a high heart rate to 12.5%.

The general rule fits on one line. The probability of Y given X equals the joint probability of X and Y divided by the probability of X. Zoom into the row then rescale it.

Turn it around and you get something just as useful. The joint probability equals the probability of X times the probability of Y given X. First choose a row then choose a cell within that row. This is called the chain rule of probability, and its information version will appear again and again.

Now look at the second table from last time, the one where each cell was simply the product of its margins. Condition on alpha high, 30% and 30% divided by 60. You get 50% and 50%. Exactly the marginal. Knowing alpha changed nothing. Do the same with alpha low, 20% and 20% divided by 40, 50% again.

That is the precise meaning of independence. Two variables are independent when conditioning on one leaves the distribution of the other unchanged. In that case and only in that case, the joint is the product of the marginals.

For continuous signals, conditioning works the same way. Take the cloud of points, keep only a thin vertical slice at one value of x and look at the spread of y inside that slice. That is the conditional distribution of y given x. If the slices all look like the overall spread, the variables are independent. If the slices are narrow and move around, knowing x tells you a lot.

Now a warning that has caught generations of scientists. The probability of A given B is not the probability of B given A.

Here is a concrete case. A detector flags rare events in a recording, say a particular kind of transient that occurs in 1% of the time windows. The detector is good. When the event is present, it fires 95% of the time. When the event is absent, it fires by mistake only 5% of the time.

The detector fires. What is the probability that the event is really there? Many people say 95%. Let's compute it.

Take 10,000 windows. 1% of them, 100 windows, contain the event. The detector catches 95 of those. The other 9,900 windows have no event, and 5% of them trigger false alarms, 495 windows.

So the detector fires 590 times in total, and only 95 of those are real. That is about 16%, not 95.

What happened? 95% was the probability of firing given the event. The question was the probability of the event given a firing. The rarity of the event, its base rate, makes all the difference.

The rule that connects the two directions is Bayes' rule. The probability of a given B equals the probability of B given A times the probability of A divided by the probability of B. Our counting with 10,000 windows was exactly Bayes' rule, done by hand.

The same trap appears when we read brain data backward. A region may respond reliably during a task. That is the probability of activity given the task. Concluding that the task is going on whenever the region is active asks the reverse question. Its answer depends on how many other tasks also activate that region, the base rate again. This step is called reverse inference. Russell Poldrack analysed it with Bayes' rule in 2006 and showed that on its own it usually provides weak evidence.

Why does this matter for information theory? Because conditional entropy and conditional mutual information, which we will meet soon, are built from these conditional distributions. And because mixing up the two directions is one of the most common errors in interpreting any result.

To recap, conditioning means keeping only the cases where you know the value of one variable and rescaling. The joint equals a marginal times a conditional. Independence means conditioning changes nothing. And the probability of a given B is not the probability of B given A. Bayes' rule and the base rate connect them.

PLATE 1.45:16

Independent is not uncorrelated

Independent variables are always uncorrelated, but zero correlation does not imply independence. A parabola, y equal to x squared plus noise, has a flat best-fit line and zero correlation while y is almost fully determined by x. Only for jointly Gaussian variables do the two notions coincide, and mutual information detects any dependence, straight or curved.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back to the candy shop for an episode that settles a confusion you will meet again and again. Two words that sound alike and are not the same, uncorrelated and independent.

Let's start with the stronger one. Two random variables are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. In the language of the last two episodes, the joint distribution is the product of the marginals for every combination of values. Every slice of the table, every row, looks exactly like the overall margin.

Now the weaker one. Two variables are uncorrelated when their Pearson correlation is zero. Correlation measures one thing only, how well a straight line describes the cloud of points. Positive when the cloud tilts up, negative when it tilts down, zero when the best straight line is flat.

Independence always implies zero correlation. If knowing x tells you nothing about y, it certainly cannot tilt the cloud. But the reverse is false. Zero correlation does not imply independence.

Here is the classic counterexample. Draw x from a distribution that is symmetric around 0. Then set y equal to x squared plus a little noise.

Look at the cloud. It is a parabola. When x is far to the left, y is large. When x is far to the right, y is also large. When x is near 0, y is small.

Now fit a straight line. The left half of the cloud pulls the line down to the right. The right half pulls it up to the right. The two pulls cancel exactly. The best straight line is flat, and the correlation is 0 up to sampling noise.

You can see why with one line of algebra. The covariance of x and x squared is the expected value of x cubed, minus the mean of x times the mean of x squared. For a distribution symmetric around 0, both terms vanish. Covariance 0, correlation 0.

Let's check it on data. On screen, a simulation with 2,000 points, x drawn uniformly between minus 3 and plus 3, y equal to x squared plus Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 1 half. The measured correlation is 0.00 to 2 decimal places. The fitted line is flat. If you stopped here, you would write in your report that the two variables are unrelated.

And yet the two variables could hardly be more dependent. Tell me x and I can tell you y almost exactly. Use the slicing test from last episode. Take a thin vertical slice at x equal to 2, the values of y cluster around 4. Take a slice at x equal to 0, they cluster around 0. The slices move. Conditioning changes the distribution. That is dependence.

So correlation is blind to this kind of structure. It sees straight lines and only straight lines. It is also blind in another way. Correlation can be dragged far from zero by a handful of extreme points, and it changes if you apply a nonlinear transformation to either variable, such as a logarithm. Whether two variables are independent does not change under such transformations.

This is not just a toy. Brain signals are full of relationships that are not straight lines. The amplitude of a fast rhythm can depend on the phase of a slow one, rising at the peak and at the trough alike. A neuron's firing rate can peak at a preferred stimulus and fall off on both sides of it. Arousal and performance can follow an inverted U. In all of these, correlation can be close to zero while the dependence is strong.

Is there any case where zero correlation does mean independence? Yes, one important case. If the two variables are jointly Gaussian, meaning that together they follow a bivariate normal distribution, then zero correlation does imply independence. For a joint Gaussian, all the dependence is carried by the correlation. There is nothing else for it to hide in.

Be careful with the word jointly. Two variables can each be Gaussian on their own and still not be jointly Gaussian. In that case, zero correlation can still hide dependence. Gaussian margins are not enough. It is the joint that has to be Gaussian.

This is exactly the gap that mutual information fills. Mutual information is zero if and only if the variables are independent. It sees every kind of dependence, straight or curved. And for jointly Gaussian variables, it carries the same information as the correlation, with a simple formula that links the two. We will see both facts in Chapter 4.

To recap, independence means the joint is the product of the marginals. Zero correlation only means that the best straight line is flat. Independence implies zero correlation but not the reverse. A parabola has zero correlation and strong dependence. The exception is the jointly Gaussian case where the two notions coincide. And mutual information is built to detect any dependence at all.

Next time, a trap that hides in every continuous signal, a density is not a probability.

PLATE 1.57:20

Densities are not probabilities

A continuous variable gives probability zero to any exact value, so probabilities are areas under a density curve, and the height is a probability per unit that can exceed 1. Rewriting reaction times in milliseconds or EEG in volts changes the height by the unit factor while interval probabilities stay put. This is the root of the unit traps of differential entropy.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the candy shop. Until now, every jar held a few kinds of candy, and every kind had a probability. Today we open a jar with infinitely many kinds, and we meet a trap that sits underneath a whole family of information measures.

Here is the jar. Reaction times measured in seconds in an illustrative experiment. They cluster around 0.45 seconds, with a spread of about 0.05 seconds.

Ask the old question, what is the probability that a reaction time is exactly 0.45 seconds, not 0.451, not 0.4499, exactly 0.45 with infinitely many zeros after it?

The answer is zero. There are infinitely many possible values packed into any interval, and the probability has to be shared among all of them. Any single exact value gets nothing.

So for continuous variables, we stop asking about single values, and we ask about intervals. What is the probability that a reaction time falls between 0.4 and 0.5 seconds? That question has a real answer.

The tool that gives it is the probability density. Draw a curve over the axis. The probability of an interval is the area under the curve over that interval. Not the height. The area.

Two rules, the continuous cousins of the jar rules. The density is never negative. And the total area under the curve is exactly 1.

Now look at the height of our reaction time curve. At its peak, it reaches almost 8. 8. A probability can never exceed 1. So how can this curve reach 8?

Because the height is not a probability. It is a probability per unit of the axis. Here, probability per second. The peak tells you that near 0.45 seconds, there is about 8 units of probability per second. Over a narrow window of 1 hundredth of a second, that gives about 0.08. A probability below 1 as it should be.

The exact value at the peak for a Gaussian with a standard deviation of 0.05 seconds is 1 divided by the standard deviation times the square root of 2 pi, about 7.98 per second.

So a density has a unit. It is the inverse of the unit of the variable. Probability per second. Probability per microvolt. Probability per beat per minute. Height times width gives a pure number a probability. That is why the area is what counts.

And here is the consequence that matters for us. Change the unit of the axis and the height changes.

Take the same reaction times and write them in milliseconds instead of seconds. The curve stretches horizontally by a factor of 1000. To keep the total area equal to 1, its height must shrink by the same factor of 1000. The peak drops from about 8 per second to about 0.008 per millisecond.

Same data, same probabilities for every interval. The probability of falling between 400 and 500 milliseconds is exactly the probability of falling between 0.4 and 0.5 seconds. About 68% here, since that interval spans one standard deviation on each side. Only the heights changed.

Now take an EEG electrode. Suppose its voltage at rest has a spread of about 10 microvolts around zero. Measured in microvolts, the density peaks at about 0.04 per microvolt. Measured in volts, the same curve is squeezed by a factor of 1 million, and the peak climbs to almost 40,000 per volt.

Again, same data. The probability that the voltage lies between 0 and 10 microvolts is about 34%, whatever unit you use. But the height of the density changed by a factor of 1 million.

The general rule is simple. If you multiply a variable by a constant A, its density is divided by the absolute value of A. Stretch the axis and the curve flattens. Squeeze the axis and the curve rises. Area is preserved, height is not.

The same rule extends to any smooth, invertible transformation, not just to changes of unit. Take a logarithm, a square root, a z-score. The density at each point gets divided by how much the transformation stretches the axis at that point. Probabilities of matching intervals stay the same.

There is one object that never changes its values when you change units, the cumulative distribution function. For every value x, it gives the probability of falling below x. It climbs from 0 to 1, it is a true probability at every point, and it has no unit. Ranks are its empirical cousin. We will meet that idea again with the Gaussian copula in chapter 5.

Why spend a whole episode on this? Because in the next chapter, we will want an entropy for continuous signals. The natural first try is to replace the probability in the entropy formula with density values. That gives a quantity called differential entropy.

And differential entropy inherits everything we just saw. It depends on the unit. Change from microvolts to volts and the differential entropy of the very same EEG shifts by a fixed amount. It can even become negative. Episodes 2.6 and 2.7 are devoted to these traps.

There is good news too. Some quantities are built from ratios of densities, and the stretching factors cancel out. Mutual information is one of them. Change the unit of either signal, and the mutual information does not move. We will prove that in Chapter 4.

Here is a small exercise to make it concrete. Take a density of 2 per unit over a short interval of width 0.1. The probability of that interval is 2 times 0.1, 0.2. Now take a density of 0.02 per unit over an interval of width 10. Same probability, 0.2. Height alone says nothing until you multiply by width.

The last practical point, software that plots a histogram with a density option divides each bar by the bin width.

That is why the bars can rise above 1. It is not a bug. It is a density, in units of 1 over the unit of your data.

To recap, for a continuous variable, any exact value has probability 0. Probabilities live in areas under the density curve. The height of the density is a probability per unit, so it can exceed 1, and it changes when you change the unit. Multiply the variable by A, and the density is divided by the absolute value of A. Probabilities of matching intervals never change. This is the root of the traps of differential entropy and the reason mutual information escapes them.

Next time we leave the ideal curve behind and ask how to estimate a distribution from a finite pile of samples.

PLATE 1.67:08

From samples to distributions

A distribution is never seen, only samples. Histograms depend on bin width and edges, trading bias against variance, and kernel density estimates replace that choice with a bandwidth, while the empirical cumulative distribution needs no setting. With few samples every estimate is rough, and plugging rough estimates into information formulas biases results systematically, for instance entropy too low and spurious dependence.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back to the candy shop with an uncomfortable confession. Up to now, every jar came with a label. Someone told us the true proportions. In real research, nobody ever does. We never see the distribution. We only see samples. Today we ask how to get from one to the other.

Here is a jar whose label we will keep hidden for now. It holds a continuous variable, say the logarithm of alpha power in short windows in an illustrative simulation. We draw 50 values.

50 dots on a line. Some regions are crowded, some are empty. What distribution produced them? Draw another 50 from the same jar, and the dots land somewhere else. The crowded regions move a little, and a gap appears where there was none. 1 jar, 2 samples, 2 different pictures.

The oldest tool is the histogram. Cut the axis into bins of equal width. Count how many dots fall in each bin. Divide by the number of dots and by the bin width so the total area is 1. You get a staircase that estimates the density.

Now try 3 bin widths on the same 50 dots. With very wide bins, the staircase has 3 or 4 steps. It is smooth but it hides any detail, two bumps can melt into one. With very narrow bins, almost every bin holds zero or one dot. The staircase becomes a comb of spikes, and its shape changes completely if you draw 50 new dots. In between, something reasonable appears.

This is the first lesson. The shape you see depends on a setting you chose. And there is no free lunch. Wide bins give a stable but blurred picture, that is bias. Narrow bins give a detailed but jittery picture, that is variance. Choosing a bin width is choosing a trade-off between the two.

There are rules of thumb. A common one from Freedman and Diaconis in 1981 sets the bin width to twice the interquartile range, divided by the cube root of the number of samples. More data, narrower bins. But a rule of thumb is a starting point, not a truth.

Now draw 5,000 dots from the same jar. The staircase becomes far more trustworthy. You can use narrower bins without the comb effect, and a feature appears that 50 dots could not show, there are two bumps not one. The jar was a mixture all along. Two overlapping groups of windows, perhaps two states of the participant, each with its own typical alpha power. 50 samples simply could not reveal that structure reliably.

Now let's reveal the label. The true density drawn as a smooth curve, two overlapping bumps. With 5,000 samples, the histogram hugs it closely. With 50, it was a rough sketch, and depending on the bins, it either showed one bump or a dozen spikes.

One more practical note on histograms, the number of samples in each bin matters as much as the bin width. With 50 dots and 20 bins, the average bin holds two and a half dots. Each bar is then a count of 0, 1, 2, or 3, and the random fluctuation of a count that's small is a large fraction of the count itself. The bars are mostly noise.

Histograms have a second weakness. The staircase depends on where the bin edges start, not only on their width.

Shift every edge by half a bin and the shape can change.

The kernel density estimate removes that problem. Instead of dropping each dot into a bin, we place a small smooth bump, a kernel, centered on each dot. Usually a little Gaussian. Every dot gets the same bump, same shape, same width, and an area of exactly one. Then we add up all the bumps and divide by the number of dots. The result is a smooth curve with no edges to place.

But the problem of choice has not disappeared. It has moved. Every kernel has a width called the bandwidth. A wide bandwidth smears everything into one broad hill. A narrow bandwidth turns every dot into its own sharp peak. The bandwidth plays exactly the role the bin width played before.

Rules of thumb exist here too. In Python, SciPy's kernel density estimator uses Scott's rule by default. It scales the bandwidth with the spread of the data times the number of samples to the power minus one-fifth. Again, more data, narrower kernels. Scott's rule is designed for data close to a single Gaussian bump. On a mixture with two bumps, it tends to oversmooth, and with few samples, it can blur the two bumps into one.

There is a third tool that needs no setting at all, the empirical cumulative distribution. For each value, count the fraction of dots below it. You get a staircase that climbs from zero to one with one step per dot. It converges to the true cumulative distribution as the number of samples grows, without any bin or bandwidth. The price, it describes the cumulative curve, not the density, and densities are what most entropy formulas want.

Let's summarize what changes with the number of samples. With 50 dots, every estimate is rough, and the result depends heavily on your settings. With 5000, estimates agree with each other and with the truth. The settings still matter, but less.

Now here is why this matters for information theory. Every information measure in this series is a function of a distribution. Entropy, divergence, mutual information. In practice, we never have the distribution. We plug in an estimate. And an estimate that is rough or that depends on a setting gives an information value that is rough or that depends on a setting.

Worse, the errors do not just add random noise. They push the result in a consistent direction. A histogram built from too few samples looks spikier and more concentrated than the truth. Plug it into the entropy formula, and the entropy comes out too low on average. Plug two of them into mutual information, and you find dependence that is not there. That systematic error is called bias. Chapter 5 is entirely devoted to it, with estimators designed to reduce it.

For now, remember the shape of the problem. A distribution is an idealization. What you have is a finite pile of samples and a choice of tool and setting to turn it into a curve. Report that choice along with the number of samples, and distrust any striking shape that appears only with one particular bin width.

To recap, we never observe distributions, only samples. Histograms estimate a density, but depend on bin width and bin edges. Kernel density estimates remove the edges, but depend on bandwidth. Wide settings blur, narrow settings jitter, bias against variance. The empirical cumulative distribution needs no setting. More samples make every estimate better. And information measures computed from estimated distributions inherit their errors as a systematic bias.

Next time one more assumption falls, the idea that every sample is a separate, independent draw from the jar. In a time series it is not.

PLATE 1.78:49

Time series are not bags of samples

Brain signals are time series, so neighboring samples carry memory, measured by the autocorrelation function. Ten seconds of simulated EEG at 1000 Hz behaves like a few hundred independent samples, not 10,000. Stationarity assumes one unchanging jar and ergodicity assumes one long recording visits it fairly; both are usually assumed rather than tested and shape estimators, statistical tests and time-based measures.

Last stop in the candy shop. So far, every draw from a jar was a fresh, separate event. Draw, look, put back, shake, draw again. Each candy knew nothing about the one before. Today, that comfortable assumption falls because brain signals do not come from a shaken jar. They come as time series.

First, the data we will follow through much of this series. It is a public dataset from Pavlov and colleagues, published in Scientific Data in 2022, and shared on OpenNeuro as ds003838. 86 participants. 64 channels of EEG and ECG, a finger pulse sensor, and pupil size all recorded together. 4 minutes of rest with eyes closed, then a working memory task. Listen to 5, 9, or 13 digits and recall them in order. Brain, heart, pupil, and behavior in the same people. We will come back to it in the estimator chapter and in the applications.

In this episode, we use a simulated EEG built in the same spirit, clearly labelled as simulated. One channel, sampled at 1000 Hz, with an alpha rhythm at 10 Hz, the kind you see at the back of the head when the eyes are closed, plus some broadband background activity.

Zoom in on one-tenth of a second. 100 samples. Now look at two neighbouring samples, one millisecond apart. They are almost identical. The signal has barely had time to move. Look at samples 50 milliseconds apart, half an alpha cycle. They tend to have opposite signs. 100 milliseconds apart, a full cycle. They tend to agree again.

That is the opposite of a shaken jar. Each sample carries a memory of the ones before it. Pull one candy off the necklace and its neighbours come along. The next candy is not a fresh draw from the jar, it is largely decided by the one you just took. That is what autocorrelation means, and it is the normal state of every brain signal you will ever record.

We measure that memory with the autocorrelation function. Take the signal, shift a copy of it by some lag, and compute the correlation between the original and the shifted copy. At lag 0, the correlation is 1. At a lag of 1 millisecond in our simulation, it is still above 0.99. Then the curve oscillates at 10 Hz, slowly dying out. The rate at which it dies tells you how long the signal remembers its past.

Now here is the first consequence. Suppose you have 10 seconds of this EEG. That is 10,000 samples. If you treat them as 10,000 independent draws from a jar, you are pretending to have far more information than you do. Most of those samples are near copies of their neighbours.

Statisticians capture this with the effective number of samples, the number of truly independent draws that would give you the same precision. For the simplest case, a process where each sample depends on the previous one with a lag 1 correlation rho, and if what you estimate is the mean, the effective number is roughly N times 1 minus rho, divided by 1 plus rho.

Plug in numbers. With a lag 1 correlation of 0.9, 10,000 samples are worth about 526 independent ones. With 0.99 about 50. With a strongly auto-correlated signal, most of your data is repetition.

The exact formula depends on the process and on what you estimate. A real EEG is not this simple process. But the message carries over, N is not the number of samples. It is something smaller, sometimes much smaller.

You can see the consequence directly. Take 10 seconds of our simulated EEG and estimate its mean. Repeat with many independent simulated recordings. In our simulation, the spread of those estimates is about 5 times wider than the textbook formula for 10,000 independent samples predicts. That is what you would expect from roughly 400 independent samples, not 10,000. The formula is not wrong, it is applied to the wrong N. Every confidence interval, every p-value, every information estimate built on the raw sample count inherits the same overconfidence.

Second idea, stationarity. A time series is stationary when its statistical properties do not change over time. The distribution of values at the start of the recording is the same as at the end. Its autocorrelation depends only on the lag, not on when you look.

In other words, there is one jar and it does not change. Every probability we write for a time series quietly assumes that there is a single distribution to describe. If the jar changes, if the alpha rhythm is strong for one minute and weak the next, then which distribution are we estimating? The answer is a blend and a blend can mislead.

Real EEG is rarely stationary over long periods. Eyes open and close. Attention drifts. Electrodes dry. Tasks alternate with rest. In practice, we look for windows that are short enough to be roughly stationary and long enough to estimate anything. That tension appears in every chapter that follows. A simple first check is to cut the recording into a few segments and compare their histograms and their spectra. If the segments look like they came from different jars, the single jar assumption is in trouble. And any distribution you estimate from the whole recording describes a mixture of states rather than any one of them.

Third idea, ergodicity. In the candy shop, we could estimate the jar's proportions by drawing from the same jar many times. With a brain, we usually have one recording, one long path through time. Ergodicity is the assumption that averaging a long time within that single recording gives the same answer as averaging across many independent copies of the process. Without it, a single time series could not tell us about the underlying distribution at all.

Ergodicity is rarely tested and usually assumed. It is worth knowing that it is there, holding up every time average we compute.

Let's put the three ideas together. When we write the probability of a voltage, for a time series, we are assuming one stable jar, which is stationarity. We are assuming that one long recording visits that jar fairly, which is ergodicity. And we must remember that successive draws are not independent, which is autocorrelation.

Why does this matter for information theory? Three reasons, each with its own chapter.

First, estimators. The bias of an information estimate shrinks with the number of samples. But it is the effective number that counts. An estimator fed with 10,000 autocorrelated samples behaves as if it had far fewer. Chapter 5.

Second, statistics. The classic trick for testing an information value is to shuffle the samples and recompute. Shuffling destroys autocorrelation. The null distribution it creates belongs to a different, memoryless signal, and the test becomes too permissive. Chapter 6 shows the fix, along with a tool called the Theiler window, which keeps nearest neighbour estimators from comparing a sample with its own near copies.

Third and most interesting, time structure is not only a nuisance. It is information. How well does the past predict the present? How much is new at each moment? Those questions lead to the entropy rate, to active information storage, and to transfer entropy. Chapters 7 and 8. So autocorrelation plays three roles in this series, a nuisance for estimators, a trap for tests, and a signal worth measuring in its own right.

To recap, a time series is not a bag of independent samples. Neighbouring samples are similar, and the autocorrelation function measures how long that memory lasts. The effective number of samples is smaller than the number of samples, sometimes much smaller. Stationarity assumes one unchanging jar. Ergodicity assumes that one long recording visits it fairly. Both are assumptions, and both should be checked when possible. And autocorrelation will come back as a source of bias, as a trap for statistical tests, and as information in its own right.
