Data with three entries

Ten people, twenty channels and one hundred instants make a block with three entries, yet flattening it into a table loses who is who. Two simulated sources are written as products of three lists. Principal components keep ninety-three percent of variance but recover maps only at 0.60, while fitting bricks on the block recovers them uniquely when people differ.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Here is a recording. Ten people, twenty channels, one hundred instants.

That is twenty thousand numbers. And they have three entries: who, where, and when.

Almost every tool in this series wants a table, with two entries. So the habit is to flatten the block. Lay the ten people end to end, and you get a table. It has twenty columns, one per channel, and a thousand rows: one hundred instants for each of the ten people.

The table has forgotten something. It no longer knows which rows belong to the same person.

Today we ask what that costs. And what to do instead.

We build the data ourselves, so we know the truth. There are two sources. Each one has a map over the channels, a curve in time, and a weight for each person. The map says where the source is seen. The curve says when it is active. The weight says how strongly each person carries it.

The two maps look alike. Their cosine is zero point seven. A cosine of one means the same direction, and zero means unrelated. The two curves are unrelated. Their cosine is zero.

Each source is a brick. A brick is the product of three lists, one per entry: a map, a curve, and a weight for each person. Two bricks need two hundred and sixty numbers, instead of twenty thousand.

Then we add noise. It makes up about twenty-nine percent of the block's size.

First, the usual move. Flatten the block into a table, and look for principal components. These are the directions where the cloud spreads most, taken one after another, at right angles to each other. They are the axes of the ellipse of episode eight point one, with twenty columns instead of two.

Two components keep ninety-three percent of the variance. That sounds like a success. And for the question how much of the variance is kept, it is one.

But they are at right angles by construction. The true maps are not. The worst recovered map resembles the true one at only zero point six zero. The components describe the cloud well. They do not describe the sources.

The reason is general. A table written as a sum of bricks has endless equivalent writings. Mix the maps with any two-by-two table, unmix the curves by its inverse, and the error does not change.

Take one person alone. The true maps give an error of zero. Maps mixed by a two-by-two table also give an error of zero. Nothing in one table can tell them apart. A table has no way to say which of the equivalent writings is the true one.

Now fit two bricks directly on the block, without flattening. Fix two of the three lists, solve for the third by least squares, and rotate through the three. We start from ten random starts, because this kind of fit can get stuck in a poor valley. Ten starts are a check, not a proof.

All ten land on the same maps, with a cosine of one point zero zero zero. The curves come back at zero point nine nine eight. The error left is zero point two eight five, ten times out of ten. That is the noise. The error is not zero, and it should not be. A fit that went below the noise would be fitting the noise itself.

What changed? The people do not weigh the two sources the same way. Person six gives the first source one point seven five times the weight of the second. Person two gives it about a fifth. That difference forbids the mixing. Mixing the maps would mix the weights too, and the people would no longer fit.

In nineteen seventy-seven, Kruskal gave a condition under which the writing is unique. Roughly, it asks that the lists of each entry be different enough from one another. The condition is sufficient, not necessary. And unique means unique up to the order and the scale of the bricks.

The idea is old. Hitchcock wrote a block as a sum of products in nineteen twenty-seven. Cattell, in nineteen forty-four, had the idea that several conditions can pin the factors down. The method was proposed twice in nineteen seventy, by Carroll and Chang, and by Harshman. Möcks proposed it again in nineteen eighty-eight, for evoked potentials.

In EEG it has been used on blocks of space, time and frequency. Field and Graupe, in nineteen ninety-one. Miwakeichi and colleagues, in two thousand four. Mørup and colleagues, in two thousand six. Cong and colleagues, in two thousand fifteen. Williams and colleagues, in two thousand eighteen, used it on neurons, time and trials.

Another family, Tucker's, from nineteen sixty-six, lets the bricks combine with each other. It fits better, and it loses the uniqueness. Rigid bricks give one answer. Flexible bricks give a better fit, and many answers.

What does this ignore? Everything that is not a product. A map that moves from one person to the next, for example.

What does it cost? Very few numbers to keep. Take a block with four entries: twenty people, sixty-four channels, forty frequencies, and five hundred instants. It holds twenty-five million six hundred thousand numbers. Five bricks need three thousand one hundred and twenty.

But the fit goes by passes, and you must choose the number of bricks. Here, the variance explained is about zero point eight with one brick, about zero point nine two with two, and then it barely moves. That is the elbow. After two bricks, the next ones explain almost nothing more. A sturdier criterion exists, from Bro and Kiers, in two thousand three.

Now the trap. Give every person the same weights.

Everything above relied on the people being different. Here they are alike. The ten starts still give the same error, zero point two eight eight. But the maps they find resemble the true ones anywhere from zero point four two to one point zero zero, depending on the start.

The third entry carries nothing anymore. The block is a table in disguise.

So that is the moment to keep. A table accepts endless equivalent descriptions. A block with three entries can accept only one. The third entry is not a detail of storage. It is what fixes the answer.

Let us gather what we saw. A table can be written as bricks in endless ways. A block can be written in one way, if the people differ. And you can only see the difference by keeping the third entry.

In practice, three habits follow. Fit from many starts. Compare the errors, and compare the maps. And if the errors agree while the maps differ, do not trust the maps.

PLATE 15.25:47

What a block costs

How many bricks does a block need? A table of two by two always needs two, but a random two by two by two block needs two bricks 78.6 percent of the time and three 21.4 percent. A three-brick block can be approached by two ever-larger, nearly opposite bricks, so the best fit never arrives; watch the bricks, not the error.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we wrote a block of numbers as a sum of bricks. A brick is the product of three lists, one list for each entry. Our block had 20,000 numbers, and two bricks wrote it with 260.

Today we ask the simplest question about a block. How many bricks does it need?

Start with a table. For a table, a brick is a column times a row. Take the column one, two, and the row three, one. Their product is the table three, one, six, two. That is one brick. With two bricks, you can write any table of two rows and two columns. Here the answer is easy to find. A short elimination gives the number of bricks, and mathematicians call it the rank. We drew 20,000 random tables of two rows and two columns. Every one of them needs two bricks. And if you allow fewer bricks, the best approximation always exists. Eckart and Young proved it in 1936. A table holds no surprise. A block of two by two by two holds three. It is the smallest block that is not a table, with only eight numbers. And it already behaves strangely.

The first. Draw random blocks of two by two by two, with eight independent numbers from a bell curve. We drew 200,000 of them. Of these, 78.6 percent need two bricks. And 21.4 percent need three. There is no typical number of bricks. There are two. Kruskal found this split by computer in 1989. The exact share is pi over 4, which is 78.54 percent. Bergqvist proved it in 2013. As a second check, we drew one million other blocks. The share was 78.59 percent.

The second surprise. Take two lists, alpha and beta. Alpha is one, zero. Beta is zero, one. Build a block from three bricks: alpha, alpha, beta, then alpha, beta, alpha, then beta, alpha, alpha. Only three cells of this block are not zero. It needs three bricks, not fewer. And yet two bricks come as close to it as you like, if you let them grow.

Here is how. The first brick is the list alpha plus beta over n, taken three times, and multiplied by n. The second brick is alpha, taken three times, and multiplied by minus n. Expand the first brick. It holds n times alpha, alpha, alpha, then our block, then terms that shrink when n grows. The second brick cancels the first term. What is left is the block, plus a small error.

With n equal to 1, the two bricks are 2.0 away from the block. With n equal to 10, they are 0.17 away. With 100, 0.017. With 1,000, 0.0017. Two huge bricks, nearly opposite, and their difference draws the block.

Now let a program look for the best two bricks. It is the same kind of program as last time. It starts from random bricks, and improves them pass after pass, one list at a time. We ran it once, from one start. We stopped it after 10, 100, 1,000 and 20,000 passes. The error was 0.108, then 0.035, then 0.011, then 0.0025. Meanwhile, each brick grew: 1.8, 2.8, 4.8, then 10.0. And the cosine between the two bricks went toward minus one. A cosine of minus one means two bricks pointing in exactly opposite directions. Here are the values: minus 0.52, minus 0.81, minus 0.935, minus 0.985. The error keeps going down, and the computation never arrives. There is no best answer to reach. De Silva and Lim described this in 2008. With three bricks, the error falls to zero.

The third surprise is about counting, and it is not a matter of patience. Finding the number of bricks of a given block is hard, in the sense of computer scientists. No known method stays fast when the block grows. Håstad proved it in 1990, for finite fields and for rational numbers. Hillar and Lim showed in 2013 that most questions we can answer for a table become hard for a block, over real numbers too. Hard does not mean impossible. It means we know no fast general method. We opened only the abstracts of these papers, not the full text.

Now the cost. Each pass of the fit reads the whole block, once for each entry. For the block of last time, with 20,000 numbers, that is 60,000 numbers read per pass. For a tiny block, bricks save nothing: three bricks of two by two by two use 18 numbers, and the block has only eight. The saving comes with size, as in last time. And one start is not enough. You need several starts, to see whether the answer depends on where you began.

Then the trap. When you fit bricks to real recordings, the program always returns something. It never says that the answer does not exist. An error that still goes down is not a computation that converges. Do not read the alarm on the error. Read it on the bricks. When two bricks grow together, and look alike up to a sign, the fit is chasing a block that has no best answer. Then ask for one more brick, or stop and say so.

To remember. For a table, the best approximation with fewer bricks always exists. For a block, the number of bricks can be two or three, by chance. The best approximation can fail to exist. And counting the bricks is hard.

Composing orientations

Three angles cannot be averaged: two orientations twenty degrees apart average to ten degrees away from the truth when the angles wrap, and pitch eighty-nine degrees nears gimbal lock. Hamilton's quaternions use four numbers, so composing two turns is one product. The price is a sign: averaging with random signs gives a 22.7 degree median error, versus 1.48 when aligned.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A head tracker, a wrist sensor, a camera on a drone. Each one reports an orientation, as three angles: roll, pitch and yaw. Two questions come up at once. How do you chain two turns? And what is the middle of two orientations?

Angles answer both badly. This episode changes the rules, and it costs one sign.

Recall the two orientations of episode ten point four. Zero, eighty-nine, zero. And ninety, eighty-nine, ninety. Same convention as there: fixed axes, x then y then z, in degrees. As triplets they are one hundred twenty-seven point three apart. As orientations, one point four one degrees. Their middle is easy: average the angles, and you land zero point seven seven degrees from the start. Here the habit works.

Now a pair on either side of the wrap. Minus one hundred seventy, zero, zero. And plus one hundred seventy, zero, zero. The orientations are twenty degrees apart. Slide the angles straight from one to the other, and the block turns three hundred forty degrees on the way. The halfway point of that path is one hundred seventy degrees from the start. The true middle is ten degrees away.

One more. Zero, sixty, zero, and one hundred eighty, sixty, one hundred eighty. Sixty degrees apart. The path through the angles runs ninety-three point two degrees, and its halfway point is forty-two point two degrees from the start, not thirty. Three angles are not a vector. You cannot average them.

Angles have a second flaw. Take a pitch of eighty-nine degrees, like our first pair. Roll and yaw now turn the block about almost the same axis. At exactly ninety degrees they turn it about the very same axis, and one freedom is gone. Our two orientations from before would become one and the same. This is gimbal lock. A gimbal is a ring on a pivot. Three rings, one inside the other, give three angles. When two rings line up, the third direction is lost.

In July nineteen sixty-nine, Michael Collins was alone in Columbia, in orbit around the Moon. Houston said: We noticed you are maneuvering very close to gimbal lock. I suggest you move back away. Collins answered: How about sending me a fourth gimbal for Christmas. The ground replied: You were unreadable. Say again please. And he said: Disregard. It was a joke, and Houston had not even heard it.

The fix is four numbers instead of three. William Rowan Hamilton found them in Dublin. In a letter of eighteen fifty-eight, he gives the day: the sixteenth of October, eighteen forty-three, while walking into Dublin, when he reached a bridge. In a letter of eighteen sixty-five to his son, he says he cut the formula into the stone with a knife. That carving is his own account, and it is gone.

The rule is short. i times j gives k. j times i gives minus k. And i times i gives minus one. Order matters, as it does for turns.

Rodrigues had published a rule for composing two rotations in eighteen forty. Hamilton found quaternions on his own, and historians still argue about how close the two rules are. Frobenius proved in eighteen seventy-eight that number systems with finitely many real parts, where you can always divide and the order of grouping does not matter, are only three: the real numbers, the complex numbers, and these.

Here is the object we will keep. A turn by an angle theta about an axis becomes one quaternion. Its first number is the cosine of half theta. The three others are the axis, times the sine of half theta, written on i, j and k. Sixty degrees about the z axis gives zero point eight six six, plus zero point five k. Call it the rotation quaternion. The next episode will meet it again.

A turn followed by another turn is now one product of two quaternions. Four numbers instead of nine. Sixteen multiplications instead of twenty-seven. That holds for composing two rotations. For turning a single point, the advantage disappears: a matrix needs nine multiplications, and the quaternion needs more.

Now chain a million tiny turns, each about a sixth of a degree, in single precision. After ten thousand products, the matrix is off a true rotation by zero point zero zero zero four. After a hundred thousand, zero point zero zero four. After a million, zero point zero four five. Its determinant is one point zero three nine: volumes swell by three point nine percent. The quaternion drifts too. Its length reads one point zero zero zero one, then one point zero zero one, then one point zero zero nine. One division by its length repairs it.

Now the middle. Quaternions of length one live on the sphere of four dimensions, the one we met in episode nine point two. The middle of two orientations is the middle of the arc of the great circle between them. Shoemake brought this to computer animation in nineteen eighty-five.

Here is the card. The object is an orientation. The distance is the angle of the rotation that carries one onto the other, which is twice the arc on the sphere. It ignores the frame, as long as both use the same one, and it ignores the sign of the quaternion. It costs one product and one arc cosine. And its trap is that very sign.

A quaternion and its opposite are the same orientation. Take two orientations ten degrees apart. If the two signs agree, the middle is five point zero degrees from the start. If one sign is flipped, the arc goes the long way around, and the middle lands one hundred seventy-five point zero degrees away.

Test it. One hundred orientations around a true one, each with noise of ten degrees along every axis, and a random sign on each. Average the four numbers directly. Repeat two hundred times. The median error is twenty-two point seven degrees, and the worst is one hundred seventy-nine point one. With the signs lined up first, the median is one point four eight, and the worst four point five.

Markley and colleagues, in two thousand seven, found a way that does not see the sign at all. Stack the outer products of the quaternions, and take the leading direction. On the same two hundred draws it gives one point four eight, and four point five.

Three angles describe an orientation, but their average is not an average orientation. Four numbers multiply, and composing becomes one product. The price is a sign: every orientation has two writings.

Planes and volumes in one product

A rotation does not turn around an axis but inside a plane. With four columns, turning 40 degrees in one plane and 70 in another leaves no fixed line. The product of two arrows holds both the cosine and a patch of plane, and two mirrors multiply into a turn. So i, j and k were three planes; a 64-column algebra explodes in size.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Planes and volumes in one product. Here is a question that sounds too simple. When something rotates, what does it turn around? With three columns, we all give the same answer. It turns around an axis. Last time we composed orientations with four numbers, a quaternion. Today we ask what those four numbers really are.

Take three columns, and turn by 60 degrees in the plane of the first two. The third column does not move. That fixed line is the axis. Every rotation with three columns has one. This is Euler's rotation theorem: there is always a line that stays in place. We checked it on a random rotation, and the line was there. So we say that a rotation turns around its axis. Keep that sentence in mind. It is about to break.

Now take four columns. Turn by 40 degrees in the plane of columns one and two. At the same time, turn by 70 degrees in the plane of columns three and four. Send the point 1, 0, 1, 0 through this rotation. It lands on 0.766, 0.643, 0.342, 0.94. Now look for a line that stays in place. There is none. The two turning angles are 40 and 70 degrees, and neither is zero, so every direction moves. With four columns, there is no axis.

What did we mix up? A rotation does not turn around a line. It turns inside a plane. With three columns, every plane has exactly one line standing perpendicular to it. So we took that line for the plane, and called it the axis. With four columns, a plane has a whole plane perpendicular to it. The shortcut is gone. The cross product, which builds the perpendicular line from two arrows, has the same limit. With its usual properties, it exists only with three columns, and with seven. Massey showed this in 1983.

So we need an algebra where a plane is an object, just like a number or an arrow. Hermann Grassmann defined the first piece in 1844. His product of two arrows gives a patch of plane, with a direction of turning. William Clifford, in 1878, joined it to the product that gives a number. One product, carrying both.

Let us see it. Take two arrows of length one, a pink one and a blue one, 30 degrees apart. Multiply them. The result has two parts. The first part is a number: 0.866. It is the cosine of the angle, the same cosine as in chapter two. The second part is a patch of plane, spanned by the two arrows. Its size is 0.5. It is the sine. Now multiply in the other order. The number is still 0.866. But the patch flips its direction: minus 0.5. One product, and it holds both the cosine and the plane that the two arrows generate.

Now the rotation. Hinge two mirrors at 30 degrees, like a kaleidoscope. In front of them, put the point 0.3, minus 1.2. Reflect it in the first mirror. Then reflect the result in the second. The point lands on 1.189, minus 0.34. That is a turn by 60 degrees, twice the angle between the mirrors. Each mirror flips the picture, and two flips bring it back, turned. The third column, 0.7, ends where it began. So a rotation is the product of two mirrors. And each mirror is one of our two arrows. Their product, in this order, has the two parts 0.866 and minus 0.5.

Those are the two numbers of the quaternion of episode 15.3, up to the sign of the plane. For 60 degrees, its parts are 0.866 and 0.5. The sign only tells which way we call positive. So i, j and k were never three axes. They were three planes. Turn by a quarter turn, do it twice, and you have turned half way round, which is minus one. That is why each of them squares to minus one.

The same recipe works with four columns, or with sixty-four. The objects sort themselves into levels: numbers, arrows, planes, and volumes. Multiply three arrows, and you get a volume, a block of space with a sign, positive for a right hand and negative for a left hand. With three columns, that volume is a single number, and the levels hold 1, 3, 3 and 1 objects. With four columns, they hold 1, 4, 6, 4 and 1. The planes, one for each pair of columns, number 1, 3, 6, 45 and 2016, for 2, 3, 4, 10 and 64 columns. With 64 channels, that is one plane for each pair of channels.

What does it cost? Each column is either in a product or out of it, so every column doubles the size. The whole algebra holds 4, 8 and 16 numbers, for two, three and four columns. For ten columns, 1024. For 64 columns, about eighteen billion billion. Nobody keeps them all. In practice we keep the first levels: numbers, arrows and planes. For 64 channels, that is 2081.

And now the trap. Everything we did here can also be done with matrices. Nothing in this algebra is out of reach of a table of numbers. Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside rewrote the quaternion calculus as vector analysis, and in the eighteen nineties their arrows won the argument in physics. That is the story told by Michael Crowe, in his history of vector analysis. David Hestenes revived the algebra of Grassmann and Clifford, with a book in 1966. Since 2023, neural networks use it too. Brandstetter and colleagues, Ruhe and colleagues, and Brehmer and colleagues. We read only their abstracts, and none of them claims a general gain in speed. So take it as one notation, for numbers, arrows, planes and volumes. Not as a faster method.

One more time. A rotation does not turn around an axis. It turns inside a plane, and the axis is an accident of three columns. Two mirrors multiply into a turn, and the same product holds the cosine and the plane.

When plus becomes minimum

Changing plus and times in a table product turns it into shortest paths: add along a path, keep the minimum between paths. On five places, two squarings give 11 from place one to place five. The same gesture counts walks with ordinary rules, scores best alignments, or sets train timetables. The soft minimum makes kitten against itself negative, so softness is a setting.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Almost every calculation in this series rests on two habits. We store numbers in a table. And we combine them with plus and times.

Chapter fifteen changes those habits, one at a time. The first four episodes changed the table. Today we change plus and times.

The question is an old one. What is the shortest way between two places? We will answer it with a product of tables.

Here are five places, and the direct links between them, with their lengths. From 1 to 2: 4. From 1 to 3: 1. From 2 to 3: 2. From 2 to 4: 5. From 3 to 4: 8. From 4 to 5: 3. Every link works both ways.

A path goes from place to place along links, and its length is the sum of the lengths of its links. The shortest path is the one with the smallest sum. The Graph Theory series searches for it step by step, in episodes 2.2 and 2.3.

Put the lengths in a table. One row for each place, one column for each place. A place is at distance zero from itself. Where there is no direct link, write infinite.

Now multiply this table by itself. In the ordinary product, each cell takes a row and a column, multiplies them pair by pair, and adds up. We change two rules. Where the product multiplies, we add. Where it adds up, we keep the minimum.

Take the cell from 1 to 2, and go through each place in turn. Through place 1: 0 plus 4. Through place 2: 4 plus 0. Through place 3: 1 plus 2. Through place 4 or 5: infinite. The minimum is 3.

The direct link said 4. The cell found a better way, through place 3. Why does it work? A shortest path through some place is a shortest path to that place, followed by a shortest path from it.

So every cell now holds the shortest path with at most two links. From 3 to 4, the direct link of 8 falls to 7. From 1 to 4, there was no link, and now there is a path of 9. From 1 to 5, still infinite. That needs three links.

Square the new table again. Each cell now holds the shortest path with at most four links. From 1 to 4: 8. From 3 to 5: 10. And from 1 to 5: 11, through places 3, 2 and 4.

Lengths are never negative here, so a shortest path never visits a place twice. Five places mean at most four links, and two squarings are enough.

This rule has a name: min-plus. Along a path, we add. Between paths, we keep the minimum.

Now keep the same gesture, and go back to the ordinary rules. Put a 1 where two places are linked, and a 0 everywhere else. Multiply the table by itself three times.

The cell from 1 to 5 reads 2. There are two walks of three steps: through 2 and 4, or through 3 and 4. A walk may come back to a place it has visited.

One gesture, two algebras. With ordinary rules, it counts. With min-plus, it chooses.

Back to episode 3.3, and its table for kitten and sitting. Each cell took three neighbours, added the cost of a repair, and kept the smallest. That was min-plus all along. The snail was multiplying tables.

Keep the cells, and keep the order of filling. Change the two operations, and the same table answers other questions.

Min and plus: 3 repairs.

Plus and times, with every step counting 1: the table counts the ways of lining the two words up. There are 19,825.

Now put a counter next to each length. Keep the smaller number of repairs, and add the counters when two routes tie. The corner reads 3 repairs, and exactly one best way.

Give every repair a score of 0.1, and every match a score of 1, and multiply along a line-up. Keep the maximum, and you get the score of the best line-up: 0.001. Add instead, and you get the total of all 19,825 line-ups: 0.00214.

The maximum-times version is the Viterbi algorithm, from 1967. The idea of keeping the table and changing the algebra was written down for parsing sentences by Goodman in 1999, and for shortest-distance problems by Mohri in 2002.

Min-plus is also called tropical. The name was given by French colleagues of the Brazilian mathematician Imre Simon, in his honour.

In this algebra, a function is a broken line. Take the minimum of three lines: 2x, x + 1, and 4. At 0 it equals 0. At 2 it equals 3. At 5 it equals 4.

With maximum in place of minimum, the maximum of x and 0 is also a broken line. It is the ReLU of neural networks. In 2018, Zhang, Naitzat and Lim showed that a network of this kind, with integer weights, is exactly the difference of two such broken lines. The word integer matters.

Max-plus also schedules trains. Here are three stations, invented for the example. A train leaves only when the trains it waits for have arrived, so each departure is the latest of several arrivals. Latest means maximum, and travel times add.

From 1 to 3 takes 4 minutes. From 2 to 1 takes 5. From 3 to 2 takes 3. From 2 to 3 takes 6. There are two loops. Stations 2 and 3 average 4.5 per link. Stations 1, 3 and 2 average 4.0.

Run the timetable, and the departures settle. Every station leaves once every 4.5 minutes, on average. The slowest loop sets the rhythm. The book is Max Plus at Work, by Heidergott, Olsder and van der Woude, in 2006.

Now the card. What does min-plus ignore? Everything except the best. A cell keeps one length. How many shortest paths exist, and the second best, are lost, and so is the path itself, unless you carry it along. The counter showed that richer entries win some of it back.

What does it cost? For 1,000 places, one product has a million cells, each needing a thousand sums. That is a billion operations. A thousand places need up to 999 links, so 10 squarings: ten billion. And nobody knows a truly faster way. Williams, in 2014, saved a factor that grows more slowly than any power of the size.

If you want the paths from one place only, Dijkstra, in episode 2.3 of Graph Theory, costs much less. The product pays off when you want every pair at once, or when you change the algebra.

Now the trap. The minimum has a corner, and a learning machine cannot slide down a corner. So people soften it. The soft minimum is a smooth version, with one knob, the softness.

At softness 1, the soft minimum of 3 and 5 is 2.873. A little under 3. The soft minimum of 3 and 3 is 2.307, below both, because it counts each route. At softness 0.5: 2.991 and 2.653. At softness 0.1: 3.000 and 2.931.

Now soften the kitten table. At softness 1, kitten and sitting come out at minus 0.78. Not 3. Kitten against itself: minus 2.17. That is not zero, and it is negative. Two lights go out: never negative, and zero only for itself.

At softness 0.5, the two values are 2.45 and minus 0.14. At softness 0.1, they are 3.000 and zero. The smaller the knob, the closer to the true minimum.

This is the trick of episode 6.5, where we blurred the transport plan. It is the same trick, not the same object. And the cheapest road of episode 4.2 can be softened this way too. Cuturi and Blondel did it in 2017.

The softness is a setting, in the unit of the lengths. Write it down.

Remember: one product of tables, and the meaning of plus and times decides what it computes. It counts, it chooses, or it scores. In min-plus, we add along a path and keep the minimum between paths.

The distance of trees

Group five points at zero, one, three, seven and eight and a half by nearest neighbour and the tree's distances make every triangle have two equal longest sides: an ultrametric. It comes from the min-plus product with plus replaced by maximum. A tree always comes out, even for random points, with cophenetic correlation 0.55 for single linkage, so check how much it keeps.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, a product of tables found shortest paths. Where the usual product multiplies, we added. Where it adds, we kept the minimum.

Today we change one rule more, and ask: when is a table of distances a tree?

Take five points on a line, at zero, one, three, seven and eight and a half. Group them by nearest neighbour, as in episode fourteen point three. This is single-linkage grouping, with no other rule.

The first to join are the points at zero and one, at height one. Then seven and eight and a half, at one and a half. Then three joins the first pair, at two. At four, the two groups meet. The merge heights are one, one and a half, two and four. Draw them, and you have a tree.

Now read a distance from this tree. Two points are as far apart as the height where they meet. Take the triangle of the points at one, three and seven. On the line, its sides are two, four and six. In the tree, they are two, four and four. The two longest sides are equal.

Five points make ten triangles. On the line, not one of the ten has two equal longest sides. In the tree, all ten do. This is a stronger triangle rule. A side was never longer than the sum of the other two. Now it is never longer than the larger of the other two.

A distance that obeys it is called ultrametric. The word dates to Krasner, in nineteen forty-four. In nineteen sixty-seven, Johnson showed that a hierarchy of groups and an ultrametric are the same object.

One caution. A grouping tree is an ultrametric only if no merge sits lower than the one before it. Single, average and complete linkage never do that. Centroid linkage can.

Where does this distance come from? From the algebra of the last episode, with one rule changed. A path goes from step to step. Its length was the sum of its steps. Now keep only its largest step. The tree distance is the smallest of these largest steps, over all paths. We call it minimax. In the product of tables, plus becomes maximum, and the minimum stays.

Multiply the table of the five points by itself this way. For the points at one and seven, six becomes four, through the point at three. Multiply the result by itself, and the table is the tree.

We checked it on thirty random points in the plane. The tree distance equals the smallest largest step, and never exceeds the straight distance. Gower and Ross showed in nineteen sixty-nine that the minimum spanning tree holds all that single-linkage grouping needs. Its edges are the merge heights of episode fourteen point three. The Graph Theory series introduces trees in its episode two point five, and builds the cheapest one in two point six.

Episode nine point four tested how tree-like data are, four points at a time. Every ultrametric passes that test. The reverse is false: the five points on their line pass it, because a line is a tree. An ultrametric is a tree whose leaves are all at the same height. A clock, as in the molecular clock of Zuckerkandl and Pauling.

Remember the tree of episode nine point three, with sixty-four leaves, all on the last level. Among them, all forty-one thousand, six hundred sixty-four triangles have two equal longest sides. Among its first forty nodes, which include inner ones, only two thousand, seven hundred fifty-two of nine thousand, eight hundred eighty do.

Here is another ultrametric, on whole numbers. Two numbers are close when their difference can be divided by two many times. Zero is at distance one from one, one half from two, one eighth from eight, and about one thousandth from one thousand twenty-four. Among the numbers from zero to sixty-three, again all forty-one thousand, six hundred sixty-four triangles have two equal longest sides.

Now the card of this distance. The object: a table, read as a tree. The distance: the height where two points meet. What it ignores: everything except that height. Inside a group, all distances are flattened.

What it costs: the table of all pairs, and a choice of tree among very many. With four named leaves, there are fifteen binary trees. With ten, thirty-four million, four hundred fifty-nine thousand, four hundred twenty-five. With twenty, eight point two times ten to the twenty-first. These trees form a space of their own, with its own geometry, studied by Billera, Holmes and Vogtmann in two thousand one.

And the trap. A tree always comes out. How much of the table does it keep? Sokal and Rohlf proposed in nineteen sixty-two to measure it with a correlation, the cophenetic correlation.

Take ninety-six points in sixteen nested groups, with ten columns. It is point nine six with single linkage, and point nine seven with average and complete linkage. Now take ninety-six random points with no structure at all. With ten columns, single linkage still gives point five five, average linkage point five nine, and complete linkage point three seven. With two columns, point six two, point six seven and point five seven. Each is the mean of fifty simulated draws, seed one fifty-five.

The method counts too. Two clouds of forty points, centred ten apart. Their mean distance is ten point zero three. Single linkage puts them seven point eight nine apart in its tree. Average linkage, ten point zero three. Now tie them with a chain of twelve points. Single linkage says zero point six eight. Average linkage still says nine point two two.

No method escapes such choices. Kleinberg proved in two thousand two that no method of grouping meets three simple demands together: the same groups when the unit changes, any grouping possible, and the same groups when you tighten each group and pull the groups apart.

Last, many columns. Episode two point six showed that almost every pair of points is almost equally far. So triangles turn nearly equilateral, with two equal longest sides. Take sixty random points. For each triangle, measure the gap between its two longest sides, as a share of the longest. The mean gap is twenty-two percent with two columns, twelve point eight with ten, four point six with a hundred, one point five with a thousand, and half a percent with ten thousand. In many columns, everything looks like a tree, as Murtagh measured in sparse spaces in two thousand four.

To sum up. A grouping tree is a distance where every triangle has two equal longest sides. It comes from the product of tables, with plus replaced by maximum. What you read on the drawing is that distance, not the distance of your data.

But two methods give two different trees. How far apart are two groupings of the same objects?

When two things cannot be ranked

Scores on several criteria often cannot be ranked: with ten criteria, 99.8 percent of random pairs cannot, and with 100 candidates about 94 are beaten by nobody. Orders have their own algebra. The variation of information gives a distance between groupings of twelve objects, up to 3.585 bits, but depends on the count: one moved object among 1,200 costs 0.017 bits.

TRANSCRIPTEN

When two things cannot be ranked. Last time, single-linkage grouping and average-linkage grouping read the same points and drew different trees. So how far apart are two groupings? To answer, we first need a way to handle things that cannot be ranked.

Start with candidates. Each one has a score on several criteria. One candidate is better than another only if it wins on every criterion. If it wins on some and loses on others, the pair cannot be ranked.

Two criteria make a plane. Put the first candidate at the centre. The other lands in one of four quadrants. Upper right: better. Lower left: worse. The two other quadrants: no ranking. That is half of the plane. With three criteria, a candidate must win on all three, or lose on all three. That is two corners of the cube out of eight: a quarter of the pairs.

Now draw scores at random, independent and uniform, and count. With one criterion, 0 percent of pairs cannot be ranked. With two, 50. With three, 75. With five, 93.75. With ten, 99.8. The formula is one minus two to the power of one minus the number of criteria. We simulated 200,000 pairs for each row, with seed 155, and the formula agrees. With ten criteria, 998 pairs in 1,000 cannot be ranked.

This matters because, to name the best, we usually squeeze all the criteria into one score. Now take 100 candidates, and ask how many are beaten by nobody. On average: 1 with one criterion, 5 with two, 14 with three, 44 with five, and 94 with ten. With ten criteria, almost everyone sits on the front. The best one has dissolved.

An order where some pairs cannot be ranked is an object in its own right, and it has its own algebra. Two gestures stand where plus and times stood. The first takes the biggest thing below both. The second takes the smallest thing above both.

Sets show it, written as rows of switches, as in episode 3.2. One set holds red, green and blue. The other holds green, blue and yellow. Below both: the part they share, green and blue. Above both: the union, four items. The distance is four minus two, which is 2. It is the count of episode 3.2: the elements that belong to exactly one of the two sets.

Divisors work the same way. Order the whole numbers by divides. For 4 and 6, the biggest number below both is 2. The smallest above both is 12.

You have met an order of this kind in the Information series, in episodes 9.7 and 9.8: the redundancy lattice of Williams and Beer. It has 4 pieces for two sources, 18 for three, and 166 for four. Which measure of redundancy fills it is a choice, and episode 9.14 shows what that means for the brain.

Now back to the question left by last time. Groupings of the same objects are ordered too. One grouping is finer than another when it only splits groups. The thing below both is their common refinement.

Take 12 objects. The first grouping has three groups of 4. The second has two groups of 6. To say which group an object is in costs 1.585 bits for the first, and 1 bit for the second.

Their common refinement has four groups, of 4, 2, 2 and 4. Saying which one costs 1.918 bits.

Go down from the first grouping to the refinement: 0.333 bits. Go back up to the second: 0.918. The total, 1.25 bits, is the variation of information. Said another way: how much you still learn about the second grouping once you know the first, plus the reverse.

Meilă showed in 2007 that it is a true distance. Never negative, zero only for the same grouping, the same in both directions, and no shortcut.

Here are its extremes and its steps. All 12 objects in one group, against all 12 apart: 3.585 bits, the largest value for 12 objects. Move one object to another group: 0.571 bits. Swap two objects: 1.08.

What does it ignore? The names of the groups. Rename them, and nothing moves. It also does not say which grouping is the right one.

What does it cost? One pass over the objects, to fill a small table of counts. There are 4,213,597 ways to group 12 objects, and none of them is searched.

Now the trap. The value depends on the number of objects. Move one object among 1,200, in three groups of 400: 0.017 bits. The same gesture, more than thirty times smaller. Other measures count the pairs of objects that two groupings treat the same way: Rand in 1971, Hubert and Arabie in 1985.

Back to order, with money. Line people up from the poorest to the richest, and add up their shares as you go: that is a curve of cumulative shares. Now split a total of 20 among five people, in two ways. First: 2, 2, 2, 2 and 12. Second: 1, 1, 6, 6 and 6. The poorest 40 percent hold 20 percent of the total in the first, and 10 percent in the second.

Two indices say the first is more unequal. The Gini index: 0.4 against 0.3. The Theil index, with natural logarithms: 0.382 against 0.226.

But the two curves of cumulative shares cross. When curves cross, the ranking depends on the measure. An index that looks hard at the poorest, the Atkinson index with aversion 2, ranks them the other way: 0.4 for the first, 0.5 for the second. Atkinson showed in 1970, for distributions with the same mean, that when such curves cross, two welfare functions can disagree.

How common is that? Among random splits of a total between five people, 44 percent of the pairs cross. That is a simulation, with seed 155 and 20,000 pairs.

Every partial order is exactly what several complete rankings agree on. That is the result of Dushnik and Miller, in 1941. And Monjardet, in 1981, surveyed distances between orders.

So, here is what to keep. Some things cannot be ranked, and with ten criteria that is nearly every pair. Meet and join give the order its own algebra, and the variation of information gives it a distance. A single score ranks every pair. But the order it adds does not come from the data. It comes from the weights you chose.

Symbols in ten thousand numbers

Ten thousand random plus and minus ones form a symbol, and two thousand of them are almost perpendicular, with a spread of 0.01. Binding, superposing and shifting build cards such as USA and Mexico, and asking what is the dollar of Mexico returns the peso at 0.23. Capacity is the trap: 200 pairs in a thousand components are right only 8 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, we met things that cannot be ranked. Today, a plainer question. Can a list of numbers hold a symbol? A word, a name, a heading on a card? And can a distance read it back?

You have a card, with headings and values. Name, capital, money. You want the whole card inside one single list of numbers, and you want to ask it questions.

Start with the cheapest list there is. Toss a coin ten thousand times. Heads is plus one, tails is minus one. Each entry of the list is a component. This list is a symbol. Toss ten thousand coins again, and you have a second symbol.

How alike are two symbols? Multiply them component by component, and average. A match gives plus one, a mismatch gives minus one. We call this the similarity. For these lists, it is the cosine of episode 2.3.

Episode 2.6 told us what to expect. In many dimensions, two random directions are almost perpendicular. We draw two thousand symbols of ten thousand components. That makes almost two million pairs. The spread of the similarity is 0.01. The most alike pair reaches 0.053. In angles, all two million pairs sit between 87.2 and 93.0 degrees.

With a thousand components, the spread is 0.032. The largest similarity is 0.164, and the angles run from 80.9 to 99.4 degrees. These are the extremes over all the pairs. They are not the middle ninety percent of episode 2.6.

So chance gives us as many different symbols as we want. Nothing is near. Nothing is far. Every symbol stands alone.

Now we must put symbols together. Three operations are enough. The first is binding. Multiply two lists, component by component. Take the symbol for money and the symbol for dollar. Their product is a new list. It looks like neither of them. Its similarity to money is minus 0.011. Binding hides.

But binding can be undone. Multiply the product by money again. Each component, plus one or minus one, multiplied by itself gives plus one. The dollar comes back, exactly.

The second operation is superposing. Add lists, and keep the sign of each sum. With three lists, there is never a tie. The result looks like each of its parts.

Let us write two cards. The USA card binds name to USA, capital to Washington, and money to dollar. Three bound pairs, superposed in one list of ten thousand components. Its similarity to the money pair is 0.499, and to the two others, 0.508 and 0.499. About one half each. And it looks like none of the nine symbols themselves.

The Mexico card is built the same way. Name to Mexico, capital to Mexico City, and money to peso.

Now ask a question. In 2009, Kanerva asked: what is the dollar of Mexico? We do not ask for a heading. We ask what, in Mexico, plays the part that the dollar plays in the USA. Multiply the two cards together. Then multiply by dollar.

The answer is a noisy list. We compare it with the nine symbols we know. The peso comes out at 0.23. The eight others lie between minus 0.009 and plus 0.014. We measured 0.23.

So the reading is the simplest distance of the series. A product, an average, and the nearest neighbour.

The third operation codes order. Shift every component one place along. The shifted list has a similarity of minus 0.019 with the original.

Now break the list. We flip a share of the components, at random, and compare the damaged symbol with a library of a thousand symbols that contains the original. Flip ten percent: the similarity to the original is 0.80, and the best of the 999 others reaches 0.03. Flip thirty percent: 0.40, against 0.03. Flip forty percent: 0.20, against 0.04. Flip forty-five percent: 0.10, against 0.04.

With almost half the components wrong, the original is still the nearest. No single component matters.

In 1990, Smolensky also bound by a product, but his product grows: each binding makes the list longer. In 1995, Plate kept the size fixed. Gayler presented the family at a conference in 2003, and gave it one name: vector symbolic architectures.

How much one list can hold was worked out by Frady, Kleyko and Sommer in 2018. Schlegel, Neubert and Protzel compared the versions in 2022. We have read only their abstracts.

Eliasmith and colleagues, in 2012, built a model of 2.5 million simulated neurons on this family. And the smell circuit of the fly, described by Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha in 2017, searches for similar odours with random connections. A neighbouring idea, not the same object.

Now the card of this distance. The object: a symbol, ten thousand plus and minus ones. The distance: the similarity, read by the nearest neighbour.

What it ignores: the place of the components. Shuffle the components of every symbol in the same way, and no similarity changes. None of them carries a meaning.

What it costs: ten thousand numbers for each symbol, and a search through all the known symbols at every reading.

And its trap: capacity. Pack a number of pairs into one list, then ask each key for its value among a thousand known values. With a thousand components, twenty pairs are always right. Fifty pairs, 63 percent. A hundred, 25 percent. Two hundred, 8 percent.

With ten thousand components, a hundred pairs are always right. Two hundred pairs, 98.9 percent. Five hundred, 63 percent. Each figure is the mean of ten runs.

Past the limit, the nearest neighbour still answers, with the same calm. Nothing tells you that the answer is wrong.

Laelaps, by Burrello and colleagues in 2019, detects seizures in intracranial EEG. Eighteen patients, 2,656 hours of recordings. After training on one or two seizures per patient, it found 79 of 92 new seizures, with no false alarm, thanks to its tuning step. The false alarms of the methods it was compared with were counted on only twenty hours. And the comparison was made by its own authors.

Chance gives thousands of symbols that are almost perpendicular. Bind them, superpose them, and ask the nearest neighbour. And watch the capacity, because the answer never says when it is wrong.

Which question does your algebra make simple?

The chapter's changes of rules are sorted into established results and untested leads. Established: three-entry blocks in EEG for over thirty years, quaternion averaging, Viterbi decoding, trees on responses to ninety-two images, and information lattices with 4, 18, 166 and 7,579 parts. Leads include hyperscanning blocks, showing pairs that cannot be ranked, and the test of redoing the calculation with an ordinary table.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Which question does your algebra make simple? This chapter changed two habits: storing data in a table, and combining numbers with plus and times. Today we ask where these changes were used, and what they found.

We sort the claims the way a curator sorts a cabinet. A drawer with a pinned specimen and a catalogue card holds a result that has held up. An empty drawer, pulled half open, holds a lead worth trying: no result, only a question. And every study wears the level at which we read it. Some we read. Some only through the abstract, or the catalogue record.

First, the pinned specimens. Start with blocks that have three entries. They have served in EEG for more than thirty years. Möcks, in 1988, and Field and Graupe, in 1991, applied the idea to evoked potentials. Miwakeichi and colleagues, in 2004, used it on space, frequency and time. Cong and colleagues reviewed the field in 2015. Williams and colleagues, in 2018, used it on neurons, time and trials. Abstracts, and one record.

Second, orientations. Markley and colleagues, in 2007, published a recipe to average orientations written as quaternions. Abstract only.

Third, plus and times, changed without being named. Viterbi's decoding of 1967 keeps the best path, so a best choice stands in for a sum. Cuturi and Blondel, in 2017, softened that choice to align signals: this is the soft version of episode 4.2. And Zhang, Naitzat and Lim, in 2018, showed that networks of rectified units with integer weights are exactly what this algebra writes as a difference of two broken lines. We read the last two.

Fourth, trees. Kriegeskorte and colleagues, in 2008, drew grouping trees on the distances between brain responses to ninety-two images, in people and in monkeys. Their tree merges, step by step, the two closest clusters by average distance. We read this paper. As episode 15.6 showed, the tree carries its own distance.

Fifth, orders. Williams and Beer, in 2010, organised the information of several sources in a lattice. It has 4, 18, 166, then 7,579 parts, for 2, 3, 4 and 5 sources. We read it. But there are several ways to measure the part the sources share, and they do not agree. See Lizier and colleagues, in 2018, and Kolchinsky, in 2022. Abstracts only. A much-quoted result on synergy in the human brain, by Luppi and colleagues, in 2022, comes from fMRI. It uses one choice: the minimum of the mutual information. We read it. For what is known and what is not, see episode 9.14 of the Information series.

Now the empty drawers.

First lead. A block of pairs of people, channels and time, for hyperscanning. The third entry would fix the maps. The search made for this chapter found no published example. That is a limited search, not a proof. For measuring a group, see episode 6.12 of the Hyperscanning series.

Second lead. Before drawing the tree of a table of distances, test whether the table follows the strong triangle rule, against random data of the same size.

Third lead. When you compare recordings on several criteria, show the pairs that cannot be ranked, instead of one score.

Fourth lead. Write sensor orientations, and pairs of channels, as planes. The writing is cleaner. We found no established gain in speed.

Fifth lead. Code signals as lists of plus one and minus one, on scalp EEG. The evidence is split. The best known case, Laelaps, uses intracranial EEG. It detected 79 of 92 unseen seizures, with no false alarm once its tuning step was used. The false alarms of the methods it was compared with were counted on only twenty hours, and the comparison was made by its authors.

One question separates the two cabinets. Did the algebra make simple a question you already had? Or did you look for a question for the algebra? A test fits in one line: redo the calculation with the ordinary table, and say what the algebra cost.

So here is what to keep. An algebra is not one more space to store your data. It is a change of rules, and it is worth only the question it makes simple.
