Prior, likelihood, posterior

A belief about a coin's chance of heads is a pile of gems over spots from zero to one. Prior times likelihood, rescaled to total one, gives the posterior, and each toss narrows the curve. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior: ten tosses then ten more gave the same curve as twenty at once. After 144 heads in two hundred tosses, the peak sat at 0.71.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Is this coin fair? You cannot tell by looking, and one toss will not tell you either.

But you do have a hunch before the first toss, and every toss should change it. Today we build the rule for changing a belief, one toss at a time.

Here is a treasure map, and HUNCH holds it. A coin has some chance of landing heads, anywhere from zero to one. Mark eleven spots along the way: zero, 0.1, 0.2, and so on up to one.

Before any toss, we pile up belief on each spot. Think of one hundred gems, shared among the spots. Most coins are nearly fair, so the biggest pile sits at one half, and the piles shrink toward the two ends. In numbers, the heights add up to one. This is the prior: what we believe before the data. These piles are illustrative.

Now the computer tosses a coin. Its real chance of heads is known only to the simulation, and we will find out at the end. First toss: heads.

How much does heads support each spot? If the chance were 0.9, heads is very likely: 0.9. If it were 0.1, heads is unlikely: 0.1. That is the likelihood: how probable the data we saw would be, if this spot were the truth. It is drawn over the spots, but it is not belief. Its heights do not add up to one.

Now multiply, spot by spot. Take the spot 0.7. The prior pile is 0.109. The likelihood is 0.7. The product is 0.077. Do the same at every spot. The products add up to 0.5, not one. So divide every product by 0.5, and the heights add up to one again.

That is the posterior: what we believe after the toss. Prior times likelihood, rescaled so that the total is one. The pile at 0.7 grew from 0.109 to 0.153.

This is Bayes' rule from chapter three, with spots instead of a diagnosis. The sick and the healthy were two hypotheses. Now there are eleven, and soon a hundred and one. Thomas Bayes' essay on this problem was published in 1763, two years after he died, by his friend Richard Price.

Now keep tossing. Toss two, heads. Toss three, tails. Each toss multiplies the curve again, and the curve narrows. With a finer grid, a hundred and one spots, here is the curve after one, two, three, five, ten, and twenty tosses.

After ten tosses, eight heads, the peak sits at 0.65. After twenty tosses, fifteen heads, it sits at 0.67. The width of the curve has fallen from 0.15 to 0.09.

Fifteen heads in twenty is 0.75. So why is the peak at 0.67? Because the curve still remembers the bump at one half. The more tosses, the less it remembers.

Now the strong moment. After the first toss, the new curve became the prior for the second toss. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. So toss ten times, and keep the curve. Toss ten more, starting from that curve. Now start over from the first prior, and use all twenty tosses at once. Guess first: will the two curves match?

They are the same curve. The biggest gap between them is smaller than the rounding error of the computer. Shuffle the order of the twenty tosses, and it is the same curve again. The order and the slicing of the data do not matter. Only the number of heads and tails does.

Now the reveal. Guess first: after two hundred tosses, will the peak sit exactly on the coin's real chance? The real chance was 0.70. After 144 heads, the peak sits at 0.71. Near the truth, not exactly on it. The curve is a narrow hill, with a width of 0.03. The raw share is 0.72, and the pull of the first bump has faded away.

Same picture, new questions. A trial of a new treatment, and we want the share of patients who respond. Earlier work makes us expect about three in ten, so the prior peaks at 0.30. Twelve patients, simulated: five respond. Multiply, rescale, and the peak is 0.34. Five in twelve is 0.42, but twelve patients are few, so the curve stays close to its prior.

A basketball rookie, watched by two coaches. This is illustrative. The first coach has no hunch: every rate is as believable as any other. The second is sure that a rookie makes about half of his free throws. The rookie shoots five, and makes four. The first coach's curve peaks at 0.80. The second coach's peaks at 0.53. Neither coach is wrong. They only started from different priors, and with so few shots, the prior matters a lot.

Now a thousand shots, 751 made. The peaks are 0.75 and 0.74. Two different starting beliefs, the same data, and almost the same curve. How to choose a prior is the subject of 16.3.

A web page. Pages like it get about three clicks in a hundred visits, so the prior peaks at 0.03. Here the grid only runs from zero to 0.10. Monday: 200 visitors, 11 clicks, and the peak moves to 0.043. Tuesday starts from Monday's curve: 300 visitors, 18 clicks, peak 0.050. Wednesday: 500 visitors, 22 clicks, peak 0.047.

Now put all 1,000 visitors in at once, 51 clicks. The same curve, with the same peak, 0.047. Three days or one, it does not matter. The raw share is 0.051, and the peak sits a little lower, pulled toward the prior's 0.03.

A production line. Thirty parts are checked, and one is defective. With no strong prior, the curve peaks at 0.034, but it is wide. A defect rate of 0.05 is still at 91 percent of the peak's height. One defect in thirty is thin evidence.

A participant answers sixty questions in a lab task, and gets thirty-six right. With no strong prior, the curve peaks at 0.60. Six situations, one picture: a prior, a likelihood, and a posterior.

Every one of these curves is a belief, given a model and a prior. How to read it, with intervals and probabilities, comes in 16.2.

Remember: the posterior is the prior times the likelihood, rescaled to one. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. Next time, in 16.2, reading a posterior.

PLATE 16.27:13

Reading a posterior

A posterior curve becomes a sentence by counting slices of a loaf. After fifteen heads in twenty tosses, the 95 percent credible interval runs from 0.49 to 0.82, and the probability of heads above one half is 96.5 percent. It says what people wish confidence intervals said, but only given the model and prior: with a wrong prior, intervals contained the truth 51.9 percent.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A curve is a belief. But a colleague does not want a curve. A colleague wants a sentence.

Last time we built a posterior curve. Today we read one.

Here is the coin from last time. Fifteen heads in twenty tosses, and a prior bump at one half. Its real chance of heads is still hidden from us.

The curve is a distribution, so we summarise it the way we summarised data in chapter two. Its peak sits at 0.67. Its mean is 0.66. Its median, the point with half the belief on each side, is also 0.66. They agree because the hill is nearly symmetric. And its spread, the standard deviation, is 0.09.

Now picture the curve as a loaf of bread, cut into two hundred thin slices. Each slice weighs its share of our belief, and the whole loaf weighs one hundred percent.

To build an interval, we count. Start a knife at the left end, and add up the slices that fall off. At 0.49, the crumbs reach two point five percent. Stop. Now start from the right end. The knife stops at 0.82, with another two point five percent in the crumbs.

What is left in the middle weighs ninety-five percent. That is the ninety-five percent credible interval: 0.49 to 0.82.

Given the model and the prior, there is a ninety-five percent probability that the coin's chance of heads lies between 0.49 and 0.82.

In chapter five, three sentences about a confidence interval were wrong, and the first was this one: there is a ninety-five percent chance that the true value is inside. Here, it is allowed. The credible interval says what everyone thinks they read in the confidence interval.

But read the sentence again. Given the model and the prior. That is the price. Change the prior, and the sentence changes with it.

The same counting answers other questions. Is the coin biased toward heads? Put one knife at one half, and weigh the loaf on its right. Given the model and the prior, the probability that the chance of heads exceeds one half is 96.5 percent.

The simulation's real chance was 0.70, inside the interval. One coin proves nothing, though.

First, the same reading in five other fields. A pain treatment, sixty patients per arm, simulated. Scores drop 1.25 points more with the treatment than with the placebo, with a standard error of 0.37. Most treatments do little, so the prior is centred on zero, with a spread of one point.

Given the model and the prior, there is a ninety-five percent probability that the extra drop lies between 0.42 and 1.78 points. Say a drop of half a point is the smallest that matters. That threshold is a choice, made for this example. The probability that the drop exceeds the useful threshold is 95.8 percent.

Two web pages, two thousand visitors each. Page A gets 112 clicks, and page B gets 121. With a flat prior, there is a ninety-five percent probability that the lift in click rate lies between minus 1.01 and plus 1.91 percentage points. The probability that B is better at all is 72.8 percent. The probability that the lift exceeds a useful half point is 47.3 percent. The loaf straddles zero: not settled.

A lab task, forty people, two conditions. The average reaction time differs by 23.9 milliseconds, with a standard error of 9.0. The prior is centred on zero, with a spread of fifty. Given the model and the prior, there is a ninety-five percent probability that the true difference lies between 5.8 and 40.5 milliseconds. If ten milliseconds is the least this lab cares about, the probability that the difference exceeds it is 93.1 percent.

A production line. Nine defective parts in four hundred, with a flat prior. There is a ninety-five percent probability that the defect rate lies between 1.2 and 4.2 percent. The contract limit is two percent. The probability that the rate exceeds the limit is 71.5 percent: likely, but far from certain.

A poll. Of one thousand people, 545 say yes. With a flat prior, there is a ninety-five percent probability that the yes share lies between 51.4 and 57.6 percent. The probability that yes is a majority is 99.8 percent.

Guess first. Will the usual confidence interval, the estimate plus or minus two standard errors, differ? It runs from 51.4 to 57.6 percent as well. The same numbers. With a flat prior and plenty of data, the two intervals nearly coincide. But they do not say the same thing. The confidence interval describes a procedure that catches the truth ninety-five times in a hundred. The credible interval describes the truth itself, given the prior.

Now the check. Is the ninety-five percent a real promise? Here is a test. DICE draws ten thousand true chances of heads from the prior. For each one, the computer tosses twenty times, builds the posterior, and cuts the ninety-five percent interval. Then it counts how often the interval contains its own true chance.

The first truth is 0.62, with 14 heads. The interval runs from 0.45 to 0.79. It contains it. Each study becomes a patch on a quilt: blue if the interval contains its truth, pink if it misses.

In the first four hundred patches, 378 are blue. Over all ten thousand studies, 9,495 intervals contain the truth, which is 95.0 percent. When the prior is the one that made the truths, the promise holds.

Guess first. Now the analyst uses a wrong prior: sure that coins are nearly fair, with a spread of 0.05, while the real coins spread by 0.15. How many intervals still contain the truth?

The intervals are narrower, 0.18 wide instead of 0.34. And in the first four hundred, only 219 are blue. Over all ten thousand, 51.9 percent. A confident interval from a wrong prior misses about half the time. The ninety-five is a promise made by the model and the prior, and it breaks when the prior is wrong.

How to choose a prior you can defend is the subject of 16.3.

Remember: a credible interval is read directly. Given the model and the prior, there is a ninety-five percent probability that the parameter is inside. The price is written in the sentence.

Choosing a prior

A prior is a hypothesis written before the data, so it must be justified and tested. In a tug of war, a flat prior is worth about two tosses and a tight one about one hundred and thirty. Posteriors meet as data grow, but a Bayes factor never forgets its prior. Simulating data from the prior, like heights of minus 476 centimetres, exposes bad choices.

TRANSCRIPTEN

How do you choose a prior? Last time, a prior that was too sure of itself broke a ninety-five percent promise. Today we choose priors on purpose, and we see what each choice does.

Back on the treasure island, HUNCH holds the prior. A prior is not a fact about the world. It is a statement we write down before the data, so we should be able to say what it claims.

Here is a new coin, simulated. Its real chance of heads is hidden. Ten tosses: heads, heads, tails, heads, tails, tails, heads, heads, tails, heads. Six heads.

Three analysts, three priors, on the same grid as in 16.1. The first prior is flat: every chance from zero to one is equally believable. The second is weakly informative: a wide bell around one half, the prior of the last two episodes. The third is very tight, and off-centre: sure that the chance is near 0.30.

Think of a tug of war. Each prior is a team, and its size is what it is worth in tosses. The flat prior is worth about two tosses, the weak one about ten, and the tight one about one hundred and thirty. The data are the team on the other side, ten tosses strong. A flag on the rope marks each posterior mean.

With ten tosses, the flat prior's flag lands at 0.58, and its ninety-five percent credible interval runs from 0.31 to 0.83. The weak prior lands at 0.55, from 0.34 to 0.75. The tight prior hardly moves: 0.32, from 0.25 to 0.40. Same ten tosses, and the three flags are 0.262 apart.

A hundred tosses from the same coin: 58 heads. The flags are 0.166 apart.

Guess first. With a thousand tosses, will the three flags meet?

A thousand tosses, 646 heads. The flags are 0.045 apart. Ten thousand tosses, 6,532 heads: 0.005 apart. The posteriors meet, because the data outweigh the prior. But a tight prior takes far more data to be forgotten. The real chance was 0.65.

The same pattern on a web page. The real click rate is 4 percent. After a hundred visitors, three clicks. The flat prior says 3.9 percent, a weak prior around five percent says 4.0, and a tight prior sure of fifteen percent says 14.1. After ten thousand visitors and 426 clicks, they say 4.3, 4.3, and 4.7 percent.

But we can judge a prior before any data arrive. Draw a parameter from the prior, then simulate the data it would produce. Gabry and colleagues, in 2019, call this a prior predictive check. A good prior produces data sets we can imagine, with some room for extreme ones, and none that are impossible.

Back to the coin. DICE draws ten thousand chances from each prior, and tosses twenty times. Under the flat prior, 28.7 percent of the data sets have eighteen heads or more, or two or fewer. The weak prior gives 3.9 percent. The tight prior says something else: only 6.2 percent of its data sets have ten heads or more.

Now adult height, in centimetres. A vague prior on the average height of a group: centred on zero, give or take ten metres. Here are five simulated data sets, ten people each. Their average heights: minus 476 centimetres. Plus 365. Plus 1,575. Plus 1,299. Minus 887. Of all the simulated people, 49.9 percent have a negative height, and 31.0 percent stand taller than five metres. Only 5.6 percent fall between one and two and a half metres, a band we take for adults.

Now a weakly informative prior: centred on 170, give or take 30. Its five data sets average between 127 and 212 centimetres, and 98.5 percent of its people fall in the adult band. It does not pick the answer. It only rules out the impossible.

That is why weakly informative priors make good defaults. In 2008, Gelman and colleagues proposed one for logistic regression: scale the inputs, then give each coefficient a Cauchy prior, centred on zero, with scale 2.5. Even a default is a choice, so say which one you used.

A flat prior is not neutral, either. A rare disease: its rate p is the share of people who have it. Take a flat prior on p, every rate equally believable from zero to one. Draw a hundred thousand rates. A rare disease has a rate below one in a hundred, and the flat prior gives that region only 1.0 percent of its belief. It gives 49.7 percent to rates above one half.

Now look at the same draws on the log-odds scale, the logarithm of p over one minus p. They are no longer flat. 76.2 percent land between minus two and plus two, in a bell. Flat on one scale is not flat on another.

Choose instead a prior that is flat on the log-odds, between minus seven and plus seven. It gives 17.4 percent to rates below one in a hundred. Both priors claim to know nothing. One says 1.0 percent, the other 17.4.

So what do we report? A teaching method, with the effect measured in standard deviations of the test score. Twenty pupils per group, simulated. The observed difference is minus 0.28, with a standard error of 0.32.

What is the probability that the method helps, that the effect is above zero? The flat prior says 18.5 percent. A weakly informative prior, a bell of width one, says 19.6 percent. A tight, sceptical prior, sure that effects are tiny, says 39.3 percent.

The sentence for the report: the probability that the method helps is 19.6 percent under a weakly informative prior, 18.5 under a flat one, and 39.3 under a sceptical one. None makes it likely.

The real effect was plus 0.30. This small study drew an unlucky negative difference, and we keep it.

Now the strong moment. A prior is a hypothesis, like any other. You write it down, you justify it, and you show what becomes of the result when you change it. With much data, the posterior forgets its prior. The Bayes factor of 16.5 never does, so there the prior must always be reported.

Remember: a prior is a hypothesis. Write it, justify it, check what data it predicts, and show what changes when you change it.

Sampling the posterior

When a grid is too large, a grasshopper walks the posterior in the dark. She proposes a hop, always climbs, and goes downhill with probability equal to the height ratio. Fireflies dropped in columns redraw the hill. Hop length matters, not acceptance rate, and four walkers with R-hat show convergence. A sampler that has not converged still gives numbers.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time ended with a promise: a walker redraws the curve, because the grid is too small. Today we find out when a grid is too small, and what the walker does instead.

Until now, a posterior came from a grid: a hundred possible values, one height for each, then a sum. That works for one unknown.

One parameter, a hundred values: a hundred points. Two parameters: ten thousand points. Five parameters: ten billion. Twenty parameters: a one followed by forty zeros. Exact arithmetic: a hundred to the power twenty. And twenty is not exotic: twenty electrodes, each with its own average level, are already twenty unknowns. No computer will ever visit that grid.

So we stop computing the whole landscape, and send a walker. Meet the grasshopper, in the dark. The hill under her is the posterior. Its height is just the prior times the likelihood, so we never have to add everything up. She cannot see the hill. She can only ask for its height, where she stands and where she proposes to land.

The coin of 16.3, simulated: ten tosses, six heads, the weakly informative prior. The hill is the posterior of the chance of heads. The grasshopper starts at 0.30. Her rule has three lines. Propose a hop, a random distance left or right. If the proposed place is higher, always hop. If it is lower, hop with a probability equal to the ratio of the two heights. This is the walker of Metropolis and his colleagues, in 1953, generalised by Hastings in 1970.

Step one. She stands at 0.30, where the height is 0.067, measured against the peak. She proposes minus 0.22, outside the coin's range: height zero, so she stays. Step two is refused as well. Step three: she proposes 0.356, height 0.204. Higher: she hops. Step four: 0.534, height 0.992. Higher again, she hops. Step five: she proposes 0.358, height 0.211, against 0.992 where she stands. The ratio is 0.21. Her draw is 0.92, above 0.21, so she stays. Step six: she proposes 0.336, height 0.144. The ratio is 0.15, her draw is 0.04, and she hops, downhill. In six steps, three hops and three refusals. A refusal still counts: she lands in the same column again.

Each landing drops a firefly into a column. After a hundred steps the columns are ragged: mean 0.53, interval 0.34 to 0.68. After a thousand steps: 0.54, from 0.33 to 0.77. After ten thousand: 0.55, from 0.34 to 0.76. The exact grid says 0.55, from 0.34 to 0.75. The fireflies have redrawn the hill.

The length of a hop is a knob. Guess first. If she hops only 0.01 at a time, how many of ten thousand steps are worth one independent draw?

Only 26. Almost every hop is accepted, 97.1 percent, but she crawls, and neighbouring positions are nearly copies. That count is the effective sample size: the number of independent draws that would tell us as much. Now hops of 2.0. They overshoot the hill, and only 6.7 percent are accepted. Ten thousand steps are worth 429. A hop of 0.2, about twice the width of the hill, is accepted 51.9 percent of the time, and is worth 2,141. So a high acceptance rate is not the goal.

Now two unknowns. Twelve simulated reaction times. The unknowns: the average and the spread. A grid of 400 by 300 is still possible: 120,000 points. The grasshopper starts in a corner and follows the same rules. After twenty thousand steps, the grid says the average is 390 milliseconds, from 360 to 421. The walker says 390, from 359 to 418. For the spread, both say 52 milliseconds.

A hiker in fog knows more than a height: she can feel which way the ground tilts. Modern samplers use that slope to make longer hops that are still accepted. Duane and colleagues proposed it for physics in 1987, under the name hybrid Monte Carlo. Today it is called Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Hoffman and Gelman made a version that tunes itself, in 2014: the No-U-Turn Sampler. The code bonus runs one, with PyMC.

How do we know she walked long enough? Send four walkers from four different places, hopping 0.05 at a time. After sixty steps, they have not met. One walker alone can look calm: only the others show it is wrong. An indicator called R-hat compares the spread between the walkers with the spread inside each. Near one, they agree. Here it is 1.74.

The sampler's answers after sixty steps: mean 0.47, interval 0.11 to 0.88, and a 45.0 percent chance that the coin favours heads. The exact answers: 0.55, from 0.34 to 0.75, and 66.9 percent. A sampler that has not converged still gives numbers. After ten thousand steps each, R-hat is 1.004, and the answers agree: 0.55, from 0.34 to 0.74, and 67.0 percent.

The version computed here is the rank-normalised split R-hat of Vehtari and colleagues, 2021. They ask for a value below 1.01, with at least four chains. The older rule, from Gelman and Rubin in 1992, was looser: 1.1. Our value hovers at that line for a while, and is clearly below it only at ten thousand steps.

Now the strong moment. We never computed the posterior. We drew samples from it, and everything we say about it is a count. The teaching method of 16.3, with the weakly informative prior: the grid said the chance that the effect is above zero is 19.6 percent. The walker took a hundred thousand steps, and 19,610 of them were above zero: 19.6 percent. The two agree up to the noise of counting: another walker moves such a share by about a third of a point.

Remember: a walker draws samples from the posterior, and what we read from them is a count. Check the hop length and the agreement of several chains, because a sampler that has not converged still gives numbers.

PLATE 16.57:06

Bayes factors

A Bayes factor compares how well two hypotheses predicted the data, like two stage lamps each holding one hundred percent of the light. Fifteen heads in twenty tosses gives 3.2 for the alternative, and the factor can favour the null. A wider alternative shrinks it, from 2.7 to 0.11, so the prior is part of the prediction.

TRANSCRIPTEN

A p-value can tell you that your data would be rare if nothing were going on. It can never tell you that nothing is going on. Today we meet a tool that can.

Back in the treasure hunt, two hypotheses are standing by. NIL is the null: nothing is going on. HUNCH holds the alternative: something is, and here is roughly how big. We ask both the same question. Before the data arrived, how well did you predict what we saw?

Think of a stage lamp. Each hypothesis gets one lamp, with a fixed amount of light: one hundred percent. Before the data are revealed, it must aim that light at the possible outcomes. Then one outcome lights up. The more light a lamp shone there, the better its hypothesis predicted.

Here is the coin from the last two episodes: fifteen heads in twenty tosses. The possible outcomes are the counts of heads, from zero to twenty. NIL says the coin is fair, so its lamp shines mostly near ten heads. At fifteen heads, it shines 1.48 percent.

The alternative says only that the chance of heads is unknown, and every chance from zero to one is equally believable. That is a flat prior. Spread over all those chances, every count from zero to twenty gets the same light: one in twenty-one. At fifteen heads, it shines 4.76 percent.

Now divide. 4.76 over 1.48 is 3.2. That is the Bayes factor. The data are 3.2 times more probable under the alternative than under the null.

This factor turns your odds before the data into your odds after. If you thought the two were equally likely, you now favour the alternative about three to one. For this coin, the p-value is 0.041, just under 0.05. A factor of 3.2 shows how much evidence that really is: some, but not much.

How big is big? In 1995, Kass and Raftery published a reading scale, adapted from Jeffreys. From one to three: not worth more than a bare mention. From three to twenty: positive. From twenty to one hundred fifty: strong. Above one hundred fifty: very strong. Our 3.2 just clears the first step.

A drug against a placebo. One hundred matched pairs, simulated, and in each pair we ask which patient improved more. In 69 pairs, the drug patient did. The null says each pair is a coin flip, and sixty-nine out of a hundred is rare: 0.00523 percent. The flat alternative spreads its light over a hundred and one counts, and gives 0.99 percent. The data are 189 times more probable under the alternative. Very strong. The p-value is 0.0002.

Now the case a p-value cannot handle. A web page change that does nothing. A hundred thousand visitors saw each version, simulated. The new page got 5,022 clicks, the old one 5,079. The lift is minus 0.057 points, with a standard error of 0.098. The p-value is 0.56: not significant. That only says we could not reject the null. It does not say the page does nothing.

The Bayes factor can say it. NIL predicted a lift close to zero, and the lift we saw sits right there: height 3.439. The alternative says changes like this rarely move clicks by more than a point, so it spreads its light from minus three to plus three, and gives 0.396. The data are 8.7 times more probable under the null than under the alternative. Positive evidence for nothing. A p-value cannot say that.

Now a twist. A reaction-time task, a hundred people, each tested in two conditions, simulated. The average difference is 10.9 milliseconds, with a standard error of 5. The p-value is 0.029: significant. The alternative says the true difference is centred on zero, with some spread. How wide? Try 15 milliseconds. The factor for the alternative is 2.7.

Now widen it: 50 milliseconds. The factor falls to 1.04. At 150, it is 0.36. At 500 milliseconds, it is 0.11. A wider alternative must cover bigger effects, so at our small difference its lamp shines less and less. At 500, the data are 9.3 times more probable under the null.

The p-value never moved. A significant result, and a factor that favours the null. It looks like a contradiction, but the two answer different questions. The p-value asks whether the data are rare if the null is true. It never looks at the rival. The Bayes factor asks which hypothesis predicted the data better, and a very wide alternative predicted enormous effects that nobody believes. Dennis Lindley described this in 1957: with a point null and a spread-out alternative, a result can be significant at five percent while the data favour the null. For these data, no width gives a factor above about three.

To avoid choosing a width by hand, Rouder and colleagues proposed in 2009 a default prior for the t-test, a Cauchy curve on the standardised effect size. A default is still a prior, so report which one you used.

Now the strong moment. Back to the web page, with three alternatives, spreads of 1, 3 and 10 points. First the posterior, as in 16.1. All three curves coincide, with a mean of minus 0.06. With plenty of data, posteriors from different reasonable priors come together. Now the Bayes factors for the null, under the same three priors: 8.7, 25.9, and 86.2. The posterior forgot the prior. The Bayes factor never does, because it is a prediction, and a prediction is made of the prior.

A gene variant. A hundred heterozygous parents each have an affected child, and the variant was passed on 62 times, simulated. The null says passing it on is a coin flip. With the flat alternative, the p-value is 0.021, and the factor is only 2.2: not worth more than a bare mention. But a flat prior says a gene could easily be passed on nine times in ten. Real gene effects are small. An alternative with a modest spread of 0.05 around one half gives 3.0, from the same data. Neither number is the answer. Each is a prediction, and you must say which one you made.

Remember: a Bayes factor compares how well two hypotheses predicted the data, and it can favour the null. The prior is part of the prediction, so report it. Next time: hierarchical models.

PLATE 16.67:17

Hierarchical models

Eight schools estimated SAT coaching gains. Estimating each alone ignores what they share, while one pooled value treats them as identical. A hierarchical model draws each effect from a common curve with width tau, set by the data. School A moves from 28 to 11.4. Groups borrow from one another, the least certain most, and together beat alone in simulation.

Last time ended with a promise: hierarchical models. Today, the last episode of the treasure hunt, groups borrow from one another. Estimating them together can beat estimating them alone.

Here is the most famous case. Eight American schools each ran a coaching programme for the SAT, a college entrance test, and each estimated its gain in points. Rubin published the eight results in 1981. This is real data. School A gained 28 points, with a standard error of 15. School C seems to have lost 3, with a standard error of 16. Each bar is a 95 percent interval, and all eight contain zero. Are the programmes really different, or is it mostly noise?

Three ways to answer. The first: each school alone. This is no pooling, as in 11.4. The second: lump all eight together, complete pooling. We average the estimates, with more weight on the precise schools. A school's weight is one over its squared standard error: one over 225 for school A. In thousandths, the weights add up to 60.3, and the weighted estimates add up to 463.5. Divide: 7.7 points, for every school. No pooling believes the schools have nothing in common. Complete pooling believes they are identical. The data never said either.

The third analysis takes the middle road. Each school's true effect is drawn from a common curve, centred on an overall level, with a width called tau. Small tau: the schools are nearly alike. Large tau: nearly unrelated. HUNCH holds the curve. The width is unknown too, so tau gets its own prior, a flat one, a common default for this very example. Is it a good one? That is 16.3.

Turn the dial. With tau at 1, the eight estimates collapse to about 7.7. At 5, they spread a little. At 25, each stays close to its own estimate. Who sets the dial? The data do. Here is the posterior of tau, computed on a grid as in 16.1, so exact. It is highest at zero, and falls slowly. The median is 5.2, and the 95 percent credible interval runs from 0.2 to 20.7. About 10 percent of the mass lies below 1, and 20 percent below 2.

Average over every plausible tau, and the third analysis is done. School A moves from 28 to 11.4. School C moves from minus 3 to 6.1. The common level is now 7.9, not 7.7: with tau in play the weights shift a little, as in 11.4. Who moves most? In points, school A, by 16.6, because it started farthest out. But the biggest share of the way goes to school H, which has the largest standard error: 86 percent. And school C, the lowest, now passes school E: its estimate is less certain, so it is pulled harder.

Shrinkage does not say school A's programme failed. It says 28 was partly luck. Two numbers may look contradictory: the raw estimates spread by 10.4, yet tau has a median of 5.2. The raw spread includes noise, and tau tries to take it out.

In 1977, Efron and Morris explained in Scientific American a result that Charles Stein had found in 1955. With three or more averages, estimating them together beats estimating them separately, whatever the true values. Their example: 18 baseball players, after 45 times at bat each. Shrinking every average about 80 percent of the way to the overall average cut the total squared error by a factor of 3.5. We test it by simulation.

Eight groups. True means drawn around 50, with a spread of 10. Each is estimated with a standard error of 10. Estimate each alone, or all together with the hierarchical model. Square the eight misses and add them up. Repeat 10,000 times. Alone: 803 on average. Together: 527. Together wins in 78 percent of the repetitions, and loses in 22.

Guess before you see: what if the true means differ a lot? Spreads of 0, 5, 10, 20 and 40. Together: 140, 267, 527, 733 and 773, against about 800 alone. Together wins in 100, 98, 78, 68 and 62 percent. The gain is large when the groups are alike, small when they differ, and never negative on average. Complete pooling is not so lucky: at a spread of 40, its error is 11,291.

Now many small groups. A reaction-time study, simulated: 40 participants, with 3 to 72 trials each, and 100 milliseconds of noise per trial. The model pulls the small groups most. Over 2,000 repetitions: for 3 to 6 trials, the typical miss falls from 48.9 to 31.7 milliseconds, and they are pulled 58 percent of the way. Few trials, big gain. Many trials, the methods agree.

The same pattern wears other clothes. Hospital death rates: 30 hospitals, from 22 to 1,926 patients. For the smallest third, the typical miss falls from 4.8 to 1.9 percentage points, and they are pulled 83 percent of the way, against 22 for the largest third. Players early in a season, with 10 to 75 times at bat: the miss falls from 11.7 to 3.1 percentage points of batting average. All simulated, 2,000 repetitions each. The studies of a meta-analysis are schools too, in 18.3.

Now the strong moment. Here is 11.4: 18 people, their slopes sorted by number of trials, the pink post at the average slope. White diamonds are separate estimates, coloured ones come from the mixed model. Beside it, the eight schools in the same picture, sorted by standard error. Same picture, same model. In 11.4, a person's slope was the average slope plus their own departure. Here, a school's effect is an overall level plus its own departure, with a width, tau. Write the mixed model with priors on the average and on the width, and you have the hierarchical model. Each group borrows precision from the others, and how much it borrows is estimated from the data, not chosen.

Two limits. With few groups the width is poorly known, and the answer leans on the prior. And the groups must be exchangeable: if school A were a different kind of school, pulling it toward the others would mislead. A package such as PyMC does the sampling of 16.4, and the code bonus does the eight schools.

Remember: a hierarchical model estimates each group and the spread between groups together, so every group borrows from the others, the least certain most. Next time: every measurement has an error.
