Predictive coding

In predictive coding, each cortical level sends a prediction down and receives only the error back up, so a good prediction silences the error. Rao and Ballard's network explained end-stopping this way, and the mismatch negativity fits the idea but also an adaptation account. Because the loop predicts the input itself, it resembles a painter more than JEPA, and the evidence is real but contested.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the cabinet of curiosities. Each display case holds one idea from neuroscience, and each case carries two labels. One says analogy. The other says mechanism shown. Our guide is BRAIN, and the rule of this chapter is simple: no analogy is ever presented as proof. Today's case holds predictive coding. By the end of this video, you will know what each level of the hierarchy sends and receives, what the mismatch negativity does and does not show, and why this idea looks more like a painter than like JEPA.

Start with the idea. Picture the cortex as a stack of levels. Each level sends a prediction down to the level below it. The level below compares that prediction with what it actually has. What is left over is the error, and the error travels up. Prediction goes down, error comes up. Each level passes on only the surprise, and the level above uses it to correct its next prediction.

Here is a toy, labelled illustrative, and computed before this video was made. The input is 0.8. The first prediction is 0.2, so the error is 0.6. The level corrects its prediction by half of the error. Now the prediction is 0.5, and the error is 0.3. Then 0.65, and an error of 0.15. Then 0.725, and an error of 0.075. A good prediction silences the error. Now the input suddenly changes. The old prediction is wrong again, and the error jumps back up. Keep that picture in mind.

This is the model of Rao and Ballard, published in Nature Neuroscience in January 1999. In their network, feedback connections from a higher level carry predictions of the activity of the level below. Feedforward connections carry the residual errors. They exposed this hierarchical network to natural images. It developed receptive fields like those of simple cells in the visual cortex. Then they looked at the neurons that carry the errors.

Those neurons showed end-stopping, and other extra-classical receptive-field effects. A classical receptive field is the patch where a stimulus drives a cell. An end-stopped cell responds more as a bar grows longer, up to an optimum length, and then responds less as the bar grows longer still. Extra-classical effects come from outside the classical patch. The paper's proposal is that such surround effects may not be only feedforward. They may also come from feedback, as a consequence of an efficient hierarchical way to encode natural images. That is the mechanism the model shows, in a model.

Now an example familiar from the EEG series, episode 8.2: the mismatch negativity. Play a stream of identical tones, the standards. Now and then, slip in a different tone, the deviant. That is an oddball paradigm. Subtract the average response to the standards from the response to the deviants. What remains is a negative wave over fronto-central electrodes. It peaks about one hundred to two hundred and fifty milliseconds after the change begins, and the latency shifts a little with the kind of change: frequency, duration, intensity, or timing. That wave is the mismatch negativity, or MMN.

The predictive coding reading is natural. The standards taught the brain what to expect. The deviant breaks the expectation, and the wave is the prediction error. Garrido, Friston and colleagues made this case in 2009. But it is one reading among others. The adaptation account says there is no comparison at all. Neurons that answered the repeated tone have become less responsive, while a new tone finds fresh neurons. In 2010, a review by May and Tiitinen argued that the MMN is a modulated expression of the auditory N one response, generated by fresh neurons under uneven adaptation. A third account, model adjustment, says the brain updates a model of the sound stream. Garrido and colleagues proposed that predictive coding unifies the two. So the wave is a measurement. Which mechanism makes it stays open.

Now the nuance that surprises. What does a classical predictive coding level predict? The activity of the level below. At the bottom of the loop sits the sensory input itself. So the error is measured on the input. Does that remind you of something? A decoder that repaints, and a scale placed in pixel space, as in the autoencoder of chapter six. A 2021 review by Millidge, Seth and Buckley describes the theory as minimizing prediction errors with respect to a generative model of the world. In the vocabulary of this series, classical predictive coding sits with the painters.

Remember episode 6.5. A loss on the input pays for every detail, even the shimmer of a leaf that nobody could foresee. JEPA does the opposite. In episode 11.2, PREDI guesses a ghost necklace, and the scale stands in representation space, never in pixel space. This is a reading of the series, not a result of either paper. Higher levels of a hierarchy do predict abstractions, so the split is not clean. But the classical loop is anchored on the input, and JEPA is not.

What does experiment say? In 2012, Keller, Bonhoeffer and Hübener recorded in layer two three of mouse primary visual cortex, while mice ran in virtual reality. Responses were strongly driven by a mismatch between the actual and the expected visual feedback. In 2016, Bell and colleagues recorded single neurons in the macaque inferior temporal cortex. Face neurons fired less to expected faces, and the expected stimulus was easier to decode from the population. They concluded that this supports predictive coding.

Now the critiques. In 2020, Walsh and colleagues reviewed the new neurophysiology, and noted that these models are criticized for lacking the empirical support to justify their status. In 2021, Millidge, Seth and Buckley pointed out that expectation suppression is also well explained by neural adaptation. They add that finding a neuron that signals a mismatch does not settle whether it carries an error or the prediction itself. And in 2025, Gabhart, Xiong and Bastos wrote that recent spiking studies suggest genuine prediction errors emerge in prefrontal cortex. That challenges classical predictive coding, and fits an alternative they call predictive routing.

So which label goes on the case? Mechanism shown: in several species, some neurons respond to violated expectations. Analogy: that the whole cortex is a stack of predict-and-correct loops, and any comparison with JEPA. As of November 2026, the evidence is real but contested.

To sum up. Predictive coding sends predictions down and errors up. Rao and Ballard's network in 1999 explained end-stopping this way. The mismatch negativity fits the idea, and fits the adaptation account too. The classical loop predicts the input, so it sits closer to a generative model than to JEPA. And the evidence is real but contested. Next time: the Bayesian brain and free energy.

The Bayesian brain and free energy

Belief is a plank balanced between a prior and the evidence, each weighted by its reliability, the inverse of its variance. Free energy is a variational upper bound on surprise, the same mathematics as the evidence lower bound, lowered by perception or by action. LeCun's energy shares the word but is only a compatibility score that ranks candidates.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Last time, the brain sent predictions down and kept only the errors. But a prediction is never a single number. It comes with a confidence. Today we put confidence on the scale, and then we meet a famous idea, and a word that two different communities use for two different things.

Start with a plank balanced on a pivot. On the left sits a weight for what the brain expected before the evidence arrived. Call it the prior. On the right sits a weight for what the senses report now. Call it the likelihood. The size of each weight is its reliability. A sharp, trustworthy signal is a heavy weight. A vague signal is a light one.

Now let the pivot slide until the plank balances. Where it stops is the belief. Bayes' rule says exactly this: the posterior is the prior times the likelihood, then rescaled. For bell-shaped uncertainty, the balance point is a weighted average, and each weight is the inverse of the variance.

Here is an illustrative case. The prior says two, with a variance of four. The sense says eight, with a variance of one. The sense is four times more reliable, so it carries eighty percent of the weight, and the belief lands at six point eight. It also gets sharper than either input: the variance falls to zero point eight. These numbers are made up, to show the arithmetic.

Is the brain really a balance like this? One careful experiment says it comes close. In two thousand and two, Ernst and Banks asked people to judge the height of a ridge, using sight and touch together. They first measured how noisy each sense was alone. Then they blurred the picture, and the sense of touch gained influence, just as the weights predict. The combined judgments had thresholds close to what the model predicted.

That is one task. Knill and Pouget, in two thousand and four, drew out the wider claim, which they call the Bayesian coding hypothesis: that neurons represent uncertainty, not only a best guess. They state plainly that behavior supports it, while direct neural evidence was almost non-existent. So the plank is a model of behavior, and its neural basis remains an open question. The same review describes a second kind of evidence, about priors. A simple prior that favors slow motion, multiplied with ambiguous local motion signals, reproduces a surprising range of motion illusions. The prior, like the weight on the plank, pulls the belief toward what is usually true.

Now a harder problem. The plank worked because the numbers were simple. In a real brain, the causes of the senses are many and tangled, and computing the exact posterior means summing over every possible cause. That sum is usually out of reach.

So Karl Friston proposed a trick, in his two thousand and ten review in Nature Reviews Neuroscience, building on his earlier papers. Do not compute the posterior. Instead, hold a guess about it, a blot of belief. Call the blot q. Then measure how badly the blot and the senses fit the model, with one number: the free energy.

This number has a useful property. It is always at least as large as the surprise, which is how unlikely the senses were under the model. So free energy is an upper bound on surprise. The gap is a K L divergence: how far the blot is from the true posterior. When the blot equals the posterior, the gap closes, and the bound is tight.

Here is a tiny, illustrative world with three possible causes. The surprise of what was sensed is one point two. A blot spread evenly over the causes has free energy of one point two seven. A blot that matches the prior has one point six. A blot that sits on the wrong cause climbs to one point seven six. Only the true posterior touches the surprise itself, at one point two.

You may recognize this. It is the evidence lower bound from episode six point two, the one the variational autoencoder trains. Friston's free energy is that bound with the sign flipped. The Information series covers the K L divergence and variational bounds. For the full derivation, see the Information series.

Friston adds a second move. Lowering free energy can happen in two ways. The brain can change the blot, so that its belief fits the senses. That is perception. Or the body can act, so that the senses start to fit the belief. Friston calls this active inference. On the plank, perception slides the pivot, and action drags the sensory weight toward it. Under bell-shaped assumptions, lowering free energy becomes a descent driven by prediction errors, each one weighted by its precision. That is the error signal of last episode, now with a confidence attached to every error.

Now the strong moment of this episode. Friston's word is free energy. But in episode eight point one, we met another energy, the one of Yann LeCun. They share a word. Are they the same thing?

LeCun's energy is a score. Give it an input and a candidate answer, and it says how well they fit together: low when compatible, high when not. It only needs to rank candidates. It does not need to add up to one, and it needs no normalizing constant, the Z of the Gibbs recipe.

LeCun also has a free energy. When a hidden variable is in play, the energy is minimized over it, or softly averaged over it, as in episode eight point three. And that soft version equals the lowest value the variational free energy can reach over all blots, when the energy is taken to be minus the log of the joint probability. So the mathematics can coincide.

What differs is the job each one is asked to do. Friston's free energy must bound a surprise, so it needs a real probability model, with a prior and a likelihood. LeCun's energy only has to rank, so it can be any score a network learns. A JEPA energy is a prediction error between two representations. No likelihood is required, and no bound on surprise is claimed. Same word, shared mathematics in one special case, different demands.

Before we close, what is debated. In two thousand and twelve, Bowers and Davis argued that many Bayesian accounts are just-so stories, flexible enough to explain almost any behavior as optimal. Others, such as Griffiths and colleagues, replied that this misreads the approach. For free energy itself, a known objection is the dark room: if organisms avoid surprise, why not sit in the dark? Friston, Thornton and Clark answered, in two thousand and twelve, that priors define which states are surprising, and a dark room is surprising to an animal that expects to eat. Whether the principle makes testable predictions remains discussed, and we will not take sides.

So, in short. Belief balances a prior against evidence, weighted by confidence. Friston's free energy is a variational bound on surprise, the same mathematics as the evidence lower bound. LeCun's energy is a compatibility score, and it only ranks. Next time: cognitive maps and the hippocampus.

Cognitive maps and the hippocampus

Tolman's rats choosing a shortcut are behavior, and calling it a cognitive map is an interpretation. Place cells and grid cells are recordings showing that position is written in cells, but calling them a spatial world model is an analogy. Two models learn such cells by prediction: the successor representation, and the Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine, which separates abstract location from what is sensed.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cabinet of curiosities. Every vitrine holds one idea from neuroscience, with two labels on it: analogy, and mechanism shown. Only one label lights up, and the rule of this chapter is simple. An analogy is never a proof. Today's vitrine holds a map. Does the brain keep a world model of space? And if it does, how could such a map be learned? By the end, you will know three kinds of evidence, two computational models, and, for each one, what is data and what is model.

Start with the rats. In the 1940s, Edward Tolman and his colleagues at Berkeley trained rats to run from a start point, across a round table, and along a winding alley to a food box. Then the alley was blocked. The table now offered twelve paths that had not been there before, fanning out like a sunburst. Tolman argued that a learned chain of turns could not pick one of them. Of fifty-three rats, nineteen, about a third, chose path number six, the one that ended closest to where the food had been. The other thirty-four spread over the eleven remaining paths. That is the data: behavior. In his 1948 paper, Tolman read it as a cognitive map of the room. The word map is his interpretation, so this vitrine lights the label analogy.

In chapter 9, we called this model-based behavior: choosing with a model instead of replaying a habit. Tolman's rats look like model-based agents. But behavior alone cannot tell us what is inside the head.

Three decades later, the map got cells. In 1971, John O'Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky pushed fine electrodes into the hippocampus of freely moving rats. A few cells fired only when the animal was in one particular part of a platform, and kept doing so when sound and smell cues were controlled. These are place cells, each with its own place field. Picture the population as a necklace: one bead per cell. The bead whose field contains the rat lights up. Walk to another spot, and another bead takes over. This is a recording, so this vitrine lights mechanism shown: in the rat hippocampus, position is written in cells. In a different room, the fields rearrange. That is called remapping, and it will matter later.

In 2005, May-Britt Moser, Edvard Moser and their colleagues found a second kind of cell, in the neighboring entorhinal cortex. A grid cell does not fire at one place. It fires at many, at the corners of a lattice of equilateral triangles that tiles the whole room. Along the entorhinal axis, the lattices grow larger and more widely spaced. In 2014, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went half to O'Keefe, and half to the Mosers, for their discoveries of cells that constitute a positioning system in the brain. The press called it an inner GPS. That nickname is an analogy. The recordings are the mechanism shown.

Now put this next to chapter 10. A world model predicts what comes next if you act. A map of space is the part that says what is next to what: which place you reach if you step north, and which paths are blocked. With such a map, an animal could plan a shortcut or a detour it never tried. Is the hippocampal formation that kind of map? Position cells are data. Calling the whole system a spatial world model is our comparison, not a result. So the label stays analogy.

But how would such a map be learned? Two computational models answer: by prediction. Both are models. The data are the recordings. Let's keep them apart.

First, the successor representation. Peter Dayan proposed it in 1993, for reinforcement learning. For every place, it stores a necklace with one bead per place in the room. Each bead says how often you expect to be on that place in the future, discounted by how far ahead, given where you stand and how you usually move. A place you will reach soon gets a big bead. A place behind a wall gets a tiny one. It is learned one step at a time, from a prediction error, like the value learning of chapter 9.

In 2017, Kimberly Stachenfeld, Matthew Botvinick and Samuel Gershman proposed that a place cell reads out one entry of this table. Here the model meets data. On a track run in one direction, the model's fields skew backward, against the direction of travel. Mehta and colleagues had recorded such a skew in 2000. In a maze with a detour, the model's fields change mostly near the blocked path. Alvernhe and colleagues recorded that pattern in place cells, in 2011, in a Tolman detour maze. Both comparisons are made on earlier recordings, and the paper calls the skew match qualitative.

For grid cells, the proposal is a compressed summary. Take the main patterns hidden in the table, its eigenvectors. In an open room, they repeat across space, like grid fields. But the model has a known gap. Its patterns are not yet convincing hexagons, and the paper says that extra constraints, such as non-negative values, are needed to get closer.

Notice what this model predicts: where you will be, not the next image. That is closer in spirit to predicting a necklace than predicting pixels. It is an analogy, nothing more. Nobody has shown that the hippocampus works like a JEPA.

Second, the Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine, from James Whittington, Timothy Behrens and colleagues, in Cell, in 2020. It is named for Tolman and for Howard Eichenbaum. Its insight is that the structure of a space, which place is next to which, is shared by many rooms, while what sits in each room changes. So the model keeps two streams. One carries abstract location, which the authors map to medial entorhinal cortex. One carries what is sensed, mapped to lateral entorhinal cortex. A hippocampus-like memory binds the two.

The network learns from one task: after each step, predict the next sensory observation. It never sees the map. It sees only observations and the steps between them, on many graphs that share a structure but differ in their objects. After training, its location units resemble grid, band and border cells, and its memory units resemble place cells that remap between rooms.

The authors also made a prediction: remapping is not random. They tested it on earlier recordings from rats and mice. For place and grid cells recorded together, the grid pattern at a place field's peak stayed correlated across rooms. The correlation was about 0.3 in two datasets, and 0.31 in the model. The authors say plainly that the model is not biophysically realistic.

One more detail. Its training signal is the error on the predicted observation, not a prediction made in a hidden space. In that respect, it sits nearer to a generative predictor than to a JEPA. The paper does not make that comparison. It is ours.

To sum up. Tolman's rats are behavior, and map is an interpretation. Place cells and grid cells are recorded: mechanism shown. Calling them a spatial world model is an analogy. The successor representation and the Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine are models that learn such cells by prediction. Each reproduces some recorded patterns, and whether the brain computes this way is still open. Next time: replay and mental simulation, what the hippocampus does while the animal rests.

Replay and mental simulation

Hippocampal replay is recorded: place-cell sequences replay in sleep, forward and in reverse, even toward goals never visited. Its resemblance to learning from imagined moves in Dyna and Dreamer is an analogy. Replay is not a simple copy of experience, preplay is disputed, and disrupting replay gives mixed results, so the evidence neither shows nor rules out that the brain does Dreamer.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cabinet of curiosities. Last time, the vitrine held a map of space, and the question was what the hippocampus does while the animal rests. Tonight the lights go down, and the rat falls asleep. The two labels are still analogy, and mechanism shown. By the end, you will know what recordings show about replay, how it resembles learning in imagination, and where the resemblance stops.

Start with a plain object: a player-piano roll. Each row of the roll is one place cell, the beads of the last episode, sorted by where its field lies along a track. When the rat runs, each cell punches a hole as the animal crosses its field. Run from left to right, and the holes draw a staircase. That staircase is the day's trajectory, written as a score. The roll is our picture, not data.

Now the recordings. In nineteen ninety-four, Wilson and McNaughton recorded large groups of hippocampal cells in rats during a spatial task, and then during slow-wave sleep. Cells that had fired together during the task fired together more often in the sleep that followed than in the sleep before. Cells that were silent, or whose fields did not overlap, showed no such effect. In our picture, the roll is played again while the rat sleeps. This is a recording, so the label is mechanism shown.

Then replay turned up in the waking animal. In two thousand six, Foster and Wilson reported that, right after a run, the hippocampus of a resting rat replays that run in reverse order. The roll plays backward, from the goal to the start. In two thousand seven, Diba and Buzsáki found reverse order at the end of a run and forward order before a run, both during sharp waves, brief bursts in the hippocampal activity. The roll now plays in both directions.

It also plays fast. In the open-field recordings of Pfeiffer and Foster, in two thousand thirteen, a replayed trajectory lasted on average about a tenth of a second, a hundred and four milliseconds. The path it depicted measured between forty centimeters and two meters.

That same study is the most interesting one for our question. Before a rat set off toward a remembered goal, the sequences traced paths from its current position toward that goal. They predicted its next movements, even when the start and the goal formed a new pair. Four rats, recorded on two days, up to two hundred and fifty cells at a time. The authors suggest that this may support planning. They say it remains unknown whether such events can reflect the calculation of optimal paths.

One more result stretches the roll further. In two thousand fifteen, Ólafsdóttir and colleagues let rats look at two arms of a maze through a barrier, then showed them food in one arm. The rats never walked there. During later rest, sequences matching the baited arm were more frequent, seven point four percent of events, against four point four percent for the other arm, which was not distinguishable from chance. The authors call it preplay.

Now the other half of the analogy. In nineteen ninety, Richard Sutton proposed Dyna, an agent that alternates between acting and imagining. In the textbook account by Sutton and Barto, Dyna learns a model from real moves, then runs the same learning rule on moves that the model simulates, as if they had really happened. Learning and planning differ only in where the experience comes from. In their toy maze of forty-seven squares, the agent without planning needed about twenty-five episodes to reach near-optimal behavior. The agents with planning needed about five, and three.

Dreamer, from episode ten point three, does this at scale. Its actor and critic practise in imagined trajectories of about fifteen steps, starting from states that were really seen. And its world model learns from a replay of stored experience. Both words live in one agent.

In two thousand eighteen, Mattar and Daw drew the link explicitly, with a model built on Dyna. Which memory should the agent replay next? The one with the highest need times gain: how soon the agent will probably be there again, times how much a better choice there would pay. In their simulations, this rule gave forward sequences before a run, reverse sequences after it, and replay biased toward the rewarded arm, as in the recordings above. It is a model, compared with existing recordings. So the label here is analogy.

Now the studies that qualify it. In two thousand ten, Gupta and colleagues recorded six rats on a maze, and found that replay is not a simple copy of what the animal did. Paths run rarely were replayed, and nineteen shortcut sequences, from three rats, joined places the animal had never connected by running. These shortcuts are a handful of events.

In two thousand fifteen, Silva, Feng and Foster recorded hundreds of cells in four rats in new environments. Events before the first experience were not correlated with the later run. Trajectory events appeared only after experience, and a drug blocking N M D A receptors during experience prevented them. That challenges preplay. Two designs, two answers: the question is open.

The models have limits too. Mattar and Daw's version replays only transitions that were really experienced, so it cannot build a path never walked.

Does replay matter for learning? In two thousand twelve, Jadhav and colleagues interrupted sharp waves in awake rats learning a spatial task, and the rats learned worse, while place fields stayed intact. In two thousand twenty-four, Deceuninck and Kloosterman disrupted them in fifteen rats on three spatial memory tasks, and performance did not change. They suggest that the effect on memory may be long-term rather than immediate. And even a causal role would not say what replay computes. None of these studies shows a hippocampus training a policy on imagined rollouts through a learned model, as Dreamer does.

To sum up. Replay is recorded: in sleep, forward and in reverse, and even toward a place never visited. That is mechanism shown. That it resembles Dyna and Dreamer, learning from imagined moves, is analogy. Studies qualify it: replay is not a copy of experience, preplay is disputed, and the models cannot yet build new paths. So the evidence does not show that the brain does Dreamer, and it does not rule it out. Next time: learning without labels, the infant.

Learning without labels: the infant

Infants look longer at impossible events, such as a screen passing through a hidden box, read as surprise at a failed prediction, though the measures are debated. Networks trained without labels on one child's headcam video learn useful visual representations and word-to-image links. These results show what can be learned from little data, not how a child learns.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome back to the cabinet of curiosities. Each display case holds one idea from neuroscience, with two labels: analogy, and mechanism shown. Our guide is BRAIN, and the rule of this chapter has not changed: no analogy is ever offered as proof. Today's case holds an infant, and one question: how much can be learned without labels?

Start with a baby in front of a small stage. In nineteen eighty-five, Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman tested infants about five and a half months old. First, the infants watched a screen swing back and forth through a half circle, like a drawbridge, until they got bored. Then a box was placed in the path of the screen. In the possible event, the screen stopped when it reached the box, after one hundred and twenty degrees. In the impossible event, the screen kept going, through one hundred and eighty degrees, as if the box were not there. The infants looked significantly longer at the impossible event.

The reading is that the infants kept track of a box they could no longer see, expected the screen to stop, and were surprised that it did not. In nineteen ninety-two, Spelke and colleagues summarized this kind of experiment: early physical reasoning accords with two constraints, continuity and solidity. Keep the logic in mind. Longer looking means more surprise, and surprise means that a prediction failed.

A caution about the method. A twenty twenty-four review by Margoni, Surian and Baillargeon discusses the objection that longer looking may only reflect a familiarity or novelty preference, and the control experiments that address it. And in twenty twenty-five, Michel and colleagues measured the pupils of nine- to ten-month-olds. They found no clear difference between expected and unexpected outcomes, in four domains that included solidity, with only twenty-one infants left in the analysis. So the classic effect is real, and the ways of measuring it are still debated.

Now the networks. To train on what a baby sees, you need what a baby sees. SAYCam is a dataset published in twenty twenty-one by Sullivan and colleagues. Three children wore a head camera for about two hours a week, between six and thirty-two months of age.

In twenty twenty-four, Orhan and Lake trained self-supervised networks on about two hundred hours of headcam video from one child, with no labels and no built-in object detectors. On average over their tests, the best models reached about seventy percent of the performance of a strong network trained on ImageNet. That is comparable to a network trained on ten percent of ImageNet. The results held with two other children. But the models were less object-centered than the ImageNet network, and the generative ones struggled with fine details.

Also in twenty twenty-four, in Science, Vong, Wang, Orhan and Lake added words. Their model, called CVCL, learned from six hundred thousand video frames, paired with thirty-seven thousand five hundred transcribed utterances. All of it came from sixty-one hours of one child's video, between six and twenty-five months. The training is contrastive, like chapter seven: a frame and an utterance that occur together are pulled together. Nobody labeled an image.

The test used twenty-two everyday words, such as ball and car. Given a word and four images, the model picked the right image sixty-one point six percent of the time. Chance is twenty-five percent, and a version trained on shuffled pairs scored twenty-six point seven. CLIP, trained on four hundred million image and text pairs from the web, scored sixty-six point seven. On sixty-four new words, with objects on a plain background, accuracy fell to thirty-four point seven percent.

Now the quantities, with care. The authors estimate that sixty-one hours is about one percent of this child's waking hours, and that the language in the training set is between zero point eight and two point two percent of what the child heard. Both are rough estimates, resting on assumptions that they state.

Back to the stage. Can a network be surprised like an infant? Benchmarks show a network pairs of possible and impossible videos, and its prediction error becomes a surprise score. In February twenty twenty-five, Garrido and colleagues reported that models that predict masked parts of natural video, in a representation space, like V-JEPA, showed signs of understanding object permanence. Models that predict pixels, and multimodal language models, stayed close to chance. Even models trained on about one week of unique video did better than chance.

Then, in December twenty twenty-five, a workshop preprint by Su, LeGris, Gureckis and Ren trained V-JEPA on SAYCam itself: four hundred and seventy-two hours from three children. On the IntPhys two benchmark, it scored zero point five zero. That is chance. A V-JEPA trained on more than one point seven million hours scored zero point five two. Humans scored ninety-six point four four percent. The authors list limits: the data are small, only visual, and leave out the child's own movements. So this is one early result, and the question is open.

So which label goes on the case? Mechanism shown: generic self-supervised learning, on slices of one child's video, gives useful visual representations and word-to-image links, with no labels added by anyone. Analogy: that the child learns the way the network does. That is not shown. The networks learn from still frames or short clips, passively, from transcribed words instead of speech, with gradient descent. The child moves, acts, hears, and lives through far more than these recordings hold.

To sum up. Infants look longer at impossible events, a classic result whose measures are still debated. Networks trained on one child's video learn a lot without labels. These numbers show what can be learned from little data, not how a child learns. And the surprise test does not yet come out the same way. Next time: self-supervised models as models of the brain.

PLATE 14.66:27

Self-supervised models as models of the brain

Networks and brains are compared through representational similarity tables, like tracing paper laid over tracing paper, or through linear encoding models. Self-supervised networks predict neural responses about as well as supervised ones, with limits, and also serve as tools for decoding speech from MEG and EEG. A good prediction is not an explanation: two systems can predict each other without sharing a mechanism.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the cabinet of curiosities. Each display case holds one idea from neuroscience, and carries two labels: analogy, and mechanism shown. Today's case holds a question that runs in two directions. Can a network that learned without labels be a hypothesis about the cortex? And can the same kind of network be a tool for reading your EEG? By the end, you will know how a network is compared with a brain, what the results show, and why predicting a brain is not explaining it.

First, a practical problem. No network unit matches any neuron or electrode. So in 2008, Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini proposed to compare the similarity structure instead. Show the same images to the brain region and to the network. For every pair of images, measure how different the two activity patterns are, with one minus their correlation. Arrange the numbers in a square table: a representational dissimilarity matrix, or R D M.

Our picture for this video is tracing paper. Draw the brain's table on one sheet, and the network's table on a transparent sheet. Slide one over the other. If the pairs that look alike for the brain also look alike for the network, the marks line up. The agreement is a rank correlation. The toy on screen is illustrative. With the right images in the right places, the correlation is zero point six three. Shuffle which image is which, and it falls to zero point zero nine. This is representational similarity analysis.

A second tool is the encoding model: fit a linear regression from one layer to each recorded site, then test it on images it never saw. It is the linear readout of episode 4.3, aimed at the brain. Brain-Score, from Schrimpf and colleagues in 2020, packages many such datasets as benchmarks.

Now the results. In 2014, Yamins, DiCarlo and colleagues recorded from two macaques, with five thousand seven hundred sixty images. Their best network was optimised for object categorisation, with only a linear readout fitted to neurons. Its top layer predicted forty-eight point five percent of the explainable variance in the inferior temporal cortex. The same year, Khaligh-Razavi and Kriegeskorte compared thirty-seven models with that region. A deep network trained with labels came closest, but did not reach the noise ceiling. The unsupervised models of that time largely failed to explain the category clusters.

But labels are costly, as we saw in episode 5.2, and no infant receives them. So the test was repeated. In 2021, Zhuang and colleagues trained networks with a contrastive method, with the labels set aside. On macaque data from V one, V four and the inferior temporal cortex, the predictions matched or exceeded the supervised control. They report limits: SimCLR did significantly worse in V one and V four, and only feedforward networks were tested.

In 2022, Konkle and Alvarez used human fMRI. Their model treats every image as its own category, and never sees a label. In fifty-six of sixty comparisons, it was equal to, or better than, its supervised twin. Now the scope. In the higher region, for everyday objects, it explained about thirty-two percent of the variance, against twenty-eight. No model fully explained that region. And at classifying, the supervised network won: fifty-eight point eight percent, against thirty-seven point three. As good at predicting these responses, not as good at the task.

Now the bridge to your EEG, in two directions. First, the network as a tool. Défossez and colleagues, in 2023, took four public datasets: one hundred seventy-five volunteers listening to speech, with MEG or EEG. A network reads three seconds of brain signal, and must find which speech segment was heard, among more than a thousand. Its target is wav2vec 2.0, pretrained without labels. Top-ten accuracy was seventy point seven and sixty-seven point five percent on the two MEG datasets, but twenty-five point seven and seventeen point seven on the two EEG datasets. That is perception, not production, and EEG is clearly harder. Episode 11.8 had the same spirit.

Second, the brain as a judge of the network. Millet and colleagues, in 2022, compared wav2vec 2.0 with fMRI from four hundred twelve listeners. Trained on six hundred hours of speech without labels, it already predicted auditory cortex activity. Deeper layers matched higher regions. Trained on the same speech with phoneme labels, it scored slightly lower: a correlation difference of zero point zero zero two. Real, but tiny.

Now the part where the case needs its most careful label. A good prediction is not an explanation. Bowers and colleagues, in 2023, remind us that two systems can predict each other without sharing a mechanism. A digital clock can predict a mechanical clock, and still be a different machine. Their remedy is experiments that change one variable, rather than a race for the best score. Kanwisher, Khosla and Dobs, also in 2023, defend another use: optimise a network for a task, then ask why the brain works as it does.

One warning about a word. A network can predict a brain recording, or learn by predicting, as in JEPA. One does not imply the other.

So which label goes on the case? Mechanism shown: on these datasets, self-supervised networks predict responses about as well as supervised ones. Analogy: that the cortex learns this way, or that JEPA is how it does it.

To sum up. Networks and brains are compared by similarity tables or encoding models. Self-supervised networks match supervised ones on some neural data, with limits. They are tools and hypotheses. Predicting activity is not explaining it. Next time: the bonus chapter, in the makerspace, B.1, a tiny autoencoder.
