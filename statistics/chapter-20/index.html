PLATE 20.17:07

Overfitting

A student who memorises practice papers scores perfectly, but the real exam tests new questions. A degree 9 curve passes through all ten points with error zero, yet on fresh points errs 0.508 against 0.117 for degree 3. Training error only falls, while fresh error bottoms out. Flexible curves aim well but shake. The goal, explaining or predicting, decides the model.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Welcome to the exam room. A student gets last year's practice papers and learns every answer by heart. On those papers, the score is perfect. Then the real paper arrives, with new questions.

Today's idea fits in one sentence. How well a model fits the data it already has says nothing about how well it will predict. A model is judged on data it has never seen.

Here is a simulation, so that we can know the truth. A hidden curve, a gentle wave. In real life we never see it. We measure ten points along it, each with a little noise: a random error of 0.3. These ten points are simulated.

Now we fit curves, and we choose how bendy a curve may be. The bendiness is called the degree. Degree 0 is a flat line at the average. Degree 1 is a straight line. Degree 3 can bend twice. Degree 9 can bend eight times.

We score each curve by its mean squared error on these ten points, the error of episode 5.1: the miss at each point, squared, then averaged. Degree 0 scores 0.676. Degree 3 scores 0.067. And degree 9 passes through all ten points, so its score is exactly zero.

Guess before you see: is degree 9 the best curve?

Now break the seal. Ten fresh points from the same hidden curve were waiting in an envelope. The degree 9 curve swings wildly between the old points, and the fresh points land far from it. On fresh points, its error is 0.508. Degree 3 scores 0.117. The perfect curve is more than four times worse than the modest one.

This fresh error is averaged over every possible fresh point along the curve, noise included, not just ten.

Plot both errors against the degree. The training error only falls, to zero. The fresh error falls, bottoms out at degree 3, then climbs back up. And it never goes below 0.09, which is the noise itself. No curve can predict noise.

Training error rewards memory. Fresh error rewards the pattern.

Is this one lucky draw? Repeat the whole study 1,000 times. Averaged over the 1,000 studies, the fresh error is 0.127 at degree 3, and 0.665 at degree 9, more than five times as much. In 992 studies out of 1,000, degree 9 loses to degree 3.

Here is a detail that could look contradictory. In our one study, degree 9 scored 0.508, not 0.665: that study was milder than the average. And in a single study, the winner varies. Degree 3 wins in 551 studies, degree 5 in 277, degree 4 in 98. The average has one best degree. A single study has its own.

Now turn a knob: the number of points. With 30 points, degree 9 scores 0.060 on its own points, and 0.117 on fresh ones. Degree 3 is still best, at 0.106, and degree 9 is only about a tenth worse. With 100 points, the best degree is 5, at 0.095, and degree 9 is only a few percent worse. More data forgives flexible models.

Now turn the noise, with ten points. At noise 0.15, the best degree is 5. At 0.3, it is 3, and at 0.6 it is still 3. But look at the gap for degree 9, the fresh error minus the training error. It is 0.169, then 0.665, then 2.664. Double the noise, and the gap grows about four times.

This is the trade of episode 5.1, seen from the side of prediction. Split the fresh error of the 1,000 curves into three parts. The noise floor. The bias squared: how far the average curve sits from the truth. And the variance: how much the curves shake from one study to the next.

For degree 1, the bias squared is 0.222. A straight line cannot follow a wave. Its variance is only 0.017. For degree 9, the bias squared is 0.001, but the variance is 0.574. Add the 0.09 of noise, and you get 0.665, exactly. Flexible curves aim well and shake. Stiff curves are steady and aim wrong. The best curve sits in between.

The same trap hides in many fields. These examples are illustrative. A student who memorises past papers: the data never seen is the real exam. A sales forecast tuned to last year's calendar: next year. A brain decoder with 2,000 features and 60 trials fits those trials perfectly: the data never seen are new trials, or a new person. A high-degree growth curve through a child's measurements predicts a nonsense height at twelve: the later years. A reaction-time model with 30 predictors for 40 people: the next 40 people. A stock-picking rule found by searching old prices: next year's market.

Each time, ask: what would be the data it has never seen?

There is a second lesson. A model can serve two goals: explaining a mechanism, or predicting a new case. In 2001, Breiman described two cultures of statistics. One assumes a stochastic model of how the data arise. The other judges models by how well they predict. In 2010, Shmueli argued that explaining and predicting are different goals. In 2017, Yarkoni and Westfall made the case for psychology: a simpler, biased model can beat a model closer to the true mechanism, because of overfitting.

Here is a simulated example. An outcome has two causes. The first has an effect of 1.0. The second has a small effect of 0.3. Noise is 3, the causes are correlated at 0.5, and each study has 15 people. The full model uses both causes. Averaged over 5,000 studies, it finds an effect of 0.99 for the first cause. It explains well. The small model leaves out the second cause, and reports 1.15: wrong for explaining. Yet on new people, the small model errs by 10.49, against 11.36 for the full model, and it wins in 75 percent of studies. Estimating a weak effect from 15 people costs more variance than it saves bias. The goal decides the model.

Remember: the fit to the data you already have only improves as a model grows more flexible, so it cannot choose the model. Judge a model on data it has never seen. Next time: how to get that unseen data when you have only one dataset, with cross-validation.

PLATE 20.27:23

Cross-validation

With one dataset, cross-validation covers each fifth in turn with a cloth, fitting on the rest: scores 70, 60, 85, 90 and 85 give 78 percent. Three leaks spoil it. Choosing features before cutting gives 90.9 percent on pure noise. Tuning on the test parts is optimistic. Cutting trials rather than people lets the model recognise people. Leave out the unit you generalise to.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the exam room. The rule of 20.1: judge a model on data it has never seen. But what if you own a single dataset? Today we make unseen data out of the data we have, and we meet three leaks and one limit.

Here is a simulation. One hundred students. For each one, ten homework marks, and whether the student passed the exam. The model is simple, and I will name it: nearest centroid. Average the marks of the students who passed. Average the marks of the students who failed. A new student gets the group whose average is closer.

Cut the hundred students at random into five parts of twenty. Cover part one with a cloth, and fit the model on the other four parts. Then lift the cloth and score the model on the covered students. Fourteen of twenty are right: 70 percent.

Turn the wheel. Cover part two: twelve of twenty, 60 percent. Part three: seventeen. Part four: eighteen. Part five: seventeen.

Every part was tested once, and never while it helped to train. The five scores are 70, 60, 85, 90 and 85. Their sum is 390, and 390 divided by 5 is exactly 78 percent. This is five-fold cross-validation. In 1974, Stone published a paper on cross-validatory choice and assessment of statistical predictions.

Is 78 percent the truth? We can check, because we are simulating. Fit the same model on all hundred students, and test it on a hundred thousand fresh students. It scores 72.4 percent. The best possible rule scores 75. So our 78 was a little optimistic: one dataset, and datasets have luck. Keep that in mind.

Now the leaks. A leak is any way for the covered data to whisper to the model. First leak: choosing variables before cutting. Picture an EEG decoder: forty trials, a thousand features, and a label for each trial, stimulus A or stimulus B. Here the labels are coin flips, unrelated to every feature, so the honest accuracy is 50 percent. Guess before you see. Pick the ten features that best separate A from B, using all forty trials. Then cross-validate. What accuracy do you get?

Ninety point nine percent. Over 2,000 simulated datasets with no signal at all, the mean is 90.9 percent, and the lowest single score is 77.5 percent.

The choosing step had seen the labels of the covered trials. Now do it honestly: choose the ten features again inside each training part. The mean falls to 48.2 percent: chance, and a little under, a quirk of holding data out when there is nothing to find. In 2009, Kriegeskorte and colleagues called the leaky version double dipping: the same data used to select, and then to test. They showed spurious effects appearing in pure noise.

The same trap with a questionnaire. Pick the twenty items most linked to a diagnosis, using everyone, then cross-validate. The items already know the answers.

Second leak: tuning on the test parts. A model has settings. Here, how many variables it reads and how many neighbours it consults: 90 combinations. Try all 90 with cross-validation, and report the best score. Simulated again: sixty observations, ten variables, a weak real signal. Over 1,000 datasets, the best of 90 scores averages 67.9 percent. But the chosen setting, tested on 5,000 fresh observations, averages 57.1 percent. The winner was the setting that got lucky on those parts.

A clinic predicts relapse from clinical measures, tries sixty model variants, and reports the best. A house price model has its knobs turned until the score looks good. Same leak.

The honest way is nested validation. Inside each training part, choose the setting with a second round of cross-validation, and use the covered part only to score. The result is 54.6 percent, a little under 57.1, because each fit sees fewer observations. It scores the whole procedure, tuning included. Varma and Simon measured this bias on datasets with no signal in 2006, and tested nested cross-validation.

One rule covers both leaks. Any step that has seen the test data is part of training. Choosing variables, tuning a setting, anything that learns from the data belongs inside the cut.

Third leak: who sits on which side. Forty people, twenty patients and twenty controls, ten EEG trials each. People differ from each other more than the diagnosis does. The model, nearest neighbour, copies the label of the most similar training trial. So it can recognise a person.

Cut the 400 trials at random into five parts, and one person's trials land on both sides. Over 500 simulated datasets, the score is 88.8 percent. Now cut by person: all the trials of a person go into the same part. The score is 62.3 percent. Which one is true? Test on 200 brand-new people: 62.6 percent. The cut by person agrees. The random cut measured how well the model recognises people it had already met.

In 11.2, a question about people in general lived on the people floor. So the unit we leave out must be the unit we want to generalise to. For scans from several hospitals, leave out whole hospitals. As in 14.4, we cut at the right unit.

One last point: the score itself has an error bar. Simulate samples of 50, 100, 200 and 1,000 observations from the exam process. For each, take the five-fold score, and compare it with the true accuracy of the fitted model on 20,000 fresh observations. Repeat 2,000 times per size.

At 100 observations, nine scores out of ten fall between 8.5 points below the truth and 7.1 above. At 50: 13.9 below, 10.8 above. At 200: 5.6 and 4.9. At 1,000: 2.4 and 2.2. Our first dataset was 5.6 points too high: an ordinary draw.

In 2018, Varoquaux reported that sample sizes of many neuroimaging studies inherently lead to large error bars, for example plus or minus 10 percent for 100 samples. He also warned that the spread across folds underestimates them. Here, at 100 observations, the score plus or minus 1.96 standard errors across the folds contained the truth in 84.7 percent of samples, not 95. Whether a score beats chance is the question of 20.4.

Remember: any step that has seen the test data is part of training, and the unit you leave out must be the unit you want to generalise to. Next time: regularization, ridge and lasso, a penalty that trades a little bias for much less variance.

PLATE 20.37:12

Regularization: ridge and lasso

With forty people and fifty predictors, ordinary regression fits perfectly yet errs 81.6 on fresh people, worse than guessing the average at 47.5. Ridge and lasso charge for coefficient size, like a heat lamp melting fifty ice pillars. Lasso reaches exact zeros and wins with a few strong effects; ridge wins with many small ones. Lambda is chosen by cross-validation, trading bias for variance.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the exam room. The rule of 20.1 and 20.2: judge a model on data it has never seen. Today, a model with too many knobs for too few people, and a way to tighten them. It is called regularization.

Here is a simulation. Forty people, and fifty predictors for each one: picture a reaction time, to be predicted from fifty EEG features. Only five of the fifty truly matter, with effects of 3, minus 3, 2, 2 and minus 2. The other forty-five do nothing, and the noise has a standard deviation of 4. In real life, nobody tells you which.

Ordinary regression has fifty unknowns, and only forty people. That is too few equations: there are infinitely many perfect fits. The one we show is the one with the smallest coefficients, the one numpy returns. On the forty people, its error is exactly zero. On a hundred thousand fresh people, its error is 81.6.

Guessing the training average every time, and ignoring every predictor, scores 47.5. So the perfect fit is worse than knowing nothing. And it is not even the worst perfect fit: another one scores 182.5.

Could we repair this by shrinking the coefficients? We can. Fit the data, but pay a price for coefficient size. There are two prices. Ridge charges the sum of the squared coefficients. Hoerl and Kennard proposed it in 1970. The lasso charges the sum of the absolute values, and Tibshirani proposed it in 1996. A number called lambda sets the price.

A price on sizes needs comparable units. So we first standardise every predictor. The intercept is never charged.

Picture fifty ice pillars, one per coefficient, under a heat lamp. The lamp is the penalty. Slide it closer, and the pillars melt. With ridge, they all melt together, and none ever disappears. The error on the forty people only rises, from almost zero to 37.2.

The fresh error does something else. It falls from 81.2 to a lowest 33.2, near lambda 20, then climbs back to 47.3, the error of the average. A little melting helps; too much melts the signal.

The lasso melts differently. Its small pillars reach exactly zero, one after another. At the weakest price, 38 pillars are standing. At the strongest, none. Its fresh error also falls and then climbs: the lowest is 24.8.

But how strong should the lamp be? We cannot peek at fresh data. So we choose the price by cross-validation, inside the training data: the nested logic of 20.2. Five folds of eight people. It picks lambda 35.6 for ridge, and 61.2 for the lasso.

Their fresh errors are 33.6 and 25.5, against 81.6 for ordinary regression. At its price, the lasso keeps 7 predictors out of fifty. All five true ones are among them, and 2 false ones too. Ridge keeps all fifty.

So far this is one draw of the dice. Is it a lucky dataset? Repeat the whole procedure on 1,000 new datasets, with the price chosen by cross-validation each time, and score each fit on 5,000 fresh people. Average fresh error: ordinary regression about 87, ridge 37.9, the lasso 30.2. The average guess scores 47.2.

The lasso beats ridge in 90 percent of the datasets. But our displayed dataset was a kind draw: over all 1,000, the lasso keeps 15.9 predictors on average, with 4.4 of the five true ones and 11.5 false ones: it does not promise the true set.

Now change the world. Same total signal, but spread thinly: all fifty predictors carry a small effect. Ordinary regression scores about 90, ridge 42.1, the lasso 49.6, and the average guess 56.6. Ridge beats the lasso in 88 percent of the datasets. A mix of the two exists, the elastic net, and we leave it there.

The choice appears everywhere. These examples are illustrative. A reaction time from sixty EEG features, in forty-five people. A child's reading score from a hundred and twenty questionnaire items, in ninety children. A disease risk from five thousand gene markers, in three hundred people. A house price from forty characteristics, in a hundred and twenty houses. A crop yield from eighty weather measurements, in thirty seasons.

A rule of thumb from our two simulated worlds: a few strong effects, try the lasso, and many small ones, try ridge.

Why does shrinking help, when it pulls every estimate away from the truth? Measure it. Forty people, thirty predictors, so ordinary regression exists. One true coefficient is 2. Estimate it on 5,000 simulated datasets.

Ordinary regression is unbiased: the average estimate is 2.00. But the estimates scatter, with a spread of 1.41. Ridge, at lambda 5, averages 1.53: biased toward zero, by 0.47. But its spread is only 0.72.

Square the misses and average them, as in 5.1: the bias squared plus the variance. Ordinary regression: 1.99, all of it variance. Ridge: 0.22 of bias plus 0.51 of variance, 0.73 in all.

The price has a limit. At lambda 160, the average estimate is 0.36, so much bias that the mean squared miss is 2.73, worse than ordinary regression.

We accept a little bias for much less variance.

Ridge has a second reading. Give every coefficient a normal prior, centred on zero, with width tau. The posterior mean is then exactly the ridge solution, with lambda equal to the noise variance divided by tau squared. On our dataset, lambda 35.6 means a tau of 0.67, and the two solutions agree to 1.8 times 10 to the minus 15 on all fifty coefficients: exact arithmetic, up to computer rounding.

It is the shrinkage of 16.6: a small tau pulls the schools together. It is the elastic strings of 11.4. And the lasso is the same idea with a pointier prior, as Tibshirani noted in 1996.

One caution: a lasso that keeps a predictor does not prove that the predictor matters.

Remember: a penalty on the size of the coefficients trades a little bias for much less variance, and its strength is chosen inside the training data. Next time: is this accuracy above chance?

PLATE 20.47:13

Is this accuracy above chance?

A decoder getting 24 of 40 trials right, 60 percent, may be luck. Shuffle the labels and redo the whole chain 10,000 times: 19 percent of shuffles score as high, so p is 0.188. The bar for chance rises when trials are few, 70.0 percent at twenty. Skipping a step, or shuffling trials instead of people, makes false alarms.

TRANSCRIPTEN

Back in the exam room. A decoder reads 40 trials and gets 24 right. That is 60 percent. Is it above chance?

Here is the simulation. Forty trials, twenty of each class, with ten features per trial. The decoder is the nearest centroid rule of 20.2, scored by five-fold cross-validation. The five folds get 3, 5, 4, 5 and 7 right out of eight. That makes 24 out of 40, 60 percent.

Before the next picture, guess. If the labels were random, how often would this whole machine score sixty percent or better?

Let the machine answer. Shuffle the labels, as in 14.1, and redo the whole chain: a new split into five folds, a new fit, a new score. Do it 10,000 times and pile up the scores. This is the curtain of chance. It is centred at 49.7 percent, but it is wide: a spread of 10.1 points. 1,875 shuffles out of 10,000 score 24 or more. So luck alone gives a score like ours about 19 percent of the time.

The p-value is the count plus one, over the shuffles plus one: 1,876 over 10,001, which is 0.188. This is the convention of Ojala and Garriga, 2010.

These features were pure noise. The decoder scored sixty percent on nothing.

There is a shortcut. If every answer were an independent coin toss, the binomial formula would give a threshold, and Combrisson and Jerbi, 2015, tabulate it. For forty trials, a score must exceed 62.5 percent to be significant at five percent. Sixty does not. But the curtain's five percent line sits at 65.0. A coin-toss spread would be 7.9 points; ours is 10.1. Cross-validated scores are less steady than coin tosses, so the shortcut is too generous.

Now turn one knob: the number of trials. For each size, run the chain on 10,000 sets of random labels, and set a bar where luck clears it only one time in twenty. With 20 trials, the bar is at 70.0 percent. With forty, 65.0. With a hundred, 60.0. With a thousand, 53.2. The fewer the trials, the higher the bar.

Their table gives 70.0, 62.5 and 58.0 for the first three sizes. Equal at twenty, a little lower after: the same trend.

So here is the moment to remember. The textbook chance level, fifty percent, holds only for infinitely many trials. On a small sample, chance routinely gives scores that look good. A score of sixty percent or better happens by luck in 27.0 percent of studies of twenty trials, in 17.9 percent of forty, in 6.0 percent of a hundred, and in none of 10,000 studies of a thousand.

That 17.9 is with fresh noise each time. The 19 before came from shuffling one dataset, so the two differ a little.

These examples are illustrative, and the thresholds are exact binomial arithmetic. Decoding a stimulus from forty EEG trials: 62.5. Telling a diagnosis from thirty brain scans: 63.3. Telling two speakers apart from sixty short recordings: 60.0. Predicting a choice from a hundred trials of physiological measures: 58.0. Classifying two handwritten letters from twenty-four examples: 66.7. A brain computer interface calibrated on twenty trials: 70.0.

Second knob: unbalanced classes. Thirty trials of one class and ten of the other. A rule that always answers the larger class scores 75 percent, without learning anything. Run the chain on 10,000 shuffles that keep the thirty and ten split. The chance level is no longer fifty. The curtain is centred at 55.8 percent. Our machine scores 62.5 percent, and the p-value is 0.288. It does not beat the lazy rule either.

Balanced accuracy, the average of the two classes' hit rates, puts the chance level of the shuffles back at 49.2 percent. One line, no more.

So the permutation test: shuffle the labels, redo every step of the chain, and count. Every step means the split, and any variable selection from 20.2, or any tuning from 20.3. Does it keep its promise? On 1,000 studies of pure noise, with 199 shuffles each, it rang a false alarm in 2.8 percent: never above five. With a real effect, best possible accuracy seventy-five percent, it rejected 64.7 percent.

Now skip a step on purpose. Take the leak of 20.2: from 1,000 noise features, keep the ten best on the real labels, then cross-validate. This dataset scores 33 out of 40. Shuffle the labels but keep those ten features, and redo only the cross-validation: 1 shuffle out of 10,000 reaches it. The p-value is 0.0002. Pure noise, declared highly significant. Over 1,000 noise datasets, this wrong shuffle rings a false alarm every single time.

Now shuffle the labels and redo the selection too. The curtain moves up to 90.9 percent, and the p-value is 0.988. Over 1,000 datasets, a false alarm in 1.2 percent. The curtain has to repeat every step the score went through. Better still, select inside each fold: the honest chain scores 23 out of 40, with p equal to 0.227.

One more way to shuffle wrongly: the unit. 20 people, ten trials each, ten patients and ten controls. Each person has a signature, the labels belong to people, and there is no effect at all. The folds go by person, as in 20.2. This study scores 69.0 percent. Shuffle the 200 trial labels: the curtain is narrow, a spread of 4.5 points, and p is 0.0001. Shuffle the 20 people, each carrying all their trials: the curtain is twice as wide, 10.2 points, and p is 0.018.

One study cannot tell which is right. So measure. Over 1,000 simulated studies with no effect, shuffling trials rings a false alarm in 21.4 percent. Shuffling people, in 4.8. That is the unit of 14.4.

Remember: a score is above chance only if it beats shuffled labels, with the whole chain redone and the right unit shuffled. Next time, chapter 21: 21.1, angles do not average like numbers.
